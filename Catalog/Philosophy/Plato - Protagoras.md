## About

A dialogue by Plato. Socrates and the young Hippocrates visit the famous sophist Protagoras at the house of Callias, and Socrates debates with him whether virtue can be taught and whether the virtues are one. Socrates narrates the meeting to an unnamed friend.

- **People:** Socrates; Protagoras; the sophists Hippias of Elis and Prodicus of Ceos; the host Callias; Hippocrates son of Apollodorus; Alcibiades; Pericles' sons Paralus and Xanthippus; and others, among them Eryximachus, Phaedrus, Pausanias, Agathon and Charmides.
- **Dramatic date:** Nails and Monoson (SEP) give 433/2 BC. Walsh (1984) reports the general view of a single meeting in the late 430s, fixed mainly by the presence of Pericles' sons (died c. 430/29); Athenaeus put it in the 420s. Walsh argues that Plato drew on more than one of Protagoras' visits, which would explain its anachronisms.
- **Date of composition:** the IEP places it in the early group, after Socrates' death and before Plato's first trip to Sicily in 387 BC. Its authenticity is not questioned in these sources.
- **Course of the argument:** Protagoras answers Socrates' doubt about teaching virtue with a myth of Prometheus and Epimetheus, in which Zeus sends Hermes to give all humans shame and justice. They then dispute whether virtue is one or many and how to conduct the argument, interpret a poem of Simonides, and end with Socrates' arguments that courage is knowledge and that being "overcome by pleasure" is a kind of ignorance.
- **Place among the dialogues:** its subtitle, *or the Sophists*, may not be Plato's (Wikipedia). Diogenes Laertius records it in Thrasyllus's sixth tetralogy, classed "critical", after *Euthydemus* and before *Gorgias* and *Meno*. Stephanus pages 309a–362a.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia names three questions it raises: the unity of virtue, whether virtue can be taught, and how pleasure relates to the good.
