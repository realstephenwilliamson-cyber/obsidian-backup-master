## About

A short Sanskrit text in which the Goddess (Devī) declares that she is Brahman, the ultimate reality. It is a late, minor Upanishad of the Shakta tradition, attached to the Atharvaveda.

- **Name:** Wikipedia's article is titled Devi Upanishad (some manuscripts have Devyupanishad) and counts it among five Atharvashiras Upanishads, but never uses the name Devī Atharvaśīrṣa. Timothy Lubin (Washington and Lee University), on the INDOLOGY list in 2014, places a Devī text among works "likewise styled -atharvaśīrṣa", imitations of the older Śiva Atharvaśiras.
- **Date:** Cheever Mackenzie Brown, cited by Wikipedia, places it between the 9th and 14th centuries CE. Lubin calls the Maharashtra recension of the group a late development but gives no date. Hindupedia gives none.
- **Canon:** number 81 in the Muktika list of 108 Upanishads; one of 19 attached to the Atharvaveda and one of eight Shakta Upanishads.
- **Contents:** 32 verses after an Atharvavedic invocation (Hindupedia: 32 mantras, in prose and verse). The gods ask the Goddess who she is; she answers that she is Brahman, the source of Prakṛti and Puruṣa, and the whole world. The gods praise her (verses 8–14, parallel to Devi Gita 1.44–48). Meditative images of her form follow, then the benefits of recitation, including at the consecration of an image.
- **Borrowings:** Wikipedia notes that verses 4–5 echo the Devī Sūkta (Rigveda 10.125). Hindupedia, following Swami Harshananda, says its verses are drawn from that hymn, the Devī Māhātmya and the Durgā Sūkta.
- **In print:** Wikipedia draws on A. G. Krishna Warrier's *Śākta Upaniṣads* (Adyar Library, 1967) and the English version of Paul Deussen's *Sixty Upanishads of the Veda* (Motilal Banarsidass, 1997).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia classes its philosophy as both Shaktism and Vedanta, and June McDaniel, cited there, finds in it the tantric terms yantra, bindu, bīja, mantra, śakti and chakra.
