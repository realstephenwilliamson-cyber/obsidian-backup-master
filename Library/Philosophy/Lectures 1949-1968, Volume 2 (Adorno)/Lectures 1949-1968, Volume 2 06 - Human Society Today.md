## Human Society Today

*16 October 1957*

Ladies and gentlemen,

First of all I would like to thank Frau Doctor Kriebel<sup>1</sup> for the extremely cordial reception she has given me and all of you here for the attention you are willing to accord me on this final morning of this series of lectures.<sup>2</sup> I realize that this entails a certain sacrifice for many of you, that many of you will be eager to get home by now. So I am all the more appreciative that you have stayed to listen to this lecture.

Now, ladies and gentlemen, the theme of ‘Human Society Today’ is certainly formulated in an extremely challenging way. The challenging aspect that is involved here, one which may have surprised some of you, does not entirely reflect what was specifically envisaged for this course of lectures. But the title is meant to suggest that this final lecture will attempt to go a little beyond the findings of the particular sciences, which it nonetheless presupposes, and speak very frankly to you about certain central questions regarding contemporary society. It is perhaps worth pointing this out straight away precisely because, in the particular theoretical field that corresponds to the theme of ‘Society Today’, namely the field of sociology, we constantly come up against the tendency to focus more and more on particular sectors, on particular problems. This is partly because these particular problems are connected with specific demands pertaining to all areas of the administrative and economic spheres of society, and partly because a certain disillusionment has taken hold within the discipline of sociology, and indeed especially in Germany.

*[p. 73]* ^pg80

There was a time when German sociology was essentially what is known as a speculative-theoretical science. It basically derived from philosophy. It cannot deny its origins in Hegel,<sup>3</sup> and indeed has no good reason to do so. But this speculative inclination to make assertions about society as a whole on the basis of rational reflective thought – though these assertions cannot always be substantiated in detail by appeal to hard facts – has also led sociology to lend some support to completely deluded doctrines. Not in a direct way – there were very few German sociologists who ever became real National Socialists,<sup>4</sup> and on the whole sociology was not regarded with any favour by the Nazis either.<sup>5</sup> But a series of speculative sociological concepts were certainly exploited in the inevitably debased and diluted form which characterized all so-called national socialist ‘cultural goods’, as they were so attractively known. I have only to remind you of the twinned concepts that furnished the title of a famous book by Ferdinand Tönnies, *Community and Society*.<sup>6</sup> These two concepts, which were originally employed in a quite different sense by this venerable scholar, eventually found their way into the vocabulary of National Socialism in the sense that ‘community’ was supposed to designate something merely given and positive, namely the natural solidarity of all those bound together by ‘blood and soil’, whereas ‘society’ was supposed to characterize the soulless connection typical of the rationalistic-capitalist Western countries. This of course, if I may anticipate somewhat, fails to acknowledge the fact that, in Germany too, as a highly industrialized country, there was no way we could say that ‘community’ in this sense essentially prevailed; or that people in Germany lived in some more direct and immediate connection with one another, like the members of a clan or a horde in a more primitive or purely agrarian society. What we are talking about here is always a question of society rather than community, and the whole idea of ‘community’ that was served up in this connection is nothing but a kind of external decoration that does not get anywhere near to the essence of society.

Now, ladies and gentlemen, I have just employed the key term ‘essence’. And perhaps this will help me to articulate and demarcate more precisely what I wish to say to you in the relatively short time available to us today. In a lecture about human society today we cannot undertake to unfold the character of this society in all its breadth and depth. That would not be possible, not only because we lack sufficient time but also because I myself and, as I believe, any sociologist today would hardly be capable of really living up to such a task, of providing a truly extensive presentation of contemporary society. On the contrary, works that actually undertake the 

*[p. 74]* ^pg81

task of presenting such a comprehensive picture soon end up looking extremely thin, tend to foist arbitrary constructions on reality, and inevitably fail to do justice to the phenomenon in all its richness. However – and this perhaps provides some justification for the fragmentary observations that I am offering you here today – we might also say that, in a certain sense, society does not consist in this kind of extensive richness at all. To clarify this in a rather drastic way: the society in which we live today is not the one that appears for example in the beautifully produced *Atlas of Hesse* that I am sure some of you will also have seen.<sup>7</sup> For society is not adequately captured when we see a couple of countrymen who live in a hamlet and cultivate rapeseed over here, or a few pig-breeders over there, and perhaps a steam hammer nearby, and then we get to see the towns and cities, with some large power stations over there, and maybe a few commercial banks – as if once we see and recognize all these things we thereby already know what contemporary society is. Now I have nothing against the *Atlas of Hesse*, and also believe that we can actually learn a lot from such works as far as certain concrete perceptions of society are concerned. But I also believe that, if you oriented your idea of society to presentations of that kind, if you stayed true to the facts and focused your attention on everything that can be observed and found together in this way over the surface of the earth, this truthfulness would lead you to become untrue to what matters here. For a particular kind of truthfulness can actually turn over into untruthfulness, as you know all too well from the way the old ‘Song of the Nibelungs’ was handed down in the German context. Society is not made up through the mere juxtaposition of various more or less interrelated individual social structures of an economic, political or cultural kind. For a specific relation of reciprocal dependency actually prevails here, within which certain social structures play a dominant role, while others are simply functions of those powerful and determining social structures. These powerful social structures – which you can examine very clearly, as if in a test tube, in America, the most advanced country in terms of our contemporary social order – are the great industrial enterprises on which everything else depends. That has not yet been realized quite as clearly or emphatically in Europe as it has been over there for a range of reasons that I cannot go into now. But one can certainly say without exaggeration that the same overall tendency can be observed here in Europe. Thus when we speak about human society today, we definitely have to speak about the laws and the phenomena that more or less derive from the concentration of social power in certain over-powerful forms and structures, and we also have to speak, inversely, 

*[p. 75]* ^pg82

about the corresponding lack of power on the part of individual human beings. Thus to return to our social atlas for a moment: the man who appears in the photograph there is certainly the owner of the very fine pig we see, in the legal sense of ownership, but when you think about this more closely, he will be dependent on all kinds of credit arrangements. He will be involved in a range of associations without which he could not possibly get by, and his independence will already be diminished to a very considerable degree, even if he regards himself as an absolutely independent individual in his sturdy rural consciousness. If we want to understand contemporary society, we should not simply study the individual social structures independently, one after another, but attempt to discern the underlying laws of the dependency that is involved, the laws to which all individual social forms and structures, and, above all, every particular human being, every specific individual, are subjected.

You will all have noticed how people constantly talk these days about the need to recognize that it is the human being that really matters, and how the human being must always be kept in view as the ultimate end and purpose in the field of economic life, in the state, in international relations, and God knows where else. Yet this never-ending talk of ‘Man’, of the human being as such, only serves to rouse my scepticism. It seems likely that many people find it necessary to talk about the human being like this all the time, to rub our noses in this all the time, only because each of us inwardly knows that this is not the case, because to an enormous extent we actually feel ourselves to be mere objects of the contemporary state of society as a whole, and because there is a sense, conscious or unconscious, that we need to be reassured that the contrary is true.

Now you may object that contemporary society is now so diversified, so ramifying, so complicated that it is already no longer remotely possible for any individual to form a picture of society as a whole. Thus according to some American social researchers, such as Robert Merton, the theory of society should focus exclusively on what they call ‘middle range theory’<sup>8</sup> – in other words, should stop trying to establish the most general laws of social movement and simply concentrate instead on identifying the laws of social movement or the social laws that pertain to specific individual fields of social life. This approach seems to be an expression of the sense that the question concerning the essence of society, which I broached earlier in relation to what I described as mere social appearance, has itself become highly questionable. But I would actually challenge this thesis about the excessive complexity and intrinsic opacity of our modern society – not only for purely theoretical reasons but also 

*[p. 76]* ^pg83

out of regard for those of you who are present here today. In this connection I would like to try and help you a little so you will not allow yourselves to be scared off in relation to this problem. For it is quite possible that we have been told so often that something is essentially unintelligible, is incapable of being comprehended as a whole, that we actually start to believe it and persuade ourselves that it cannot in fact be understood. But it is surely one of the intellectual achievements that should be required of every individual self-aware human being today, of anyone who wishes to affirm their autonomy, that they should resist succumbing to such a suggestion and thus refuse to say: ‘Oh well, that is all far too complicated for me, I had better ignore it and leave it alone.’ It is certainly true that modern society is extraordinarily ramified, and that, in purely quantitative terms alone – simply through the constantly increasing scale of the population itself – it has assumed a certain aspect or appearance that makes an adequate overview of society seem very difficult. On the other hand, its fundamental structures have not really become more difficult to make out as a whole and have actually become more transparent than they used to be, so much more transparent indeed that every human being who looks at these things fairly and squarely and refuses to mistake one thing for another should be capable – and in terms of intrinsic abilities is capable – of seeing through the prevailing mechanism by which so many have been exploited. But how it is, in spite of everything, that most people don’t manage to actualize the relatively simple intellectual insights that are required for this is another question, and one to which I hope I shall be able to give at least a provisional answer in the course of this lecture.

But first, in the context of my claims about the transparency of contemporary society, I would like to indicate certain dimensions and certain themes which will allow you to see that we are not talking about some empty general assertion but addressing a very real social phenomenon. The increasing transparency is itself a function of the actual contemporary development of society as a whole. Drawing attention to these particular features or moments may give you some initial idea of a series of fundamental aspects of this society. To start with, I would just like to remind you of a certain expression that has become very famous, and which was used by the now deceased American presidential candidate Wendell Willkie.<sup>9</sup> This is the expression ‘one world’. The development not only of technology but also of social and political forms of organization more generally has meant that all countries have effectively been brought closer together, though what seems to me as far more significant than this aspect of much easier and much faster travel and communication 

*[p. 77]* ^pg84

between countries is the way in which individual countries have become dependent on one another in a quite new way. The play of forces that operates throughout the world today must really be seen as something unitary. If certain dynastic conflicts had played out in a country such as Afghanistan a hundred years ago, that would still simply have been a matter for the Afghans themselves and would have had relatively little effect on the other powers of the time. If something similar happens in Afghanistan today, we can be quite sure that it involves some conspiracy on the part of the Soviets or is somehow connected with the oil interests of America, and that what appears to happen independently in one country such as this is actually a function of much broader conflicts of power within the world as a whole.

Here you might object that there are surely many countries that have lagged far behind these social and economic developments, namely the so-called underdeveloped countries, which have been the subject of so many studies and which have also made their presence felt so strongly in the context of the newly emerging nationalisms. Now I have no intention of denying the existence of these ‘developing countries’, for goodness sake, and nor do I wish to claim that these countries have all already been entirely absorbed or will entirely be absorbed into these political force fields, whether we are talking about the Soviet Union or about the United States. But in terms of their current structure and their current policies they are all essentially and fundamentally affected by the oppositions or the tensions to which they are exposed in the context of the increasingly powerful unifying tendencies at work in the world as a whole. These countries are essentially defined by the fact that in some sense they already participate in the blessings promised by the most advanced forms of production, and may thus succeed in raising the standard of living enjoyed by their respective populations. In other words, they will probably also become more industrialized over time, and to that extent the whole dynamic of these so-called underdeveloped countries is itself a function of the capitalistic or rationalizing tendency of the world at large. Thus society today, if I may put it this way, is now integrally or totally socialized. There is no longer any sector that is not encompassed by the universal functional connectedness of society in general. There is no longer any extraterritoriality in relation to society. Almost everything socially particular or individual that we can find develops in terms of its function within the whole. This corresponds to the shrinking of the world which results not only from the technological advances that now allow us to travel from Frankfurt to New York, to Pakistan, or wherever it may be, in the 

*[p. 78]* ^pg85

shortest possible time, but also from the current means of communication such as radio and other media, which have long since allowed people in every part of the world, language permitting, to hear and receive news of what is happening in another part of the world. This alone is not insignificant, because one of the salient tendencies of the present age – and this already intimates something of the contradictions under which we live today – is the way that in every individual country, and not only in the totalitarian states, the news we receive is all so highly filtered, although if we avoid using these means of international communication we only get an extremely one-sided and confused picture of the most important developments, such as the disarmament talks that are taking place in London at the moment.<sup>10</sup> Thus on the one hand, technology places us in a position to know more or less immediately about anything that happens anywhere in the world, while at the same time all countries employ these filters that prevent us from learning about certain things, or from learning in an accurate and objective way about things which are incompatible with the specific interests of the regimes and governments in power at any given time. It would be particularly important to investigate how in the free countries that still exist in the world, and in spite of the political freedom they enjoy, these remarkable filtering mechanisms exert their effects in the context of public opinion. You can see from this how we sociologists actually engage with a whole range of problems that are not only difficult to resolve and very interesting in their own right but are also directly relevant in a practical sense, if such problems could genuinely be resolved.

All these things, however, are still relatively external and contingent aspects or moments that contribute their share to the phenomenon that I have just described, namely the tendency of society in a certain sense to become more transparent. I can perhaps best illustrate what is crucial here if, without going into any technical controversies in economic theory, I just remind you of the contrast between the kind of economy that operates in almost all countries today and the kind of economy that was characteristic of the so-called High Liberal era in the nineteenth century, or in England even earlier, from the end of the eighteenth century. The famous free play of economic forces, the idea of free competition, through which the economy and ultimately society as a whole was supposed to constitute and reproduce itself in a blind fashion simply in accordance with the law of supply and demand, all that has been largely dispensed with. Now I don’t want you to misunderstand me here. In the Western world there are certainly no planned economies, strictly speaking. Nonetheless, the concentrations of economic power that exercise their influence 

*[p. 79]* ^pg86

throughout the world have grown so much that the old competitive mechanism has been essentially weakened by the effect of such growth, and the anti-trust legislation in various countries has done relatively little to change this situation. But the dangers of repeated economic crises – since the last major one that began in 1929, and in America lasted until 1934, and helped to lead to fascism and ultimately to the last war – the dangers of leaving this society to its own devices have grown so much that even in the exemplary capitalist countries, where they still absolutely believe in the idea of free competition and the free play of economic forces, we actually find that an enormous number of controls have been tacitly built in to the system, controls which up till now have prevented the repetition of such a catastrophe.

If we take this all together, it means that, while in one part of the world we basically maintain the façade of a market economy, and while in certain sectors something like a market economy really does continue to operate, an element of planning has nonetheless found its way into society as a whole, an element that largely abolishes the blindness and irrationality that had prevailed earlier. But the more things in society we find that we are able to plan or anticipate, the more that things can be brought about on the basis of prior calculation rather than by relying on some blind and unpredictable process, the more transparent the social mechanism itself also becomes as a result. The supposed lack of transparency really relates only to the quantitative increase on the part of society. The universal tendency in the world towards an increasing rationalization of society, if I may put it this way, is intrinsically connected with the way that the irrational institutions that were formerly characteristic of society – such as monarchy, the feudal system where it still survived, and the churches insofar as they represented a political and formative social force – have increasingly forfeited their social function as forms of control. Thus the process of social control now increasingly passes over to those forms of power that do in fact reproduce the real life of human beings in society, namely the specifically economic ones. The old forms of privilege, at any rate, have largely become obsolete in our society. As some of you may know, Hegel developed the theory of ‘the cunning of reason’, which is actually already implicit in Kant’s philosophy of history, according to which major historical tendencies unfold behind the backs of those who are involved and indeed often against the will of those who make history.<sup>11</sup> We can study this process very clearly here in Germany, in the Germany of the last few decades, where the Hitler regime, in accordance with its ideology and its own official claims, set itself in the most emphatic opposition to 

*[p. 80]* ^pg87

the supposedly egalitarian or levelling tendency of liberalism, where it believed in a new nobility and attempted to create a new privileged class – here you have only to think of the idea of the SS state<sup>12</sup> and the *Ordensburgen*, the special military establishments, that were created for the SS,<sup>13</sup> and various other things of this sort. In truth, however, in spite of such ideologies, the older traditional forces and powers were effectively eliminated in the context of the total rationalization of society, even if this was a total rationalization for the purpose of war. In this sense one might say that Hitler – who repeatedly declared that he wanted to wipe out liberalism<sup>14</sup> – in Germany and against his will actually promoted the bourgeois revolution that had previously miscarried. For, in order to secure his own total domination, he liquidated the feudal privileges along with the privileges of those who were still connected or associated in any way with feudalism and the last vestiges of monarchy. This domination has now fortunately been broken, but the old traditional forces and powers are not coming back. Since we actually live today in a pure market economy, in a society that is governed purely by the laws of the economy, the total structure of society has thereby also become much more transparent.

This has a rather remarkable consequence. Among the various laments that are addressed to contemporary society, one is particularly common, namely the lament about the phenomenon of increasing specialization and thus about the fact that the different roles, functions and competencies assumed by particular groups and individuals now involve very little understanding of one another. Now I will certainly not deny that there are a whole range of fields where this is indeed the case. Thus unless someone has spent many years studying the most recent data and results of physics, they will be incapable of understanding the problems of atomic research that are so relevant and pressing for us today, and unless someone has engaged very intensively with specific tendencies in the field of music, they will generally be quite baffled by certain phenomena of modern music.

But I have very serious doubts, ladies and gentlemen, whether the specialization we see all over the world, the disintegration of the world into a range of unconnected functions administered by experts, is in fact the crucial thing here; or whether this specialization really does entrench a new kind of privilege where such specialization is not actually required by the subject in question, as we are so noisily informed all the time. I believe I can give you a very solid reason for these doubts, simply with the intention of alerting you to what is going on when people start regaling you with tales about specialization and the dangers of specialization. For it seems to me that not 

*[p. 81]* ^pg88

only the increasing rationalization of industrial production, which furnishes the model for all other social functions, but also rationalization in the context of social life itself mean that the individual functions that fall to individual human beings are actually becoming smaller and smaller. But the smaller a social function is, the more unqualified or unskilled it becomes. This basically means that, the more you reduce the process of production of some industrial article to ever smaller partial operations – and the whole of the modern productive process has depended on these partial and reduced operations since the manufacturing period, namely for the last three hundred years or so – the more these partial functions come to resemble one another. The smaller a functional unit becomes, the more it forfeits its difference from the others. Thus when people stand at a conveyor belt, where each person has to perform only a single specific intervention, until the finished automobile comes out at the other end, it is a matter of relative indifference to each individual person what position he or she occupies at the conveyor belt because the functions that each individual is to perform, being a minimal intervention in each case, are very similar to one another. However, I believe that this sort of process is by no means restricted to the sphere of industrial production but also extends to every other possible function. In America, for example, it is already the case that students embarking on the study of psychology or sociology will find that what they learn is really not so different in character. But this is not because the substantive unity between psychology and sociology is genuinely recognized here; rather, it is because in both fields the students essentially learn only about techniques, methods, procedures and especially statistics, and these things are not actually that different in the two fields, so you could almost exchange the psychologists for the sociologists without anyone noticing. In other words, we see on the one hand how the tendency of modern society over the last three hundred or three hundred and fifty years has unquestionably promoted specialization by breaking up the once unitary artisanal processes into a range of individual functions. And even today there are still a number of major sectors where this specialization is meaningful and functionally justified. But this tendency towards specialization, ladies and gentlemen, can also reach a pitch where these functions may finally resemble one another so closely that this facilitates a transition from one function to another.

If what I have said to you here, ladies and gentlemen, is right, then it is important for the claim I made at the beginning about the increasing transparency of society. For it means that we are not, as so many people are keen to tell us, so confined in our own particular 

*[p. 82]* ^pg89

corner of the world that we can only really understand one particular segment or cross-section of the world. For it is possible for us to be transposed, as it were, from one point or position to another without undue difficulties, so that we would be able to function at any given point or position and to perform what is expected of us in each case. This is of course directly related to the potential for the internally connected structure of the world to become ever more transparent to each and every one of us. If my claim about the increasing rationality of the world is right – an idea that was first formulated in really emphatic form by Max Weber,<sup>15</sup> although this development has already gone far beyond anything that Weber could possibly imagine – then society as a whole is coming more and more to resemble the production plan of a gigantic factory, an enormous factory for the exploitation and domination of nature. And such a factory of course has nothing really mysterious about it and can therefore be seen for what is in all its essential aspects or moments.

Now this is all intimately connected, ladies and gentlemen, with something that is so strongly emphasized by many sociologists, and something that you will certainly also have observed if you are concerned about the progress of science, namely the levelling tendency at work within contemporary society.<sup>16</sup> I have to say that one should be rather careful with this concept of a levelling tendency. On the one hand, it is certainly quite true that the old class differences, for example, have become increasingly hard to discern on the level of the façade of society. In other words, what might still be seen as traces of the older system of estates, of older feudal distinctions, in society – such as the contrast between the master and individuals of significantly lower social status and other categories of this kind – obviously disappears in a time when every daughter of a foreman can afford nylon stockings, where young workers can buy themselves the celebrated motorbike that everyone loves to write about today. But this levelling tendency only applies to the external forms of life, and in particular to the consciousness that is shared by the members of different classes. Indeed, these days there is far less difference than there used to be, even in terms of language, between a young worker with a good position and a young teacher or academic. A worker of this kind may even be materially better off than many members of the older middle class such as white-collar employees, civil servants, and so on. Yet basically nothing has really changed insofar as the contrast between those who exercise social power and those who lack such power, insofar as the simple opposition between rich and poor, continues to exist as it did before. If we want to do justice to this much discussed levelling tendency, we should pose the problem 

*[p. 83]* ^pg90

in a quite different way. We should ask ourselves how those who are in truth so unequal, those who are certainly not the same, seem to look the same and to think the same. But not in the sense that we simply say: ‘Ah well, classes, different kinds of social status, and things of that sort no longer really exist; today we live in a society of universal equality.’ Now the possession of a motorbike is a long way from equality, and if a foreman or a head foreman in a colliery earns more today than a high school teacher that doesn’t mean that we live in a classless society, as someone once put it.<sup>17</sup> It means that the differences between the classes must be sought elsewhere rather than being simply identified with the different income levels of a head foreman and a high school teacher.

But we also have to mention another fundamental tendency today, namely the development that suggests the state of contemporary technology is already able in principle to guarantee a tremendous abundance of goods to all human beings and to satisfy human needs in a way that goes far beyond anything previously imaginable. This is probably even true of the countries in the Eastern bloc, where, as we all know, the standard of living is certainly much lower than it is here and in the whole of the Western world, but in all likelihood is still higher than it used to be in times when countries such as China and also Russia were periodically plagued by famine. Of course, there is also a remarkable story to tell about the growth in the ready availability of goods in the Western world. For, as we all know, only death comes free of charge. All of these goods are produced not only in order to satisfy our wants and needs but initially and primarily for the sake of profit. Nonetheless, the very quantity and the quality of these goods has increased so much that – contrary to the claims of the traditional socialist theory of pauperization<sup>18</sup> – there is no question that the living standard of countless human beings has actually grown enormously. And one would have to be a most stubborn dogmatist not to recognize this. We might say that, specifically through the developments of the last twenty years – developments which of course are always massively promoted by the phenomenon of war – the level of technological productive forces has risen so much that this growth in quantity has taken on a new quality, for we can now envisage a condition in which human beings no longer need to live in a state of constant need or want – as President Roosevelt rightly put it in his famous ‘Four Points’.<sup>19</sup> And a world in which we know that no child actually has to go hungry any longer has taken on a quite different look, has become much more transparent in a positive sense than the world of our parents ever was, where one simply shrugged and said there would never be enough caviar for 

*[p. 84]* ^pg91

everyone. Given the current level of technology, it would probably even be possible to farm sturgeon in a suitable environment in such a way that the famous ‘caviar for the people’ would indeed be available to everyone. But this proverbial saying too, which is meant to suggest that there isn’t actually enough in the world for everyone, has fallen out of fashion today, like so many sayings of this sort. I believe that you can form a good impression of the structural changes to which our world is subject if you just look through the rich store of traditional sayings that we still used to learn as children. You will then see, beginning with ‘a trade in hand finds gold in every land’ and moving on to ‘the pitcher that goes to the well too often soon gets broken’, i.e., you will soon come to grief if you go on this way – while Franco still rules unperturbed and happily enough<sup>20</sup> – that you will hardly find a single saying or proverb that can still be used in the way our parents believed it could be used.

We now have to consider the question regarding our control of natural forces that has introduced an entirely new aspect into the world, namely the capacity to regulate and direct productive capacity in an unprecedented way. Although we know that the full positive utilization of these natural forces lies ahead of us, this still shows that the old idea of a world inevitably based on want and lack is anachronistic, and that we continue to live in such a world because there are interests that resist the possibility of this world of want finally coming to an end. I believe that we really ought to express this very simple and straightforward truth as clearly as I have just done here. Once we do so, we shall no longer yield so easily to the suggestion that the world is intrinsically opaque and unintelligible to us.

The very aspects that I have just emphasized become particularly evident to anyone who lives in America, where I spent almost all my time during the period of emigration. In a certain sense America is certainly the purest example of a capitalist country, namely a country where almost nothing is really for free. If you then come back to Europe, you almost feel as though you were now being given things – in other words, you feel as though we are somehow freer from the complete domination of exchange. Nonetheless, in America you always also have the feeling that you are in the land of milk and honey, that everything is just there ready to be enjoyed. There is such an indescribable sense of available abundance that our old ideas of lack, need, want and poverty, along with all the terrible things that want and poverty bring with them in terms of narrowed and stunted human existence, come to look entirely outdated. And I believe that in Germany, especially in this regard, we have not entirely 

*[p. 85]* ^pg92

participated, either psychologically or economically speaking, in this development to this day.

In connection with the tendencies that I have been describing we also see how certain anthropological changes, that is, specific changes in human beings themselves, are emerging that may help to hasten the development I have just mentioned. I am talking about a more sober attitude among the young, a certain scepticism on the one hand but also a sense that what happens depends on us, that we can achieve something for ourselves, that things do not have to go on as blindly and absurdly as has been the case until now. All human beings today can in principle know better, and it is rather less easy to take them in after all the lies and deceptions to which they were exposed during the Third Reich. Unless other and admittedly very powerful forces serve to counter this process, they may well be able on their own to see through how everything works.

I would like to take a break at this point and use the second half of the session that is still available to me to say something about the other tendencies that work against those that I have mentioned so far. This may help to offer at least some insight into what seems to me the really essential thing about human society today. I am talking about its antagonistic character, about the conflicts and tensions that have become almost unbearable in the world – and which tomorrow are capable of turning a world that could be a paradise into an inferno once again.

\[Second part of the lecture\] Ladies and gentlemen, After what I have tried to suggest earlier, we should at least be able to expect, among those who belong to this society, what we might call a certain ‘heightened sense of life’, as in the famous saying proclaimed at the beginning of the bourgeois epoch by Ulrich von Hutten, when this mortally ill and persecuted man declared that it was still a joy to be alive.<sup>21</sup> Yet the striking thing, of course, is that, in spite of all that I have said, and which hardly exaggerates the potential of humanity today, there is precious little evidence of any such joy to be alive. The prevailing feeling of human beings seems in large measure to be, rather, one of apathy or one that simply reflects an obstinate pursuit of our own particular interests. And if we look more closely, we come upon a widespread sense of unease or discontent, as this was formulated by Sigmund Freud in his book *Civilization and its Discontents*, a text that was written almost thirty years ago but has lost hardly any of its relevance since.<sup>22</sup> In Switzerland they have specifically adopted 

*[p. 86]* ^pg93

the word ‘malaise’ in German to capture this peculiar feeling that is so widespread throughout the civilized world.<sup>23</sup> This feeling of malaise is much discussed there, in spite of the well-known prosperity that flourishes in Switzerland especially. This itself is also remarkable precisely because – after the catastrophe that was unleashed upon humanity through Hitler – the development in the years that followed goes beyond everything that one could really expect in the countries that were most directly affected in that period.

But then you all already know that in great literature throughout the world – that is, in literature that can really be taken seriously and is not content with simply fabricating comforting sentimentalities – the concept of anxiety, and if not the concept of anxiety the actual expression of anxiety and horror, occupies an absolutely central place. And you also know that, for us – if we believe that we can sense what is truly appropriate to the time and what is not – any product of art that fails to express or respond to that sense of anxiety has become almost intolerable.

One essential aspect of all this is unquestionably the memory of that which happened, the realization that, after Auschwitz, no one – no human being who is still connected with society – can still, in some unbroken fashion, feel joy to be alive. It seems to me an express symptom of disaster that our consciousness of these things has become so repressed that many people really appear to think that Hitler was simply some kind of technical hitch or mishap on the continuing path of progress, as if the murder of millions of human beings on the battlefields and in the concentration camps was merely an exceptional phenomenon that is certainly regrettable but best not talked about. I believe that we can only deal with this trauma when we really bring it to full consciousness, rather than indulging in convenient counter-charges and saying things such as: ‘Well, of course, but Dresden was utterly destroyed as well.’ It is not as simple as that. The very fact that such things were possible in our world, in a world whose potential I have already talked about, the fact that this world allowed Hitler to flourish, that it watched all this until he was in a position to perpetrate these things, already sufficiently proves that everything is not as rosy as the so-called potential in question.

How can we come to terms with these experiences? Only through genuine self-reflection, as I believe. But this also implies that we cannot regard the outbreak of Hitlerism itself as something merely alien and external to the system. The society in which we live also produces forces that harbour this destructive potential, forces of which Hitler may well not be the last or only representative. I believe that we have to recognize Hitler as a piece of ourselves, as it were, as 

*[p. 87]* ^pg94

the legitimate child of our society, rather than simply regarding him as a kind of monster who somehow broke into the civilized world from the outside, in the manner of Tamburlaine or Genghis Khan.<sup>24</sup> Only if we do this can we acquire the inner freedom to acknowledge and come to terms with those phenomena that cast such tremendous shadows over our world – shadows that only threaten to become deeper the less human beings dare to remember the real disaster of those twelve years. Just how little all this represents something wholly unique may well be intimately connected with that sense of unease and discontent that I spoke about earlier. The anxiety under which we all suffer in one way or another is not, as Herr Heidegger would have us believe,<sup>25</sup> a kind of metaphysical mood or attunement, is not some primordial phenomenon that belongs to the human being as such in the face of death etc., but is something very historical and specific. The philosophy that is still imbibed in Germany today does a great wrong by effectively evaporating the specific aspects and features of anxiety under which we stand into a kind of spurious eternity.

Human beings have certainly achieved a leap into the cosmos. You all know that a satellite now passes more or less noiselessly over our heads and, we hope, will not come crashing down on top of us.<sup>26</sup> Yet at the same time – and we could hardly find a crasser expression of our current situation than this – the human beings who are preparing to conquer the moon and other celestial bodies are still incapable of shaping their own affairs in a rational enough way to prevent them from trembling before the worst possibility that threatens to befall them. The highest form of control over nature that humanity has achieved hitherto – and the history of humanity is a history of the increasing control and domination of nature – is the control over the forces that are stored in the core of the atom; but it is also certainly no accident, since this is connected with the structure of the society in which these discoveries are made, that this control over nature has until now essentially developed only its destructive potential. By recourse to these forces of the cosmos, which we can now in part control, we could probably soon render the earth uninhabitable or effectively blow everything up, yet we have still not succeeded in using these forces responsibly to serve the reproduction of our own life and the life of nature. In view of the magnitude of these forces, there are certainly difficulties that would have to be addressed here. But for me it seems to symbolize our current situation that – in the light of the potential for war – we continue to expend our greatest efforts on exploiting these forces to prepare for disaster, while their positive application has hardly gone beyond the promises that one 

*[p. 88]* ^pg95

day we will be able to use atomic energy for central heating and while other almost inexhaustible forces and resources for the reproduction of life, such as solar energy, have not yet been tapped at all, as they would say in America.

Now if I may use an old-fashioned expression from the language of socialism – and one that, we are repeatedly told on every corner today, has been rendered completely obsolete by the contemporary development of humanity – I would refer here to the growth of social contradictions. A certain extreme has been attained. On the one hand, the productive forces, both the capacities of living human beings and the powers of technology that they use to extend the reach of their own powers, have been incredibly intensified. At the same time, however, society has taken on certain features of rigidity, of ossification, that not only hinder these forces from fully benefiting humanity but also express tendencies that threaten in the end to destroy the whole system. And the task of changing this situation, ladies and gentlemen, is not a matter of some wholly unrealistic utopian vision, as people have tried to convince us in the past, for the task of making the world a more rational place is now synonymous with the survival of humanity itself. Either we succeed in finding an appropriate form for our society – one in which this society does not collapse through its own economic and its internal and external political contradictions – or we are really finished. The old-fashioned question about gradual improvement of the world is indeed out of date. Only those who are concerned with bringing about genuine change in the structure of society can still be seen as true conservatives, whereas those who claim to stand for conservatism and for the preservation of the existing order are only bringing more straw to the pyre that one day will burst into flames and end up consuming everything.

We should not allow any scientific or political objection to obscure this insight that the powers of humanity and the technology available to it have come into irreconcilable opposition to the continuing order of the world. This opposition threatens not only to disrupt or destabilize existing social relations – as has happened repeatedly over the course of history – but to make the continued existence of humanity itself impossible. That is the true and concrete ground of anxiety that we should not allow any high-flown words or vatic pronouncements to obscure, just as we should recognize that the use of noble rhetoric today generally serves only to veil some very ignoble things.

But first I want to say something very concrete to you about the reasons for that sense of unease and discontent which is so widely felt in the world today, and that every single one of us feels in one 

*[p. 89]* ^pg96

way or another in spite of the prosperity around us. And here I would like to leave aside the relatively harmless question as to how long such prosperity may last and whether or not we may well be threatened by other economic catastrophes in the future. I have tried to show that the overall tendency of social development is one that is characterized by a growing concentration on the part of social forms and structures, and thus by the disappearance of all those mediating and intermediate levels, both those of the various social strata and the social structures that once made it so difficult to grasp the functioning or otherwise of the whole social mechanism. But that also has an extraordinarily fateful aspect. The process through which society becomes more transparent in theoretical terms is not the same as one in which humanity becomes more autonomous in practical terms. For we find that the social forms and structures – which are now formed and structured in ever simpler, ever more rationally transparent ways – lie before us as if they were simply given. They are something solid, anonymous and overpowering with which we are confronted and to which we are subordinated. If I spoke before about the increasing rationality of the world in the sense of Max Weber’s famous claim, we now have to introduce a decisive qualification in this connection. For all the infinite rationality of instrumental means in terms of the domination of nature, and in terms of social domination, the end or purpose of the whole – which could only be the self-preservation of humanity – remains irrational in character. For then as now our life depends on the blind interests of the most powerful structures to which we are subjected, which certainly plan everything in their respective sectors but are not themselves subject to any plan that would be worthy of living human subjects. And the infinitely rational structure of this world – rational in the sense of an enormous factory, as I said before – is also irrational insofar as it cannot be called our world in the sense of being determined in terms of freedom and solidarity, in terms of our own reason. For we are essentially dependent on the reason, or unreason as I would say, of those same social structures. If I may use an expression from classical German philosophy, this world continues to be an alienated world,<sup>27</sup> a cold world that confronts us as something alien, which increasingly threatens to degrade us to little more than the functions that we discharge. As official employees or civil servants, you know this better than anyone. When you sit behind your desks, you basically just have to fulfil the functions that are prescribed for you by the tasks you are set in each case, in a sphere where there is relatively little room for individual decisions. But I don’t want you to misunderstand me here. As a university teacher I am an official employee of the state, 

*[p. 90]* ^pg97

just as you are. And in every examination that I am involved in, in every question about the awarding of grants that I have to decide on, I find myself facing the same issues. We all know that today even the school, where some individual freedom of decision used to lie with the teachers themselves, is increasingly becoming an object of administration, as my friend Hellmut Becker has very clearly shown in his work ‘The Administered School’.<sup>28</sup> Analogous tendencies can also be observed in the universities where we work. This is not just a question of particular failings or abuses that one could in principle remedy or eliminate, for these tendencies arise with a kind of infernal necessity from the situation in which we find ourselves. Thus you can only deal with the tremendous influx of students in certain faculties, and with the sheer amount of material that must be provided for them in these faculties, through a certain standardization of the course of study itself and the relevant examinations. Here we can see what I once tried to encapsulate with the expression ‘the administered world’.<sup>29</sup> Within this administered world each of us is constantly assailed by the feeling of powerlessness. One has the feeling of being confronted by social powers and forces in the face of which our own decisions hardly count for anything. This feeling does not arise merely in the case of so-called subordinate functions but also in the context of extremely important ones. This is the other side, as it were, of what I talked about earlier as the prevalence of specialization. The person who stands at the head of some institution or organization is just as constricted by prior decisions, by a narrow range of alternatives that have already been imposed, by political considerations of one kind or another, is just as much a functionary of the apparatus in question as the lowliest official whose functions are all clearly prescribed in advance. We are all caught up in the same mechanism. Thus the situation of the official employee, with which I began, is not even the most characteristic one here, for it has always been like this in our sector since the time of absolutist bureaucracy. But we should think to begin with about the structures of economic life, where the ideal type<sup>30</sup> of the free independent economic agent who acts on his own initiative and weighs up the situation has long disappeared from view. We should think instead of the employee in this connection. This is something that is also reflected in human consciousness today. In our investigations into the consciousness of students we have found that countless numbers no longer have any ambition to reach the top of the businesses or organizations they want to work in, no longer wish to bear independent responsibility, but would rather settle for a middling position with a certain degree of security, where they will be more or less looked after but where they will not have to make any 

*[p. 91]* ^pg98

really important decisions.<sup>31</sup> In this way, human beings all effectively underwrite their own lack of power once again and are more or less content with this powerlessness. Hence that apathy, and especially that political apathy, which ultimately helps to ensure that everything basically remains as it is, yet drives us towards the sort of catastrophe we have to talk about if we are not to fall back into empty chatter.

Even the least powerful person could see through all this in principle; it is certainly not some privilege of intellectuals or academics. Nobody who refuses to be blinded by superficial appearances would really want any longer to accept a world that could be a paradise yet threatens to become the opposite. For the reason of every individual would be sufficient to carry out the really quite simple thoughtful operations for which I have attempted at least to suggest certain models here today. The fact that human beings still do not manage to do this, the fact that they still make countless decisions of a political kind that we can only fear will contribute further to strengthen the possibility of disaster, all of this is not because human beings could not exercise thought in principle. Rather, it springs from the way that they renounce thought precisely because increasing insight into those contradictions of which we spoke earlier makes life harder for them and only makes for more suffering. In a sense they follow the line of least resistance, basically cling to the little happiness that the existing relations allow them, and bury their heads in the sand, for if they seriously saw where the whole thing is heading, life in this world would become very difficult for them, and their psychological economy obviously does not permit them to acknowledge all this. Thus the old Berlin saying ‘Don’t be stupid, you’ve got it good!’ seems to be one of the few proverbial sayings that has not yet lost its meaning. If I were to offer you any advice in this regard, it would be this: don’t allow yourselves to be over-impressed even by apparently scientific or objective claims but rely, for goodness sake, on your own common sense, which has long ceased to agree without qualification with the pronouncements of science – even if many institutions<sup>32</sup> of social science are concerned with wresting some insight from ‘healthy common sense’ rather than simply confirming or strengthening the latter. Do not let yourselves be misled by high-sounding book titles such as ‘Man at the Centre of Society’ – I do not know whether there is such a book with this title, but, even if there isn’t, one like it is bound to appear tomorrow; do not let yourselves become more foolish than you are, and, above all, do not let anyone tell you that what happens to prosper in the academic world inevitably represents some higher insight. You should remember instead that, in contrast to the official world of culture and education, a certain tradition 

*[p. 92]* ^pg99

of scepticism, of irony, of clear-sightedness has also always been at work to some extent in the ordinary world outside, and that tradition may perhaps be the best source that humanity has today for effectively changing the world.

That feeling of unease and discontent that I mentioned is also provoked by the unequal relationship between human productive forces and technological productive forces. It is hardly some special discovery on my part that human beings increasingly feel that their own powers and forces are somehow being lost to them, and that in many respects they cannot keep pace with the developments they have themselves unleashed. What I am talking about here is not so much the difficulty of understanding atomic physics itself but, rather, about the way that in many sectors we lag behind in terms of psychological resources and can do so little to remedy this. In his most recent book, Günther Anders has placed this problem right at the centre of attention – in a rather too psychologistic fashion for my taste<sup>33</sup> – but he has rightly emphasized that, precisely because human beings feel that technology is running away from them, that technology has somehow become incommensurable with them, they are assailed by a sense of anxiety that can explode in all kinds of irrational ways. This disproportionate relationship between human and technological forces could probably be changed if human beings were able to develop a much greater awareness that technology is really only an extended arm, as people used to say, of human beings themselves, and that even some of the most alarming consequences of modern science, such as the field of cybernetics,<sup>34</sup> namely the development of intelligent machines, that is starting to develop today, are in the last analysis things that point right back to us. For how we use them depends on us, not on the machines themselves. Without wishing to sound too much like a schoolmaster here, I would warn you against taking technology itself to be responsible for the malaise, for the feeling of unease that we mentioned before. Technology is not the reason for the disaster, and indeed it could become the opposite. The disaster lies in the way that the rationality of technology is harnessed to the demands of a society that basically continues to remain irrational. In a genuinely rational society, technology would relinquish those alarming aspects or moments, just as in earlier times – when that opposition between technical productive forces and society itself did not yet exist – we find that technology did not yet reveal that demonic aspect that is so familiar to us. People like to distract our attention by focusing on technology in the way they do. The issue does not ultimately depend on technology itself, any more than it does in a merely subjective sense on human beings. It all 

*[p. 93]* ^pg100

depends on the objective state and condition of the world, of which every one of us is a function, and of which technology, namely the use of technology, is also a function.

Finally, we have to say that what contributes to that malaise is our conscious awareness of the blind character of the historical process itself, namely the awareness that I already adumbrated at the beginning of the second part of this lecture. There I claimed that the ends and purposes we pursue in society continue to be irrational, and it is only the means that are massively rationalized; in other words, we effectively fail to ask about the ultimate end or purpose of technology, with the result that technology is fetishized, as we say in the context of the philosophy of history, is pursued for its own sake, and thereby largely loses touch with the human end that it is supposed to serve. The same thing holds, of course, for the large-scale processes of society as a whole to which we are all subjected. Thus in a recent article that appeared in the magazine *Der Spiegel*, and which you have probably all read, the medical specialist Haber simply raised the question – and I have to say in an entirely admirable way – whether we actually know why we have a satellite at all, and why we actually want to fly to the moon, and said that the solution to this whole complex of issues depends entirely on what these things were good for, on what end they served.<sup>35</sup> It is only once we have come to a much clearer consciousness about the ends of technology and the ends of the social process itself, only once we are no longer stubbornly sworn to the development of technologies and the particular means involved, that these tendencies stand any chance of being changed.

To conclude, I would just like to draw your attention to the way that the adaptation that is demanded of human beings today clearly expects so much of them that they are no longer capable of living up to these demands. This in turn leads human beings towards a certain over-identification with the state of the world, to a numbing of their critical faculties, so that they no longer kick against the pricks, but kick with the pricks, as I put it once before.<sup>36</sup> In other words, they practise what psychoanalytic theory has described as ‘identification with the aggressor’<sup>37</sup> – that is, they turn themselves into the executants of what blindly befalls them rather than attempting to change the situation itself. They do this because they no longer experience any consciousness of possible change, because the very prospect that the situation could be changed has effectively died within them. Like the prisoner eventually comes to love the four walls that surround him, so too they can no longer help but love the world as it now is.

Since we are running short of time, I would just refer you once again to Sigmund Freud’s book on *Civilization and its Discontents*, where he developed the thoughts that I have just been outlining for you from quite a different perspective, namely from the perspective of the economy of human drives, and does so in an extremely radical and entirely consistent way. In his book he gave particular attention to the destructive drives, to the destructive tendencies, at work within human beings<sup>38</sup> – processes which are only encouraged by the pressure that civilization exerts on each one of us, and which certainly furnish one of the most dangerous potential sources that can lead to the eventual destruction of the world as it currently exists. And I would also like to add that this mood of unease and discontent ultimately has a reason that is nonetheless easily overlooked. This is that there is a very high price to be paid for progress by the large strata of people who are thereby becoming poorer, who are expropriated, or at least end up forfeiting their social status. The greater the reservoir of people who have to pay this price for progress, the greater of course becomes the reservoir of resentment and the greater the unease with regard to this progress. And this only promotes the growth of those forces that are ultimately prepared to turn the path of progress into one of pure destruction. But it would be a mistake to locate this unease essentially or merely in human beings as such, or simply to register it as a social-psychological fact about human beings, for it has entirely real objective grounds. The most important of these is that society, in spite of every form of progress that it has made, in spite of the prospect of doing away with want, is still characterized by the contradiction between power and powerlessness and, to a large extent, also by the contradiction between wealth and poverty. And this contradiction persists largely unchanged even in the midst of prosperity here in the Federal Republic of Germany. This contradiction has merely become invisible in a certain sense, and this question of ‘visibility’ – as we call it in sociology – namely the question of which group is visible at any given time, of which social phenomena can readily be recognized and which cannot, becomes ever more important in a society that creates its own veil as effectively as ours does. For example, the very large group of pensioners, who still just about survive at the limit of what is necessary for life even in Germany, is a widely dispersed one and is hardly visible as a result. It has no strong organization to represent it, and no one to speak for it. I think it is good to remember these things because all our talk about ‘the economic miracle’, even when this is intended in a slightly ironic fashion, leads us to forget the simplest thing of all, namely that the range of those who participate in this miracle is quite limited even 

*[p. 95]* ^pg102

here. The unease I have talked about is essentially bound up with the fact that, in terms of its underlying social-economic relations, the world, now as before, is by no means easy, is still an extraordinarily difficult place for most people. It is just that this difficult and discomforting situation is by no means as immediately evident today as it was in the nineteenth century, when workers and labourers returned home with sunken cheeks, just as you see them depicted in the drawings of Käthe Kollwitz. In the meantime, however, society has developed techniques of integration that have allowed it not to abolish poverty but to make it largely unrecognizable.

Here too we must think of the ultimately quite irrational partition of the world into two blocs bristling with weapons of destruction, two blocs that not only directly threaten each other as systems but threaten every single human being with death. The widespread feeling of unease in society essentially springs from this knowledge that everything could explode at any moment. In addition, we see that this possibility depends not so much on the will or intention of governments but is one that, under certain circumstances, might well be unleashed through some kind of anonymous fate or compulsion, where no one actually willed or planned for this to happen, yet the disaster transpires all the same. The most important thing here is to learn how to change this division of the world into two fatally hostile blocs that are prepared for mutual destruction. It is not my task or intention to talk about politics here directly, but I would say that the very fact that this problem does not appear immediately before our eyes as the most urgent problem of all, that we act as if the possibility of such a catastrophe can be reduced or postponed by actually preparing for it – this entire repression of potential catastrophe seems to me to be an essential part of the calamity we face. Of course, this does not mean that we have to believe in the crocodile tears that are shed on the Soviet side in the name of preserving peace. I certainly have no illusions in that respect. But the readiness for war, the preparation of the means to wage it, possesses its own dynamic.

Finally, just let me mention, as one element of the dire state of things, the undiminished persistence of nationalism in the broader international context. While nationalism has really lost its meaning today, there is no longer a single undeveloped or even somewhat underdeveloped people that – with the assistance of demonstrating students or of military juntas that hope to seize power – is not now suddenly accorded a central place to the concept of the nation. And this in an age in which – in contrast to the concept of humanity that has long since ceased to be an abstract concept – the naturally grounded concept of the nation has really lost its substantial meaning. 

*[p. 96]* ^pg103

And this process is only confirmed and strengthened through the tendencies to enforced consumerism and through the mass culture in which human beings are ineluctably caught up and in which they are constantly<sup>39</sup> and inexorably subjected to everything that encourages them to cling, even in their own consciousness, to the current state of things rather than to question or understand it in a critical way.

So, ladies and gentlemen, in the second part of this lecture I have tried to draw your attention to those negative aspects of our situation that run counter to the more positive ones we discussed in the first part. Now I should just say that I do not want you to understand this to mean that we are confronted by two more or less independent sides of the question here – on the one side the positive aspect and, on the other, the negative aspect that can then simply be removed. For the internal dynamic of this society, which is characterized by progressive rationality and the increasing domination of nature, also involves the growth of those negative aspects or moments. In a certain sense they are brought about by the progressive ones themselves. For reaction is itself a function of progress. This entwinement of both aspects or moments is the fateful historical character from which a humanity that would become mature and responsible is obliged to break free at last. I do not believe that you will require me to offer some ready prescription for how we are to do this. That certainly goes beyond the powers of any individual human being, and we know how little humanity learns from history, how little such prescriptions really help. But I believe that it would certainly be helpful to some extent at least if you can acknowledge both the unparalleled positive potential and the horrific threat that are bound up with each other here without yielding to unwarranted preconceptions and without allowing your own vision to be clouded by the prevailing slogans – if you bear all this constantly in mind and thus acquire some force of resistance against the countervailing forces. That does not mean that you should oppose the notion of progress itself and call instead for older forms of solidarity and commitment. But you should certainly not blindly entrust yourselves to an unfolding dynamic whose fateful character is surely evident. And when the question is raised: ‘What should one do?’, never forget one thing above all: this question really presupposes something like a general social subject – as if human beings of various groups, classes or power blocs could sit around the same round table and discuss in common what it would be best to do. This idea, which roughly corresponds to the rules of direct democracy, seems to me to be obsolete within a state or condition of the world as a whole, where the opposing interests of individual groups in society are so extreme that there really is no such general 

*[p. 97]* ^pg104

subject that could constitute itself through discussion and agreement and thereby set the whole in order.

But the power of consciousness – and I can do no more here than encourage you to develop and embrace such consciousness – is the same thing as the growing power of resistance. I am not talking about resistance to particular groups or particular forces here, but about a resistance to blindness, to the fateful character of what is befalling us \[…\]. And here we have nothing to set against all this than the rigour of our own thinking. At the present moment there is little left to us, little more we can do, than strengthen this resistance through consciousness – that is, remain true and responsible to ourselves in thought. And perhaps that might still give rise to forms of realization that could halt the disaster.
