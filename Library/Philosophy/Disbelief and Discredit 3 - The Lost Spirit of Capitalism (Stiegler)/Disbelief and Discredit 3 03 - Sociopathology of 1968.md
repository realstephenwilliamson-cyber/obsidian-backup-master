The paradox of the super-ego in the 

transformations of capitalism 

The question of the spirit of capitalism is paradoxical from the outset. Weber showed that if capitalism, even before its concretization as industrial society, was in its earliest phase constituted by a new age of the spirit of Christianity, by the Protestant ethic, then this heralds, from the beginning, capitalism’s inescapable vocation for secularization, for the evacuation of religion and for ‘rationalization’ (that is, the total rule of calculation): it is froma the outset dedicated to disenchantment. In the course of this disenchantment, however, a new spirit was also formed, a scientific, political and secular spirit, termed ‘progress’ and ‘modernity’. But when disenchantment reaches its culmination, that is, that limit that in my view hyperindustrial capitalism represents, it becomes the symbolic and ultimately spiritual misery of a capitalism that completely loses its spirit. This is what the French business organization MEDEF (Mouvement des entreprises de France) wants today to overcome by ‘re-enchanting’ the world. 

In The New Spirit of Capitalism Boltanski and Chiapello argue that what they call ‘artistic critique’, which they see as being characteristic of 1968, has also been the source of a transformation of capitalism, through which a ‘new spirit’ has been forged — the third spirit of capitalism, after the first described by Max Weber, and the second, that of the age of the ‘welfare state’ (which was, in truth, the age of consumerism built on Fordism and its avatars). While I am frequently in accord with their analyses, I do not, for all that, believe that today there is any ‘new’ spirit of capitalism whatsoever — let alone one that would represent its third stage. My own view, on the contrary, is that what is at stake here is the libidinal economy of capitalism, and the way it has led to the liquidation of all those sublimities that constitute the super-ego, which is always supported by sublimation processes the fruits of which we call ‘works of the mind’ |oeuvres de l’esprit\]. 

In this chapter, spiritual misery will be analysed as the extreme consequence, and therefore the limit, of a symbolic misery whose lineaments were established at the beginning of the twentieth century, but the reality of which was fully revealed only at the 

*[p. 7]* ^pg13

end of the twentieth century, and which, when it reached this extremity of complete disenchantment, expressed itself as spiritual misery. Disenchantment is no longer just the thesis of a philosopher (Max Weber), or the academic gloss on this thesis, describing a process that works away at everything while remaining hidden from those who put it to work. Rather, it has become an everyday experience that is obvious to all — and, as such, is the express concern of MEDEF. 

This limit is reached when desire itself seems to be exhausted by the completion of disenchantment, destroyed by symbolic misery carried to its paroxysm. What is here being referred to as symbolic misery is the way that the exploitation of libidinal energy tends to exhaust it, with the result that, when this process approaches its conclusion, libidinal energy is replaced by the unleashing of drive-based tendencies. Only when it reaches this point is it expressed as spiritual misery. The binding force that was libidinal energy, as the premier energy of capitalism, then becomes an unbound force, decomposing the drives that were bound by libidinal energy, the latter no longer being able to contain them because it has been destroyed. At this point capitalism has, manifestly, lost its mind — the spirit that was its ability to unify multiplicity and to multiply the One in the age of the machine, that is, in the age of technical reproducibility. This ability played out not only on the plane of machines, but through the translation of this plane onto the psychic and collective planes, a plane of their common consist-and through the projection of ence, that is, by projecting the temporality of a political and social project that, for a long time, was called progress. (For a long time, in a small street in Toulouse leading to the Basilica of Saint-Sernin near the Place Arnaud-Bernard, this trace of 1968 could be seen: ‘Progress = a giant fart’.) 

The completion of symbolic misery, that is, the encounter with its limit in spiritual misery, as proven disenchantment, and where capitalism loses spirit, presupposes a mutation in the becoming of capitalism such that, in the aftermath of 1968, it shows itself to be a historical process of the liquidation of the super-ego: the death of god is the destruction, foreshadowed by Weber, of the spirit of capitalism by the rationalization that this spirit sets to work, and, in its everyday social reality, it is the liquidation of the 

*[p. 8]* ^pg14

super-ego. The paradox is that this liquidation was initially portrayed as being against capitalism, as its very contestation: initially ideological in origin, it was subsequently concretized as the unfurling of a new form of capitalism, for which it confers a functional advantage that is not only strategic but central, structural, principled and processual. This new form of capitalism is the liquidation of the super-ego made inevitable by the need to totally reconfigure ways and modes of life. This need is required by the technologies of the grammatization process, and in particular by a service industry tightly wedded to those dream industries that are the cultural and program industries. The latter are themselves altered by their context within the framework of the digital convergence of information, the audio-visual and telecommunications, in the context of networks in the age of the internet protocol (IP). This program of reconfiguring ways of life was inscribed in the essence of capitalism from its origin: it was already defined in specifically this way by Max Weber.’ 

By evoking, in 1939, a ‘decline in spirit value’, Valéry too anticipated these analyses. It was in 1923, after the shock of the First World War, that he first examined this question in terms of a crisis of spirit, in ‘The Crisis of the Mind” (1923 was also the year that Freud wrote The Ego and the Id, which too, following Beyond the Pleasure Principle, was written in the context of the trauma of war, and to take account of the traumatic effects of war on soldiers; later, in 1936, Husserl would write The Crisis of the European Sciences). The ‘crisis of spirit’ referred to the mortality of civilization (as concretizations and historical concretions of sublimation processes), But the examination of this question of the ‘decline of spirit value’, reformulated in 1939 as the question of this decline and of its value, requires a new, deeper analysis of the questions of sublimation, the super-ego and the pleasure principle. 

Now, these questions — sublimation and desublimation, the super-ego, pleasure and reality — will themselves come to lie at the heart of the thought of Herbert Marcuse, a principal point of reference for the ‘movement’ of 1968. But, as I shall attempt to show here, they do so on the basis of ambiguities contained within Freudian thought itself. The paradox would thus be: if it is true that the ‘subversion’ of 1968 largely accompanied, if not fed, the transformations of capitalism, by having brought it to the point 

*[p. 9]* ^pg15

of a symbolic and spiritual misery placed entirely in the service of industrial populism and addictive consumption, then it was indeed Marcuse’s critique of capitalism and its libidinal economy that, itself having fed into a great deal of 1968 thought, then contributes, in its repercussions — that is, via the misunderstandings provoked by this thought — to the reconfiguration of capitalism into something far more ‘alienating’, to use a word dear to Marcuse and his era (and here it would be necessary to include in the understanding of this word, ‘alienation’, the sense of the psychiatrist as ‘alienist’). 

It is in order to deepen and verify this apparent contradiction, and to draw new prospects from the results of this examination, that I will propose, in this chapter, a reading of Boltanski and Chiapello’s The New Spirit of Capitalism, and, in the following chapter, of Marcuse’s Eros and Civilization.

The question of spirit is that of the we 

The question is therefore that of spirit — and of the spirit of capitalism in an age in which capitalism has lost any notion of it. The question of spirit is in general the question of the we (as, for example, in the most ancient societies, the question of the spirits of the dead returning among the living as their provenance and as their common fate — and as what I call, after Simondon, but in a slightly modified sense, the pre-individual funds of the living insofar as they form psychic and collective individuation proc-I have shown elsewhere,’ collective individuation, like 

esses). If, as a process of adoption, and though this 

psychic individuation, is adoption is in part linked to what Freud called ‘identification’, the question of the we is that of its consistence’ insofar as it does not exist (just as spirits do not exist, any more than phantoms or ghosts, which does not prevent them from being effective: this is what is referred to as symbolic efficacy). 

The French people do not exist, the French language does not exist, any more than does justice: what do exist, however, are French communities, French ways of speaking, both individual and collective, and, just as there is a French grammar (which is a norm through which the collective and multiple individual is projected, and which never corresponds to the spoken reality of 

*[p. 10]* ^pg16

French, which therefore individuates itself psychically: by the fact of being spoken by a speaker of the language), so too there exist French law and French judgments, however unjust they may or may not be. And yet the French people, as a French we, consists, the French language consists, and judicial decisions, even when they are not perfectly just (it is rarely just, if not always unjust, to condemn), are consistent — but on another plane than that which exists. 

Spiritual misery is thus the misery of the we. In other words, the question of spirit today (that is, of the spirit of today) is the following: what remains of the we in the epoch of capitalism and, especially, in the epoch of hyperindustrial capitalism? According to The New Spirit of Capitalism, the genesis of the spirit that I myself call hyperindustrial, or of the spiritual misery that this epoch constitutes, would be essentially tied to what the authors characterize as the ‘artistic critique’ of 1968, which was largely inspired by the critique of the super-ego, itself founded on the Freudian notion of the libido and of its repression. This thesis, which is interesting, stimulating and workable, is, however, probably not sufficient, precisely because it does not revisit Freudian theory, which it seems to consider as merely one historical element among others. 

Even today, psychoanalysis still does not equip us to think the contemporary misery that stems from a misunderstanding of the super-ego. Nor does it enable us to conceive another politics, or the question of the we, that is, of spirit. This is because psycho-analysis neglects the question of the passage from the psychic to the collective, or rather, the question of the psychic as that which only realizes itself in conditions of the individuation of the we. It is politics that articulates these spiritual, symbolic and economic dimensions within a political economy, which is a libidinal economy wherein the psychic and the collective conjugate through the mediation of technical individuation, within a global process of individuation that promulgates laws and configures a super-ego, supported by works of the mind, that is, by the work of sublimation. 

The way forward with these questions is strewn with formidable obstacles. How, for example, should we rethink the critique of the family, and, more generally, of those structures that 

*[p. 11]* ^pg17

concretize identification processes, without succumbing to their restoration? And yet it is precisely because of this risk and these obstacles that such questions must be urgently confronted, by unearthing rather than burying them, and, if we cannot overcome them, by attempting to open up a serious debate, rather than repressing the problem. The political world has renounced any confrontation with this problem of the super-ego, and this is why politics is so appalling - either spineless or populist. And yet we have also recently seen, read and heard, in December 2005 in France, the depths of indignity to which were prepared to sink that verbose and nauseating swill of ‘intellectuals’ who claimed to occupy the place of public debate after the November riots. 

That from which society suffers is the disappearance of the super-ego, and, correlatively, the disappearance of any thought concerning the question and the problem of the super-ego, that is, any critique of the super-ego as a critique of the necessary default \[critique d’un défaut qu’il faut\]. 

Psychoanalysis has been incapable of thinking this question of the super-ego politically. And it is because of this that the mirages and demagogy of ‘artistic critique’, the reappropriation of ‘artistic critique’ by management and marketing, and, finally, the political renunciation of any thought, critique or change in relation to this state of affairs, have all become possible. And this is also why there has been a general rise in suffering in the wake of Marcuse’s critique, itself justified, of the repression, or ‘surplus-repression’, of the super-ego, which brought about that worldwide explosion referred to as ‘May 68” — especially in France, across the planes of politics, sociology and economics, as well as those of the intellectual and the university. The conditions were being put in place for what would subsequently become industrial populism, which was very quickly wedded to political populism, as for instance that pitiful performance during which a French president played the accordion on television, one sad day in the 1970s, an episode that, initially, affected me greatly, then disaffected me. 

Now, in 2006, I am attempting to develop a new understanding of political questions in terms of political economy — which intellectual thought seems to me long since renounced, and specifically since 1968 — and this means returning to a Marxian way of thinking, and to a Weberian analysis that ‘Marxists’ have 

*[p. 12]* ^pg18

tended overwhelmingly to reject, seeing it as associated with the position of Raymond Aron. This new direction involves inscribing the question of belief (condition of trust), and therefore of spirit, at the heart of the whole process, and as the very mechanism of economics. But it is also necessary to initiate a rereading of these two thinkers, Marx and Weber, after Freud, since as a general rule belief is another name for desire, and because the liquidation of belief by capitalism is also a liquidation of desire, even though capitalism has an absolute need for desire (and for belief, without which there can be no trust’), in order to divert the energy of producers towards work, as well as to divert the energy of consumers towards the objects of this form of production.

Knowledge and the super-ego: towards a new 

spirit of capitalism 

I argue here for an industrial political economy founded on the terms of an industrial ecology of spirit: spiritual misery, which has been engendered by capitalism, now directly threatens it, through a reign of stupidity occurring at the very same time that people are talking about ‘cognitive capitalism’ and ‘knowledge societies’? The stakes here are the conditions in which techno-logical individuation, from which cultural and cognitive technologies emerge, is articulated with or, on the contrary, disarticulated with psychic individuation and collective individuation. Currently, these conditions must submit in a hegemonic way to the very short-term interests of financial capitalism, resulting in psycho-social disindividuation and leading to uncontrollable societies - which means that, as argued by the leading economist Patrick Artus, capitalism is in the course of destroying itself far more than it is acquiring some new spirit. This situation is not an inevitable fate: cultural and cognitive technologies could and should be placed into the service of an elevation, and a re-evaluation (bringing about a ‘transvaluation’), of spirit value — that is, equally, of the reconstitution of a psycho-social and collective industrial individuation borne by and bearing a process of sublimation. 

In The Decadence of Industrial Democracies 1 argued that capitalism is, today, what must be defended (against itself) and not what must be fought against. In the current state of things, it 

*[p. 13]* ^pg19

is possible that capitalism could indeed come, brutally and prematurely, to a very bad end, and we along with it — as these tendencies decompose. In Uncontrollable Societies of Disaffected Individuals 1 argued that control societies, which are the effective reality of contemporary capitalism, have reached the point of becoming uncontrollable, disaffecting and disindividuating psychic and collective individuals. We must prevent capitalism from ending badly, and we must find the path that will enable this epoch of individuation to continue and to end well, that is, to lead to something else - to something that today, however, we cannot even imagine. But this is something that will occur only if, somehow, things manage to improve, and at a time that remains completely unknown. 

Such a path would indeed be that of a new spirit of capitalism, that is, of a new form of the spirit generated by a new form of capitalism, or in other words by a new form of political economy. It would put to work, through the mediation of a public authority rethought in terms of its principles, composition and aims, an industrial politics conceived on a completely new basis, and corresponding to the specificities of what must be analysed as the age of technologies of the spirit. This new industrial civilization must take care of spirit value to the strict extent that, at the same time: (1) capitalism, having become cognitive, makes knowledge the first principle of its development — as already anticipated by Marx; and (2) this knowledge, being sublimated libidinal energy, is destroyed by capitalism in proportion to its destruction of libidinal energy in general and sublimation processes in particular — and therefore at the same time destroys the super-ego, which depends on sublimation processes for its authority. 

In a context of technics having become industrial technology, taking care of spirit value requires the conception of a political and industrial economy of this spirit. Disindividuation is a desubjectivation that affects social forms as much as the psychic subject, and as such it generates disbelief, miscreance, discredit, demotivation and irrationality. I argue, however, that existence is distinguished from subsistence by the capacity to project itself onto the plane of consistences, and that the plane of consistences must be cultivated through practices, that is, through the putting to work of specific techniques, memory-technics, hypomnemata — practices 

*[p. 14]* ^pg20

that cannot be reduced to mere usages. According to this analysis, then, I posit that we must conceive a new industrial society, according to another industrial model, and based on a socialization of the technologies emerging from grammatization, in the sense established in De la misére symbolique 1 and The Decadence of Industrial Democracies, technologies that are part of what I call hypomnemata. 

The Decadence of Industrial Democracies concluded with the following hypothesis: there is a future for industrial society only to the extent that it once again cultivates an otium of the people — the question is how to specify what I am here calling the ‘people’ (which is that which is lacking |celui qui manque\], according to Klee, but which I prefer to say is in default/deficient fait défaut(s)|).!° It is not that the question of libido in the capitalist industrial economy has been completely ignored: Herbert Marcuse in particular addressed it explicitly as the question of what is characteristic of the capitalist libidinal economy, and it just as clearly lay at the heart of the thought of the ‘desiring machines’! of capitalism. Nevertheless, this question has yet to be thought in the way it must be thought, namely, as the pure and simple destruction of libido and, with it, of all motives, that is, the destruction of the super-ego and the sublimities, of what are called idealities, and therefore as the liberation and unleashing of drive-based forces (and not merely of the ‘impersonal powers’ of technics!?), which was the theme of Uncontrollable Societies of Disaffected Individuals. 

Desublimation is the fact. And the problem is that the process of psychic and collective individuation - where the ‘and’, both conjunctive and disjunctive, constitutes, I believe, spirit itself, and does so as the power of transindividuation constitutive of a we — presupposes sublimation.

intermittency of noetic action 

The origin of the ego ideal, and therefore of the super-ego, consists in an internalization of the objects of sexual libido that have been lost and that generate a process of identification, these objects being, initially and principally, one’s parents, those who form the 

*[p. 15]* ^pg21

ego ideal that then becomes the super-ego. This being so, this internalization, insofar as it transforms narcissistic libido into object libido, inevitably involves sublimation. The capacity for sublimation, therefore, lies at the very origin of the super-ego. Or, more precisely, idealization and sublimation, even if they must not be conflated, nevertheless form a system and are, as such, in a co-constitutive relation, or, to speak in Simondonian terms, in a transductive relation. 

This is why Lacan, in Discourse to Catholics,'’\* can simultaneously posit: 

© that earth and sky have been emptied of God in the epoch of disjunctions ‘constituted by our technology \[techniques\]’; 

¢ that the fundamental question is thus sublimation as art, religion and science, and that this is equally the question of the death drive; and 

e that ‘no force in man is more readily sublimated’ than the libido. 

In The Decadence of Industrial Democracies I tried to show that the noetic soul is noetic only by default, and only intermittently, thanks to its originary facticity, that is, its technicity. It is because of this intermittency of the noetic, that is, of spirit, that it must act as if it was noetic, precisely at the moment when, because it regresses (being originarily exposed to this tendency that is its being-in-default, that is, its stupidity —a stupidity Deleuze believed could be called ‘transcendental’ \[and which is therefore also ‘as if’\] — its stupidity but not its fault), it must conform to a law that may indeed be unjust, but through the practice and application of which, alone, justice can consist to the infinite and find its authority without limit, and as the point of convergence of all motives, all legislation, and even all religion, however diverse and seemingly antagonistic these may be. 

This is why the super-ego, which is necessitated by being-indefault, cannot fail or disappear without bringing about the reign of barbarism. 

Human creations are easily destroyed, and science and technology, which have built them up, can also be used for their annihilation. 

*[p. 16]* ^pg22

We have spoken of the hostility to civilization which is produced by the pressure that civilization exercises, the renunciations of instinct which it demands. If one imagines its prohibitions lifted ... then, one may take any woman one pleases as a sexual object, . one may without hesitation kill one’s rival for her love or , one can carry off any anyone else who stands in one’s way . . . 

of the other man’s belongings without asking leave.'\* 

The intermittency of being makes necessary the always provisional morality of the super-ego. We must rely on artifices and fetishes, whether they are rosaries or maxims, proverbs or strict rules, to orient ourselves when we ourselves are not in the passage to the noetic act, that is, the critical act — the critical act through which, far from simply conforming to a law, we interpret and invent, that is, through which we pass into action. And conversely, the temptation of ‘hostility to civilization’, generated by civilization itself when it is no longer capable of producing motives to individuate, leads to passages to the act of negative sublimation, to the explosive consequences of desublimation, that is, of the loss of collective individuation. At such a point, however, the hostility to civilization changes form: it manifests itself as hostility to what, in civilization, is perceived as destroying this civilization - and here, Freudian thought is no longer sufficient. 

It remains the case that only the passage to the noetic act enables disindividuation to be overcome — the question being the techno-logical conditions, that is, the industrial conditions, of this passage, given that, as I argued in De la misére symbolique 2, noesis is originarily technesis. To think is to engage in politics, that is, to fight.

The crisis of capitalism as ‘ideological disarray’ 

and as crisis of spirit after May 1968 

‘Capitalism is flourishing’ thanks to a ‘regeneration’ that essentially benefited, at least in France, from the 1968 movement, and yet Luc Boltanski and Eve Chiapello argue in 1999 that it is in danger: it is undergoing a crisis that shows itself in particular through the demotivation of the managerial class. And this demotivation, analysed in Constituer l'Europe 2" as a consequence of 

*[p. 17]* ^pg23

the fact that managerial techniques for motivating and manipulating libido have become counterproductive, is here tied to the destabilization of social structures, and especially families, and the weakening of the notion of the common good. In addition, the managerial model itself has been profoundly transformed by real-time technologies and global reactivity,'\* and the managers of the apparatus of production, who belong to what used to be called the middle class and the petite or moyenne bourgeoisie, suffer from the decline of the family (which is also to say, necessarily, of the super-ego) and from the spread of workplace flexibility, which is here posited as a major reason for this destruction of the stability of the family. Capitalism’s search for maximum flexibility ‘chimed with a depreciation of the family as a factor of temporal and geographical inflexibility, so that . . . similar ideological schemas are mobilized to justify adaptability in work relations and mobility in emotional life’." 

These changes seriously weaken the future prospects of more recent generations (but weaken, too, their amorous desire, which is increasingly exposed to the end of love, to celibacy, and to the likelihood of being a single parent), and do so at a time when unemployment has become a chronic reality. The result is deep “ideological disarray’, and the disappearance of reasons for hope, if not indeed actual despair. 

If motives are disappearing, however, this also means that the spirit of capitalism is disappearing, and that ‘capitalism will face increasing difficulties, if it does not restore some grounds for hope to those whose engagement is required for the functioning of the system as a whole’.”® This state of affairs leads to an even greater malaise, in that, at the same time: (1) it results from the ‘recuperation-implementation’ by capitalism of propositions deriving from 1968, through which a good proportion of the management of the apparatus of production more or less reinvented themselves; and (2) there has been a renunciation (on the part of ‘intellectuals’) of any analysis of the profound transformations experienced by capitalism over the last three decades, leaving this management helpless in the face of these changes, given that: 

opportunities to resist micro-changes with ominous consequences also emerged that were missed by those who should have been 

*[p. 18]* ^pg24

especially vigilant about the risks entailed by these transformations — in particular, because they did not realize that the ‘recuperation-implementation’ by capitalism of some of their proposals required them in turn to reinvest in analysis and shift ground.”! 

Boltanski and Chiapello define politics as the will for a way of life,2? and they argue, referring to the Weberian tradition, that remuneration alone is not enough to sustain engagement in capitalism. The spirit of capitalism is the ‘ideology that justifies engagement in capitalism’,” in particular through the collective benefits and the common good that it is supposed to bring, but also through the moral reasons that must be developed as the motives to exist rather than merely to subsist, and that must, therefore, consist through these existences by giving consistence to themselves and by relating to subsistences: ‘people need powerful moral reasons for rallying to capitalism’.\*\* And for Max Weber, according to Boltanski and Chiapello, this spirit ‘refers to the set of ethical motivations which, although their purpose is foreign to capitalist logic, inspire entrepreneurs in activity conducive to capital accumulation’.’° 

It is only these moral and ethical reasons and motives that, as the internalization of the justifications for the systemic constraints of capitalism, allow ‘people not to find their everyday universe uninhabitable — one of the conditions of a sustainable world’.”\* Almost 40 years after 1968, it does indeed seem that the everyday world has become uninhabitable, and hardly sustainable, that is, extremely threatening, and has done so in a far more serious way. If the spirit of capitalism is a collection of motivations more powerful than the weight of those constraints through which capitalism constitutes itself as a system of production, then only the existence of such a spirit will make it possible to overcome ‘the despair or nihilism which \[it\] constantly induces - not only in those whom it oppresses but also, on occasion, in those who have responsibility for maintaining it and, via education, transmitting its values’.”” 

The constitution of the spirit of capitalism was based on the rationalization and ‘generalization of the tendency to calculation’,’8 and a second spirit of capitalism arose later, between the 1930s and the 1960s, the decades that saw the rise of both mass 

*[p. 19]* ^pg25

consumption and ‘a faith in rationality and long-term planning’.”” In the 1990s, however, a crisis of meaning arose for wage-earners and managers, and in turn a motivational crisis among executives, a crisis illustrated in The New Spirit of Capitalism via a quotation from Jean Giono: ‘The main thing isn’t to live, but to have a 

reason for living.” 

For there has been a ‘change in the spirit of capitalism after the evolution of management between 1960 and 1990°,?? which was itself a ‘response to \[those\] demands for “artistic critique”, liberty and authenticity’ that characterize the ‘values’ of 1968. These values, coming from ‘leftism, and especially the self-management movement’,”? tried to constitute a new spirit of capitalism: ‘{T\]he qualities that are guarantees of success in this new spirit — autonomy, spontaneity, rhizomorphous capacity, multitasking . . . conviviality, openness to others and novelty... are taken directly 

from the repertoire of May 1968." 

This inversion is experienced in a more or less confused way, and it struggles against the various forms of cynicism to which the critique of ‘bourgeois morality’ leads when it is recuperated and implemented by a capitalism that has lost its spirit. Faced with this inversion, the former ‘artistic critique’ now seeks out ‘new values’ in the form of human rights, ethics (in the soft meaning of this term), environmental protection, animal rights, and so on. 

‘Artistic critique’ and ‘social critique’, or the 

6 

jargon of authenticity 

According to my own analysis (but not that of The New Spirit of Capitalism, which remains highly ambiguous on this point), the recuperation of these ‘qualities . . . taken directly from the repertoire of May 1968’, accompanied by a discourse on the liquidation of the ‘surplus-repression’ of the super-ego, as Marcuse terms it, constitutes a liquidation of spirit much more than the appearance of a ‘new spirit’. Translated into management and especially marketing terms — the two being, however, inseparable, and implemented on the same ideological ground, on the side of production and consumption respectively — these ‘qualities’ sanction the establishment of a symbolic misery and a spiritual misery (not to 

*[p. 20]* ^pg26

mention the political misery from which they originate). This misery arises at the very moment ‘faith in rationality and long-term planning’ collapses, the very moment the first symptoms of demotivation appear, which it is the job of these ‘qualities’ to contain, just as it is their job to prevent criticism of economic and psychological misery. Ultimately, given that this containment comes at the price of destroying libido, these ‘values’ only aggravate this profoundly toxic, headlong flight. This leads to what, in Uncontrollable Societies of Disaffected Individuals, 1 refer to as disaffection, which is also dis-affectation (withdrawal, a loss of role, disindividuation), these alleged ‘values’ turning, therefore, into the very opposite. 

The paradox of the ecology of spirit is that capitalism, which needs desire to survive, destroys desire — and hence suffers from the tendency of libidinal energy to fall, a tendency that would, in the twentieth century, be incited through thousands of techniques designed to struggle against the tendency of the rate of profit to fall. The analysis of Boltanski and Chiapello in truth suffers from the disease denounced by Marx as idealism, a disease that consists in taking effects for causes, the effects being in this case ideas. Ideas do not cause phenomena. This is not to suggest that there would be some other thing that causes ideas. It does not mean, for example, that technical individuation, or the transformation of the relations of production, would be causes of the ‘superstructural’ production of ideas. 

*[p. 31]* ^pg37

rl 

All this constitutes one process of co-individuation, a multiplicity that is unified, therefore, by a spirit - which is not at all an idealist conception, but nor is it a materialist conception. In this process, the supports of production would be the technical system inasmuch as it is a system of tertiary retentions, and the relations of production would be the economic and social relations between the roles and the individuals who play these roles, and through the play of which ideas occur. Through this process, psychic secondary retentions become, via transindividuation, collective secondary retentions, a transformation that is also the becoming of the world, insofar as it bears the supports and relations of production just as it is supported by and related to them. And within this process, these supports and relations, and the ideas of psychic and collective individuals, are all co-produced together. 

And this means that the discourse of Boltanski and Chiapello remains mired in the opposition of infrastructure and superstructure, even if it is inverted into a new idealism (and it is a naive inversion, since it is not declared as such), when The New Spirit of Capitalism finally posits that the ideas of 1968 lie at the origin of the transformations of capitalism, which everything points towards being absolutely false. And at the same time, it is an interesting idea, which leads to the paradox that it is fruitful to unravel this idea to the extent that it in turn leads us towards another paradox, yet more fruitful, and even more disturbing: the tendency of libidinal energy to fall. The thesis of Boltanski and Chiapello rings true, it sounds plausible, and so we say: ‘That’s it’; but upon reflection, we instead say: ‘That’s not it. It is more complicated than that. What, then, is an idea? An idea is a passage to actuality \[un passage 4 l’acte|. A passage into the noetic act that individuates a pre-individual potential is always brought forth pre-individually on three planes at once: the psychic plane of the individual who has the idea; the social plane of the world in which this individual lives; and the technical plane that supports this world as a system of tertiary retentions, and that, as a constantly evolving technical system, itself individuates. The idea always participates in these three types of individuation. It is in this way that one can, for example, explain that, on the one side, there are many psychic

ideas; on the other, there are social structures that transindividuate all forms of organizational models, political as well as managerial; and third, there is a set of interconnected technical networks that support but also require these models and the ideas that are supposed to ‘inspire’ them, like that spirit that breathes into souls the truth of the world. So what, then, is the origin of anything in all this? Do networks lie at the origin of organizations and ideas, or is the converse true? False problems. 

Nothing is the origin of anything else: all this - individuals, ideas, organizations, technics — is formed through a process. Ideas are secreted by the process, just as a swollen river will carry mud along with it and deposit it as silt. In the second half of this book we will look at Marcuse, and the reason for doing so is that he clearly tried to articulate the psychic plane with the collective plane, the latter, moreover, being analysed in terms of production. Consequently, he failed to see that what must be confronted in Freud is the fact that, within psychoanalysis, technics remains unthought. Marcuse was unable to see this because his reading of Freud was conducted through Marx, and because, contrary to received wisdom, Marx suffered from the same weaknesses as Freud in relation to the role of technics in the life of the mind or spirit. 

What goes on between the ‘ideas of ’68’ and capitalism amounts to what could be called a ruse of history: the activists of 1968 imagined they were fighting capitalism, whereas in reality they helped it evolve, accompanying and legitimating its transformation while believing they were being transgressive; they helped it become, to give birth to what was brought forth as the historical necessity of a cloud that itself bore an unprecedented drive-based storm, which then became the principle of a capitalist system devoid of all spirit. The 1960s saw the rise in global industrial society of a completely new phenomenon, of which it is clear, from reading The New Spirit of Capitalism, that the academic world has yet to take the measure: the hallucination of the small screen. More specifically, they fail to see that différance is the movement of desire and singularity towards singularity, that it is the singularity of this movement itself, thought as the spatialization of time and the temporalization of space. 

Likewise, Deleuze’s thought of simulacra does not mean there is no singularity. On the contrary: it is indeed necessary to think singularity at a time when the universality of the market shows itself for what it is, namely, a global system of calculation. But what the fact that singularity is constituted through simulacra

shows, precisely, is that the question of singularity and the question of authenticity are completely different questions. 

The question that remains obscure in The New Spirit of Capitalism, but also in our age itself, notably because we still lack a theory of sublimation (as Laplanche and Pontalis point out), is the question of the structure of fantasy. The market is fantasy. And singularity, which cannot be reduced to the calculations of the market, and which is literally incomparable, and therefore incalculable and, as such, irreducible, is also a fantasy. It is the oscillating play between calculation and the incalculable in the fantasy that constitutes desire, and therefore in the life of capitalism and its spirit. But it is also this play of oppositions that, in the current stage — where there is a loss of spirit and where stupidity, cynicism and resentment reign - tends to become impossible.
