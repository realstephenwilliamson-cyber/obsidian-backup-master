By at least the 13th century ce, when Jayaratha, Śivānanda, and Vidyānanda composed their commentaries on the *Vāmakeśvarīmata*, Śrīvidyā emerged as a sophisticated pan-Indian tradition with a well-developed exegesis drawing on the “Krama-influenced, Pratyabhijñā-based exegesis of scripture in the Trika,” which had become a dominant textual resource of “exegetical and spiritual inspiration” (Sanderson, 2007, 427– 434; for dates of these commentators, as well as Amṛtānanda, who composed a commentary on the *Yoginīhṛdaya* \[c. 14th century CE\], see Sanderson, 2007, 412–419; for Krama, Pratyabhijñā, Trika, see also → Kashmir Śaivism). In time, in South India an expurgated form of Śrīvidyā became subsumed by the *śaṅkarācārya*s, the spiritual authority of the broader community of the orthodox Śaiva Brahmans known as → Smārtas (Sanderson, 1988, 689, 704). Later commentators, such as the 18th-century Bhāskararāya and the 19th-century Śivarāmasvamin (author of the *Gayatrīmantravivrṭti* and the *Śrīvidyāmantravivrṭti*), attempted to reconcile the vedic and the tantric domains, and contemporary branches of Śrīvidyā include the Advaita → Vedānta theologian → Śaṅkara, author of the commentary on the *Brahmasūtra*, as one of the preceptors of their lineage. While Śrīvidyā ritual has been traditionally practiced in the homes of the initiated in relative secrecy, some of its features have been incorporated into the mainstream Hindu temple worship, and the most treasured hymns of the tradition, the *Lalitāsahasranāma* and the *Saundaryalaharī*, have remained the focus of popular devotion. Initiation into Śrīvidyā has been traditionally open to women and men, regardless of caste (see e.g. Brooks, 1992, xiii) – however, in practice, many contemporary *guru*s deny initiation to those who are not male Brahmans (see Dempsey, 2006, for a study of Śrī Rājarājeśvarī Pīṭham in Rush, NY, for a notable exception to this practice; in particular see Dempsey, 2006, 92–93). 

The Nityā Cult and the Emergence 

of Śrīvidyā 

Although the early history of Śrīvidyā is obscure, several extant texts suggest the existence of an antecedent Tantric cult that propitiated the *nityā* goddesses, serving as the subordinate deities to Tripurā. In this earlier tradition, “rites for success in love predominated,” and the Tripurasundarī’s consort was Kāmadeva (the god of love), not Śiva, as in the mature Śrīvidyā tradition (Sanderson, 2009, 47; for textual evidence of the *nityā* cult, see Sanderson, 2009, 47–49). Among the earliest sources for the worship of Tripurasundarī is the text of the Sanskrit *Nityākaula* surviving in a single incomplete palm-leaf manuscript from Nepal. In this text, Tripurasundarī is accompanied by Kāmadeva and a retinue of 11 *nityā* goddesses. The *Siddhakhaṇḍa* of the *Manthānabhairavatantra* preserves a variant of the *nityā* cult, providing instructions for the worship of Tripurasundarī with her consort and a retinue of nine *nityā*s. The third text identified in connection with the *nityā* cult is a syncretistic text belonging to the cult of the goddess Kubjikā, the *Ciñciṇīmatasārasamuccaya*. This text “contains a section drawn from the Nityākaula, or from some lost text closely related to it” and shows an early classification of the Kaula doctrines, in which the *nityā* cult is classified as the tradition of the southern order (*dakṣiṇagharāmnāya*; Sanderson, 2009, 48–49). 

At this time, not much can be said about the dating of this antecedent cult with certainty, except that it flourished prior to the 11th century and perhaps significantly earlier than that, based on the references to the worship of *nityā*s in the *Kubjikāmata* and the later *Jayadrathayāmala* (see Sanderson, 2010, 47–48; see also below). Furthermore, the cult of the *nityā*s was known to → Abhinavagupta (*TĀ.* 28.123–124 \[*nityātantravidaḥ*\]; *PaTrV.* \[*śrīnityātantreṣu*; see Gnoli, 1985, 238\]). The rites of love magic, predominant in the ritual of the *nityā* cult, formed the backdrop against which the mature cult of Tripurasundarī developed. Although this connection has been deemphasized in the later Śrīvidyā scriptures and exegesis, references to love magic have been preserved in the names of the subordinate goddesses (e.g. the 16 deities of attraction \[*ākarṣa*\], worshipped in the outer petals of the *śrīcakra* \[*VāMa.* 1.138–141\]), the descriptions of the 

*[p. 817]* ^pg3

physical beauty of Tripurasundarī (who is portrayed as red, the color most vested with amorous connotations), and the expected results of worship, which encompass the amorous attraction of women to the ritualist (e.g. *VāMa.* 4.38– 41). Furthermore, the bow, five flower-arrows, noose, and goad, which Tripurasundarī holds in her hands, are the standard attributes of the four-armed Kāmadeva (for his visualization see, e.g., the *Īśānaśivagurudevapaddhati, Kriyāpāda*, 22.32–35). Although the benefits of amorous attractiveness have ceased to be the focal point of the worship of Tripurasundarī over time, perhaps it is this very capacity of this tradition to embrace all aspects of human existence – from the mundane to the sublime – that contributed to its popularity. 

Śrīvidyā, Brahmanism, and the 

Cults of the Local Goddesses 

There is no evidence that the ritual practice of the *nityā* cult has survived the reshaping of the landscape of the Hindu Tantra following the tremendous creative development of Śaivism during the 10th to 12th centuries. However, the Śrīvidyā tradition, which likely developed out of this earlier cult toward the end of the early medieval period, flourishes to this day in pan-Indian ritual practice. Many Śrīvidyā texts and commentaries are extant. The mature Śrīvidyā developed a sophisticated commentarial tradition, which upgraded a modest cult of love magic with the exegesis within the Kashmirian nondualistic Śākta-Śaiva discourse. The worship of Tripurasundarī flourished in → Kashmir, in the Kathmandu Valley, in Tamil Nadu, in Andhra Pradesh, and among the Nambūtiri Brahmans of → Kerala, as well as in Gujarat and Uttar Pradesh. In east India (see → Assam), Tripurasundarī (also known as Ṣoḍaśī) was incorporated into the tradition of the ten *mahāvidyā*s (on geographical distribution of Śrīvidyā, see Sanderson, 2010, 80–83; for *mahāvidyā*s see → Kālī). 

In a later development, “Śrīvidyā appears to have undergone something of a reformation in the south” (Brooks, 1992, 48). Tripurasundarī came to be seen as the esoteric essence of the local goddesses in the major temples of Shringeri, Kanchipuram, Srisailam, and Madurai, and in less well-known temples such as the Akhilāṇḍeśvarī (Tam. Akilāṇṭēcuvari) temple in Tiruchirappalli, Tamil Nadu, and the Mūkāmbikā temple in Kol-lur, Karnataka. In Kashmir, as in South India, the cult of Tripurasundarī provided the “framework for the conceptualization of the cults of the local goddesses” (Sanderson, 2007, 433). With the adoption of an expurgated form of Śrīvidyā by Smārta Brahmans, whose tendency was to “distance themselves from Kashmiri Kaulism in order to distinguish Śrīvidyā from . . . Tantrism,” a number of texts on the worship of Tripurasundarī, “which were acceptable to even the most conservative Hindus,” came to be attributed to Śaṅkara and his *paramaguru* ( *guru*’s *guru*) Gauḍapāda, who were “viewed as pillars of conservative orthodoxy” (Brooks, 1992, 49–50). 

Classification 

The classical Śrīvidyā is among the latest traditions of the non-Saiddhāntika Mantramārga and postdates the goddess-oriented Vidyāpīṭha doctrines, including the broader Kālīkula tradition of the worship of goddess Kālī/Kālasaṃkarṣaṇī and its Kashmirian branch, known as Krama (for Mantramārga, Vidyāpīṭha, Kālīkula, and Krama, see → Kashmir). 

However, the antecedent *nityā* cult is older than the Kubjikā tradition and at least some of the texts of the Kālīkula (for a classification and features of the Śaiva and Śakta-Śaiva doctrines, see Sanderson, 1988; 2006). The *Ciñciṇīmatasārasamuccaya*, a syncretic text of the Kubjikā cult, includes the *nityā* cult in its classification. The *Kubjikāmata* and the later Ṣaṭkas (sextets) of the Kālīkula’s *Jayadrathayāmala* refer to its deities, and the former also to one of its *mantra*s (Sanderson, 2010, 47–48). 

What particularly sets Śrīvidyā apart from the earlier traditions of the Vidyāpīṭha is the prevalence of the imagery of love magic, inherited from the antecedent *nityā* cult, which is in striking contrast to the Vidyāpīṭha’s Kāpālika imagery of the cremation grounds from the earlier Atimārga traditions (Sanderson, 2009, 49; for Kāpālika imagery in Mantramārga, see Sanderson 1988, 669–75; for Kāpālika, Atimārga, see → Kashmir). According to the *Ciñciṇīmatasārasamuccaya*, the *nityā* cult was associated with the southern transmission, or the southern order. This system of classification of the Kaula traditions included 

*[p. 818]* ^pg4

Kālīkula as the teaching of the northern order, a system related to Trika as that of the eastern order, and the Kubjikā cult as the tradition of the western order (Sanderson, 2009, 48–49). 

In the later schemas, Śrīvidyā saw itself as transcending the four Kaula teachings associated with the cardinal directions (*VāMa.* 1.12). *Parātantra*, a syncretic scripture probably produced in the Kathmandu Valley and a synoptic text of the *Vāḍavānalīya*, quoted in the *Puraścaryāṃ̇ava*, included this new configuration (Sanderson, 2003, 367–368). Both *Parātantra* and *Vāḍavānalīya* provided an artificial schema of six transmissions, which preserved the goddesses associated with Krama in the north and the cult of Kubjikā in the west. The goddesses of the now obsolete Trika were replaced with Pūṃ̇eśvarī in the east. Instead of the *nityā* cult, which had been supplanted by the mature cult of Tripurasundarī, Niśeśvarī was established in the south. The goddesses Pūṃ̇eśvarī and Niśeśvarī have probably been artificially constructed to fill in the gaps in this classification, since they are not attested outside of this context. The Buddhist Vajrayoginī, along with Nairṛteśvarī and other minor goddesses, was added as the tradition of the nadir. 

The cult of Tripurasundarī (i.e. Śrīvidyā) was added as the transcendent, upper tradition of the zenith (Sanderson, 2003, 366–368). 

Śrīvidyā Literature 

The Early Tantras 

Śrīvidyā practice has always required “ritual literacy in Sanskrit” (Brooks, 1992, xiii), and the tradition has produced a great variety of scriptures, commentaries, devotional hymns, and ritual manuals. According to T. Goudriaan, Śrīvidyā is “conspicuous by the literary standard of at least part of its texts, and by the mere coherence and elaboration of its doctrine” (Goudriaan, 1981, 58). While the majority of these texts have been composed in Sanskrit, some literature relevant to the tradition, which is yet to be fully examined, is in vernacular languages. The foremost among these is the Tamil *Tirumantiram*. 

Although the text is traditionally dated quite early, and some have dated it as early as “the fifth, sixth, or seventh century,” it “may have been dated six or seven centuries too early” as it “contains a complex of concepts with Sanskrit labels the development of which one can trace in Sanskrit (not Tamil) literature that must certainly post-date the fifth century” (Goodall, 2004, xxix). The first extant Sanskrit Śrīvidyā text, the *Vāmakeśvarīmata*, also referred to as *Nityāṣoḍa-śikāṃ̇ava* or *Catuḥśatī*, was probably composed in the early 11th century. It is often grouped together with the later *Yoginīhṛdaya*. However, significant differences in terminology and the types of practices in the *Vāmakeśvarīmata* and the *Yoginīhṛdaya* suggest that the two texts represent “distinct intellectual and historical traditions” (Brooks, 1992, 38). 

In the *Vāmakeśvarīmata*, the magical means for attracting the desired partner were given the most prominence, compared to practices focusing on speech and eloquence, and rituals for overcoming the negative effects of poisons, gaining the power to cure snake bites, and the control over *bhūta*s (ghosts), *preta*s (ghouls), and *piśāca*s (goblins). The *Vāmakeśvarīmata* incorporated the subordinate *nityā* deities of the antecedent cult as part of a much larger ritual system comprising 78 deities, arranged on the nine levels of the *śrīcakra* (see → *maṇḍala*s and → *yantra*s). The text included a detailed exposition on the *vidyā*s (*mantra*s of feminine deities), that is, the *mūlavidyā*, given in the coded form, the supplementary *vidyā*s, and the *bīja*s (the so-called seed syllables; see → *mantra*s). Detailed instructions for the drawing of the *śrīcakra*, its worship, the practice of *nyāsa* (installing the divinity in the body of the ritualist), and additional practices for attraction, subjugation, protection, and the attainment of various *siddhi*s (supernatural powers) were also provided. A variety of *mudrā*s (particular hand gestures common in Tantra that are seen as the physical analogue to the deities and their *mantra*s/*vidyā*s) are also discussed. A list corresponding to nine out of ten *daśamahāmudrā*s (lit. the ten great *mudrā*s), which are still in current practice, is given with only slight variations and in the same order (*VāMa.* 1.164; detailed discussion in *VāMa.* 3, a short chapter on *mudrā*s). Among the *daśamahāmudrā*s, the *trikhaṇḍamudrā* is not mentioned in the text, but Jayaratha in his commentary, the *Vāmakeśvarīmatavivaraṇa*, creatively interprets *ādi* (first, etcetera) to refer to this *mudrā*, completing the list of ten. Instructions for *homa* (fire ceremony), *japa* (*mantra* repetition), and *dhyāna* (meditation, visualization) are also given. Among the meditations mentioned

While the 13th-century commentators, particularly Jayaratha and Śivānanda, reevaluated the *Vāmakeśvarīmata* in light of the Kashmirian nondual philosophy, the text itself did not display explicit awareness of these doctrines. 

The *Yoginīhṛdaya*, introduced as an esoteric elaboration on the points that have not been sufficiently clarified in the *Vāmakeśvarīmata*, was evidently composed after the time of Abhinavagupta and Kṣemarāja, probably in the mid-11th century or soon after. The text incorporated terminology and doctrinal concepts derived from Trika and Krama Pratyabhijñā-based metaphysics and cosmologies that updated the cult with the Śākta-Śaiva nondualist paradigms, dominant from the 11th century onward. While retaining the configuration of the subordinate deities to be worshipped in the *śrīcakra* discussed in the *Vāmakeśvarīmata*, in addition to the external ritual, the *Yoginīhṛdaya* introduced a number of sophisticated meditative practices and ways of mapping the *śrīcakra* onto the subtle body of the practitioner, reflecting the trend toward the esotericization and internalization in this tradition. The *śrīcakra* in the *Yoginīhṛdaya* is viewed not only as an external ritual diagram, but also as a map of cosmic emanation (*YHṛ.* 1.36–49; see also Padoux, 2003, 240). The text describes the cosmic consciousness, which has the nature of *\[pra\]mātṛ* (cognizer), *\[pra\]meya* (object of cognition), and *\[pra\]māṇa* (cognition itself) coming into being in the form of the universe (*YHṛ.* 1.9–12; 1.50–51). This cosmic consciousness in the *śrīcakra* is fourfold, manifesting as Vāmā, Jyeṣṭā, Raudrī, and the Great Goddess, Ambikā (*YHṛ.* 1.36–40). The four goddesses are equated with the four levels of speech, inherited from Pratyabhijñā – *paśyantī*, *madhyamā, vaikharī*, and the transcendent fourth level of *parā vāc*, the supreme word in its primeval, undifferentiated state (*YHṛ.* 1.36–40; for a detailed exposition on Kashmirian conceptions of the levels of speech, see Padoux, 1990, 166–223; see also → *mantra*s). Among the extant commentaries on this text, the 14th-century *Yoginīhṛdayadīpikā* by Amṛtānanda and the 18th-century *Setubandha* by Bhāskararāya have been published. 

The Later Tantras 

The text usually referred to as the *Tantrarājatantra* calls itself the *Kādimatatantra*, with *Tantrarājatantra* being merely an epithet of that title (Goudriaan, 1981, 64). The text contains new features in the doctrinal system, such as the division into the two *mata*s, or doctrinal lines of interpretation (Goudriaan, 1981, 64). Traditionally, Śrīvidyā lineages and their texts distinguish themselves by the two *mata* divisions: *kādi* and *hādi* (see Brooks, 1992, 87–90). The *hādimata* is not preserved in the contemporary tradition. Among the doctrinal differences between the two *mata*s, the primary divergence is in the extraction of the *mūlavidyā*. In the 

*[p. 820]* ^pg6

Kāmarāja Sampradāya, the root *vidyā* is extracted as *kādividyā* (i.e. the *vidyā* beginning with *ka*). In the Lopāmudrā Sampradāya, *hādividyā* begins with *ha* (Brooks, 1992, 89–90). The text of the *Tantrarājatantra*, however, uncharacteristically names the two *mata*s as *kādimata* and *kālīmata*, the latter likely referring to the Kālīkula (Brooks, 1992, 42; Goudriaan, 1981, 64). While the early texts discussed above outlined the main features of the Śrīvidyā external and internal ritual, the *Tantrarājatantra* includes considerably more detail. The text offers a thorough exposition of the worship of the *nityā*s, with Lalitā named as the foremost of them, and introduces two deities, Vārāhī and Kurukullā, to whom sacrificial offerings (*bali*) are given at the end of the *pūjā*. 

The *Tantrarājatantra* covers a great number of subjects, including *mantraśāstra, vāstuśāstra,* *homa*, and *yantra*s, among others, in a “learned, sophisticated, non-repetitive style,” combining “an impressive quantity of ślokas with a considerable literary achievement” (Goudriaan, 1981, 66–67). 

Among other later Tantras, one of the most popular is the *Jñānāṃ̇avatantra*. The text follows the *kādimata* and was probably composed prior to the 16th century (Goudriaan, 1981, 67–68). 

Although of lesser literary quality than the texts that preceded it, the *Jñānāṃ̇avatantra* “contains precious information on the worship of Tripurā and other subjects which it often presents in a clear and unpretentious language” (Goudriaan, 1981, 68). In addition to expounding on the triadic nature of Tripurasundarī (see below) and elucidating a number of technical details of her worship, the text discusses the worship of Bālā, a manifestation of the goddess as a young girl, who is portrayed seated on a white lotus, holding a rosary (*akṣamālā*) and the → Vedas (Goudriaan, 1981, 68). Tripurabhairavī, a fearsome emanation of the goddess, is also described (Goudriaan, 1981, 68). The exoteric *Jñānāṃ̇avatantra* and the Kaula *Paraśurāmakalpasūtra* are among the authoritative references for contemporary Śrīvidyā practice (Brooks, 1992, 43, 52–53; 217n42; for a summary of Paddhatis and other texts of ritual instruction, see Brooks, 1992, 51–53). The tradition has preserved a number of other later Tantras, a number of which are yet to be edited (see Goudriaan, 1981, 68–74, for a summary of the later Śrīvidyā Tantras). 

Stotras 

→ Stotras (hymns) and other devotional literature of the tradition not only describe the popular image of the goddess, but often encode technical meanings pertaining to the cult’s doctrine and ritual. In the short text of the *Khaḍgamālāstotra*, the arrangement of names of the subordinate deities mirrors the ritual in which these goddesses are worshipped in the *śrīcakra* or internally in the order of evolution (*sṛṣṭikrama*). The text lists the subordinate goddesses worshipped in the nine levels of the *śrīcakra* ritual and the emanations of Tripurasundarī controlling each level. Among the groups included in the *Khaḍgamālāstotra* are 15 *nityā*s, the Lopāmudrā Sampradāya (Khanna, 1986, 49), 11 *siddhi*s beginning with *aṇimā*, and the 8 *matṛkā* goddesses. The daily chanting of the *Khaḍgamālāstotra* is understood to have the same efficacy as the complete *pūjā* of the *śrīcakra* (Khanna, 1986, 11).

The *Lalitāsahasranāma* is a popular Stotra containing one thousand names of the goddess Lalitā. It is widely used in contemporary devotional practice. Many people chant it daily and know the hymn by heart, while others use it for a simple *pūjā*, in which an offering, usually a pinch of red *kuṃkum* powder or flower petals, is offered to the *śrīcakra* with each name of the goddess. The Stotra is included in the *Lalitopākhyāna* section of the *Brahmāṇḍapurāṇa*. Enclosed within a framing story, it is presented in the form of instruction imparted by Hayagrīva to Agastya. The text of the hymn reflects several puranic myths, such as the arising of Tripurasundarī from the fire pit of the sacrificial fire of consciousness and the slaying of the demon Bhaṇḍāsura, described in the *Lalitopākhyāna*, as well as the slaying of Mahiṣāsura, and Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa from the *Devīmāhātmya*. The *kuṇḍalinī* system of *cakra*s/*ādhāra*s (subtle energy centers) grouped into the three *kūṭa*s (sections), with the corresponding *granthi*s (knots) is also introduced (see → Yoga). 

Another popular Stotra widely used devotion-ally is the *Saundaryalaharī* (The Wave of Beauty). While containing devotional expositions on the oneness of Śiva and Śakti and descriptive passages on the physical beauty of the goddess, the text also encodes technical information on *śrīcakra* ritual and internal worship. The first part of the text is known as the *Ānandalaharī* (The Wave of Bliss) and is considered to be the essence of the hymn (Brown, 1958, 1). In highly poetic and evocative 

*[p. 821]* ^pg7

language, the many facets of → *śakti*, the feminine divine energy, are carefully laid out, and the goddess’s mystic union with Śiva, who cannot accomplish anything without her, is described:

*Ānandalaharī* contains many “technicalities of this originally Kaula system,” with which “many late South and East Indian commentators, such as Kaivalyāśrama, Ānandagiri, Gaurīkāntanta Sārvabhauma, Narasiṃha and Lakṣmīdhara, are fully conversant” (Sanderson, 2002, 23n24). 

The second part of the Stotra, often called the *Saundaryalaharī*, extols the beauty of the goddess, each metaphor carefully crafted to follow her detailed physical description from head to toe. The text concludes with “the poet’s prayer that he may receive Devī’s grace, have the vision of her supernal form, achieve self-realization, and savor the sweetness of supreme brahman . . . which she is” (Brown, 1958, 1). The *Saundaryalaharī* is attributed by tradition to Śaṅkara, who is usually dated to the 8th century – however, the text must have been composed significantly later (for a summary of the scholarship on the authorship and dating, see Brown, 58, 25–30). 

Practices 

In the literature overview above, we have already outlined some of the Śrīvidyā practices, which are similar to other tantric traditions, as well as the content of the practices unique to Śrīvidyā. A detailed exposition of Śrīvidyā ritual and theology is of course outside the scope of this article (for a discussion of tantric ritual, see Sanderson 1995; for a discussion of the worship of Tripurasundarī according to *hādimata* in the works of the 13th-century Śivānanda, see Khanna, 1986; and for the analysis of Śrīvidyā ritual, see Brooks, 1992). Standard tantric practices, as they are preserved in the contemporary tradition, include *dhyāna* (meditation, visualization), *pūjā* (ritual worship), *tarpaṇa* (offering of libations), *nyāsa* (installing the divinity in the body of the ritualist), *homa* (offering oblations into the fire), *japa* (mantra repetition), and *mudrā* (ritual gestures). From the earliest texts, the worship of Tripurasundarī has been described as tripartite, that is, centered on *pūjā, mantra*, and *cakra* (e.g. *YHṛ.* 1.6). These three types of worship correspond to the three forms of the goddess, which are her physical form (*sthūlarūpa*; represented in anthropomorphic images), her subtle (*sūkṣma*) or *mantra* form, and her transcendent, supreme (*parā*) form, worshipped in the *śrīyantra*, or the *śrīcakra*, a two- or three-dimensional aniconic representation of Tripurasundarī (Brooks, 1992, xviii; see also Dempsey, 2006, 53). The geometrical design of the *śrīcakra* is formed by the *bindu* (dot) in the middle of the nine intersecting triangles, surrounded by two concentric rows of lotus petals and a threefold circle within an enclosure with four gates (see → *maṇḍala*s and *yantra*s). The principal *mantra* of the tradition is the *pañcadaśākṣarī* (a 15-syllabled *mantra*) or *ṣoḍaśākṣarī* (a mantra of 16 syllables, formed from the *pañcadaśākṣarī*; see Brooks, 1992, 90, for the details of the extraction of the *mūlamantra*). A variant of the 15-syllabled *mantra* is already articulated in a coded form in Śrīvidyā’s earliest extant Sanskrit text, the *Vāmakeśvarīmata* (1.93–101), and *kādividyā*, just as it has been preserved in the contemporary tradition – in the Tamil *Tirumantiram* (see Brooks, 1992, 31). The *ṣoḍaśākṣarī* appears to have been a later addition. The contemporary tradition refers to the 16syllabled *mantra* as the *laghuṣoḍaśī* and its expanded form as the *mahāṣoḍaśī* (see Brooks, 1992, 108, for a variant of the latter). The *bālāmantra*, a ninesyllabled *mantra* of the goddess as a nine-year-old girl, is also used. From its earliest attestation, the *mūlavidyā* has been presented as threefold; its 15 syllables divided into three groups. These groups are identified in the *Vāmakēśvarīmata* as *vāgbhava, kāmarāja*, and *śakti* (*VāMā.* 1.94–101). Each of the three stages has a corresponding syllable (*VāMā.* 1.82–85). Together these three syllables are considered to be the 

*[p. 822]* ^pg8

contracted form of the *mūlavidyā*. It is these syllables that form the *bālāmantra* and are the most basic form of initiation into Śrīvidyā at present day.
