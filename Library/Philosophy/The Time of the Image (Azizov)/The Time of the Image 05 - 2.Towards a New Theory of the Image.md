## 2.	Towards a New Theory of the Image

2.1	

### 2.1	The Work of Bernard Stiegler

Bernard Stiegler’s philosophical work articulates his views on the techno-logical conditions of thought and its impact on the global transformation of culture. The role of the image  –  which makes the ›battle of the intelligence‹ possible  –  is central to his concerns (Stiegler 1998). His longstanding engagement with visual culture, especially cinema, TV, new media and the extension of the latter in social networks, entails a critique of the culture industry and technology that are crucial to our times. In a dialogue with Husserl (Husserl 1982), Derrida (Derrida 1998) and other philosophers of technics such as Heidegger (Heidegger 1967) or Simondon (Simondon 2008), Stiegler responds to Western philosophy’s exclusion of technics from its metaphysical questioning. His work explores the origins of the philosophical and political aspects of the global process he calls »the industrial temporalisation of consciousness« (Stiegler 1998: 137).

The question of modern technology and its impact on culture was first problematised by the Frankfurt school (Adorno 1981) and also was very important for the philosophy of Martin Heidegger (Heidegger 1968).<sup>7</sup> Unlike these philosophical figures, Bernard Stiegler perceives the role of technics in a new philosophical manner. There exists a technics to assist humans, and this technics has a history running from the invention of writing to the latest digital technologies. To orchestrate the work of paleontological research with philosophical ideas drawn from modern philosophy, Bernard Stiegler asserts that the human is the product, not the ›cause‹, of technical evolution, an evolution whose grounding concept is ›technics‹. »Today, we need to understand the process of technical evolution given that we are experiencing the deep opacity of contemporary technics; we do not immediately understand what is being played out in technics, nor what is being profoundly transformed therein, even though we unceasingly have to make decisions regarding technics, the consequences of which are felt to escape us more and more« (Stiegler 1998: 21).

Being human is inseparable from technics, »and in day-to-day technical reality, we cannot spontaneously distinguish the long-term processes of transformation from spectacular but fleeting technical innovations« (Stiegler 1998: 21). This is because the human is technics, which enables the condition of living through the retention of memory.

Existence depends upon memory. In remembering the past, we transform ourselves. Experience of growth and cultural transformation through the technics of remembering constitutes us as cultural beings. It is also the defining characteristic of the constitution of consciousness, which in our audio-visual industrial age is programmed by cinematic technics. 2.3	

### 2.3	Cinematic Images and the Recorded Memory

Cinematic media also emerged in this context. It is not just a matter of what is projected onto the screen. It is also a specific form of thought and a technics of exteriorisation of recorded memory. The word ›cinematic‹ is understood here to refer to all forms of audio-visual recording and time-based reproduction, montage and their effects in the media and everyday life. The differences played out in cinematic images are a combination of the physical, the technical and the virtual; they all temporarily help the viewer to become immersed in the lost world of memory, where every image is associated with the ›remembering of the past‹. We constantly inhabit the world of culture in order not to forget. Recorded memory is the prosthesis and reminder of our cultural behaviour.

Because the growth of the humanity consists of the move from one artefact to another, all culture is a constant encounter with these ›artefacts‹.[^14] Cultural memory occurs as retention. ### 2.4	Proliferation of Images and Exteriority

#### 2.4.1	The condition of the image

2.4.1	

›Proliferation of images‹ is perhaps one of the most commonplace phrases in contemporary culture and media discourse, yet it lacks sufficient definition.[^19] It may be correct to say that this phrase is not easily definable, but it can be described and explained because of the impact of the phenomenon of proliferation on image production.[^20] Borrowed from political debates around nuclear and chemical proliferation, the expression is simply accepted as characterising the global circulation of images. This usage makes some sense inasmuch as the proliferation of images at the turn of the 21st century is similar to the proliferation of nuclear and chemical missiles after the Second World War; nevertheless, the use of the term with reference to the visual world still requires further clarification. Such clarification is all the more urgently needed because this single phrase has generated many debates and discussions and has formed the basis of many projects addressing the ›condition of the image‹.

In the words of German filmmaker and theorist Alexander Kluge: »Since the Ice Age approximately, streams of images, of so-called associations, have moved through the human mind, prompted to some extent by an anti-realistic attitude, by the protest against an unbearable reality« (Kluge 1981/82: 209).

This is the ultimate cultural oxymoron, making possible the Nietzschean »mutable knowledge«, in which »there are no facts, only interpretations« (Nietzsche 1954: 458). The meaning is never there waiting to be discovered, to be recognised for exactly what it is. Interpretation always constitutes the relation between the possible meaning at the present moment and the prospect of its loss. Consequently, fear of the present leads to the illusion of the lost. The lost is not for living in; it is a well of conclusions from which we draw in order to act. The cultural illusion of the lost entails a double loss. Meaning comes into existence at the moment of its discovery.

In this world meaning still plays a role, even when the spectacle overshadows it and the image of illusion makes it invisible. But »the world has to be made to mean« (Hall 1973: 355). Meaning is not given but produced; it is a production and a practice. Meaning does not exist until its production. Things and events in the world do not contain an intrinsic and single meaning that is later taken up by language. On the contrary, things and events receive their meaning with their entrance into language. Meaning now can only be discovered in fragments, by deciphering their assemblage: to think is to assemble and reassemble the means and instruments of the production of meaning. On the one hand this process breaks true meaning into fragments, on the other it poses the question of infinite multiplicity of linguistic codes, which doesn’t destroy the process of encoding but always entails the implosion of any arbitrary ›closure‹ (Hall 1973: 363). The axiom that meaning is not natural but arbitrary is helpful in the attempt to understand the foregoing (i.e. re/assemblage). The intervention of the real in language produces the plurality of signs. This multiplicity must be conceived not as an end to meaning but as the possibility of infinite generation of meaning, in which difference produces difference.

This difference marks entry into language and the separation of the object of thought from the subject of the action of signifying. Meaning  –  or rather, the production of meaning  –  is the force articulating moments of production, and it is ephemeral. Because meaning is not given but produced, it is possible that different kinds of meaning could be ascribed to the same events, so that previous meaning may appear to be lost or found again. If things are

lost and found, becoming absent and present, disconnected and connected again, there must be something that makes this process possible. This condition of possibility is dissemination, a term used extensively in French philosophy, particularly by Jacques Derrida (Derrida 1981a: 317) and later by Bernard Stiegler. This notion is the starting point for any understanding of disconnected ideas and of meaning as a force connecting them. Images are disconnected by their use in new contexts where their meaning is altered.

Ideas that are produced are always change their use in new contexts, by translation etc. But because of their dissemination, what seems to come to an end repeatedly returns and renews what had become old and was often forgotten. In this sense a thoroughly forgotten ›old‹ circulates, ›returns‹ from its repression and creates a new story by making visible the missing links in other stories. Occasionally in intellectual debates, what really becomes a problem is the place, the location of meaning. In other words, there is always the need to position ideas invented in the discourse. The position can only be found in a careful struggle with meaning, because meaning is what has absorbed inside itself its own beginning, and furthermore it slips so that it cannot be grasped so easily. Meaning is neither lost nor found, but not visible, and it only works ›when directly confronted‹. For this reason, it may seem to be lost or disconnected. Meaning emerges as soon as the intention is initiated.[^60] This is what creates the illusion of the lost. This is not to say that the world is loss of meaning: the world is full of meaning, but the question lies in the use of it. Rather than disappearing, meaning is vectorised and determines multiple coordinates. Proliferation is a superabundance of meaning, and meaning is disseminated. The proliferation of the image is its dissemination.

### 2.5	Images and New Subjects

#### 2.5.1	Temporal objects

2.5.1	

Edmund Husserl coined the term ›a temporal object‹ in order to show that the object of inquiry, the intention of the consciousness directed towards the objects of the world, is a temporal state of the investigation itself. This temporal state creates the condition for the existence of a temporal object, which offers ›striking evidence‹ (Husserl 1962). A temporal object means that the object is not only in time but is constituted through time and that 

*[p. 56]* ^pg57

its flux coincides with the flux of consciousness.[^74] A temporal object plays a role in the constitution of the subject, since it is an object towards which the consciousness is directed. The temporal object is part of the content which it translates (this content is the world). Consciousness is also a part of the content, but there is a difference: the temporal object perceived as a result of intention may be developed by consciousness differently: consciousness may accept this object but also may reject it. In both cases ›consciousness‹ performs the evidence, whereas the temporal object makes evidence available. Consciousness is the intention of the subject; the temporal object is the intentionality of the world. The subject takes intentionality from the object, as the trace of the world, in some cases putting it in brackets when the trace doesn’t explain the phenomena, while in other cases opening brackets. Temporal objects remain as such after being grasped too, again because of difference. For example, in the arithmetical operation of dividing a smaller number by a bigger one, the subtracted number is memorised to be added later. This shows that being in time is the same thing as holding something in memory to be re-membered later.[^75] Temporal objects function in relation to consciousness in the example of watching a film. An interesting aspect of watching a film is that to watch it is to watch the discrete image of the world that marks the difference between the world surrounding us and the discrete image, the fragment which is cut and fit into the format of the screen. The discrete world is integrated into the world we see. While watching the film, which is a temporary arrangement of discrete images, the viewer is in the state of perception, which endures until the film ends. It is the state of the suspension of all judgments concerning the spatio-temporal existence which one assumes to be ›out there‹. While watching the film the viewer doesn’t question the world s/he encounters in the screen; instead, this world stands immediately before his/her eye.

Memory crystallised in the idea is contagious, it expands itself from the older form to the newer, and it is always present virtually. Even if in technical terms technically no montage is ultimately indispensable because post-production is the true matter of the process, editing understood as the constitution of the subject is enormously important. The question here is the methodological difference produced by the shift from the production to programming. The rapid growth of programming not only challenged the production of images and their perception, it also brought into existence something that always existed though outside of it. What is outside is a virtual entry into memory; it is a temporal object, it is the image of the outside as retained by programming. Editing suspends the world and turns into the details of this world, captured in these temporal objects. While meticulously working with details, thought becomes assimilated instead of keeping hold of its essence.

Unlike editing, programming is not simulation; if anything it is storing and re-storing of memory, an assimilation of a past to the programme. It also differs from Baudrillard’s notion of the simulacrum, because pro

Editing as the cross-circulation of images stands in relationship not only with a past and the present but also with the outside and the inside, the interiority and the exteriority. Editing develops ideas taken from the past while virtually inserting them into the present. This is how editing captures the constitutive outside. The constitutive outside, the other, the ›missing time‹ is in the materiality of the work. The excessive materiality itself presents this missing element while excluding it. The subject is never present in the discourse. It is outside, and only by questioning what is outside it is possible to bring it back into the discourse. For example, what kind of outside subject is questioned in the image of Tarkovsky’s apples? They are brought to the world of being through the meaning given to this fruit by the religious mythology of Adam and Eve as apples of heaven alongside with the artistic vision.[^136]

Editing is the exteriorisation of knowledge through its missing dimensions. If the relationship of the missing time to the excluded subject is stressed here, it is in order to show their inseparable nature. As we see, time and subject are constantly separated and joined again. This is one of the reasons of the constant need for the improvement of subjects. The time and the subject are never coinciding, and they are never able to find each other in their being together. They can only be assimilated to each other through the interference of the indirect image of time.
