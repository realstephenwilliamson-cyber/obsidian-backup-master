## About

A Chinese classic named for, and partly attributed to, Zhuang Zhou (Master Zhuang). With the *Daodejing* it is one of the two foundational texts of Daoism. It argues largely through stories, dialogues and parables.

- **Author:** most historians put Zhuang Zhou's birth around 369 BC in Meng, in the state of Song; his death is variously dated 301, 295 or 286 BC (Wikipedia). The SEP (Chad Hansen) places him in the late fourth century BC and says the Han biographers' details are hard to confirm.
- **Composition:** modern scholars generally date the surviving chapters to the 4th–2nd centuries BC. Tradition gives Zhuang Zhou the Inner Chapters; the SEP treats even that as a hypothesis, and Wikipedia reports that close textual analysis does not show them to be the earliest layer.
- **Shape:** 33 chapters: Inner (1–7), Outer (8–22) and Miscellaneous (23–33). Guo Xiang (d. 312) cut a 52-chapter text to this form around AD 300; his is the only complete version to survive, about 63,000 characters. A. C. Graham sorted the later chapters among the School of Zhuangzi, Yangists, Primitivists and Syncretists (SEP).
- **Passages:** the butterfly dream (ch. 2), the death of Wonton (ch. 7), the debate with Huizi over whether fish are happy (ch. 17), and Zhuangzi drumming after his wife's death (ch. 18).
- **Titles and translations:** in 742 Emperor Xuanzong of Tang canonized it as the *Nanhua zhenjing* (True Scripture of Southern Florescence). English versions include James Legge (1891), Burton Watson (1968), A. C. Graham (1981) and Victor H. Mair's *Wandering on the Way* (1994), now listed by the University of Hawai'i Press.
- **Why it matters:** the SEP calls Zhuangzi the pivotal figure of classical philosophical Daoism. Both sources note its shaping of Chan (Zen) Buddhism, and Wikipedia says nearly every major Chinese writer and poet absorbed its ideas.
