## Part III The Other god: Defining Avoda Zara

 Overview 

 As suggested already, some of the alternatives to the biblical-rabbinic paradigm of scorn and rejection emerged within the matrix of Jewish attempts to develop a positive appreciation of Christianity and Islam. These attempts provide us with approaches to another religion that are different in tone and attitude from the biblical-rabbinic paradigm. The same historical period also saw the rise of the first systematic treatments of Avoda Zara and the earliest attempts to identify what is wrong with it and to define its scope. It is therefore appropriate at this point in our discussion to revisit some of the classical articulations of Jewish views of Avoda Zara in general and of Christianity in particular, and to consider how they might, and in certain cases how they have, served as resources for developing a Jewish view of Hinduism. In relating these resources to the case of Hinduism we are called not simply to assess if and how they may be applied to Hinduism, but also to use the case of Hinduism as an occasion for considering the adequacy of these resources, approaches, and constructs to Judaism’s present-day encounter with other religions. 

 This part of the book is its core, in terms of providing the building blocks for a view of another religion, as these may be culled from rabbinic (late rabbinic, i.e., medieval up to contemporary) literature. It is important to state that in most of what follows we do not come across specific views of rabbinical authorities concerning Hinduism. For the most part, our task is to extract models, theoretical possibilities, that could inform our view of Hinduism. This is why this part is the densest and most demanding part of the book. If we are to suggest models or approaches that could be applied to Hinduism, we must engage the sources thoroughly, understanding their concerns, their limits, and how we might legitimately work with them. Making these sources speak to our situation requires close reading of some fundamental texts, so that our application of their approach is responsible. Because of the detailed nature of the argument in this part of the book, I assume that some readers who are less familiar with rabbinic reasoning may have a hard time working through some of the details of the argument. Let 

*[p. 42]* ^pg50

me, therefore, provide a brief summary of key points that emerge from each of the chapters in this part of the book. 

 Chapter 6 presents the view of Maimonides on Avoda Zara. Maimonides’ work is possibly the most influential on the subject. It consists of specific rulings concerning Christianity (which he considers to be Avoda Zara) but also theoretical discussions of the historical formation of Avoda Zara as well as its core legal definition. In terms of precedent, Maimonides’ ruling on Christianity, if extended to Hinduism, would lead to the conclusion that Hinduism too is Avoda Zara. For many, this has been a default position and it readily accounts for the ease with which the category was applied to Hinduism in cases such as the sheitel crisis. But Maimonides does provide additional resources, which leave pause for thought regarding how they might be applied to Hinduism. He offers the core legal definition of Avoda Zara—the worship of any being besides God. This definition opens up a space for inquiry into the intentions and the theological understanding of practitioners of other religions. Could it be that what seems to us the worship of another God is really the worship of God? 

 Of great interest is also Maimonides’ historical presentation of the coming of Avoda Zara into human history. Following Maimonides’ track of thought suggests a very particular view of how Avoda Zara is understood. Another being is worshipped in order to please God, in a mistaken understanding of His will. This ultimately leads to forgetting God and to the widespread growth of superstition. Contrasting this description of the evolution of Avoda Zara with the self-understanding of Hinduism suggests there are significant gaps. Hindu thinkers would not recognize the nature of their worship of deities as conforming to this model, or for that matter to the broader medieval understanding of Avoda Zara as a consequence of approaching intermediaries. A close reading of Maimonides suggests there are gaps in his presentation, specifically the worship of God Himself through wrong means, such as images. The gaps in Maimonides’ presentation are precisely where an engagement with Hinduism becomes particularly interesting. It may be that what was taken for granted, or left unexplored, by Maimonides’ presentation is exactly the position that Hindu thinkers might choose to identify with. If so, what bearing could that have on our view of Hinduism as Avoda Zara? Beyond that, thinking of Hinduism in light of Maimonides’ presentation raises the question of whether we can import theoretical and theological criteria from one religious system to another, which thinks in entirely different ways. 

 Chapter 7 presents the views of Nachmanides, an equally prominent voice in medieval rabbinic Judaism. Unlike Maimonides’ work, which is incorporated in a legal corpus, Nachmanides’ work is encountered in his commentary on the Torah. This is likely the reason why his views have had little impact on the history of halachic thinking concerning other religions. The present analysis seeks to integrate his views into the broader context of views of other religions, by making them address a novel situation that neither Nachmanides nor any of the other figures discussed in this part of the book had ever encountered or engaged. 

 Two principles are fundamental to Nachmanides’ understanding of Avoda Zara. The first is that the core definition of Avoda Zara concerns the making 

*[p. 43]* ^pg51

of a bond of faith and commitment, through which a relationship to a being as one’s “God” is affirmed. Matters of ritual, including the use of images, would be secondary to this core definition. The second principle of his presentation is that the prohibition of Avoda Zara is relevant to the Jewish people in ways it is not relevant to the rest of humanity. As part of the special covenant that exists between God and Israel, Israel is committed to give divine allegiance to no other being save God. The rest of humanity is not under such a commitment, even though some dimension of Avoda Zara applies to them as well. Unlike Maimonides, who applies identical criteria for Jews and non-Jews, Nachmanides distinguishes between them. This would lead us at every juncture, then, to pose the question of whether what is being spoken of as Avoda Zara is meant for Jews or for non-Jews as well. This is one of the most fundamental questions that a discussion of another religion as Avoda Zara must consider and it is one that is often overlooked. 

 Nachmanides recognizes various forms of worship or allegiance to other beings, which are appropriate for other nations. These are couched in a cosmic, angelological framework and are based on a cosmic structure, which when understood based on correct knowledge reveals the celestial, astrological, and even demonic powers that are the lot of each nation. Nachmanides’ structure even includes the possibility of worship of humans, within this broader cosmic view. All these are considered by Nachmanides to be legitimate and appropriate under certain circumstances. 

 The conditions under which worship of other beings may be condoned is first and foremost that it does not eclipse knowledge of God who, by this cosmic view, is at the head of the cosmic hierarchy. Nachmanides does not require that God be worshipped, but knowledge of Him must not be forgotten due to worship given to other beings. The second condition implied in Nachmanides’ presentation is that such worship conform to the divinely ordained cosmic structure, in other words that the nations worship powers that really are associated with them. 

 On the face of it, Nachmanides’ discussion is neither relevant to the Christianity that he knew, nor to the Hinduism he had no idea of. A strict reading of his commentary in context would confine his views to powers and practices that are not the ones under discussion. My analysis, however, examines what might be extracted from Nachmanides’ views as resources for the present discussion of Hinduism. It assumes that his view provides us with a model that can be applied under different circumstances. The “scientific” dimension of his view of permissible worship of other beings may find a correspondence in other kinds of “scientific” approaches, characteristic of Hinduism. In any event, his discussion is certainly relevant to revising our thought on Avoda Zara. What we might have been certain would be Avoda Zara, based on the common Maimonidean perspective, turns out to be permissible and part of a divinely given cosmic order. This certainly suggests there is more than one possible approach to Avoda Zara. It invites us to continue in our search for precedents and in further interrogating the category of Avoda Zara. 

 Despite significant substantive differences that have bearing on a possible Jewish position on Hinduism as Avoda Zara, both Maimonides and Nachmanides rely on a similar understanding of Avoda Zara, in metaphysical and theoretical 

*[p. 44]* ^pg52

terms. This understanding will be contrasted with common understandings of Hinduism. The question at hand is to what extent the basic understanding of Avoda Zara, developed during the Middle Ages, coheres with the self-understanding of major schools of Hindu thought and what, if anything, would be the significance of the differences between these understandings. 

 Chapter 8 introduces a position and a concept that has played a major role in a Jewish view of Christianity as (non) Avoda Zara. This position, in many ways the opposite of that of Maimonides, shares with Nachmanides the view that different criteria apply to Jews and non-Jews when it comes to Avoda Zara. Non-Jews may worship another being alongside God. This position is referred to as *Shituf* , the association of another being alongside God. The position that Shituf is permissible to non-Jews is more demanding than the view of Nachmanides. For Nachmanides, all that was required was that knowledge or awareness of God’s existence not be lost. For proponents of the permissibility of Shituf, the requirement is that God be worshipped, with the realization that the worship of another being at the same time does not constitute Avoda Zara. This position has evolved from an ad-hoc solution to certain practical problems to a principled view of other religions, primarily Christianity. It has come to inform a Jewish ethos of tolerance. Different paths and different standards apply to Jews and non-Jews. The practices of non-Jews can therefore be condoned, while the integrity of Jewish practice and identity is maintained by insistence on the special demands made of Jews, as part of their special relationship with God. 

 The position affirming permissibility of Shituf has been one of the main resources available to contemporary Jewish authors, as they sought to think of Hinduism. Hinduism, it was suggested, could be seen in much the same way that Christianity was, a religion valid for its practitioners but off limits for Jews. This assumed Hindus worship God, in some sense familiar to us Jews, but at the same time they also worshipped various forces of nature, gods, and holy men, while never forgetting God completely. The discussion presents in detail the position of Rabbi Adin Steinsaltz, an important contemporary rabbinic voice, who applies this strategy also to Hinduism, as part of a broader effort at establishing a Jewish theology of religions, under the concept of tolerance. 

 Chapter 9 continues the discussion of Shituf. Whereas Chapter 8 presented the category and its possible applicability to Hinduism, Chapter 9 queries the category from various angles, critical and constructive. On the critical side, it poses the question of whether the category has theological value and veracity, beyond the value it obviously possesses in terms of historical precedents. A closer scrutiny of the category suggests it is ill-suited to describe Christian faith. In a similar vein, it may not describe Hindu faith in a way that conforms to Hindu self-understanding. We are faced, then, with the challenge of applying a category that can be helpful in terms of relations, as well as in terms of internal rabbinic processes and precedents, while recognizing its theological shortcomings. 

 On the constructive side, the discussion in Chapter 9 takes the notion of permissibility of Shituf and asks what kind of a broader religious understanding can be constructed out of what began as a legal construct for solving day-to-day issues. Can we develop a theology of Shituf? The discussion offers suggestions 

*[p. 45]* ^pg53

for how one could account theologically for the permissibility of Shituf. This is understood here in terms of collapsing the end and means into one totality, which points Godward. This discussion takes place primarily in relation to holy people, following the classical application of the category to Jesus. This precedent is extended to attitudes to *gurus* and holy men and women in Hinduism and their view as divine. If we are to take Shituf as a serious theological possibility, then two parallel theological models must be set up, one for Jews and one for non-Jews. And if we see in this an ideal teaching of the Torah, then these two views must be able to speak to each other in a valid theological dialogue representing two views on Shituf. In such a dialogue the Jewish refusal of Shituf must be heard as a testimony to one understanding of the spiritual life, while at the same time Judaism could develop a finer appreciation of the spiritual possibilities entailed in the permission for non-Jews to worship through Shituf. 

 Chapter 10 introduces us to the thought and legal position of Rabbi Menachem Meiri, a fourteenth-century rabbinic authority. Meiri is principled in his view of both Christianity and Islam, and for that matter probably all religions of his time, as not being under the charge of Avoda Zara. Like Maimonides, Meiri does not distinguish between Jew and non-Jew, applying the same criteria to both. His criteria, however, are unlike those of Maimonides or of the proponents of permissibility of Shituf. Meiri uses moral living and the religious ordering of a moral society as the primary criteria for recognizing the validity of a contemporary religion. While morality does not replace theology, it does serve as a pointer and indicator to the quality of the religious life, and therefore to the God who is behind the religion. A religion that shows moral living, grounded in some understanding of God, is legitimate. Meiri is willing to overlook theological error, as well as all that relates to the domain of worship. In many ways, it is the most promising classical approach available, if we seek to develop a positive appreciation of Hinduism. 

 Upon further consideration, we discover that Meiri does not simply apply the criterion of morality to absolve a religion of the charge of Avoda Zara, but actually operates with a robust notion of what a “religion” is. This notion is informed by the ultimate teleology of the religion, witnessed by the moral living and based upon some minimal theological premises required to uphold the entire structure. Once a religion is recognized as being a “religion,” it is valid, free of charges of Avoda Zara, and ultimately on a par with Judaism, as far as serving mankind on the ultimate journey and providing meaning for life are concerned. 

 Meiri’s work provides a very promising basis for a view of other religions, Hinduism included. It provides criteria that are readily met and recognized, without forcing on another religion theological understanding that distorts its own self-understanding. By focusing on what matters most to him and putting aside concern for worship and the concrete forms of the religious life, Meiri provides us with a position that could readily apply to a Jewish view of Hinduism, without requiring much theological acrobatics. 

 Meiri’s work has been outside the rabbinic mainstream for certain historical reasons and has only in past decades received broader attention. Because of this, many authorities feel uneasy adopting his position, rather than those that 

*[p. 46]* ^pg54

have been part of rabbinic discourse for centuries. However, in terms of doing the required theological and halachic work in a clean way, based on a thorough and systematic view of religion, without doing injustice to the other’s self-understanding, Meiri could serve as a guide to contemporary Jewish views of other religions. Some contemporary rabbis have indeed applied Meiri’s position to Hinduism, thereby taking it beyond the charge of Avoda Zara.

### 6 Understanding Avoda Zara: The Maimonidean Model

 The first chapter of Maimonides’ Laws of Avoda Zara is likely the most inf luential discussion of what constitutes Avoda Zara. Probably no other single 

text has been as influential in providing a theory of what Avoda Zara is. Its echoes reverberate not only in relation to other religions, but also in relation to internal Jewish concerns, such as the appropriateness or inappropriateness of prayer to angels, the legitimacy of kabbalistic understandings of God, and more. Maimonides offers what has become the default understanding of idolatry for many later Jewish thinkers. Let us, then, examine the first chapter of Maimonides’ Laws of Avoda Zara:

 In the days of Enosh, the people fell into gross error, and the counsel of the wise men of the generation became foolish. Enosh himself was among those who erred. Their error was as follows: Since God, they said, created these stars and spheres to guide the world, set them on high and allotted them honor, and since they are ministers who minister before Him, they deserve to be praised and glorified, and honor should be rendered them; and it is the will of God, blessed be He, that men should aggrandize and honor those whom He aggrandized and honored, just as a king desires that respect should be shown to the officers who stand before him, and thus honor is shown to the king. When this idea arose in their minds, they began to erect temples to the stars, offered up sacrifices to them, praised and glorified them in speech, and prostrated themselves before them—their purpose, according to their perverse notions, being to attain the Creator’s will (or: favor). This was the root of idolatry, and this is what the idolaters, who knew its fundamentals, said. They did not however maintain that there was no God except the particular star (which was the object of their worship). Thus Jeremiah said “Who would not revere You, O King of the nations? for that is Your due, since among all the wise of the nations and among all their royalty there is none like You. But they are both dull and foolish; \[Their\] doctrine is but delusion” (Jer. 10,7–8) . This means that all know that Thou alone art God; their error and folly consists in imagining that this vain worship is Your will. (Laws of Avoda Zara 1,1) 

 Maimonides constructs a narrative accounting for how Avoda Zara came to be and in the process also offers us his definition of what constitutes Avoda Zara. 

*[p. 48]* ^pg56

The starting point is true and proper faith, a faith lost and corrupted. Idolatry is founded in a certain understanding of the cosmic order and in a concomitant error concerning the divine will. Idolatry is founded upon the fact that God orders the universe in particular ways and that stars and astral bodies govern the world. This is a fact, in itself a value-free fact. Idolatry is born of a confusion growing from this fact. The position of governance afforded to stars and astral bodies is wrongly interpreted in terms of the divine will. The mistaken understanding of divine will is that God wishes to give honor and praise to these astral bodies and that they are worthy of praise. While the initial intention is to please God, it takes the inappropriate form of worshipping His servants. Ritual, another religion, is born of the misdirected quest to fulfill God’s will and to grant Him honor. Thus, Avoda Zara is a mistaken approach to divine will, regardless of good intention. <sup>1</sup> 

 Two further points should be noted here. In the first stage, there is no denial of God or confusion of the divine identity with the identity of the ministering angels. The cosmic hierarchy remains clear. The error touches upon relationships within a correctly perceived cosmic hierarchy and involves a mistaken understanding of God’s will as it applies to celestial beings. One also notes that Avoda Zara translates itself from mistaken theory to wrong action. Following the mistaken view of God’s will, specific actions are undertaken, all of which involve worship in concrete physical terms—construction of temples, sacrifices, praise, and prostrations. At this first stage God is also worshipped in some sense. While the objects of worship are the celestial bodies, as clearly distinct from God Himself, there is some dimension in which God is worshipped, as it is His will, mistakenly perceived, that motivates the birth of these novel forms of the religious life. However, Maimonides never states that the attempt to fulfill the divine will is actually a form of service of God. As service seems to be defined in terms of worship offered to other beings, it is considered that it is they who are worshipped, even if underlying this worship is the quest to fulfill the divine will. 

 Maimonides teaches us in this opening paragraph what is wrong with idolatry. It is an error. We note the repetition of the notion of error in this passage. The opening statement of the Laws of Avoda Zara refers to a great error, a loss of the counsel of the wise. The concluding statement of this opening paragraph repeats the notions of error and folly. What is wrong with Avoda Zara is that it is plainly wrong, a mistaken view of the divine will, an error in relation to how things ought to be. In theory, had God willed that His angels be worshipped, there would have been nothing wrong with such worship. The worship does not seem to be inherently wrong. What makes it wrong is that it is not in accord with God’s true will, even if it is born of a genuine religious attempt to act in accordance with it. Intention matters little when it comes to idolatry. What count are the actions and the degree to which they conform to divine will. One either gets the divine will right or wrong. Avoda Zara is born of an erroneous understanding of the divine will. 

 The model of religious life is one of fulfilling the divine will and adhering to it. Religion, by this recounting, is about getting the divine will right and conforming to it. This is a conceptualization of the religious life that is very characteristic of prophetic religion, where the commanding presence of God determines 

*[p. 49]* ^pg57

the forms, both appropriate and inappropriate, of the religious life. How deeply indebted Maimonides’ entire thought structure is to prophetic paradigms becomes evident in the next stage in the \[d\]evolution of idolatry:

 In the course of time, there arose among men false prophets who asserted that God had commanded them and expressly told them, “Worship that particular star, or worship all the stars. Offer to it such and such sacrifices. Pour out to it such and such libations. Erect a temple to it. Make a figure of it, to which all the people—the women, children, and the rest of the folk, shall bow down.” The false prophet pointed out to them the figure which he had invented out of his own mind and asserted that it was the figure of that particular star which had been shown to him in his prophetic vision. And then they began to make figures in temples, under the trees, on the mountaintops and the hills. There they would assemble, bow down to the figures, and tell all the people that this particular figure conferred benefits and inf licted injuries and that it was proper to worship and fear it. Their priests would say to them, “Through this worship, shall you increase and prosper. Do this and do not do that.” (ibid. 1,2) 

 Prophecy comes to the fore in the second stage. Here we recognize a move from mistaken sages to false prophets. False prophecy is characterized not simply as getting God’s will wrong, but as speaking falsely in God’s name and ascribing to His will that which He in fact does not will. The notion of command is fundamental to the prophetic view, as is the implied recognition that the command ideally expresses the will of God. False prophecy generates an entire religious system, initially attributed to divine will. That its adherents are convinced they are following God’s will matters little. False prophecy finds expression in images, as well as in an inappropriate or misdirected system of worship. While the components of the worship system are recognizable, being identical to the worship revealed in the Torah and truly expressive of God’s will, the worship is misdirected to false objects, thereby making it idolatrous. Wrong commandment, resulting in the creation of idols and false worship, also leads to superstition or the ascription to images of powers that they do not possess in reality. Worship of idols is utilitarian. The benefits of idol worship are immediate and it seems that the promised prosperity takes over as the ultimate motive of religion. Thus, what starts out as motivation based on the desire to fulfill the divine commandment degenerates into self-serving interests, where worship is supposed to achieve certain benefits of well-being, associated with the idolatrous image. Thus, complementing the move to inappropriate object of worship we also find a lower religious motivation driving the people to the worship of these new religious forms. 

 The gradual degeneration of religious life undergoes still a further step:

 Other impostors then sprang up, who declared that the star, celestial sphere or angel, had communed with them, and said to them “Worship me in such and such a fashion,” had taught them a definite ritual and said to them “do this and do not do that.” So gradually the custom spread throughout the world of worshipping figures with various modes of worship, such as offering up sacrifices to them, and bowing down to them. 

*[p. 50]* ^pg58

 Here we see again the prophetic pattern of religion, downgraded one step further. Worse than the false prophets who prophecy in God’s name are the impostors who speak in the name of the stars or angels. Everything about the religion is false. One no longer pretends to do God’s will or act in His name. The star is the source of authority and command, and the entire religious structure, the worship, the sacrifice, as well as the dos and don’ts of the religion are all attributable to the celestial power that has replaced God. This replacement leads to the eradication of God’s memory and to the complete takeover of either the celestial beings or their crude representations in stone and wood:

 As time gradually passed, the honored and revered name of God was forgotten by mankind (literally: all the world), vanished from their lips and hearts (literally: from their mouths and minds) and was no longer known to them. All the common people and the women and children knew only the figure of wood and stone and the temple of stone in which they had, from their childhood, been trained to prostrate themselves to the figure, worship it, and swear by its name. Even their wise men, such as priests and men of similar standing, also fancied that there was no other god but the stars and spheres, for whose sake and in whose similitude these figures had been made. But the Creator (literally: the Rock) of the Universe was known to none, and recognized by none save a few solitary individuals . . . until that pillar of the world, the patriarch Abraham, was born. (ibid.) 

 Idolatry is problematic because it replaces God with something else. God is completely forgotten. The distinction between the wise men and simple folk is that the former know there is something beyond the images of stone and wood, while the latter consider these images to be the sum total of the religious life. In either case, something other than God is worshipped, be it the celestial reality or its physical representation. What started out as a way of pleasing God and doing His will, wrongly construed, led to the total forgetting of the divine name and to the full substitution of God by other beings. 

 Maimonides presents us with several ways of stating what is wrong with Avoda Zara. On the one hand, Avoda Zara is about the object of worship, the worship of a being other than God, particularly as it comes at the expense of God, who is thereby forgotten. At the same time, Maimonides’ presentation also highlights worship and the forms it takes as fundamental aspects of Avoda Zara. Avoda Zara is the totality of the mistaken and falsified religious life. Grounded in a mistaken view of God’s will, it leads initially to a wrong way of worshipping God and eventually to the worship of stars, as well as idols of stones and wood. Furthermore, it includes wrong forms of worship: sacrifices, images, and the entire series of dos and don’ts attributed to the commanding voice of the false gods. Maimonides includes in the Laws of Avoda Zara 51 commandments, some of which extend significantly beyond worship as practiced toward images or mistaken perceptions of God. The broad scope of Avoda Zara is best understood in view of Maimonides’ description of an entire religious system evolving out of a primary error, leading to the evolution of a false religious path that replaces God with an other. On account of this substitution, all that derives from it, including the customs and instructions, both positive and negative, are part of the broader complex of Avoda Zara. 

*[p. 51]* ^pg59

 The twofold emphasis that we find in the description of Avoda Zara frames the corrective path, spelled out in the following passage. In what follows, Maimonides describes Abraham’s discovery of the one God, in opposition to the theological conventions of society around him. Significantly, Abraham comes to this knowledge by the power of reason, not of prophecy. This fact is particularly striking in light of the Muslim view of Abraham as a prophet, a view that Maimonides could have adopted, based on biblical usage. <sup>2</sup> Abraham’s reasoning leads to the shattering of idols and removal of idol worship. Worship is due to God alone, though Abraham does not innovate a method of worship. Presumably, the same method of worship previously practiced continues to apply, except that it is addressed to the proper recipient. Abraham’s greatest contribution seems to lie in the smashing of the idols and in the recovery of the knowledge of the one God. The two are completely incompatible, and Abraham is the teacher who makes people aware of this incompatibility. 

 Following the exile to Egypt, most of Israel falls back into the worship of idols. The Egyptians become a corrupting influence. No effort is made to justify the relapse on grounds of ideology or principles as in the original lapse from true monotheistic faith. Maimonides concludes this narrative of the origins of Avoda Zara and of the Jewish faith with the following description:

 When the Israelites had stayed a long while in Egypt, they relapsed, learned the practices of their neighbors, and, like them, worshipped idols, with the exception of the tribe of Levi, that steadfastly kept the charge of the patriarch. This tribe of Levi never practiced idolatry. The doctrine ( *Ikkar)* implanted would, in a very short time, have been uprooted ( *Ne’ekar* ), and Jacob’s descendants would have lapsed into the error and perversities universally prevalent. But because of God’s love for us and because He kept the oath made to our ancestor Abraham, He brought forth Moses, our teacher, the teacher of all the prophets, and charged him with his mission. After Moses had begun to prophesy and Israel had been chosen by the Almighty as His Heritage, He crowned them with commandments, and showed them the way to worship Him and how to deal with idolatry and with those who go astray after it. (ibid. 1,3) 

 As mentioned, Abraham is not described as a prophet, but as someone who operates by power of reason. Abraham’s teaching is not powerful enough to withstand the temptations of the neighboring cultures. More is required, prophecy is needed. Thus, Moses is sent as a prophet to advance the mission of Abraham, the philosopher. It appears that what Moses offers, which Abraham could not, is the spiritual path, the commandments. Abraham can only redirect existing patterns of worship to the true God. Moses provides the complete religious path that only a prophet can provide. It is this religious path that makes for the fullness of worship, and it is only in this fullness of the prophetic path that truth can be upheld. Thus, the twofold emphasis upon the identity of who is worshipped and the proper path of worship is mirrored in the two figures of Abraham and Moses. The former teaches whom to worship, the latter teaches how to worship. It is interesting that even when setting straight the errors of Egypt, Moses is not described as offering a teaching on God. Rather, the teaching cannot be 

*[p. 52]* ^pg60

maintained without the fullness of a spiritual path, which depends on specific prophetic revelation. <sup>3</sup> 

 Chapter 1 of the Laws of Avoda Zara is narrative. It is one of the few narratives introduced by Maimonides into his great legal code. Chapter 2 opens the halachic discussion. This discussion opens with the statement: 

 The essential principal ( *Ikar)* in the precepts concerning Avoda Zara is that we are not to worship any creature, not an angel nor a heavenly sphere, and not a star nor one of the four elements nor any one of the creatures composed of them. And even though the worshipping person knows that the Lord is God and he worships the created being in a manner similar to that of Enosh and his generation initially, this is a worshiper of Avoda Zara . It is against this that the Torah warns us when it says “And when you look up to the sky and behold the sun and the moon and the stars, the whole heavenly host, you must not be lured into bowing down to them or serving them. These the Lord your God allotted to other peoples everywhere under heaven”(Deut. 4,19). This means that when your mind roams around and you observe that the world is guided by these spheres and that God allotted them to all peoples, since they lie and endure permanently, and they do not disintegrate, like all other things in the world tend to, lest you say that it is proper to bow down to these spheres and worship them, concerning this God commanded and said “Take heed lest your heart be deceived” (Deut. 11,16), that is to say, do not be led astray by the fancies of your mind, to worship these beings as intermediaries between yourselves and the Creator. (Laws of Avoda Zara 2,1) 

 The essential definition of Avoda Zara is the worship of any being other than God. Compared with the lengthy description of the introduction of idols of stone and wood in Chapter 1 , it is interesting to note that the physical form of the object of worship does not figure in the core definition. What defines Avoda Zara, once Maimonides comes to the core halachic definition, is the worship of a being other than God, rather than the form that worship takes. Worship of another being is forbidden even if one has not lost sight of God. Thus, even the first stage in the history of Avoda Zara, prior to the false prophets and the false commandments, is also considered Avoda Zara. That the intention was to do God’s will matters little as something other than God has been worshipped. 

 In Chapter 2 we encounter a new nuance—the worship of intermediaries. The notion was absent in Chapter 1 . The worship of stars was not instrumental to the worship of God, thereby leaving important gaps in the understanding of Avoda Zara. If, as described there, stars were worshipped due to a mistaken view of God’s will, what if instead the stars were worshipped rather as intermediaries who would facilitate God’s favors? Had the worship of stars been approached as a worship of intermediaries, in the process of obtaining God’s favor, the end result of Maimonides’ narrative could not have been attained. A situation where God’s name is forgotten is not possible when God is approached via an intermediary. As much as an intermediary comes between the worshipper and God, it also keeps alive the awareness that God is the ultimate source of power and therefore the ultimate object of worship. If Chapter 1 might have kept open the possibility that 

*[p. 53]* ^pg61

the worship of intermediaries does not constitute Avoda Zara, in Chapter 2 we learn that this too is considered Avoda Zara. Strict definition that focuses on the object of worship leads to a more comprehensive view of Avoda Zara. 

 Maimonides’ description of the birth of religious images is that they are the images of stars and astral bodies. Here too we encounter a serious gap. What if someone worshipped an image as the image of God, not as the image of another lower being? It seems Maimonides never entertained such a possibility. <sup>4</sup> As God was incorporeal, one could simply not make an image of Him. Still, if someone entertained an imperfect sense of God and considered Him to possess a form, <sup>5</sup> would worshipping God in such a form constitute Avoda Zara? Even if our instincts tell us that surely Maimonides would have ruled that way, the fact remains that his narrative construction as well as his legal definition leave this possibility unexplored. 

 There is one other fundamental understanding of Avoda Zara that is completely ignored in Maimonides’ presentation. The plain sense of the biblical text refers to other gods that we must not worship. This assumes there are indeed other gods and that the term has some meaning. For Maimonides there are other beings, but there do not seem to be any other gods. In all likelihood, Maimonides could not account for what an other god is within his metaphysical worldview, and therefore simply substituted other beings for other gods. But this omission points us back to the gaps in Maimonides’ presentation. If we do not speak of other gods, then we must consider when we might be talking of the same God. And if we were able to establish that the recipient of worship is indeed the same God, how would that alter the view of another religion, even if its patterns of worship are different from our own? Maimonides’ consistent reference to beings, rather than gods, and his insistence that they are worshipped in their distinct separateness, even if it is in some way related to God, either by appeal to divine will or through the notion of intermediaries, raises issues regarding how apt this entire structure is to describe some of the religious phenomena that Judaism encounters. We now return to Hinduism. 

 It is conceivable that Maimonides would consider Hinduism as Avoda Zara. It is likely the case. However, even if we were to reach that conclusion, we ought not do so without noting the gaps between Maimonides’ understanding of Avoda Zara and Hindu self-understanding and without considering how those gaps would be bridged by a view that would consider Hinduism to be Avoda Zara. An alternative perspective might highlight these gaps and consider to what extent Maimonides’ description really provides an adequate basis for considering Hinduism to be Avoda Zara. For most latter-day authors who take for granted that Hinduism is Avoda Zara, what counts most is the worship of images of deities as practiced in Hinduism. Maimonides certainly has much to say about the worship of images, but he understands these images as images of other beings, not as expressions or manifestations of God. The core definition of Avoda Zara offered in Chapter 2 completely sidesteps the issue of images and concentrates exclusively on the identity of who is worshipped. Worshipping another being is what constitutes Avoda Zara. The possible gap between these perspectives is of great significance when we consider Hinduism. 

*[p. 54]* ^pg62

 We have already acknowledged the complexity of Hindu self-understanding and the problem of who speaks for Hinduism. Interestingly, Maimonides’ presentation of different levels of Avoda Zara, distinguishing between the masses and the wise and the priests, is of consequence for our discussion. As Maimonides presents it, both the wise and the masses forgot God. The difference between them was only whether they were aware of a reality beyond the wood and stone. But in any event, both groups forgot God. Had the wise men, unlike the masses, maintained an awareness of God beyond the images, and moreover had God Himself been the object of worship through images, would Maimonides’ narrative have made sense? And how does Maimonides’ definition of the worship of another being hold up to a religious system in which both deities and their images are understood as expressions or manifestations of the absolute, that is beyond of God in the highest? The religious reality of Hinduism fills the very gaps left open by Maimonides’ narrative and halachic constructions. 

 Within Hinduism is a serious option that sees the gods as manifestations of the one supreme being. When these manifestations are worshipped, whom is one worshipping—the supreme God or the temporary manifestation? Of course, one may claim that one is in fact worshipping another being, or its representation in the form of an idol, and therefore one is guilty of Avoda Zara. It is likely Maimonides would hold such a view. However, this is a fundamental interpretive choice. It does not grow out of the situation, but is read into it. The choice of how to understand another’s worship of a form *as* God is as much conditioned by prevailing attitudes to otherness, idolatry, and identity as it is by the pure application of classical criteria in interpreting a given situation. If we take seriously the intention of the worshipper, rather than outward appearances, we may come to the conclusion that what is being worshipped is none other than God. <sup>6</sup> 

 Maimonides’ narrative suggests that there is more to Avoda Zara than a wrong determination of the divine will. Ultimately, what is at stake in Avoda Zara is the forgetting of God. If Maimonides’ structure is to make sense, we have to not only insist on a coherent definition of what constitutes Avoda Zara but also consider it in light of its ultimate goal—maintaining the remembrance of God, protecting it from the forgetfulness that Avoda Zara generates. The case of Hinduism is very interesting in this regard. <sup>7</sup> Within Hindu culture is great awareness of God, alongside a flourishing of forms of worship that Jews readily declare to be Avoda Zara. Of course, those who make such declarations may not recognize that God’s memory is upheld and they may see the religious drive and the memory it evokes as further expressions of Avoda Zara. However, it is significant that God, rather than this or that manifestation, let alone this or that image, is at the forefront of the teachings of so many of the thinkers and spokespersons of the religion. Their goal is God and the worship of His manifestations and their physical representations is a means of reaching God or an expression of devotion to God in a more ultimate sense. Now, this might sound like “classical” worship of intermediaries, but it is not. How the beings that are worshipped are presented in relation to God beyond is particularly Hindu, appealing to notions of manifestation, taking on form, and of psychological and spiritual instrumentality, rather than the metaphysical agency usually ascribed to worship by proxy. In other words, the 

*[p. 55]* ^pg63

very categories and conceptualization that inform the typical Jewish discussion, indebted as it is to Maimonides, are radically different from the categories with which the Hindu religious mind operates. 

 The question at hand is thus not simply whether or not the specific case of Hinduism is or is not covered either by the narrative presentation of Avoda Zara ( Chapter 1 ) or by its core legal definition ( Chapter 2 ). The question is really to what extent we can import criteria from one religious system to another system that thinks in entirely different ways. Let us note the centrality of prophecy in the Maimonidean description of the evolution of Avoda Zara. Prophecy is the ideal religious archetype and doing the will of God is the primary religious motivation. What goes wrong in the generation of Enosh starts with a mistaken understanding of the will of God and continues with misapplication of prophetic principles. The entire unfolding of Avoda Zara takes place within such a prophetic-religious matrix. Now, it may not matter much that Hinduism is on the other side of the spectrum, in terms of a phenomenology of religions. Some typologies consider it a religion of mysticism, rather than a religion of prophecy. <sup>8</sup> Whatever the most useful typology may be, Hindu scriptures are not prophetic, nor is the primary religious drive the fulfillment of the divine will and the divinely ordained way of life. These ideals may be identified within a Hindu worldview, but they are conceptualized in other ways, more cosmic, more mystical, more automatic, and above all entailing less of a sense of the personal God, particularly the commanding personal God, in the application and maintenance of these religious principles. 

 The application of a Maimonidean worldview to a Hindu context runs the risk of gross injustice to the actual theological workings of the Hindu system. None of the claims made by Maimonides, and used by us as the basis for judgment, necessarily makes sense in a Hindu context. One does not necessarily worship to fulfill the will or the command of God. One does not necessarily worship something other than God. Significantly, one may not even recognize the very notion of otherness, affirming instead the divinity of all. <sup>9</sup> If all is God, true idolatry is not possible. And if we insist on distinguishing between God and non-God, that forces us into a much more nuanced understanding of difference and error of another faith. More fundamentally, it forces us to consider what is God and what our metaphysics really are. We readily apply Maimonidean principles on idolatry without necessarily sharing his metaphysical worldview. For those forms of Judaism that have adopted an alternative, pantheistic or panentheistic, metaphysic, it becomes almost impossible to condemn prevalent forms of Hinduism as Avoda Zara, except using the most superficial of external criteria. In short, one of our most fundamental discussions of Avoda Zara, one that has defined the field for generations, is either found lacking or raises serious issues that require much further reflection when it comes to the application of its principles to Hinduism. 

 Maimonides is a resource not only because he furnishes fundamental understandings and categories through which to consider Avoda Zara. Maimonides is also outspoken in relation to the one religion that poses challenges that in some sense resemble the challenges posed by Hinduism—Christianity. Maimonides is 

*[p. 56]* ^pg64

explicit in his ruling that Christianity is Avoda Zara. <sup>10</sup> Maimonides’ enormous prestige may account for this position appearing often as the default Jewish position. An examination of contemporary rabbinic writings reveals how common is the Maimonidean view of Christianity as Avoda Zara, usually with little attention going into the matter, beyond the evocation of the Maimonidean position as self-evident. <sup>11</sup> On the face of it, it would seem that if Christianity is to be viewed as Avoda Zara, all the more so would be Hinduism, with its divinities and idols. To the extent that the Maimonidean perspective does function for many communities as the default position, we can understand how readily rabbinic authors were willing to consider Hinduism as Avoda Zara during the sheitel crisis. If Christianity, officially monotheistic and culturally familiar, is considered Avoda Zara, how much more so the many gods of the distant religion of India and their strange worship. However, such a conclusion makes many assumptions, some of which must be challenged, either in relation to Christianity or in relation to Hinduism. 

 Maimonides himself sees no need to account for his ruling, thus considering it as self-evident as later authorities considered his own ruling. In accounting for Maimonides’ position, some authors have appealed to his philosophical worldview. <sup>12</sup> For Maimonides, “getting it right” theologically was of the utmost importance. Proper theological formulation was, for him, a condition for entry into the world to come. Hence the Maimonidean opposition to a range of theological misconceptions, assessed from the absolute vantage point of his philosophical worldview. According to these authors, the Trinity is the theological error on account of which Maimonides proclaims Christianity to be Avoda Zara. This same worldview would be damning to many forms of Judaism, especially the kabbalistic tradition. <sup>13</sup> But more to the point, nowhere does Maimonides account for his ruling on Christianity as Avoda Zara by appeal to the Trinity. We may thus consider other grounds for this ruling. 

 Several other possibilities present themselves based on allusions or references in Maimonides’ writings. The worship of images of stone and wood could hardly be a self-standing reason, <sup>14</sup> if we consider the core definition to be worship of another being. If anything, such images point to beings other than the absolute God. Thus, the more likely reason is that another being is worshipped. <sup>15</sup> If so, the worship of Jesus is considered as worship of another being. By analogy, the worship of any Hindu manifestation of God would be equally deemed to be Avoda Zara. However, in both cases, we would be judging not based on the worshipper’s intentions, but based on our own criteria and how we view reality. I have already suggested that this is not problem-free with reference to Christianity as well as to Hinduism. 

 Given that Maimonides does not actually explain why Christianity is Avoda Zara, perhaps we should not seek one single reason, which in turn could be applied to other religions. If Christianity’s status as Avoda Zara is so self-evident that it does not require justification, this self-evidence may be based on more than a single factor. While the factors noted above—Trinity, images, and the worship of Jesus—certainly qualify as possible reasons for considering Christianity as Avoda Zara , Maimonides’ self-evident position may rely on other 

*[p. 57]* ^pg65

considerations that may in fact precede these factors. Cultural distance, <sup>16</sup> reliance on tradition, and an intuitive sense of otherness may have all played a far more important role in his ruling than either theology or an analysis of foreign patterns of worship. Given how natural this ruling is to him, and how little he discusses it, Maimonides may simply be propagating earlier views. <sup>17</sup> We are hard-pressed to state just how differences in theology, differences in worship, and a sense of otherness converge with a view received from earlier tradition, making Maimonides’ ruling on Christianity something that is more self-evident than something argued. If so, the precedent from his ruling on Christianity can aid us in approaching Hinduism only if we are willing to rule based on the kind of self-evidence that was made manifest during the sheitel crisis. Short of that, the ruling on Christianity does not advance the kind of discussion we seek to hold, pointing us instead to the Laws of Avoda Zara , which constitute a much more pliable resource for our discussion. 

 One final thought on Christianity and Hinduism from a Maimonidean viewpoint: in some ways a vedantic worldview may be easier to reconcile with a Maimonidean understanding than a Christian worldview. The tension posited between the absolute and the relative, the unmanifest and the manifest, means (for those who remain aware of it) that one is always cognizant of the beyond and its various manifestations, all transitory and part of the impermanent order of the relative. Because the unmanifest manifests itself in various forms, the worship of those forms may be transparent to the unmanifest, as all forms are relative. This is all the more so when we consider that idols are understood as temporary dwelling places for something beyond. If so what is worshipped is not the image, but what dwells in it. <sup>18</sup> A strong presence-based understanding of images actually points beyond them. While Christianity’s weaker sense of images, seeing in them symbols, makes the charge of worshipping wood and stone ludicrous, <sup>19</sup> it does lack the tension that relativizes all concrete expressions in relation to the beyond. This is also true for different understandings of the incarnation in Christianity and Hinduism. <sup>20</sup> What it means for the Divine to take form may be different in both traditions. <sup>21</sup> These contrasts are suggestive of how one must think afresh with regard to novel encounters with religions. One cannot simply carry over attitudes developed in relation to one religion to another. In some ways theological understanding found in Hinduism may be more readily reconciled with the theological premises that inform a Maimonidean discussion, even though it is likely that these distinctions might not have been significant for Maimonides himself. Thinking of religions today, from a Maimonidean platform, requires at the very least an examination of those religions from Maimonides’ theoretical foundations, rather than from actual positions taken by Maimonides, conditioned as they were by his time, place, and understanding of the other. 

*[p. 59]* ^pg66

### 7 Avoda Zara between Jews and Gentiles: Nachmanides’ Model

 An important and very much underutilized resource for thinking of Avoda Zara is found in the works of Nachmanides. <sup>1</sup> Nachmanides’ thoughts 

on Avoda Zara are expressed through a third literary genre, neither halachic nor narrative—through exegesis, in his celebrated commentary on the Torah. Nachmanides’ discussion of the prohibition of worshipping other gods, in his commentary on the ten commandments, is an important resource for our own discussion. In terms of the basic worldview and the conceptual tools through which Avoda Zara is understood, Nachmanides is strikingly similar in his views to Maimonides. Both approach Avoda Zara through a hierarchical worldview, grounded in the cosmic order. Both pose the question of the legitimacy of the worship of beings who are lower than God, within this particular cosmic-hierarchical worldview. And both operate from a conceptual matrix involving a cosmology and an understanding of ensuing Avoda Zara that may be significantly different from the premises of a Hindu worldview. Juxtaposing Nachmanides and Maimonides thus shows us how pervasive is the particular worldview that has framed most discussions and understandings of other religions as Avoda Zara and that continues to inform Jewish thinking till today. It is this worldview that is echoed time and again in hundreds of homiletical and exegetical contexts, providing the basic tools through which Jews think of other religions. 

 But juxtaposing Nachmanides and Maimonides also serves another purpose. For Maimonides, the history of Avoda Zara is a history of humanity, and while it is the family and disciples of Abraham, later known as the people of Israel, who are the fortunate ones who have broken beyond prevailing ignorance of God and the worship of Avoda Zara, the concerns remain universal. Thus, there is no difference, for Maimonides, between Jew and non-Jew. <sup>2</sup> Both are equally implicated in Avoda Zara and the rules and guidelines that concern Avoda Zara apply equally to both. <sup>3</sup> Avoda Zara is considered one of the seven noachide commandments, in other words an obligation that is incumbent upon all humanity, prior to and independently of the specific covenant made with the Jewish people. While it is universally recognized by all Jewish authorities, based on talmudic sources, <sup>4</sup> 

*[p. 60]* ^pg67

that Avoda Zara is one of the noachide commandments, it is not clear that what Avoda Zara means for non-Jews is necessarily the same as what it means for Jews. Here we come to a major difference between Maimonides and Nachmanides. Maimonides clearly states that whatever is forbidden to a Jew, as an expression of Avoda Zara, is equally forbidden to a non-Jew. <sup>5</sup> Nachmanides, by contrast, suggests there is a major distinction between Jews and non-Jews in this regard. <sup>6</sup> The criteria and demands for a proper approach to God are different for Jews and non-Jews. <sup>7</sup> An examination of Nachmanides can thus be useful both for affirming how pervasive, central, and possibly inadequate, a particular cosmic-religious metaphysical worldview of Avoda Zara is and for broadening the possibilities of recognizing other religions as suitable for their worshippers, given the different criteria of Avoda Zara that apply for Jews and non-Jews. 

 The distinction between Israel and other nations emerges initially from a comment Nachmanides makes on the expression in Ex. 20,4: “For I am a jealous God.” Nachmanides first quotes Maimonides who claims “that in the entire Torah and in all the books of the Prophets, you will not find the term burning anger, wrath or jealousy applied to God, except with reference to idolatry.” Nachmanides notes that this is not the case in relation to all expressions noted by Maimonides: 

 However as far as the term “jealousy” is concerned, \[Maimonides\] is correct \[in maintaining that it is not applied to God except in reference to idolatry\]. And so did the Rabbis say in the Mekhilta: “I zealously exact punishment for idolatry, but in other matters I am gracious and merciful.” 

 In my opinion, jealousy is mentioned only with reference to idolatry in Israel. The reason for the jealousy is that Israel is the treasured possession of the Glorious Name, which He has separated for Himself . . . Now if His people, His servants, turn to other gods, God is jealous of them even as a man is jealous of his wife when she goes to other men, and of a servant who makes another master for himself. But Scripture uses no such term of jealousy with reference to other peoples to whom He has allotted the hosts of heaven. <sup>8</sup> 

 The scriptural observation regarding the limited use of jealousy in biblical language leads to an understanding of idolatry. Idolatry is a relational issue and is therefore specific to those with whom God has a particular relationship. The problem with Avoda Zara is relational. Avoda Zara infringes on the exclusivity of God’s relationship with Israel. Nachmanides returns to the most fundamental biblical notion of the covenant and its exclusive demands. <sup>9</sup> Israel is demanded to have an exclusive relationship with God and not to compromise that relationship by approaching any other being in faith or in worship as God. 

 If the issue is indeed relational, then the core of Avoda Zara would have to do with the forging of alternative relationships. This would clearly privilege the act of approach and recognition of another god, over the problematics associated with alternative forms of worship, and in particular the worship of images. Indeed, I wish to claim that Nachmanides reaffirms the centrality of Avoda Zara as something relational, establishing a relationship of believer and god with a being other than God, rather than as a form of inappropriate ritual, directed at 

*[p. 61]* ^pg68

objects of wood and stone. Thus, contrary to what might be an instinctive identification of worship of images as the defining feature of Avoda Zara, Nachmanides defines the act of faith or the formation of relationship as its constitutive feature. This emerges already from a difference of opinion that Nachmanides expresses in relation to Rashi’s commentary. Rashi, following the rabbinic Midrash, understands the prohibition in Ex. 20,3 “thou shalt have no other gods before My face” as a prohibition against keeping an idol in one’s possession. As other verses teach that idol making is prohibited, this verse, following rabbinic hermeneutical procedures, remains available for the additional teaching that even keeping an idol in one’s possession is prohibited. Nachmanides wonders about the exegetical logic of making this the primary meaning of this verse in the ten commandments and offers instead a different understanding:

 The correct interpretation even according to the literal meaning of Scripture is that the usage of the language of the verse here is similar to the expressions “and the eternal shall be my God” (Gen. 28,21), “to be your God”(Lev. 11,45). The verse here thus states that excepting the Eternal only, we are not to have others as gods, neither from all the angels above nor from all the hosts of heaven who are called *elohim* (gods) . . . It is thus a prohibition against believing in any of these beings, accepting them as gods, or saying to them, thou art my god. <sup>10</sup> 

 So, contrary to Rashi, the prohibition of not having other gods does not focus on the images of these idols, which at least symbolically would represent what the core problem with Avoda Zara might be and what would define Avoda Zara. Instead, “other gods” designates relationships alternative to the exclusive relationship that should exist with the one God, “present, eternal, from whom all came about, through will and power, who is alone their God” worthy of their worship. <sup>11</sup> Focusing the initial and key verse of Avoda Zara on relationship teaches us where the problem with Avoda Zara lies. Avoda Zara compromises the exclusive relationship due to God alone. 

 This is a very different understanding than the error-based view of Avoda Zara that informs Maimonides’ presentation. For Maimonides, the history of Avoda Zara is a history of error. God never willed the stars and planets to receive homage, and all that followed is a tragic chain of errors. For Nachmanides, error is not the key factor. In fact, his own presentation of the various stages of Avoda Zara amounts to an alternative typology of Avoda Zara. One characteristic of this alternative typology is that rather than being based on error, falsity, and deception, it is based on a valid approach to reality and recognizes the spiritual validity of the powers being worshipped. Nachmanides notes that the angels and hosts of heaven are called, in biblical usage, *elohim* , gods. Israel is demanded exclusive allegiance to the one God, creator of all. The various forms of Avoda Zara, which are prohibited to Israel, are all based on an understanding of their metaphysical realism. It is in light of such understanding of reality that the demand for exclusive allegiance is made of Israel. 

 Let us return to the first quote from Nachmanides’ commentary. After limiting God’s jealousy to Israel, Nachmanides clarifies that in relation to other peoples no such term of jealousy is used, for to those peoples God has allotted the 

*[p. 62]* ^pg69

hosts of heaven. The reference to allotting the hosts of heaven is an allusion to Deut. 4,19. The plain sense of that verse is that indeed God made the planets or angels legitimate objects of religious worship, and possibly also sources of care and provision, for nations other than Israel. The distinction between Israel and the nations would thus not rely exclusively on the observation that jealousy is unique to God’s relationship with Israel. The legitimacy of other nations worshipping stars and planets seems to have explicit scriptural support. <sup>12</sup> We already encountered this verse in Maimonides’ presentation. Maimonides, realizing fully well that this verse legitimates the worship of stars, thereby upsetting his scheme of how Avoda Zara came to be, offers an alternative interpretation. Maimonides paraphrases “all peoples,” meaning for the biblical text all peoples other than Israel, as “the whole world.” <sup>13</sup> Thus, Israel and the nations are in the same situation in relation to the heavenly bodies. They had been placed in a position of permanence in relation to the world, but neither Israel nor the nations may worship them. That would amount to an error in relation to the divine will. Error and issues of truth and falsehood are universal, hence they apply equally to Israel and the nations. Hence, the universal prohibition on Avoda Zara according to Maimonides. Nachmanides returns to the original sense of the verse, as well as of the biblical intention in prohibiting Avoda Zara. Because the demands of relationship are exclusive, the standards that apply to Avoda Zara are specific to Israel and do not apply to the nations. <sup>14</sup> These may, as the plain sense of Deut. 4,19 suggests, worship other beings. 

 Following this fundamental distinction, Nachmanides proceeds to unpack three different levels of what constitutes Avoda Zara. That these three levels follow immediately after the limitation of jealousy to Israel suggests that while what follows may describe the worship of other peoples, the reference to these habits of worship using the category of Avoda Zara is made from Israel’s perspective. In other words, even though Nachmanides is about to describe various forms of Avoda Zara, as these emerged in the religious history of humanity, it is not necessary to view these forms of religious worship and understanding as Avoda Zara for their practitioners. <sup>15</sup> In what follows then we have a typology of different types of Avoda Zara. This typology can function as a parallel text to Maimonides’ history of Avoda Zara. While Maimonides presents a history of humanity, relevant in its problematics to all humanity without exception, Nachmanides presents a typology that is designed to offer us a better understanding of Avoda Zara and how it works. Concomitantly, while Maimonides presented us with a history of the growing falsity of Avoda Zara and how it is removed from truth, stage by stage, Nachmanides presents us with a typology that moves through the various stages of the cosmos, suggesting how Avoda Zara might apply in relation to each stage. Instead of historical downgrading we get cosmic downgrading. And because the issue for Nachmanides is not one of falsehood, the various cosmic levels are characterized by the conformity to reality that each cosmic level of Avoda Zara enables. Thus, Avoda Zara is actually grounded in reality. This makes it real, potent, and, in its own way, valid under certain contexts and premises. The spiritual reality and validity of Avoda Zara grounds the permissibility of worship of these beings by non-Jews. It also makes the challenge of Avoda Zara and the nature of what it 

*[p. 63]* ^pg70

is that is forbidden to Jews much more focused, a consequence of the structure of the cosmos and of the possible religious responses to it. Let us then move on to a presentation of Nachmanides’ typology of Avoda Zara. Nachmanides continues:

 At this point, I make mention of what Scripture teaches concerning *Avoda Zara* , for there were three kinds. The earliest <sup>16</sup> began to worship the angels, who are the Separate Intelligences, for they knew the authority and jurisdiction that some of them had over the nations, as it is written, “The prince of the kingdom of Greece” (Dan. 10,20), “The prince of the kingdom of Persia” (Daniel 10, 13), and they thought that \[these angels\] have in them the ability to be beneficial or harmful, and so each people started worshipping its own minister, for the earliest \[generations\] knew them. Now these are referred to in the Torah and in all the Scriptures as *other gods* , *the gods of the peoples* , for the angels are called *gods (elohim) . . .* \[they worshipped the angels\] even though they recognized that supreme strength and infinite power belonged only to God, the Most High. <sup>17</sup> 

 Let us contrast this first stage in the history of Avoda Zara with the first stage described by Maimonides. For Maimonides, the first stage involves the worship of stars and astral bodies. For Nachmanides, the first stage involves the worship of Separate Intelligences. These are clearly not the stars and astral bodies. As will be clear from the next category, the stars are the physical bodies of these angelic beings. Nachmanides thus identifies the root of foreign worship in a higher metaphysical sphere. This has ramifications for the question of veracity and error. For Maimonides, Avoda Zara is nothing but an error. It grows out of a mistaken notion of fulfilling the divine will: astral bodies are given honor they do not deserve. Thus, there is a twofold error. God does not will such honor and the planetary bodies seem, in any event, to be devoid of any real power that might occasion worship. <sup>18</sup> By contrast, the higher angelic beings that are the historical root of Avoda Zara, according to Nachmanides, are indeed empowered. In fact, Avoda Zara does not grow out of a mistaken idea of the divine will but out of a proper recognition of the workings of the universe and the empowerment of angelic beings to rule over individual nations. Nachmanides takes seriously the designation of foreign gods as *elohim acherim* and the use of the name *elohim* , common to God and to these angelic beings. It suggests the spiritual level of these angels and the fact that they belong to a high spiritual realm and are divinely empowered in relation to the continuing management of the world. The earlier generations possessed a knowledge that has since been lost, the knowledge of their respective governing spiritual powers. Avoda Zara is thus based on a correct understanding of reality. It is not a complete error. This recognition is crucial in determining whether or not the designation of these forms of worship as Avoda Zara is intended purely from the viewpoint of Israel, which is commanded not to engage in Avoda Zara, or whether the practices of the earlier generations also constitute Avoda Zara for those early peoples. Nachmanides then moves on to the second category:

 The second type of *Avoda Zara* appeared when people turned to worshipping the visible hosts of heaven, some worshipping the sun or the moon, and others worshipping one of the planetary signs. Each of the nations knew the power of the 

*[p. 64]* ^pg71

astrological sign, according to how it governed their land. And they thought that by worshipping them, the astrological signs would be empowered, which would be beneficial to them. . . . As it is said in the Torah with reference to the prohibition of *Avoda Zara* “And lest you lift up your eyes unto heaven, and when you see the sun and the moon and the stars, even all the host of heaven, you be drawn away and worship them and serve them, which the Lord your God has allotted unto all the peoples under the whole heaven” (Deut. 4,19). That is to say, since God allotted them to all the peoples and gave each people a star or astrological sign, you should not let yourself be allured to worshipping them. <sup>19</sup> 

 Once again, Nachmanides appeals to Deut. 4,19. The worship of planetary bodies is based on a divine allotment that establishes the natural order. This order is an extension of the knowledge of the ministering angels. People knew their ministering angels as well as their ruling planets. And the two are closely related, inasmuch as the planets are the physical bodies of the ministering angels, the Separate Intelligences. <sup>20</sup> While chronologically later, the second kind describes a metaphysical descent, rather than a historical degradation, as we find in Maimonides’ presentation. The transition from the first to the second type of Avoda Zara is not a move into greater error, but to greater manifestation or corporeality. In terms of the metaphysical foundation of the worship, hence in terms of its truth content, both stages seem to be equally founded. Both may be viewed as complementary dimensions of Avoda Zara, equally valid or invalid, distinguished by the metaphysical level to which intentionality and practice are directed. 

 The second stage in the formation of Avoda Zara is characterized by a move toward physical concretization and manifestation of spiritual powers. These are channeled through the astrological signs. The powers of the stars and the attempt to capture and to harness them for beneficial purposes account for the various forms of ritual and worship that later emerge:

 Now, these are the people who began making the many forms of graven images *,* *asheirim* and sun images. They would make the forms of the astrological signs in the hours of their strength, according to their rank, and they would empower the people, so they understood, with force and success. . . . Closely related to this kind of *Avoda Zara* was the worship of human beings. When people of a country saw that a certain individual, such as Nebuchadnezar—had great power and that his star was very much in the ascendancy, they thought that by accepting his worship upon themselves and directing their intentionality towards him, their sign would rise with his. <sup>21</sup> 

 An astrological understanding thus provides the basis for various forms of Avoda Zara, as practiced by its followers. Their acts are coherent and rely on a valid view of the power of astrological signs and on correct observation of the impact of the planetary powers on groups and individuals. The move from worshipping the angelic Separate Intelligences to worshipping forms and peoples is related to the attempt to capture the astrological energy for one’s own purposes. This astrological theory accounts for the worship of graven images as well as of people. While worship already characterized the first stage of Avoda Zara, 

*[p. 65]* ^pg72

images, as well as attempts to capture the power and to harness it to one’s own benefit, characterize the second stage. 

 Nachmanides’ astrological theory assumes a correct observation of the governance of nations and individuals. However, the legitimacy of such worship can be disputed. In relation to the first category, Nachmanides affirms that the worshippers did not forget the God on high, who is source of all power. He makes no similar assertion in relation to the second category, thereby suggesting that it may involve forgetting the full cosmic view, where God is the ultimate source of all power. This accords with his description, later in this passage, of Sennacherib and Pharaoh, who are worshipped as gods in light of this astrological understanding, as “wicked, but not absolute fools.” It would seem that the claim for their divinity, while based on correct astrological foundations is nevertheless an expression of wickedness, since appropriating the title “god” leads to a forgetfulness of the one true God. One imagines that had such worship not led to their claims of divinity and had it been undertaken with awareness of the ultimate power of God, they might not have been wicked. It is noteworthy that in referencing the worship of stars, Nachmanides quotes Deut. 4,19, suggesting worship of these stars is appropriate. The tone changes in relation to the capturing of the astral energies in embodied forms. But the change may be the result of reference to the particular application of these principles in the cases of individuals whose claims to divinity eclipsed recognition of God. The principle itself might be justified, if one did not to lose sight of God. In other words, the second type of Avoda Zara itself consists of two stages: the worship of astral bodies and of their various embodiments. It is not fully clear whether the boundaries of possible legitimacy of such worship correspond to the two stages, or whether in either case it is the broader maintaining of awareness of God that determines whether such worship is permissible or not for those nations that God has placed under the control of the astral bodies, following Deut. 4,19. <sup>22</sup> 

 Nachmanides goes on to describe the third category of those who engage in Avoda Zara:

 The third kind of idolatry appeared afterwards when people began worshipping the demons, which are spirits . . . Some of them too are appointed over the peoples to be masters in their lands and to harm their enemies and those who stumble among them, as is known of their activity through the art of necromancy, as well as through the words of our Rabbis. It is with reference to this \[third type\] that Scripture says “They sacrificed unto demons, non-gods . . . ”(Deut. 32,17). Scripture ridicules the \[Israelites\], saying they sacrifice also to the demons who are no gods at all. That is to say, they are not like the angels, who are called *elohim* . Instead, they are “gods that they knew not,” meaning that they found in them no trace of might or power of rulership. <sup>23</sup> 

 The third kind of Avoda Zara is both chronologically later and metaphysically inferior. Again, it is based on a valid knowledge of some dimension of reality. The demons do have some powers associated with their particular territory. However, they are at the very opposite end of the power-scale, compared with the first group. Those were *elohim* , gods. These are non-gods. This suggests their lower 

*[p. 66]* ^pg73

metaphysical rank. They have power, but it is not divine power. Perhaps the fact that their power is exclusively destructive and harmful accounts for the difference between the power recognized as divine and the power recognized as non-divine. Though theoretically one might still entertain approaching these beings while maintaining an awareness of the broader theological perspective wherein God is the master of all, such reconciling seems improbable in view of their designation as non-gods. Indeed, the sources by Nachmanides and by his followers that admit the possibility of worshipping other beings within a broader theological framework that maintains awareness of God only speak of the worship of astral powers. This may simply be a matter of what was highlighted, though there does seem to be greater incompatibility between this type of worship and the worship of God. This point will be useful to us further along when we attempt to construct a composite sense of what Avoda Zara means and how it might apply to other religions today. 

 Having concluded his presentation of the three types of Avoda Zara, descending in historical and metaphysical order, Nachmanides sums up what it is that is forbidden in their worship with reference to the ten commandments, the literary context in which this analysis has been carried out: <sup>24</sup> 

 Thus, in this second commandment, the Torah forbade all \[forms of\] worship, except for that directed to God alone. <sup>25</sup> It is for this reason that He first admonished “Thou shalt have no other gods before My face,” which is a reference to the first kind . . . He then further admonished against graven images as well as likeness, which alludes to something spiritual that is imagined . . . And so the Rabbis said: “ *That is in the heaven* , this includes the sun, moon, stars and astrological signs. *Above* , this includes the ministering angels . . . *And that is in the water under the earth* , this includes the demons” <sup>26</sup> . . . of all of them He said “Thou shalt not bow down unto them, nor serve them” in any manner of worship whatsoever, even if the worshipper’s intent is not to remove himself from the domain (or: authority) of the Holy One, blessed be He. Thus He has ordered that all manners of worship be directed exclusively to the Special Name, blessed be He. <sup>27</sup> 

 The concluding statement sums up the preceding discussion in relation to the verses of the ten commandments. The ten commandments refer to all three types of Avoda Zara, all of which are forbidden to Israel. The conclusion of this passage makes clear the basis for the prohibition. Worshipping any other being, whether angelic or demonic, compromises the exclusivity of the relationship with God. Nachmanides is aware of the fact that exclusivity is as much a matter of intention as it is of practice. However, he is not willing to allow for any compromise of absolute dedication of all forms of worship to the one God, even if the intention of the worshipper does not involve rebellion or break of authority otherwise associated with removing oneself from God’s domain. The ten commandments are thus a teaching in exclusive allegiance, in the framework of a broad and hierarchical metaphysical worldview. Hierarchy might have allowed us to consider permissible worship of other beings, provided an awareness of the fullness of the hierarchical structure is maintained. The message of the ten commandments is that this is not so. 

 The religious understanding that informs the ten commandments is that of covenantal exclusivity. Approaching any other power or being is tantamount to 

*[p. 67]* ^pg74

compromising the exclusive relationship Israel is to have with God. However, what of other nations? Precisely the fact that Nachmanides grounds his view of Avoda Zara in a covenantal relationship, rather than in the problem of metaphysical error, raises the question of whether the description of Avoda Zara applies equally to Jews and non-Jews. As we have seen, Nachmanides mentions the special demands of Avoda Zara upon Israel, due to their exclusive covenantal relationship. At several points in Nachmanides’ discussion it seems like different rules apply to non-Jews: they are allowed to approach their ministering angels, perhaps also their stars and maybe even the other forms of worship that he mentions, provided they do not lose sight of the fullness of the theological picture, that is, provided they maintain due awareness of the one God above it all. This possible understanding is borne out by some other passages in Nachmanides’ commentary, as well as by the testimony of his followers:

 He is Thy Praise and He is Thy God. The meaning thereof is that He should be your \[sole object of\] praise, that you are to devote all your praise to Him, praising Him always, and not to give His glory to another, neither His praise to graven images (Isa. 42,8), nor to the gods and lords mentioned. Or he may be stating: He is thy praise, for through Him you are praised above all peoples . . . for He has especially addressed His name to you, that you be unto Himself for a people and that He may be unto thee a God (Deut. 29,12), unlike the rest of the peoples to whom He is the God of gods, that he allotted to them (Deut. 4,19), as I have explained, because He has done for you the great and tremendous things from which you can recognize that you are His portion and His inheritance, and His eyes are always upon you for good. <sup>28</sup> 

 The biblical verse refers specifically to Israel’s relationship with God. Nachmanides understands this relationship in contrast with those of other nations. Appealing once again to Deut. 4,19, Nachmanides understands that other nations have been allotted other gods. Their relationship to God is as the God of gods. <sup>29</sup> Only Israel had witnessed the special miracles that are a sign of its particular relationship with God. Israel is God’s lot and share, and God always watches over it. In exchange, exclusive allegiance is demanded. Such exclusive covenantal allegiance is understood not in terms of equally potent gods, among which one chooses to whom one has to make allegiance, but within a clearly defined hierarchical worldview. Only Israel is demanded to focus its devotion and commitment to the top of the metaphysical hierarchy. Others may form allegiances to the lower beings in the hierarchy, the gods, while maintaining awareness of God’s position as the God of gods. <sup>30</sup> 

 The metaphysical organization, according to which angels of divine status, *elohim* , are appointed over other nations, but not over Israel, is again affirmed in Nachmanides’ commentary on Deut. 29,25:

 The correct interpretation is “gods that they knew not” as gods, that is to say \[they worshipped gods\] whom they knew not as having acted with any Divine power inherent to their essence. And the Eternal, the God of their fathers, did not allot them \[these gods\] to them, meaning that He did not designate them as lords over Israel as He placed them over the nations. I have already explained this. <sup>31</sup> 

*[p. 68]* ^pg75

 God has arranged the world in such a way that different ministers are appointed over different peoples. Israel has God as its share, while others have appointed ministers. We note here an interesting metaphysical understanding of divinity. Divinity is present in the angelic ministers as an extension of God’s own power. They do not have divinity in them on their own account, but by extension. God, we assume, by contrast is divine by His very own nature. This metaphysical distinction could be significant as we seek to apply Nachmanides’ insights to a Jewish view of Hinduism. Israel are commanded to only worship God directly, encountering the divinity in its primary and originary state, in God Himself. By contrast, other nations have been appointed ministers to oversee their affairs. Israel does not recognize them as *elohim* on their own right, but only by extension. A distinction thus emerges between first-order and second-order divinity. This distinction is of twofold significance. The very recognition that divinity may be encountered second order is itself of great importance for a consideration of other religions. Secondly, it offers a clear formulation of what distinguishes Israel’s spiritual life from that of other people. While noting this major divide, we also note how respectful the approach to other religions, practiced by the nations, is. To speak of second-order divinity is a far cry from what might otherwise be imagined as derogatory expressions of ridicule, annulment, and deprecation aimed at other religions and their gods. According to Nachmanides, there is a way in which other religions and their gods might be validated as appropriate for their worshippers. The two guiding principles of this way of thinking are the uncompromising distinction between Israel and the nations and the corresponding cosmic hierarchical gradation that provides the basis for both recognition of other gods and the distinction between them and God, Israel’s share and lot. 

 Allotting gods to the nations would make little sense if they were forbidden to worship them. To say that the sole purpose of these angels is to provide and care for the people, while in Israel’s case it is God who directly and personally cares for Israel, makes for bad theology. If the nations receive divine provision and supervision from certain divine beings, why would they be forbidden to worship them? Israel is supposed to worship God because of the special favors shown to it. The nations have not received such favors. It would make little sense to demand exclusive allegiance to a God who only cares for the nations by proxy. Why should the nations not worship their own divinely appointed gods, and how can one demand of them allegiance to the God who does not show them direct care? Unless one adopts a theology of perdition, one cannot make sense of such a worldview. <sup>32</sup> The only way to therefore make sense of Nachmanides is to assume that if these ministers were appointed over the nations as *elohim* , they may also be worshipped by them. 

 This understanding is made explicit by Nachmanides’ disciple, the Rashba, Rabbi Solomon ben Abraham Adret. The following passage appears in one of the Rashba’s responsa: <sup>33</sup> 

 What happened to us, in relation to the rest of the nations, may be likened to what happened to a people who serve a great king. That king appointed for the management of the city a minister, and commanded him to govern the state <sup>34</sup> and to judge 

*[p. 69]* ^pg76

all the people who are found in it in justice. He also permitted the entire people to follow that minister, if they will, for all their needs. For he will lead them, following the king’s command, in whatever they require, provided they remain cognizant that he is appointed by the king and he as well as them are slaves to the king. Indeed, \[the king\] commanded the minister that he should have no jurisdiction over one of the members of his palace, for he has separated them <sup>35</sup> unto himself and he will lead them and judge among them and will provide for all their needs. Similarly he commanded and ordained the members of his palace and the servants who stand before him to never follow the minister, but only \[follow\] the king. And whosoever among them follows the minister, this will be considered for him as a rebellion and as transferring the rulership from him (i.e. the king) and handing it over to that minister. 

 This is precisely what happened to us in relation to the other nations, concerning the laws of faith and providence (or: the surrender of our actions). <sup>36</sup> For God divided the lands to the astrological signs, and gave them rulership over the land, so that a given planet governs a given place. This is why they are divided in their countries and localities in faith, this worships one image while another worships another. And whoever worships the particular star that guides a particular place is not like someone who engages in *Avoda Zara* , provided he knows and recognizes that the power of the star and its rulership are only due to God, who made it a ruler in that particular land. As it says concerning the Samaritans, they feared God and served their gods. <sup>37</sup> But concerning us, He set us apart as His heritage unto Him. And our land He also set apart unto Himself, for His Temple to be there. And He commanded us not to worship a sign or a star at all, and that we should not direct our actions to them in any way whatsoever, but rather we shall serve before Him and He will answer us, whenever we call out to Him. And whosoever among us worships anyone besides Him, it will be considered for him a great fault, as someone who rebels against His kingdom and as someone who worships *Avoda Zara* , God forbid. 

 For this reason you will not find in all Scriptures accusations of *Avoda Zara* except in relation to Israel alone, and He called our land the land of life, and He testified that His providence is in it, and He always seeks it. And the prophets spoke of this explicitly. It says in the Torah, when warning us regarding this matter, “And when you look up to the sky and behold the sun and the moon and the stars, the whole heavenly host, you must not be lured into bowing down to them or serving them. These the Lord your God allotted to other peoples everywhere under heaven. But you the Lord took and brought out of Egypt, that iron blast furnace, to be His very own people.” <sup>38</sup> When referring to the iron blast furnace <sup>39</sup> this teaches us, a true sign, that He did not leave us under the rulership of the astrological signs and the celestial princes. For now He has taken us out of the iron furnace, a reference to the strength of the astrological sign, according to which it would have been impossible to come out of \[Egypt\], had our fate been determined by the laws of the stars. 

 The continuity between the Rashba and his teacher, Nachmanides, is unmistakable. We encounter the same theology with the same key prooftext, Deut. 4,19. Had we entertained any doubt concerning the status of non-Jews and their worship of beings other than God, the Rashba is quite explicit that such worship is not considered Avoda Zara, provided one has not lost sight of God who is the ultimate source of power and governance of the individual planet or astrological sign. We 

*[p. 70]* ^pg77

also note that the Rashba speaks of astrological signs and their representation in images and statues. This corresponds to the second type of Avoda Zara, presented in Nachmanides’ commentary on Exodus. We previously entertained the question of whether Nachmanides condones only worship of the first type of Avoda Zara for non-Jews, or whether any form of worship, of any of the three types of Avoda Zara listed by him, would not be Avoda Zara, provided the worshipper remained aware of God as the supreme power. The Rashba has certainly taken our discussion an important step forward by including not only the first stage, those beings explicitly called *elohim* , in permissibility of worship, but also those of the second stage, planets and their representation in images. The logic could be extended to the third type, the worship of demons, provided one remained aware of God as the ultimate power. On the other hand, perhaps their being called explicitly non-gods and their harmful nature make their worship undesirable. Either way, what really matters is the worship of planetary signs and their representation, as the Rashba seems to consider this as the prevailing mode of worship by non-Jews. Non-Jewish worship of other beings is thus cosmically grounded, part of the natural order, ordained by God. It would seem pointless to oppose or criticize something that is part of how God arranges the cosmos. The only thing that is of real concern is that Israel, which has been taken out of the angelic and astral realms and entrusted to God’s exclusive providential care, should devote its worship exclusively to the one God who established with it a special relationship. The relational dimension and its grounding in Jewish history, particularly in the Exodus, provide continuity not only with Nachmanides’ thinking, but also with the foundational biblical theological notion of the covenant. One does note in Rashba’s discussion one dimension that we did not see in Nachmanides. This is the territorial dimension. It is not only that nations knew who their governing ministers were, <sup>40</sup> but that there is a full correspondence between angelic forces, astral forces, people, and land. This territorial dimension is also derived from Nachmanides’ thought, as expressed in other passages. For present purposes, the nuances that distinguish a territorial from a nonterritorial perspective are not central to our discussion, and I will therefore not engage them. <sup>41</sup> 

 Let us now consider the possible implications of Nachmanides’ view of Avoda Zara to a contemporary Jewish view of Hinduism. <sup>42</sup> This is one of the major theological formulations of the Middle Ages and one of the most elaborate statements on Avoda Zara. It should therefore be taken into account as we grapple for the conceptual tools with which to think through contemporary challenges. Over and against Maimonides’ view, it gives us a theory that distinguishes between Israel and the nations and what accounts for this distinction. The possibility of making such a distinction is one of the fundamental steps that can be taken in any attempt to legitimate other religions. Later in our discussion, we shall study the halachic trajectory that makes this very distinction by appealing to the notion of Shituf, association, the worship of another being alongside God. Nachmanides’ discussion does not refer to the notion of Shituf, nor does that of the Rashba. The trajectory that elevated Shituf to the level of a theological principle of tolerance is both chronologically later and geographically different. We find this movement primarily in Ashkenazi literature from the seventeenth century onward, 

*[p. 71]* ^pg78

not in Sephardi literature of the thirteenth century. But the logic seems to be the same. In fact, the proponents of Shituf, whom we shall study below, do not really provide an adequate theory that would justify why non-Jews may worship other beings alongside God. Nachmanides provides precisely such a logic, accounting for why Shituf may be permissible for non-Jews. Accordingly, it is only a matter of time until Nachmanides’ thought provides the rationale for the permissibility of Shituf for non-Jews, as these two trajectories come together. <sup>43</sup> 

 There is a possible distinction between the position affirming the permissibility of Shituf and Nachmanides’ view of Avoda Zara. This distinction has implications to the potential relevance of Nachmanides’ discussion to a consideration of Hinduism. Nachmanides describes the various forms of Avoda Zara as based on some correct knowledge of spiritual reality. The first kind is based on the knowledge of the heavenly ministers, appointed over every nation. The second is based on knowledge of their respective astrological signs and the third upon knowledge of the demonic powers that reside in a given territory. In all cases, Avoda Zara is real inasmuch as it is a response to how reality has been divinely organized. What determines Avoda Zara would accordingly be how encompassing and total the religious worldview of the believer is. For Nachmanides, non-Jews may worship the appointed ministers, possibly other levels within the cosmic hierarchy as well, provided they recognize their relationship to God and their ultimate dependence on Him. The difference between Jews and non-Jews is not in their respective view of reality. Both would recognize the supreme rulership of God and the fact that He has appointed various beings, at various rungs of reality, to govern and manage the world. The difference is one of religious focus and orientation. Jews are demanded to have exclusive allegiance to the supreme power only, while non-Jews may strike a balance between their religious knowledge and their worship. So long as their knowledge maintains an awareness of the fuller metaphysical structures of the universe, worship may be directed at other beings as well. Only Israel is obligated to a full correspondence of knowledge and worship in relation to God. <sup>44</sup> When considering the potential relevance of Nachmanides’ views to our discussion of Hinduism, we must ask how crucial is the correct knowledge of the appointed ministers to his overall scheme. There are two possible ways to apply Nachmanides. The first is to insist that any worship of another being be based on divine allotment. After all, Deut. 4,19 provides the scriptural basis for the position. Accordingly, only those beings who have been allotted to other nations may be considered in this discussion. This would make Nachmanides completely irrelevant to any contemporary discussion. Cast as it is in terms of the astrological science of the Middle Ages, <sup>45</sup> the discussion would refer to no known religion, either ancient or contemporary. It would thus be a completely theoretical construct, whose sole contribution would be the organization and clarification of the various biblical references that are integrated into his presentation. 

 The alternative position would be that Nachmanides presents us with a thought structure, through which the basic criteria of what constitutes Avoda Zara emerge, or perhaps it is better to say what makes something not Avoda Zara, that is for non-Jewish believers. Recognition of the one supreme God protects and excludes the worshippers of other beings from the specter of Avoda Zara. If so, it 

*[p. 72]* ^pg79

matters little whether the being really was appointed by God over a people or not. What matters is that alongside faith in and worship of that being, one remains cognizant of the one supreme God. Is the primary condition for not being Avoda Zara the recognition of God or the following of those ministers and planetary powers appointed by God as the objects of possible legitimate worship? The latter possibility would lead us back to a definition of Avoda Zara that is related to the divine will. Unlike Maimonides, Nachmanides recognizes that the divine will could include the worship of beings other than God. The former position would highlight knowledge and recognition as the primary criterion. As long as some degree of awareness of God is maintained, the worship of other beings is not tantamount to Avoda Zara. Significantly, Nachmanides does not highlight will at all in his presentation. <sup>46</sup> This might make the former option more plausible. It is only according to such a reading that Nachmanides’ presentation would have any relevance to a contemporary discussion of other religions. 

 If we are willing to consider the structure of Nachmanides’ thought, independently of the correctness of the identity of the gods worshipped and their allotment by God as part of an astrologically informed scheme of governance, we could paraphrase his message as follows, allowing his teaching to address the present concern of the encounter with Hinduism. Whoever worships a deity, or some manifestation of the Divine, is not like someone who engages in Avoda Zara, provided he knows and recognizes that God, the supreme source of all, is the power behind the particular deity that is worshipped. Nachmanides, thus restated, offers us a framework for approaching Hinduism. 

 If we take Hinduism as approaching God through the dual dimensions of the absolute-unmanifest dimension of the Divine and the relative-manifest dimension, knowledge, rather than worship, is more crucial for bridging this divide. Worship is addressed to various gods, who are seen as manifestations of the absolute, which is in itself the source of all being, the source of creation. <sup>47</sup> Indeed, worship is not offered to the absolute, to Brahman. <sup>48</sup> Brahman remains instead the conceptual focus of the religious system, its ultimate goal. The absolute is recognized primarily through knowledge while the gods are approached through ritual. This situation corresponds to Nachmanides’ structure. 

 Working with the model developed by Nachmanides is particularly important if we seek to develop positive categories by means of which to address Hindu reality. Many of the major religious figures, the gurus*,* one encounters nowadays are models, at times outstanding models, of wisdom, spirituality, and life lived in God. The high degree of spiritual life achieved by them makes dismissing them as mere idolaters a luxury that only those who are willing to lock themselves within narrow confines and continue to judge the world irrespective of what they see (or can’t see) in it can afford. Any serious and genuine person who seeks to assess Hindu religious life must acknowledge that Hinduism has produced great spiritual giants who not only know something about God, but who may also have important lessons to teach us. At the same time, these same personalities often engage in the worship of gods as well as of their idols. Indeed, the term “idolworship,” which for Jews is usually derogatory, is for them purely descriptive and value free. <sup>49</sup> 

*[p. 73]* ^pg80

 Let me make the paradox even more extreme, by appealing to the example of one major Hindu religious figure—Shirdi Sai Baba. This nineteenth-century saint is the object of veneration throughout India. His cult is fast becoming one of India’s most popular. Following Hindu patterns of worship, his image is venerated in hundreds of temples throughout India. From a superficial, visual-based perspective, this is one further expression of Avoda Zara. On a visit to one of his temples, I was struck by the following sign, which captures the essence of his teaching:

 He, the infinite and absolute, Incarnates Himself and descends to earth From time to time To vanquish the evil Reestablish the divinity and uphold the cosmic principles. 

 He has made Himself perceptible As Lord Krishna, Gautam Buddha Jesus Christ, Hazrat Mohammad, Guru Nanak and many more Divine masters and eventually, various religions have also come into being. 

 To merge all these religions into one He then manifested Himself as SAI and evinced the ultimate truth—We are one God is One 

 From a Jewish perspective, how are we to reconcile image worship with recognition of the unity of God? Nachmanides would offer us one way of accounting for what from a classical Jewish perspective might be considered a paradox. The worship of other beings is clearly balanced by the knowledge of the ultimate God. This balance is permissible for non-Jews and might hold the key to their spiritual success. They seek to reach God in the absolute, while approaching Him through various divine beings, who aid them in their path. 

 We may be able to apply Nachmanides to present concerns in ways other than abstracting core principles from his teachings. Jews must not worship any being other than God and non-Jews may worship other beings, provided they do not lose sight or awareness of God. Nachmanides speaks of each nation having its own god, appointed by God. This statement of metaphysics can be psychologized in fruitful ways. Each religion imagines its gods in particular ways. The gods of China are not as the gods of India, or of Africa. Now, the gods of Hinduism are indeed the gods of a people, as that people has thought, reflected, and, above 

*[p. 74]* ^pg81

all, imagined the divine realm. Gods, with their representations and myths, are above all an expression of the realm of the imagination. This imaginative realm is often interpreted in philosophical or metaphysical terms. A more philosophical understanding tends to abstraction, such that it may be carried over from one religious tradition to another in search of a common understanding. By contrast, the pictorial and representational dimensions, those that translate into the problematic realm of Avoda Zara as expressed in worship, are the particularities of the psyche of a given people and its religious imagination. Accordingly, we might consider the specific representations of deities in Hinduism as part of what God has allotted this people, not through astral governance, but through the expression of their religious imagination. <sup>50</sup> Such a psychologizing restatement of Nachmanides would leave room for a rich mythical, imaginative, and artistic life, which is particular to any given religious tradition, while balancing it with a more abstract, philosophical, if we will, “pure” understanding of God. Thus, we could learn much more from Nachmanides and apply it directly to Hinduism, if we were willing to substitute the national faculties of imagination, themselves God given, for the nationally appointed divine ministers. 

 There might be yet another way of reconfiguring Nachmanides, so that his thought structure remains helpful, despite the fact that it is articulated based on a scientific theory that is no longer current. In doing so, we explore other dimensions of what “scientific” might mean in a religious context. Nachmanides bases his description on a proper understanding of reality. In that sense, underlying his reconstruction of the birth and typology of Avoda Zara is a certain scientific worldview. Now, many Hindu practitioners conceive of their own religion and its practices in scientific terms. This is actually one of the distinguishing features of modern Hindu thought, and one of the dimensions that makes it distinct from the thought of Abrahamic religions, Judaism included. I do not wish to buy into facile stereotypes that represent Judaism (or Christinaity) as a faith-based religion, while Hinduism is something else. Religions are complex entities and have varying doses of orthodoxy, orthopraxy, and various other ingredients in all of them, distinguishing one version of a given religion as well as one religion from another. As part of the recognition of different nuances that characterize religions, and without drawing a firm typology, it is worth noting that many contemporary Hindu authors have developed a self-understanding of their religion as scientific. In many ways, Hinduism as well as Buddhism have fared much better in the science-religion exchanges than have the more literal scripturally oriented Abrahamic faiths. For them, science and religion often come together in creative synergies, rather than through polar dichotomies, as we often encounter in Abrahamic religions. Such synergies occur not only in relation to theories of creation and the cosmos, but also in relation to internal psychological and spiritual processes. 

 Several of the Hindu traditions, particularly the Yoga and Vedanta traditions, approach the spiritual life as though it were a science, a science of consciousness and spiritual development. The goal is to attain spiritual liberation, or union with the absolute. The various religious practices and the complex forms of the religious life are then understood as a means for such attainment. The systematic, 

*[p. 75]* ^pg82

practice-oriented nature of religious ritual and discipline then invites comparison with the systematic and repeatable nature of the scientific experiment, leading to the conclusion that the practice of religion is itself a kind of scientific practice. I think it is fair to say that some of the contemporary draw to Hinduism can be accounted for as a result of this scientific emphasis. Many Westerners are drawn to a scientific approach that is result oriented, at least in theory, compared with a faith-based approach, whose results are not marketed in the same way, even if in fact they may be close or even identical. Now, within this scientific understanding, deities and their worship are often approached as instruments in an internal spiritual and psychological process. The ultimate goal of the process is beyond, leading to and uniting with God in the absolute. The gods who are worshipped are instruments in attaining this goal. They provide focus, orientation, opening of the heart, protection, and grace. They are real, <sup>51</sup> but they ultimately serve as aids to self-transcendence in a process that is understood scientifically. Thus, there exist various influential and popular constructions of Hinduism wherein the deities worshipped correspond to a particular psychological state (as well as a cosmic state, according to Hindu cosmology) and where the goal of the spiritual life, including the worship of these deities, is to go beyond these manifestations, to reach God in the absolute. 

 Now, this is in some ways similar to what Nachmanides considers as the foundation for non-Jewish worship; in fact it seems spiritually more potent and much more in keeping with the overall goals of Jewish teaching. If we are willing to substitute the scientific approach to cosmology with a scientific approach to psychology and the spiritual process, many Hindu forms of worship will do much more than simply recognize the one supreme God while worshipping other beings. For these Hindu practitioners, the worship of the deities, their forms, and the various rituals and exercises associated with them are actually instrumental in attaining conscious knowledge and presence of the supreme God. Thus, the supreme God is not some distant idea that non-Jews dimly recognize, but the active and conscious focus of a personal transformative spiritual process. Conscious focus and the quest for entry into the conscious presence of the highest God frame much of Hinduism, as practiced in philosophical schools such as Yoga and Vedanta. More broadly speaking, the overall emphasis on God and coming close to Him are present in Hindu religious life in ways that impress the outsider and that often leave the Jewish observer envious. We have recognized this to be a strong part of the contemporary Jewish draw to Hinduism. <sup>52</sup> If Nachmanides posits that only Jews have an exclusive relationship with the one God, while others may worship His ministers, Hinduism may both affirm this view and challenge it simultaneously. It may affirm it, if we consider that the structure of worshipping other gods, while recognizing, indeed striving toward the higher God is characteristic of the spiritual path of non-Jews. It challenges it inasmuch as it allows us to consider the spiritual life of different faiths and their respective strengths and weaknesses. Jews enjoy the strengths of protection from the pitfalls of worship of other beings and their representation, which could lead them away from the memory of and exclusive allegiance to the one God. Hinduism, however, goes beyond the portrayal of the nations, as found in Nachmanides. Hinduism, or some of its 

*[p. 76]* ^pg83

schools, does not offer a second-order divinity or a second-rate approach to God. For the spiritual seekers to whom it is home, it offers a first-order approach to God, through the second-order gods, aiming for God in the highest. As it aims for God in the highest, it often keeps Him much more in the center of its awareness than Judaism, which is often content to declare God’s unity, while focusing its religious energies on goals other than coming to know Him and reaching union with Him. <sup>53</sup> 

 There is one further point to be made with reference to Nachmanides and to how his thought may be relevant to Hinduism. Nachmanides’ theory offers a scientific, or reality-based, view that justifies the worship of beings other than God as part of a cosmic structure. It is based on the knowledge of the inner workings of the universe and the appropriate sense of belonging of each nation, on the three levels discussed by him. One thing is missing in his presentation—a view of the worship of other gods as intermediaries and intercessors in approaching God, the highest. It is common to find in Jewish sources a view of Avoda Zara as based on the worship of intermediary powers. <sup>54</sup> According to such an understanding, Jews would have an unmediated approach to God, while non-Jews, if their worship is recognized, would approach God through the mediation of their spiritual forces, powers, angels, etc. It is noteworthy that Nachmanides says nothing of the sort. The nations worship their gods because they contain some dimension of divinity, they are *elohim* , not because they help one gain access to God. In fact, God is kept, in this system, as the exclusive lot of Israel. The nations know Him, but they do not even try to approach Him via their governing angels. <sup>55</sup> We have already noted that Maimonides too does not present the fall into idolatry as a consequence of a quest for intermediaries, who would facilitate one’s approach to God, even though he does seem to appeal to such a notion when offering his core legal definition of Avoda Zara, in the Laws of Avoda Zara 2,1. Thus, mediation and the worship of intermediaries do not figure prominently in these two fundamental presentations of Avoda Zara. 

 Nevertheless, given how prominent the problem of intermediaries has become in later Jewish presentations of Avoda Zara, it is worth noting that the notion of intermediaries is not the optimal category through which to grasp the role of gods in Hindu thought. Obviously, there is some sense of instrumentality, hence, of their being intermediaries, as just mentioned. However, this is more an instrumentality of spiritual process, aiding the individual along the way, rather than the classical understanding of the intermediary as a being that is more powerful, who can therefore achieve, on behalf of the believer, what the believer herself could not, obtaining for her favors from the higher God. While various Hindu myths may be read in such a light, it is not the common understanding of the function of divinities in the Hindu pantheon. The major deities, those that are objects of worship in most forms of Hinduism, are rarely if ever understood as intermediaries to the supreme God. <sup>56</sup> They are not thought of as lower beings on a cosmic rank whose job is to aid the believer in obtaining favors from the higher rank. Rather, the prevailing notion, and herein lies one of the singularities of Hindu religious thought, is that God, the absolute, takes the form of these beings, or put differently: *comes as* these beings. Nachmanides, Maimonides, and 

*[p. 77]* ^pg84

most of Jewish tradition, approach the issue of other gods from within a clearly structured and highly hierarchical worldview, even if they do not appeal to a notion of intermediaries. The absolute God has no form, and all forms that are worshipped belong to lower rungs on the metaphysical chain. Hindu thought often defies such hierarchical thinking. Phenomenologically, it approaches reality and its forms as something fluid. Forms change and beings can take up one form or another. <sup>57</sup> God can therefore take up form. Incarnations are common. They may be the incarnation of one specific aspect of God, particularly Vishnu, who comes time and again through his many manifestations, his *avataras* . But often religious thought simply thinks of God as coming. 

 The fluidity of form and how reality can morph correspond to another metaphysical principle, the all-pervasiveness of God. It is not simply that God is everywhere, but that everything is pervaded by divine being, thereby making it on some level divine. Thus, God can be recognized in anything. Stories of saints report how their spiritual illumination took place when they were able to perceive God in the form of something particular. This is a mode of encounter with God, within a culture that has so made God the focus of its quest that it seeks to find Him in all forms and appearances. Let me cite here a prayer composed by Swami Sivananda. It is called “universal prayer” and is recited daily in his ashram. Let us take note of how God is described in this prayer. 

 O Adorable Lord of Mercy and Love ! 

 Salutations and prostrations unto Thee. 

 Thou art Omnipresent, Omnipotent and Omniscient. 

 Thou art Sat-Chid-Ananda. 

 Thou art Existence, Knowledge and Bliss Absolute. 

 Thou art the Indweller of all beings. 

 Grant us an understanding heart, Equal vision, balanced mind, Faith, devotion and wisdom. 

 Grant us inner spiritual strength To resist temptation and to control the mind. 

 Free us from egoism, lust, anger, greed, hatred and jealousy. 

 Fill our hearts with Divine virtues. 

 Let us behold Thee in all these names and forms. 

 Let us serve Thee in all these names and forms. 

 Let us ever remember Thee. 

 Let us ever sing Thy glories. 

 Let Thy Name be ever on our lips. 

 Let us abide in Thee for ever and ever. 

 The prayer opens by affirming the various great statements concerning God, one might say both those typical of Christianity and those typical of Hinduism. Thus, God is Lord of love, and is characterized by the attributes often associated with Him—omniscience, omnipotence, omnipresence. It also refers to God in terms of the classical vedantic threefold description of what may be said of God the absolute—existence, consciousness, and bliss. Following this initial 

*[p. 78]* ^pg85

description comes a series of petitions concerning the spiritual life of the individual. A non-Hindu would have little difficulty identifying with these requests, which are foundational to all spiritual life. Indeed, the prayer may be called universal inasmuch as its basic view of God and the requests associated with the spiritual life are universal to all, and can therefore be recited by believers of any religion. <sup>58</sup> As the prayer draws to a close we find the striking statement of beholding and serving God in all these names and forms. The point is that the one God absolute is known in all forms and therefore can be approached and worshipped through them. This is precisely where the unique Hindu viewpoint is articulated most forcefully. The conclusion expressing the desire to abide in God forever expresses a universal religious sentiment. Judging by the thematic unfolding of the prayer, one could argue that the means to constantly dwelling in God is precisely through recognizing Him in all things, leading in turn to God’s constant memory and praise. Even if such recognition is not a prerequisite for the desire to permanently abide in God or its fulfillment, it seems that the religious sensibility captured in this prayer provides a bridge between the absolute God, truth, consciousness, and bliss, and the ability to cultivate a permanent relationship with Him, through the recognition of the multiple manifestations of God in all names and forms. Herein lies the unique Hindu perspective. 

 This is but one typical example of a fundamental mode of thinking wherein God is known through myriad manifestations, “all these names and forms.” This approach is so different from the classical Jewish view that it calls into question the very usefulness of our attempt to interpret it through the categories and precedents developed by authors such as Nachmanides and Maimonides, as well as later notions of the worship of intermediaries. <sup>59</sup> On one level, Nachmanides provides us with conceptual tools that make it easier for us to account for alternative religious phenomena. On another level, he does so within a distinct framework that is so different from prevailing modes of Hindu thought as to make us wonder how appropriate the categories are to an appreciation of the Hindu religious mind. 

 Having come this far we may be called to examine an entirely new way of thinking of Avoda Zara. Perhaps the very drive to see God in everything and to identify God with all is itself at great odds with a Jewish view of divinity and what gives rise to Avoda Zara . Perhaps the issue that Jews should be concerned about is indeed not the failure to worship God, substituting instead the worship of other beings, but how pervasive the worship of God has become, so as to lead one astray from Him, into the worship of other beings, thereby cheapening God’s name and causing us to lose sight of it. The question might accordingly be framed whether the intensification of approach to God, by recognizing Him in all things, might not itself be the foundation of Avoda Zara. If this is the theoretical justification for the worship of divinities and their forms, perhaps it is this very conceptual foundation that is at odds with a Jewish view. Perhaps. 

 We come now to a much more subtle point in our discussion. The philosophical differences just spelled out do not only distinguish “Judaism” from “Hinduism,” they distinguish some schools of Judaism from others. One may debate whether a more intensive view of God, such that sees Him and finds Him in everything, is a religiously higher or lower view. If one does espouse such a view, one would be 

*[p. 79]* ^pg86

reluctant to make this very view the basis for defining Avoda Zara in theory. Too many noted Jewish thinkers, especially over the past 200 years, have espoused this view. From the founder of Lubavitch Hassidism, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Lyday, to the twentieth century’s greatest spiritual luminary, the first Chief Rabbi of the Holy Land, Rabbi A.I. Kook, pantheistic and panentheistic theologies have flourished and have become normative in Judaism. <sup>60</sup> Rather than make exclusion of such beliefs the cornerstone of what defines Avoda Zara, we might actually be inspired to find in Hinduism, or several of its philosophical schools, paths that apply and practice a spirituality geared at implementing the very pantheistic or panentheistic worldview, held in common by Jewish and Hindu thinkers. If so, the common worldview would provide the platform against which the differences in ritual and practice would emerge. Accounting for these differences might bring us back to Nachmanides, and his attempt to justify the worship of other beings for non-Jews, or to the notion of Shituf, to be explored shortly, that legitimates worship of other beings within a broader common theological understanding. Either way, these strategies could then be understood from within a common conceptual platform wherein Judaism and Hinduism come remarkably close to one another. 

 There is one final point, already raised in our discussion, to which we must return as we conclude this assessment of Nachmanides’ work and its relevance to a view of Hinduism. The preceding discussion focused on issues of faith, allegiance, and relationship to God, exploring the parameters of allegiance and recognition of God. In all this, we have followed Nachmanides in highlighting the centrality of attachment to God, knowledge of Him, and the formation of a committed faith relation with Him as the primary criterion for defining Avoda Zara. As noted in our discussion above, this criterion excludes another criterion, one that some Jewish observers of Hinduism implicitly appealed to and understandably identified as the immediate and most applicable criterion, namely the worship of forms and images. What is noteworthy in Nachmanides’ presentation is that image worship does not serve as the criterion for defining Avoda Zara. We already saw that the core commandment of the ten commandments, understood by Rashi in terms of images, is understood by Nachmanides in terms of relationship. Thus, even though image worship is understood as an extension of approaching a wrong God by both Maimonides and Nachmanides, for both the image itself does not define Avoda Zara. Image worship for both authors is secondary to the relationship formation. It is therefore subject to whatever theological understanding informs the worship of images. Furthermore, one notes that while Nachmanides focuses on the identity of the god worshipped, his discussion of image worship appears exclusively in relation to other gods. <sup>61</sup> Like Maimonides, Nachmanides never seems to consider the possibility of making an image of God Himself. Image making is associated with the worship of the various levels of Avoda Zara, as these emerged historically, and as these emerge from his typology. Thus, the hypothetical possibility of making an image of God Himself is never considered. As noted already in relation to Maimonides, it is this very possibility that must be included in our reflections, when we consider Hindu religious thought’s self-understanding. When Hindus make an image to 

*[p. 80]* ^pg87

be worshipped, it is not necessarily approached as the image of some being of an inferior position on the metaphysical ladder. Rather, the religious sentiment is that God Himself is being worshipped through that form. The intention is to worship God, through the image, not some other being. This is a possibility not accounted for by the discussions of the giants of the Jewish Middle Ages. 

 The issue of image worship may be of further significance for Nachmanides. Nachmanides’ discussion, as well as that of the Rashba, focuses upon the appropriateness and the possibilities of entering into a relationship of follower-deity with beings other than God. Their focus is upon the recognition and worship of those beings and not upon the concrete forms that such adherence takes. One of the implicit assumptions of their discussion is that the worship of other deities also involves the use of forms and the various accoutrements of foreign worship. <sup>62</sup> These, however, do not emerge as foci of their discussion. It would seem then that once the permissibility of worship of other beings is established, <sup>63</sup> the expression of worship through images is not problematic. <sup>64</sup> So, not only can Avoda Zara not be determined by appeal to the worship of images, according to Nachmanides. It would seem that once we make the distinction between Jews and non-Jews, the sole issue that must be borne in mind in establishing whether non-Jews engage in Avoda Zara is theological. Once the determination has been made on the theological plane, the practical consequences in terms of worship are a natural, and unproblematic, extension of the theological permissibility of the worship of other beings. 

 In important ways, Nachmanides’ construct is similar to another construct that developed in the Middle Ages, and to which I have already alluded, that of Shituf. This construct has furnished discussions of Jewish attitudes to other religions more readily than Nachmanides’ discussion, because it appears in another literary genre—halachic, legal, rulings concerning other religions, primarily Christianity. Because of the context in which it was born, it has defined a large section of Jewish attitudes to Christianity and other religions. Before contrasting the two constructs, let us take a closer look at the Shituf construct.

### 8 Shituf: Applying a Construct of Christianity to Hinduism

 I 

have already spoken of the dual heritage of biblical-rabbinic attitudes compared to attitudes that were later formulated with reference to Christianity. During the formative rabbinic period, when the key concepts defining Avoda Zara were developed, Christianity was not yet one of the other religions, the validity of which Judaism had to assess. <sup>1</sup> The consideration of Christianity in these rabbinic terms was primarily the work of halachic authorities of the Middle Ages. <sup>2</sup> The decision as to whether Christianity should or should not be considered Avoda Zara would have far-reaching ramifications on the daily life of Jews in Christian society. If Christianity would be considered Avoda Zara, the strict application of talmudic law concerning commerce with idolaters would effectively preclude any possibility of commerce, hence of making a livelihood, in a Christian milieu. The definition of Christianity in this context was thus not a purely theological matter, but also a matter of developing strategies for Jewish survival. It is worth considering this fact as we ponder the application of precedents developed in relation to Christianity to Hinduism. The realization that halachic categories and attitudes to other religions do not exist in a vacuum, but interplay with a variety of contemporary considerations, is crucial. Indeed, what is driving the present inquiry is the sense that circumstances of the time require fresh reflection, with regard to both Christianity and Hinduism. 

 The question of the halachic status of Christianity, in terms of Avoda Zara , is twofold. Strictly speaking, what is under discussion is whether a trinitarian understanding of God constitutes the kind of theological error that would lead to considering Christianity as Avoda Zara. <sup>3</sup> In addition, Christian use of images in worship plays significantly into the question, informing both attitudes and positions to Christianity. The outward resemblance of forms of Christian worship and forms of worship typical of biblical and rabbinic idolatry enhances the perception of Christianity as Avoda Zara. <sup>4</sup> Given our earlier discussion, we realize that these are the same forces that play into the view of Hinduism as Avoda Zara. Accordingly, the changing emphases that characterize discussions of Christianity could well be carried over into a consideration of Hinduism. 

*[p. 82]* ^pg89

 There is no accepted and unequivocal position regarding Judaism’s view of Christianity in terms of Avoda Zara. A range of possibilities was formulated throughout Judaism’s rich history of contact with Christianity. This range is reflected in the diversity of halachic approaches to Christianity down to present times. Clearly, the establishment of the state of Israel and the need to determine fundamental issues pertaining to other religions should have been the occasion for a systematic formulation of Judaism’s attitude to other religions, including Christianity. <sup>5</sup> Such a challenge has not been met in more than 65 years of renewed Jewish life in Israel. <sup>6</sup> One notes with regret that rabbinic discourse limits itself on the whole to citation of the authorities of the Middle Ages and to siding with one authority or another. A fresh examination of Christianity and its theology, especially its formulations since formative rabbinic responses to Christianity were articulated, is almost never part of rabbinic discourse. <sup>7</sup> A systematic reexamination of the topic, in light of a range of considerations—historical, sociological and theological—seems overdue. <sup>8</sup> 

 One position with regard to Christianity that continues to impact much of present-day halachic thinking is that of Maimonides, discussed earlier. An alternative position was formed initially as a response to practical needs, arising out of the financial needs of Jews living in Christian countries. Over time it evolved into a principled position with regard to Christianity. The position was articulated in talmudic commentaries known as Tosafot and is associated with the twelfth-century figures of Rabbenu Tam or his nephew R. Isaac. <sup>9</sup> It considers the Christian God in terms of the rabbinic category of Shituf , association. One must not worship another being alongside God. The origins of this concept, though not the fully developed concept itself, are found in a rabbinic prohibition of sharing God’s name with the name of another. The original sense of the prohibition refers to the propriety of speaking of God in the same breath as another being, rather than to the worship of both, let alone incorporating another being into one’s notion of the Divine. <sup>10</sup> However, as used by the Tosafot it is applied in the framework of worship. Here the point is made that the prohibition against Shituf , the worship of another being alongside God, applies only to Jews. Non-Jews are not prohibited from such worship. Therefore, the worship of another being, Jesus, <sup>11</sup> alongside God, does not violate the norms by which non-Jews are expected to act. The upshot of this position is that restrictions that would apply had Christianity been considered Avoda Zara do not apply. 

 By the eighteenth century, the position that non-Jews are not prohibited from worshipping God in association with other beings had become a principled acceptance of Christianity as a valid religion. <sup>12</sup> What started out as a mechanism for solving a specific legal problem involving financial transactions with Christians became a principled recognition of the legitimacy of Christianity. Christianity was religiously legitimate and was not considered as Avoda Zara because Judaism could not fault the Christian understanding of God as valid for Christian believers. The move was a brilliant one. This position recognized the legitimacy of Christianity for non-Jews. For Jews, however, it was considered invalid, as they were prohibited from worshipping another being alongside God. Tolerance of 

*[p. 83]* ^pg90

the other and protection of one’s own from the lure or threat of Christianity were thus achieved through the same theological move. 

 The Shituf position considers that a lesser level of theological stringency and correctness is required of non-Jews. Instead of the black-and-white relations that characterize the Maimonidean position, namely that one either worships God properly or falls into the category of Avoda Zara, this view offers a more graded perspective on how God is understood, approached, and worshipped. The dual perspective of true and false is replaced by a graded approach to truth that accommodates the possibility that not all people will have a perfect understanding of God. One could add, though this is not part of the halachic discourse itself, that such proper understanding remains part of Judaism’s messianic vision, or perhaps part of its ongoing mission. In any event, Jews, who have a special relationship with God and who have received God’s Torah, must worship God with perfect understanding and may therefore not worship another being alongside Him. Non-Jews, however, may worship other beings, provided they also worship the one true God. Accordingly, the details of the given theology of any religion are less significant than the concern that the religion references the true God in some meaningful way through its worship and teaching. As long as the true God is known through a religion, even in part, this partial knowledge proffers legitimacy upon the religion, taking it out of the realm of Avoda Zara. 

 Shituf is the category used by those very few halachic authorities who have attempted to consider the halachic status of Hinduism as Avoda Zara. The earliest application of this strategy of acceptance to Hinduism was made by Israel’s first Chief Rabbi, Rabbi Isaac Herzog. Rabbi Herzog struggled with a broad range of questions relating to how a state would be run according to halachic considerations. One of the issues that he addressed was the status of other religions in the soon-to-be-built state. Most of his attention was devoted to Christianity and to dealing with the problems that might result from the declaration that it is Avoda Zara. Rabbi Herzog makes clear how Christianity is to be understood as a case of Shituf. When Christians worship the trinitarian God, they are, in fact, worshipping a human being who had been elevated through religious imagination to the status of the Divine, alongside the true God, known through Judaism. Seen in this way, God was worshipped alongside another being, a human being. However, for non-Jews such worship is permissible, following the evolution of the halachic tradition of the Middle Ages that permits non-Jews to worship God through Shituf. 

 Toward the end of his discussion, Rabbi Herzog goes beyond Christianity and Islam and poses the question of the status of other religions. While acknowledging his own limited knowledge of Hinduism, he raises the possibility that Hindu worship too can be considered a kind of Shituf. Herzog does not enter into a detailed discussion of the substance of Hindu faith. However, when we review his understanding of Shituf and how he applied it to Christianity, his suggestion seems possible. If Hinduism recognizes a formless God, source of creation, beyond the myriad manifestations of divinity worshipped in a variety of ways, then we may apply to Hinduism the same logic that was applied to Christianity.
