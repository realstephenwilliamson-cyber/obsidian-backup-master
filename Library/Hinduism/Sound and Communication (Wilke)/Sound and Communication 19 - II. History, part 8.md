With his view of the Veda, Śaṁkara actually constructs a new category of Veda text, which one has to call a “universal myth” in a exceptionally strong sense because Śaṁkara’s statements are, on the one hand, always based on the mythical lore of the Veda and consequently are always surrounded by the nimbus of the Vedic, while on the other they also always represent highly rationalist statements of philosophical reflection and are not intended to be understood in a dogmatic way at all. What we term participatory and emancipatory orientations merge here completely, and this is also reflected in Śaṁñkara’s strictly non-dual construction of reality and liberation. 

So the sea-change from the early Pūrva to the Uttara-Mīmāmsā lay in the development of a Veda-based soteriology and the introduction of a category of the non-verbal into hermeneutic study. Characteristically, radical non-duality (Kevala Advaita) is gained in the Mīmāmsā by a radical text view. It is typical of the radical text view which the Uttaras share with the Pūrvas, that the fundamental soteriological work is provided by the original text itself, and even the final goal, the noetic possibility of experiencing what is “free of language,” results directly from the Veda — the language par excellence. The two Mīmāmsā schools reflect in an extreme fashion the search for orientation and the pluralism of orientation during the transition from late Indian antiquity to the early medieval period. We find diverse, non-integratable models of understanding the world and radically contrasting philosophies, which are also typical of the beginning of the twentieth century: positivism, skeptical agnosticism and the (1775८7८ turn are irreconcilably juxtaposed with radical transcendentalism, subjectivistic phenomenology and the consciousness turn.

##### 4.5.1 Linguistic Theory

In the linguistic part of the Vāēkyapadiya (“Treatise on sentence and 

word \[order\]”),”\* Bhartrhari devotes himself to questions of syntax, a topic which was considered marginal and was therefore hardly given any treatment by earlier grammarians. The main concern of early grammar traditions was the material of language: letters, etyms and word formation. The sequence of words in a sentence was considered natural and self-evident. With Bhartrhari we encounter a shift of interest from linguistic atoms to linguistic sequences and structures. But, of course, his starting point was also the ever-returning fundamental question of Indian linguistics: what links sound and meaning? Even the early grammarians were confused in the face of the how little the sounds of a word often have to do with its meaning. The words nadi, “river,” and dīna, “miserable, sad,” are composed of the same letters, but their meanings appear to have no connection whatsoever. It is not true that all rivers are sad, and neither is it true that all sad people are rivers. Creating such relationships all the same, using complicated etymologies, was the specialty of the Brāhmaṇas. The rational grammarians no longer had such means available. They were interested in general theories, not in explanations of individual cases. However, the question remained the same: what do sound and meaning have to do with each other? The various positions of the phonemes in “na-dīI” and “dī-na” obviously throw up extra information. It even seemed as if a large proportion of the actual meaning lay in the sequence of the letters, and not in the letters themselves. Grammar in India produced something

For Kumārila, the meaning of the sentence is determined by the information from the individual words along with the information from their sequence. But Bhartrhari draws attention to the fact that the meaning of the sentence does not suddenly become obvious to a listener after the last word has been heard. Even before the last word is heard, there is some sort of preliminary understanding on the part of the listener - he can estimate more or less what the sentence is about. For this reason Bhartrhari speaks of meaning “forming” or “clarifying” (vyajyate) itself; it does not arise in an additive fashion, but gradually. So, like some one who has heard the numbers 1, 2 and 3 one after the other can suppose that the next number will be 4, the listener develops an idea of the sense of the sentence while he is hearing it, and this idea gradually improves as the sentence unfolds. ##### 4.5.2 Metaphysical Theory

Bhartrhari’s linguistic postulate of the sphota and his unitary sentence view have far-reaching consequences. There is one aspect in Bhartrhari that sounds strikingly modern. In the first part of his grammatical work Vākyapadīya, he presents a linguistic philosophy that implements the linguistic turn even more radically than the Pūrva-Mīmāmsā. The indissoluble interlinking of language and thought, which also connects with contemporary philosophers,” is found in the Vākyapadīya 1.131 expressed in these words: 

“In this world there is no perception of things without the capability for language \[and our\] whole perception seems to be pervaded by language.” ”5 It also seems extremely modern to us that Bhartrhari conceives of human beings as cultural beings and includes a historical dimension in his deliberations. For instance, he remarks that the power of words varies in different periods of time and dependent on the speaker.”® He is very aware of the relativity of human reasoning and different points of view. Even the same person sees the same thing differently at different times. Bhartrhari takes into account the speaker’s intention, but knows that others may understand it in a different way according to their own backgrounds. He acknowledges that there is cultural and historical change, despite his belief in an “uninterrupted tradition.” Even the Veda is not excluded: due to changes in ritual customs the Vedic injunctions can become obsolete.” Most striking is Bhartrhari’s highly perspectivist attitude: large sections of the Vēkyapadiya are devoted to collections of different views and Bhartrhari seems deliberately to abjure final judgments.
