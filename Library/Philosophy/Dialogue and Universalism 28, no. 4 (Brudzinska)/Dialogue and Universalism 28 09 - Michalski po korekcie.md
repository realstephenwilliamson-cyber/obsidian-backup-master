## Michalski po korekcie

*Rafał Michalski* 

ARNOLD GEHLEN’S ANTHROPOLOGICAL THEORY 

OF INSTITUTION 

*ABSTRACT* 

The article reconstructs main assumptions and the theoretical context of Arnold Gehlen’s conception of institution. I argue that this conception is mainly a theory of action. At its centre Gehlen sets not so much specific institutions but rather specific forms of human activity that bring to life the over-individual normative structures. He describes them by means of a series of categories which, in his opinion, have a universal character. We do not find any genealogical analyzes here, but only a constellation of concepts deduced *a priori,* referring to empirical facts. 

Keywords: Arnold Gehlen, philosophical anthropology, institution, action, relief, habituation, typification, unspecific obligation, ideative awareness. 

INTRODUCTORY REMARKS 

Arnold Gehlen is one of the most outstanding co-creator of classical philosophical anthropology and the original theory of institution which transfers anthropological categories into the realm of social order. In opposition to the classical sociological approach, Gehlen rejects the purely functionalist definition of institution as a system of rules regulating social and individual behaviours. In fact, he also perceives the institutional order as the assurance of a society’s survival and safety. Nevertheless, in his view the essence of the order lies in the hidden normative structure of autotelic character, and, eventually, does not serve external purposes. Gehlen’s theory does not describe in detail contemporary institutions, nor their origin and development. He leaves such inquiries to sociologists and historians. An example of the creative but at the same time critical continuation of Gehlen’s theory of institution, directed towards detailed historical analyses, is the work of Schelsky’s and Karl 

*[p. 178]* ^pg180

Rehberg’s.[^1] Gehlen confined himself to presenting general categories which explain how, based on a human-specific action, higher-level normative structures stabilise cooperation and relieve individuals of the burden of undirected drive energy. 

In this paper, I reconstruct basic categories used by Gehlen to explain the anthropological sense of *institution*. First, I sketch main assumptions of his anthropology and analyse the problem of action (social performance) and its function in the process of the emergence of social order. Eventually, I reconstruct main functions he attributes to the aforementioned problems. 

THE NOTION OF INSTITUTION IN THE CONTEXT 

OF THE ANTHROPOLOGICAL PROGRAMME 

Gehlen’s anthropological project can be seen as an attempt to justify and maintain the independence of philosophical discourse on the human being. His critical attitude towards the classical theory of evolution—the idea of linear causal model in which the specificity of *Homo sapiens* is concluded based on comparative studies of isolated morphological and behavioural aspects—should also be understood in this light. Gehlen introduces the asynchronic analysis of features forming humanity, moving the problem of anthropogenesis to the background. This move is consistent with a more general change of the paradigm which took place in social sciences under the influence of functionalism in 20s and 30s of the 20th century.[^2] 

The main assumption of Gehlen’s anthropology is the hypothesis on the special position of man in nature. This hypothesis allows him to consider jointly man’s physical and intellectual activity. Theories presenting the human species as emerging from the animal world cannot explain the phenomenon of the mind without falling into reductionism. Metaphysical theories, on the other side, lose sight of the natural, corporeal aspect of existence. To avoid both these extremes, biological anthropology should examine the structure of conditioning—the system whose elements complement and condition each other, and allow a biologically defective creature to survive. Gehlen describes many human characteristics, among others, ability to learn, the use of symbolic language, conceptual thinking, etc. including the complex sensorimotor basis of these abilities and cultural achievements that correlate with them. In describing these characteristics he refers to the specific human morphology and anatomy. Yet, both the domains of analysis boil down to the notion of action and are subject to the

In regard to *Homo sapiens,* “survival” means the ability to create mechanisms reducing the burden of biological shortages: Man becomes, in Marquard’s words, a compensating being—*Homo compensator.*[^4] 

Into his compensation model Gehlen introduces culture as an obligatory element that forms the basis of the human relief system. The synchronic perspective of the description forces him to admit that the human condition is exceptional; the human being is, by nature, a being condemned to culture. In fact, the notion of the “original state of nature” is non-realizable, since man deprived of cultural alleviation ceases to be human; rather, he becomes an unprepared social animal. Culture fills the gap left by the evolution. However, it does not mean that culture is rooted in human nature. Gehlen opposes the existence of the biological *a priori* of human cultures, and opts for static dualism: the human as a social being *versus* the animal. Based on this problem, his theory revolves around three borderline ideas: of the biologically deficient human (*Mängelwesen*), of the cultural being (*homo compensator*), and of the perfect biological being. The last idea constitutes a secularised version of the garden of Eden where one can find animals living in harmony with the environment. These three ideas function as heuristic rules, not derived from findings of specific sciences. The model view of animal, similar to the concept of *Mängelwesen*, serves as a set of temporal hypotheses which found a theory explaining the specificity of *Homo sapiens.*[^5] 

The outstanding behavioural plasticity, resulting from the lack of a stable driving structure, constitutes one side of the human condition; its other side is its volatility and the need for self-discipline and stabilization. Habits, rituals and institutions create the grammar and syntax of human expressing—they act as locks channelling or suppressing driving forces. Perception and communicative behaviour are based on pre-controlled motor skills obtained in childhood and fallen under social and cultural regulations (understood as institutions). 

The key claim of Gehlen’s conception of institutions is that of “the human as a deficient being.” In analogy to Helmuth Plessner’s and Jürgen Habermas’s views,[^6] the *Mängelwesen* theorem is based on three realms of reference to the world:

1\. In reference to the objective world, human imperfection manifests itself as the lack of instinctive adaptation to the environment; the human is, as Gehlen repeats after Max Scheler, “a world-open being.” 

2\. In relation to the intersubjective world, human imperfection is based on the lack of instinctively regulated social behaviour. The human needs alternative cultural ritualisations to co-exist in peace with other members of society. 

3\. In reference to the internal world, human imperfection manifests itself as the excess of drives that needs a disciplined self-control and auto-creation. 

The “Man as *Mängelwesen*” hypothesis serves to spotlight the preliminary conditions for the human nature which facilitate the emergence of mechanisms relieving biological shortages. The reduction of instincts and excessive drives can be overcome by means of broadly understood actions. The human is forced to actively form his mind and intellect as well as the external reality, because the deficits of the system condemn his existence and deprive him of natural mechanisms of adaptation. Nevertheless, Gehlen did not understand *praxis* in instrumental dimensions only. From the ontogenetic perspective, acting precedes the conscious acting subject. It also precedes the representing of the objective world. The differentiation between the subject and the object constitutes a result of early communicative achievements of an unreflective action. In its initial development stage, the child is neither able to harness its own body to obtain certain aims with it, nor is able to determine its preferences, objectives and means for their realisation. In other words, the child lacks self-consciousness and symbolic representation of their own needs. 

Understanding the specificity of human action requires philo- and ontogenetic deficits, including the not-yet-discussed phenomena of excessive drive. Due to the openness towards the world and the reduction of instincts, human drives generate the energetic potential which enforces on man an activity that exceeds the direct satisfaction of organic needs. Following Thomas Hobbes, Gehlen states that we are characterised by constant orienting towards the future. Man goes beyond here and now to meet the not-yet materialized existential plans—this is the result of having the excess of the insatiable drive energy. To act, the human being has to master the anarchic, elemental stream of drives which are not instinctively oriented. The need for auto-discipline, a long-term formation of inner nature makes the human a co-creator of his own identity and the world he lives in. 

According to the briefest definition, action is a circular process that goes through indirect psychological links, observations, motor reactions, and movement towards the world. When the acting subject meets successes and failures it modifies its approach: it changes movements or explores the emerging phenomena by the trial and error method until it becomes successful. The very acting is a complex, circular movement which is triggered by external phenomena. Action manifests itself at the level of basic sensorimotor processes, constitutes 

*[p. 181]* ^pg183

a language foundation and higher intellectual functions. Even abstract thinking gets its dynamics, the ability to “move” thanks to its form governed by language. 

 Acting defines the intellectual abilities of man. Gehlen does not limit action to planned instrumental behaviour. Human *praxis* primarily shapes needs, but at the same time it changes under the influence of these needs. Action aimed at satisfying needs enables to discover the properties of an object or the autonomy of another person. Hence it breaks the instrumentality of behaviour directed towards the materialization of practical goals facilitating survival. Gehlen writes about the communicative achievements of action, *id est* about the ability to make selfless contact with the environment. The human being can shape his subjectivity due to the fact that being free from the pressure of human drives, he is not limited to a simple *consumption action*. 

Acting at the level of basic motor reactions unburdens the psychophysical from unnecessary energy, eliminates indirect links in the process of the acquisition of new predispositions and behaviour. Human development therefore is based on involution—the simplification and schematization of functional domains. 

“Motor communication” and object manipulation leads to the emergence and enrichment of the repertoire of motor predictions. This enables one to react to the environment and to develop sensory experience. The emergence of sensory imagination (representation) creates a sphere in which differentiation and specialisation of human motor abilities can take place. This leads to the forming in children of such abilities as manipulation. Already in the early stages of this process, functional spheres enrich themselves with elements of other sensory modalities: visual and acoustic stimuli. The eye-hand coordination plays a crucial role in this process. The complex and multidimensional sensorimotor communication with objects gains its manipulative character due to the fact that stimuli reaching various senses are moped to the realm of visual perceptron, and hand movement are monitored by the eye. Optic impressions, however, can be detailed by other stimuli: tactile, olfactory and gustatory. Gehlen defines this process by using the term “communication,” because along the active, explorative perception, a crucial role is assigned to the phatic-receptive moment linked to the central impact of the objects of perception which resonate and release bodily dispositions and movement. 

The heuristic theorem of man as *Mängelwesen* induces Gehlen to broaden the concept of culture to the realm of irrational, embodied predispositions. They create a covert system of well-established experiences and abilities which constitute the framework of the action shaping personality and interpersonal relations. The normative potential being the core of every culture cannot be a result of a purposeful-rational action as the axiologically neutral rationality of means and goals demands. Hence, Gehlen, apart from the instrumental and communicative potential of action, gives it stabilizing and normative function as well. 

*[p. 182]* ^pg184

He states that culture is not only an “artificial nature”—the result of rational, goal-oriented human activity, but most importantly it is a “cultivated nature.”[^7] Amongst the irrational guarantees of experience, he indicates items of ethical self-evidence which manifest themselves as imperative rules that need no empirical legitimisation. Everyday compromises, opinions or valuations regulate social interactions. Habits and routine actions constitute the most important stand against fears of hazard that life brings. They create the basis for the elementary feeling of trust regulating proper social functioning. 

Man directs flexible and overwhelming drives by means of long-term and strenuous self-discipline based on imitation and imagination.<sup>8</sup> Cultural patterns comprised of unwritten norms that regulate social interactions, tactfulness, gestures of reconcilliation and exclusion become an indispensable part of human’s existence. Similarly, implicit value systems that facilitate decision-making of existential importance, not fully articulated ideas that reinforce human motivations or facilitate the discontinuation of socially harmful activities grow into the person’s social body. Culture is based on the strict regime of disciplining the body. In order to be competent participants of social life we need to be able to control our expression and behavior. The routine body control is necessary for maintaining the protective cocoon in everyday social interactions. 

Institution as an implicit normative order, a set of the so-called “leadership ideas,” controls human corporeality, its habits, routines and the course of action and cooperation. Dominant ideas become alive; they gain the causative power when individuals literally embody them in their behaviors, gestures and facial expressions. The cultured consciousness that brings to life regulatory ideas emerges, according to Gehlen, from the deep layers of collective imagination. Its subject is a performance with strong emotional charge; its proper meaning of is not immediately available. Phantasmagoric images exert an overwhelming influence on consciousness, leaving in it an irresistible feeling of an indefinite commitment which “is anchored in a confusing diversity of highly concrete, plastic, and thoroughly fantastic interpretations that form a system which, in turn, constitutes the skeleton that supports a particular culture.”[^9] Primary religious beliefs thus response to the “indefinite commitment” flowing from the vegetative depths, which through the language of archetypical symbols demand intensification of an individual’s and community’s vital forces. The original imagination idealizes the world, and by ignoring the requirements of instrumental awareness creates the fictitious interpretations of the world, which break the narrow horizon of self-preservation and this way contribute to the development of culture. “For this reason, the interests of powerlessness, the expression of this inexhaustible excess of driving force, actually serve life; man’s imagination is

Gehlen illustrates this process by the example of the institution of totemism. The origins of totemism cannot be explained by use of psychological, functional or instrumental categories. Awareness oriented towards achieving particular goals by use of optimal means would not promote the creation of totemism which originally did not serve any specific individual or social interests. The ultimate goals non-directly related to experience can be created by the ideative consciousness competing with the instrumental consciousness. The latter one originally was “directed primarily towards the external world and was to a small extent a reflective awareness of itself.”[^11] Norms, therefore, are not effects of a conscious establishment, a convention, but arise as a surplus, which, to an extent, controls human behavior. In this case, the dominant idea is embodied in the institution itself, e.g. totemism. It is not the subject of reflection which should be legitimized in any way. Due to the prohibition, the person is relieved of the difficulty of constantly choosing between alternative options; his motivations gain stability, and cooperation with others loses antagonistic character. The ideative consciousness is always embodied in a specific action that is exclusive by nature, because it grows out of the active inhibition of other possibilities. This action of breaking the instrumental self-preserving attitude creates the foundation for secondary purposeful practices. Ascetic duties enable the later development of key social regulations, such as norms establishing relations between kinship and gender, rules of exogamy, rules of property inheritance, etc. Over time, ritualistic behavior acquires a symbolic meaning, engages the processes of bodily identification, and at a later stage of social development is partly replaced by a mythological narrative. 

The dominant idea, even in such a primitive form, plays a key role in the life of the whole community and generates unintentional effects. In the case of taboos, the secondary result consists in mastering the cultivation of plants, breeding animals or overcoming anthropophagy.[^12] The protective power of the prohibition regulates activities, stabilizes motivations and sets non-instrumental goals. This kind of secondary objective can be materialised only through an institution.[^13] The dominant systems emerging in the process of civilization gain their own dynamics and are freed from the needs that have brought them to life. 

Only in the implicit way of sublimation man can learn to control himself, to create a distance towards himself. For Gehlen, alienation is the requirement of freedom. Freedom in the natural state is fictitious: “If it is said that serving institutions means alienation, it is a right view, but this alienation is no different

Human action is thoroughly social; it requires rules coordinating long chains of activities. Psychological processes that are inherently uncoordinated and subjected to the pressure of contradictory driving forces also need an external yoke. Only within the framework of common practices, of the system of everyday evidences and unintentional decisions the individual can develop creative potential, discover his own uniqueness. The principles of conduct sanctioned by the community allow to maintain tolerance in interrelations with others, to achieve common life goals. 

ASSUMPTIONS OF THE THEORY OF INSTITUTIONS IN THE *URMENSCH*. 

RELIGION. NON-INSTRUMENTAL ACTION 

In the *Urmensch*, Gehlen asks about the genesis and basic psychosocial functions of the institution. One of *Urmensch’s* most important assumptions is the belief that institutions can be properly described only on the basis of the categorical analysis of the various forms of human *praxis*. The category of ideative action, barely outlined at the end of the *Man, His Nature and Place in the World*, is later renamed into the notion of a “ritually depicting” action. Gehlen’s use of new categories stems from the change of the objective of his research. The *Urmensch und Spätkultur* is a consistent continuation of his earlier anthropological project. The new concepts and issues emerging in this work do not result from a revision of previous findings, but from putting emphasis on new problems. Just like in *Man, His Nature and Place in the World* Gehlen postulates the development of philosophical categories that are objective and critical and cannot be reduced to more general concepts. His postulate brings to mind Aristotle's theory of categories regarded as the most general predicates by means of which one can speak of an entity as such, whereas Gehlen replaces this “entity” with the notion of institution. The anthropologist tries to weaken this metaphysical feature of the proposed concepts, and gives up the attempt to present an exhaustive catalogue of categories. Critical, that is independent of subjective assumptions, and objective, that is, referring to the sphere of collective phenomena, and general, that is, independent of the historical context and applicable to all the known cultures, concepts should be derived from a concrete empirical reality. Their incompleteness opens the perspective for future anthro-

pological investigations based on the findings of specific sciences such as ethnology, cultural anthropology, sociology and religious studies. Another common feature of the categories is their psycho-physical neutrality: they should describe all the layers of human existence, going beyond the Cartesian dualism. 

Gehlen begins his categorial analysis with the description of emergence of two fundamental assumptions that are not, as he points out, hypotheses, but the result of empirical findings: the first comes from the *Man, His Nature and Place in the World—*man is an acting being, and therefore, he can most accurately be characterized in the specific for him perspective of *praxis*. The second assumption states that man, due to his systemic biological deficits, relies on institutions that create the framework necessary for man’s survival and development. 

One should pay attention to the peculiarity of Gehlen’s theory which is usually overlooked. Although he understands the idea of institution very broadly, the majority of detailed analyses is devoted to religious institutions. Thus, it can be assumed, as F. Ley does, that the *Urmenschund Spätkultur* contains an anthropological conception of the genesis of religion.[^16] In fact, in opposition to contemporary religiosity and post-Durkheimian sociology, Gehlen does not treat religion as a cultural subsystem, but as a fundamental form of human life in community. He also claims that from religion other cultural systems emerge such as: prohibitions, taboos, rituals, myths, exchange systems, institutions hierarchizing communities etc. In his opinion, religion is a specific, original reality within which it is possible to describe the fundamental and universal dimensions of human existence. 

Already in the *Der Mensch*, he emphasizes that the most original management system that enables the survival of the individual open to the world and deprived of instincts is granted by religion that fulfills three functions fundamental for human culture. First, religious institutions provide a holistic image of reality and the existential advice that help people recognize their position and role in the social world and the cosmic universe. Also, the community subordinated to religious practices becomes stabilized, pacified and structured from the inside. Secondly, religion organizes human activities, facilitates orienting and regulating drives, and above all creates norms that shape interpersonal relations. Thirdly, religion provides symbolic tools to overcome the contingency inscribed into the human condition.[^17] Random, unpredictable events such as death, suffering, disease, birth or fear of the unknown are tamed within the religious framework. Religion gives man comfort and hope for a better life. 

Gehlen most probably refers here, though not explicitly, to Sigmund Freud’s theory of religion, subjecting it to modification. Similarly to the author of

*Urmensch und Spätkultur*, Freud mentions, however in a different order, the functions of religion, which boil down to: the interpretation of the world, the conquest of the contingency and the practical normalization of human actions. Following Freud, Gehlen repeatedly emphasizes the culture-forming function of religion, but differs in assessing its role in contemporary society. Freud takes a pessimistic and critical position; for him religion, a form of phantasmatic depression of reality, has no chance to keep its position in the rationalized world of modern man. Gehlen, on the other hand, emphasizes the fact that religion is rooted in the human need to deal with the randomness of existence, which can be satisfied neither by scientific worldview nor art. Religiosity is not just an unripe compensation of suppressed drive forces, from which a mentally mature individual should be released, but it still holds a key function as a means of taming the contingency of human life. Displacement of traditional religious institutions deprives the modern man of a stable axiological compass and leaves him alone in extreme situations. Gehlen admits that religion has now lost its interpretive function (the explanation of the cosmos) and practical (organising human actions). The culture-forming power of religious norms, rituals and taboos undergoes inevitable atrophy which is replaced with science and religiously neutral social regulations. The significance of religion remains key for the individual experience of the randomness of fate. In the reality devoid of the metaphysical sense, the disenchanted reality of late capitalism, only religion can help the individual come to terms with the negative effects of events independent of his will. However, the process of unburdening does not take place, in this case, on the social plain, but it takes place at the level of particular personal existence. This phenomenon is a symptom of subjectivisation and moralisation of the secular ethos, characteristic of modernity. The choice of the religious worldview increasingly becomes the matter of an individual decision, outside framework of the institution. 

Gehlen reconstructs the genesis of religion; he opposes the functionalist approaches typical of contemporary sociology and ethnology, which described this genesis as part of a conceptual means. 

Most interpreters share the opinion that the *Urmensch* contains an extensive presentation of the concept of institution, but a more careful reading shows that Gehlen does not give there a systematic concept of the institution as such, but focuses on the whole complex of stabilising moments that are encapsulated in non-instrumental actions. So he primarily attempts to create the theory of *praxis* that allows to capture the specificity of institutional social structures. The oldest forms of religious rituals and normative systems related to them derive from a particular type of behavior. 

Even the elementary use of primitive tools cannot be described, according Gehlen, within the framework of the means and purpose. The action can become systematic and open to new possibilities of using things only when the direct purpose of survival resulting from a current need is suspended. Gehlen 

*[p. 187]* ^pg189

describes the earliest non-instrumental actions in analogy to communication activities from the *Man, His Nature and Place in the World* which characterized the ontogenetically early phase of the development of human activity. Acting in these primitive forms is not, used for the purpose of satisfying drives, as Scheler claimed, but is experimental in nature. 

An instrumental action aimed at transforming the external reality cannot be adequately described within the static model “need–goal setting–choice of means.” The manipulation of things does not come from rigidly defined goals, because motivations is rooted in the type of behaviours that reflect the reach and spectrum of possible variants of a given action determined by reactions of encountered objects. The experimental manipulation is not guided by a predetermined utilitarian purpose, but rather discovers “purposefulness without the aim” in the objective world—a new spectrum of previously unpredicted uses of things. “Action is no longer the action that uses means for the realization of drive goals, but begins and ends on the plane of states of affairs.”<sup>18</sup> In this way, action is relieved of subjective purpose, although *ex post* the resource of possible goals significantly increases. Gehlen discusses the utilitarian view of the means-purpose scheme. According to it, action is judged only in terms of appropriateness and usefulness towards the desired effect. The same standards of means and purpose apply to the product itself. Although it is a goal with regard to the means by which it was created, it never becomes a purpose in itself, at least as long as it remains a subject intended for use. *Regressus ad infinitum* is connected with the fact that the relation between the means and the purpose resembles the chain in which each goal can serve as a means in a different context. In this way, utilitarianism can be caught in an endless chain of purposes and means, never reaching the principle that could justify its usefulness. Therefore, it cannot derive or justify a system of autonomous values that already fund the oldest institutional forms. 

The purposeful-rational consciousness cannot set ultimate goals, which is evidenced by the fact that at the most elementary level of contact with things, manipulative behavior is not motivated by a prior calculation of goals, but is an autotelic activity that is already visible in the child’s early communicative behavior. In the autotelic instrumental action, as in the communication operation, there is suspension, direct virtualization of the need and increased sensory sensitivity to the objective features of the surrounding world. Experimental action, relieved of subjective purposefulness, is free from the pressure of impulsive drives and external situation, revealing the “secondary objective expediency,” which would not be done by any purposeful-rational action based on the scheme of measure and purpose.

THE CATEGORIAL ANALYSIS OF INSTITUTION 

The anthropological categories proposed by Gehlen in the *Urmensch* refer to specific aspects of actions that constitute and stabilise institutions. Gehlen also explains how different types of practices call into existence the objective properties of social order correlated with them (standards, values, symbolic objects, etc.). The first category he takes into account is production by means of tools. Although this kind of action aims at transforming the outside world, one can indicate non-instrumental moments comprised in it: the virtualization of the goal (an action becomes subject to self-satisfaction), exceeding the utilitarian scheme of the means and the purpose. Throughout production, a drive is transferred onto the object which begins to function as an independent entity that sets the direction of action. “Separating the motif from the purpose” is another general category describing how the emergence and autonomisation of secondary objective purposefulness arise—the goal of the action that does not result from direct human needs. In this context, the state of “background-filling” (*Hinter-grundserfüllung*) plays a key role. When the community is organized in such a way that all individuals are certain to satisfy their basic needs these needs lose their urgent character and move into the background of daily activities. The category of “background-filling” is complemented by the concept of storing which indicates the fact that man, unlike animals, keeps not only food supplies, but also tools, productive items, weapons, fire and all kind of newly acquired skills that become independent from the context and can be used in different situations. 

Habitualisation is of key importance for the establishment of institution. This category includes all activities based on repetition and habit. The typification of social behaviour which is subject to the indisputable norms tested in everyday life is the effect of these repetitions. Habitualization creates a “behavioral framework” of the institution and the basis for innovative activities. In order to understand the key importance of routine activity for culture forming we should pay attention—Gehlen claims—to the need of formalizing behavior.<sup>19</sup> This enforcement of stereotyping behavior results from a deeper need to reduce the fear of contingency and the complexity of the world. It also applies to the internal world of thoughts and feelings that are transmitted according to the set up forms. This regulative function is fulfilled by language; it systematises thoughts, forms metaphors, and gives direction to mental processes: “The constancy of ideas and institutions are mutually conditioned.”<sup>20</sup> 

Every institution has a resource of knowledge on proper behaviour. Such knowledge ensures the motivating dynamics of institutionalized behavior. It defines its domains and indicates all situations that belong to the category. It

defines and creates roles that are to be played in the context of the institution’s data. *Ipso facto*, it controls and foresees all such behaviour. Since this knowledge is socially objectified as a resource of general truths about reality, any radical deviation from the institutional order appears to be a departure from reality. Such a deviation can be referred to as moral depravity, mental illness or simply ignorance. In this way, a particular social world becomes the world *tout court*. What is commonly socially accepted as knowledge is equal to the scope of the cognisable. This knowledge the person acquires throughout socialisation. This knowledge somehow “programs” means through which externalisation creates the image of the objective world. With the help of language and the cognitive apparatus based on it it objectivises this world, that is, organizes it by establishing such objects that can be understood as reality. It is re-internalised during socialisation as an objectively valid truth. 

Another important category, i.e., of reciprocity explains the nature of social relations. It is based on the principle of exchange and interpersonal communication, with its purpose of reaching an agreement. In Gehlen's opinion, reciprocity has biological roots: it originates as a result of key stimuli that trigger an “unspecified obligation.” The whole range of behaviours, attitudes and manifestations of the *alter ego* evokes in an individual impulse to reciprocate them. The institutionalization of collective life begins with the emergence of imitative rituals, which at some community phase are transposed onto the plane of representation—a figurative or a linguistic one. Then, norms regulating action become permanent and autonomous. In archaic cultures, representation takes the role of the externalisation of needs. The needs, externalised in the forms of symbolic representations, form a conventional but impassable boundary for human *praxis*. 

According to Gehlen, the imperative of reciprocity lays at the basis of the earliest moral standards. In highly developed societies, the formalized exchange principle has also become the basis for individual claims for equality (equality before the law, opportunities, initial social status, etc.). It is thus the basic category that enables to understand the genesis of the institutional order in the modern world as well. 

Due to the inborn biological deficits, man always relies on the help and support of others. His “gregarious” nature expressed in the need for participation in community would not be possible without assuming the principle of reciprocity. In fact, this need is heard only when the individual is deprived of the opportunity to fulfill it, and therefore in the state of isolation, exclusion from community. When the institutional order functions properly, no vacuum can appear in which the individual would be troubled with loneliness and alienation. The social instinct is then materialized, as well as most of human needs, “in the background,” that is, as a side effect, and not as a goal to which one should strive directly. 

“Stabilized tension” is the last category characterising the anthropological structure of the institution. Gehlen describes the behavior created on the basis of 

*[p. 190]* ^pg192

ritualization which balances the contradictory affects. The connection of competing drive impulses constitutes a new kind of behavior which gradually releases itself from the original ambivalent motivation and begins to serve objective social goals. Gehlen gives an example of a stereotypical reaction to the stabized form of a tribe chief when intertwined feelings of fear, aggression and subordination create a new pattern of behavior in the individual’s mind. On the one hand, it contains instincts, on the other—a state of increased sensitivity to the features of the object (in a broad sense including people and animals). The action derived from the state of stabilized tension opens up to new rational motifs that flow from the material plane. The impulse leading to the stabilization of affect comes from an external object with a symbolic meaning sanctioned by institutional regulations. The place occupied by an individual in a given social hierarchy defines boundaries that cannot be crossed in his behaviour towards the leader. This generates the spontaneous emergence of groups of people who stay closer or further from the center of power. 

The discussed category explains a number of reciprocal behaviours that embrace the hierarchical structure of society. The *tension stabilisée* mechanism underlies many social relations also in the modern world; it is based on the principles of social and diplomatic conduct, relations between the supervisor and the employer, behaviour towards the dead, towards the government, etc. It integrates various needs, drive impulses, instinctive residences, and at the same time creates the basis for activities that over time can completely free themselves from the original motivation. The majority of the ritualized behaviours is still cultivated, although no tensions are hidden in them anymore. The described effect of the release from the original motivation also explains how the institution becomes autonomous. The stabilized tension subordinated to institutional norms loses its original irrational character and transforms into a rational form of action. Relieve of the pressure of conflicting impulses transforms the affective state into neutral motor coordination that serves objective, social purposes. 

RECAPITULATION 

All the normative moments of *praxis* mentioned in the previous section refer to modernity. Even in the case of the enterprise focused solely on the optimization of performance, new needs and situations arise spontaneously and cannot be predicted within the utilitarian calculation of profits and losses. The primarily dominant need of the workers who want to ensure their well-being gradually moves to the background and becomes an obvious assumption. The organization acquires independence which defines its system of values and attitudes related to the ethos of work and interpersonal relationships such as loyalty, respect, reliability, trust, security, sense of community, etc. As the business 

*[p. 191]* ^pg193

develops, profitability is replaced by various social benefits, non-instrumental goals, new motivations and needs.<sup>21</sup> 

For Gehlen, the institution in the most general sense is a coherent system of partially covert behavioural patterns and indisputable norms that relieve individuals of an excessive number of decisions, direct the drive energy and solidify an objective, lasting order. A man subjected to an objective order acquires habitual confidence of his orientations, and thus his behaviour becomes unreflective and schematic. However, it is thanks to this that he acquires permanence and can act similarly in various situations. In this sense, Gehlen understands institutionally regulated behaviour (feeling, thinking, valuing) as restoring the animal certainty of instinct at a higher level. Contemporary, well-functioning institutions create a chance for the development of a real personality based on individual experience and rational creativity. Moreover, they neutralize troublesome situations and subjective quarrels, thus enabling uninterrupted communication between people. A clearly defined horizon of collective actions allows us to undertake and implement common life goals, and at the same time it helps people make important decisions, bringing additional profit in the form of stabilization of internal life. While in archaic institutions, stabilization concerned mainly the system of instincts and affects, in contemporary ones we can see the integration of more complex personality structures. The disciplinary mechanism, however, is similar in both the cases. 

The key role here is played by the moment of non-conditioning, the autonomy of the institution obtained through the symbolization of the basic values of life. Immortalized in the form of symbols and beliefs, and at the same time detached from the everyday context of values such as security, certainty of satisfying basic needs, motherhood, peaceful co-operation, etc., they become “intrinsic existential values” that protect people from the randomness of fate and the dangers of the outside world. In this case one cannot speak of the rational functions of institutions, because the leading concepts embodied in them, that are not subject to discussion, are of a key role. Following M. Hariou and C. Schmitt, Gehlen repeats that the durability and efficiency of institutions are determined not so much by rational decisions but by the power of implicit and indisputable normative structures. Ideas can shape institutions only as long as they are anchored in specific actions and supported externally (by externalised structures of power). *Idée directrice* defines social practice as a regulative principle which allows for a limited number of acceptable behaviours to be selected and favored from a multitude of possible actions, thus creating an institutional basis for cultural progress. Te stability of principles paradoxically guarantees the effectiveness of innovative changes within the institution. Contrary to functionalism, Gehlen assumes that human institutions do not serve to satisfy human needs, but they constitute a plane of action on which man 

<sup>21</sup> Ibid., 39. 

*[p. 192]* ^pg194

relieves himself of their pressure and discovers a selection of higher-order needs.<sup>22</sup> 

In Gehlen’s anti-functionalist and thoroughly conservative theory we distinguish two complementary notions of institution. In a broader sense, derived from Aristotle’s philosophy, institution encompasses all norms, the patterns of action based on habitualization. In the scheme of transcendence, it would encompass the first two degrees (the sphere of objects of perception and objects of everyday use), being close to the third degree of “self-existent value.” In a narrower sense, the institution is identical with the ideal structure of rules spread over the heads of social actors. Gehlen developed a broader approach in the first part of *Urmensch*, while the narrowest approach is presented in the second part. These levels of social order constitute a whole within which individuals find both material and moral support, a sense of connection and security as well as space for individual development. Leading ideas gain efficiency, that is, the ability to regulate needs and behaviours subordinated to them, only on the basis of an already-existing formalised system of independent behavioural patterns. On the other hand, this formal stabilising basis of the social habitus functions properly only when there is a supreme, regulative structure of ideative norms. 

“Great thoughts can penetrate the supra-individual state of self-acting only as the content of the institution. This is clearly visible in religion, for which the institutions are not something external. Without a church, religion would have undergone ‘idealization,’ that is, it would be subjectivistically processed, falling down to the level of an isolated experience, as it is in contemporary art.”<sup>23</sup> According to Gehlen, institutions are significant because they as autonomous and reliable instances of the social whole stabilize the existence of community as well as individuals. Gehlen exaggerates the conflict between the declining institutional order and contemporary-promoted individualism. Archaic culture functions as a contrasting pattern of a “healthy institutionalization.” Gehlen is not interested in idealizing the “golden time” of the origins of humanity, because he states repeatedly that the present and the archaic world are separated by an impassable gap. In fact, specific cultural and psychological matters that form a sort of “core” of ancient cultures are now completely unavailable. However, analysis can reproduce some general, purely formal properties of the original institutions. The anthropological categories reconstructed in the article should form, according to Gehlen’s plan, a kind of “material *a priori*” (in the Schelerian meaning) that organizes social life independently of all historical and psycho-cultural changes that these categories are always subject to.
