We are concerned here with intended reference as a phenomenological structure of his act, experience or belief, rather than as a semantic relation between the expression he uses and whatever in the world truly satisfies the description made by using those expressions. 

The point then is that an intention whose sense is articulated in a definite description does individuate its object for the speaker, provided the sense also contains a perceptual component such as "The person over there ... " We thus come back to the phenomenological idea of "perceptual sense." If the idea of fulfilment was too wide to be of use in accounting for *de re* intentionality, the idea of perceptual sense may exactly be able to do so. 

It is first of all important for our purpose to make sure that the Husserlian noema is not the same as the Fregean *Sinn.* It is the failure to distinguish between the two which has led to the suspicion that the Husserlian intentionality, mediated as it is by a noematic *Sinn,* must be incurably *de dicto.* However, according to T 3 , the noema may belong to non-linguistic acts as well as to linguistic ones, whereas the Fregean *Sinn* is the sense of signs, and - leaving aside the case of indexicals where Frege's theory jars with his general theory of sense - is conceptual. In our present context, what directly concerns us is the idea of perceptual noema or the sense of a perceptual act. 

Husserl held the general thesis that the core of the noema of any intentional act is analysable into a set of component predicate senses F, G, H ... , and an X which is the "bearer" of those predicates. This means that in any intentional act, an object - its object - is known as that which is characterised by the predicates F, G, H, ... We thereby get a descriptive content "the X which is F, G, H, .... " In the case of perceptual noema, the expression "X" serves as an indexical, it plays the role of "this-here-now." The noematic structure of a perceptual act thereby fully individuates its object as a this-here-now that is F, G, H, ... , making it possible for the intentionality to be *de reo* Now there are two things about this account which are likely to be missed, but both are important for our purpose. In the first place, a mere "this" or even a mere "this-here-now" does not individuate, for it refers to a total slice of the spatiotemporal environment or a world-slice, but does not carve out of it a definite piece. The descriptive content signified by the predicate senses is needed to do that. This is the truth in the Kantian reading of C - namely, that botha predicative component and an indexical, demonstrative component are needed to complete a definite *de re* perceptual intentionality. In the second place, the role of the indexical

*[p. 53]* ^pg82

as a component of the noematic *Sinn* should be precisely understood. We are concerned with how the object is individuated for the perceiver in that perceptual act. If that is so, then we have to insist on the following: it has been rightly pointed out by many philosophers that an indexical takes you from the context to the object. One should know who is saying "this," but where and when as well. Now that is undoubtedly true, but that thesis holds good from the point of view of the audience. The context which the audience considers is the objective context: objective spatial and temporal locations of the speaker and the utterance, for example. But in so far as the perceiver himself is concerned, the "this" or its demonstrative equivalent takes him to an uniquely individuated object *without* the mediation of determination of the objective context. We have then to say that the perceptual noema contains within itself elements which individuate the object *for* the perceiver. These elements are not the predicate senses F, G, H, ... but have to be context-indicators. For example, although the noema itself is an irreal structure, it may contain within its structure a *temporal value* to be attached to the object as "now" or as "past." The temporal horizon of the act will be represented within the noema, and so the object will be intended as having a certain temporal predicate such as "now." Roderick Chisholm has recently developed a theory according to which an object becomes my intentional object through my ascription of a certain property directly to myself. The point that I am trying to make is somewhat like Chisholm's: a perceptual act, by virtue of its noematic structure, "ascribes" to itself the property of having an object *as a here and now,* which makes it possible for its object to be individuated *for the act.* Central to this theory is the distinction between the idea of objective context which is necessary for a semantics of indexicals, and the idea of phenomenological context which is necessary for a theory of perceptual reference. The latter is gotten by enriching the available notion of perceptual noema by including within it temporal and spatial i.e. horizontal values which are correlates of the act's horizontal features. 

In his unpublished commentary on John Searle's and my papers at the APA, Eastern Division Symposium on Intentionality in December, 1981, Richard Aquila suggested the point that in such an act the *de re* feature derived from the unthematised horizon or background and that when this background was "representationally thematised" it became *de dicto.* But recognition of such an unthematised background as making *de re* intentionality possible leads, according to Aquila, beyond noematic phenomenology to an ontology of the Heideggerian sort. I differ from him in two points: first, the noema need not have to be a dictum, intentionality through a noema may as well be *de reo* Secondly, 

*[p. 54]* ^pg83

recognition of the role of context in determining *de re* intentionality is compatible with a Husserlian noematic phenomenology in the manner stated above. I agree with Aquila that as so functioning, the noema is not itself thematised. 

One might take a different route by saying that what makes perceptual acts *de re* is the presence of the hyletic component in the act. Now this would indeed be a different strategy from the one adopted by me here if the concept of *hyle* is taken to imply a causal theory of perception. In that case, the *hyle* would be those components of my experience which are causally produced by the object being perceived. This, of course, would make it possible for the act to be *de re* by being causally linked to an object out there. Quite apart from the question whether a causal theory of perception is or is not viable, we cannot adopt it simply because of the way the philosophical problem is formulated here, which makes the notion of "being about" logically independent of "being caused by." 

But there is another way the concept of *hyle* may be made to bear positively on the issue at hand. The hyletic component may be taken as accounting for what John Searle has called the causally self-referential character of perception. The intentional content of perception is adequate to individuate its object because this content includes a causal component, namely that the object out there is causing my perception. Now the large philosophical question about the relation between intentionality and causality cannot be taken up here. But this much must be recognised: it is *not* causality as a *real* relation between two real entities, but the *felt or experienced* causality that belongs to the phenomenologically reduced act. The object is experienced *as* acting upon me. Thus in the structure of the intentional content or noema of an act of perception there is this causal component. The *sense* of perceptual "this" cannot be fully explicated without taking this into account. 

IV 

In the foregoing sections, I have tried to give an account of the *de re* *- de dicto* distinction in terms of the Husserlian theory of intentionality. This account has obvious limitations, limitations which suggest that there is something wrong and arbitrary in that very distinction itself. In this concluding section I will indicate why from a strictly phenomenological point of view the very distinction between *de re* and *de dicto* needs to be cast in doubt. 

Already the above account of the distinction, provisionally held in 

*[p. 55]* ^pg84

view, applies only to those clear cases of *de re* intentionality which are cases of perceptual reference to the object being perceived. The account contains no theory of *de re* intentionalities which are not perceptual, of intentionalities which though empty, are yet about a *res.* As a matter of fact, the distinction concerns not the forms of the sentences expressing intentional acts, but rather the structure of those acts themselves. The same sentence, as is well-known, may have either a *de re* or a *de dicta* reading. One cannot therefore simply from the forms of sentences concerned read off whether they express the one or the other. This makes a general theory so difficult and the search for one look fruitless. To say that *de re* cases are relational, are about a definite *res* or permit exportation, and the *de dicta* cases are notional, are about a proposition or a sentence, and do not permit exportation, is not to give a theory, but simply to lay down what it is that the theory is supposed to give an account of. One linguistic feature that stands out appears to deserve recognition in any such theory, it is the distinction between proper names and indexicals on the one hand and descriptions on the other. While it seems to be true that indexicals express *de re* intentions and, combined with descriptions, succeed in individuating their objects, it is not at once clear that in all cases the presence of ordinary proper names serves to signify the same. It is at least a matter of controversy whether "Pegasus is a winged horse" expresses *de re* belief on the part of any one who asserts it, or whether the alleged entity Pegasus is not defined by the truth of certain sentences in the context of a certain discourse. The same is also true of sentences about Sherlock Holmes who, it may reasonably be claimed, is defined or constituted by exactly all those true sentences about him which occur in the famed detective stories. In so far as Pegasus and Sherlock Holmes and such entities are constituted exactly by the true sentences of appropriate discourses, they are far from being the definite individuals that we have wanted the putative *res* to be: they contain, as Roman Ingarden put it, "spots of indeterminacy." 10 

This leads us to the deeper reason why the distinction between *res* and *dicta,* as it figures in the literature, is unsatisfactory. In fact, there is, in the discussion, an unexamined ontological presupposition underlying the stipulation that for an act to be *de re* its object must be an individual concrete thing, person or event. The ontological presupposition is that the world can be neatly divided into *res* and *dicta.* Attempts have been made to have theories of *de re* attitudes towards numbers and towards

How about the other component of the received theories? This is the thesis that *de re* acts relate to their objects, those things themselves, independent of any conceptual mediation, mode of description, or theory. We have thus a contrast between context-dependence and concept-dependence, between context and concept. Perception stands at one extreme, because although perceptual sense includes descriptive predicate-senses and so concepts, it also contains a purely non-conceptual but context-dependent sense articulated in the demonstratives "this" and "I." Beyond these, our contexts are mingled with, often determined by, concepts and theories, and we tend to loose our grip on the possibility of pure *de re* achievement.

RORTY, PHENOMENOLOGY AND TRANSCENDENTAL 

PHILOSOPHY\* 

I 

Richard Rorty's book! bridges the gap, much talked about in the English-speaking philosophical world, between" Analytic philosophy" and "Continental philosophy" by showing, first, that they both inherited from Descartes, Locke and Kant, the same themes, concerns and ambitions and, second, that they both are on the verge of meeting the same fate. In fact, he does something more: he subsumes all, or most, recent analytical philosophical concerns under the rubric "Transcendental philosophy." If some philosophers - or, better, some of their arguments - do not fall under it, then it is only because they are directed against it. Amongst "continental philosophers," of course, a continuing concern with transcendental philosophy was almost a sufficient reason for regarding them as bad philosophers. If Rorty is right, then Russell, Quine, and a host of other minor luminaries come under the same genre; not unlike Heidegger and Derrida, Sellars, Quine and Davidson also fight transcendental philosophy, some from within, some from without. For having shown, with impressive historical scholarship, that this common concern runs through modern philosophy Rorty deserves congratulations. The further claim that they - the analytical as well as continental philosophies, in fact philosophy as a scientific enterprise, are destined for oblivion is a prophecy about whose value, though, there is considerable room for controversy. In my view, he is just wrong. Possibly, I have not got his prophecy entirely correct.

In this review, I will single out some themes which the readers of this journal are most likely to be interested in. First, a remark about Rorty's strategy. Much of the book consists in an impressive account of the central issues - such as the mind-body problem, the evidence-theory problem, the meaning-reference problem - of contemporary analytic philosophy. We are taken along the main highways, but also sometimes along the more scenic lanes and by-lanes. For those not familiar with the intense debates that have taken place or continue to take place around such issues - as those concerning "raw feels" and "convention T" - Rorty provides a most fascinating review, always bringing out the larger philosophical significance of even the minor skirmishes being fought out. Now, there is a peculiar "provincialness" about some of these discussions that is initially disconcerting for "continental" philosophers: words like "epistemology," "metaphysics" and "philosophy of mind" are pre-empted for pretty narrow, recently born and inbred concerns. \[One colleague of mine said - to illustrate what I mean - that Sartre's essays on imagination and emotion or Scheler's on sympathy do not come under "philosophy of mind," by definition.\] To be fair to Rorty, he is free from that narrowness. He writes about Ryle, Sellars, Quine, Davidson, Putnam and the lot, because that is what he knows best. This piece of confession is worth quoting, and should be a lesson to many "provincial" spirits: 

The reason why the book is largely written in the vocabulary of contemporary analytic philosophers, and with reference to problems discussed in the analytic literature, is merely autobiographical ... Had I been equally familiar with other contemporary modes of writing philosophy, this would have been a better and more useful book, although an even longer one. (p. 8) 

If he had chosen the other style, instead of discussing Ryle, Sellars, Quine and Davidson in three-fourths of the book and Gadamer, Habermas and Foucault in just under twenty-five pages, he would have talked, in the main, about Kant, Hegel, Husserl and Heidegger. To Sellars' critique of the given there would have corresponded the criticisms of Landgrebe and Gurwitsch of the concept of the *hy/e* in Husserl; to Quine's critique of "meaning," there would have corresponded the criticism of Merleau-Ponty of the distinction between *a priori* and *a posteriori,* meaning and fact. These are but two examples of how the alternative story, but probably with the same moral, would have been written. To complain that Rorty has not written that story is to complain unjustifiably that he has written the book he wanted to. However, it is also important to see what new insights the alternative story would have added to the moral. I will return to this later in this review. 

*[p. 59]* ^pg88

Before I take more substantial matters for consideration I want to draw attention to one frustrating feature of Rorty's strategy. Impressive as he is in his scholarship, he has given very few arguments of his own. He uses Sellars' arguments against the given and Quine's against meaning, as though they cannot be answered, but he has done little to show they cannot be. He plays one philosopher against the other, and would have one or both dismissed, according as it suits his pre-delineated moral. These are rhetorically effective but argumentatively poor techniques. What does it matter if Sellars rejects the concept of the given - one may equally rhetorically ask - if there are other good philosophers who accept the viability of that concept? There is also an *implied* historicist, argument that has little cutting edge. If the Cartesian concept of the mental had a *historical* genesis (who in fact ever wanted to say that any philosophical concept or philosophy itself did not have one?) whatever and however that origin may be, that fact is taken to imply that there is something *wrong* about the concept. Furthermore, it is also assumed that if a concept had a historical genesis, it cannot herefore have any basis in sound common sense or in one's ordinary intuitions. Some of these strategies are in consonance with his avowedly historicist procedure, some not. It is good to keep this point in mind. 

II 

There is a discipline called "epistemology," which in modern times has replaced the old discipline of metaphysics. Its origin goes back to the seventeenth century, to Descartes' introduction of the concept ofthe inner and the mental in the sense of the region containing privileged representations. This gave rise to the sceptical problems about the possibility of knowledge and the justification of its validity. Modern philosophy, in one way or another, is concerned with this problem. Epistemology is also transcendental philosophy, the search for the ultimate foundation of knowledge with a view to *blocking scepticism.* In transcendental philosophy, one looks for "an overarching permanent neutral matrix" for all inquiry, past and future (p. 266). This search for a foundation may take various forms; it may be either rationalistic or empiricistic; it may be subjectivistic or linguistic. There are many possible combinations of these four, with differences in emphasis on one or the other. But in any case, so runs Rorty's basic theme, transcendental philosophy, or epistemology, or foundationalism is committed to three propositions: 

*[p. 60]* ^pg89

(1) Knowledge (and morality and the rest of our culture) is in need of a foundation. 

(2) This foundation is to be provided by a set of privileged *representations* (eventually, beliefs, propositions, sentences; or intuitions, data, evidence; or concepts, invariant meanings, analytic truths). 

(3) Knowledge (and morality and the rest of culture) consists of two heterogenous elements: intuitions and concepts (data and interpretations, evidence and theory, facts and language, contingent and necessary truths. 

Rorty's story consists in (i) showing how this conception of transcendental philosophy first came into being (i.e., through Descartes, Locke and Kant); (ii) showing how it determines contemporary analytic philosophy (through Carnap, Russell and Quine); (iii) showing how, nevertheless, it has come under attack from within analytic philosophy ("intuition" from Sellars and "concept" from Quine; the fact-language distinction from Wittgenstein; and the evidence-theory distinction from Kuhn and Feyerabend). Critics like Sellars and Quine have not been radical enough: Sellars gives meaning-analysis, Quine has his stimulus-sentences. Supplement the one by the other. Once the content-form scheme is rejected, epistemology is doomed, as is also philosophy as a discipline. So runs the story in its barest outlines. 

What is the positive moral? (1) Let us have the notion of culture (including science, the humanities and morality) as a conversation rather than as a structure erected upon foundations. (2) The only source of epistemic and moral authority (p. 319) is the community (pp. 188,210). (3) "Conversational justification" is holistic (p. 170). (4) Edifying philosophy - which Hegel had condemned, but which is all that remains for us in future if Rorty's story is correct - shall consist in genuine conversation that does not degenerate into inquiry (p. 372). It is in the last paragraph of the book, where a greater sense of realism prevails, that Rorty acknowledges that systematic philosophies of quite another variety, having nothing to do with epistemology, may come into being in future. But the philosopher's *moral concern,* is to continue "the conversation of the West." So, after all, he is not prophesying the end of philosophy, not even of systematic philosophy! One only wonders how - if the great systematic philosophies, including the epistemologies and the transcendental philosophies, belong to "the conversation of the West" (or, don't they, as they exhibit the spirit of *inquiry?)* - we can be morally obliged to continue that conversation, and yet beware of doing philosophy as the great participants in that conversation - Descartes, Kant, Husser! and Russell - did. 

*[p. 61]* ^pg90

Side by side, as the main story unfolds, Rorty also comments on, and makes his own philosophical choices of, some major issues. Thus on the mind-body issue he shows preference for physicalism; in psychology, for empirical behaviorism (pp. 210-211); in epistemology, for "epistemological behaviorism" (p. 174); in philosophy of language, for the "pure" (i.e., purified of all epistemology) Davidsonian holism. What is common to all these choices is the expulsion of anything like the Cartesian "inner," mental, subjectivity. The question I will ask, taking one of the choices, namely, physicalism, is how it stands related to the overall holistic, historicist, relativist, conversational project. The same sort of question may be raised with regard to his other choices. But before that, let me come to phenomenology. 

III 

For Rorty, phenomenology is a transcendental philosophy. Like Russell, Husserl wanted to make philosophy scientific, by providing an apodictic foundation for all our knowledge. In fact Rorty almost always couples the two names. Nowhere does Rorty evince any appreciation of the novelty in the transcendental-philosophical project of Husser!. This diagnosis, together with his insistence that Husserl remains tied to the idea of privileged representation, is in line with the understanding of Husserl of even his most famous protege, Heidegger. On Heidegger's reading, consciousness, subjectivity and intentionality remain inextricably tied to the idea of "representation." I think that Heidegger is mistaken in this view. It was the great merit of the *radicalised* intentionality thesis that it overcame the idea of representational consciousness. In view of even Heidegger's failure to appreciate this, Rorty's is understandable. Before I comment on Rorty's understanding of phenomenology, and the way it makes him overlook, at each crucial turn, one other alternative, perfectly in consonance with part of his overall thesis, let me briefly sketch where the novelty of phenomenology as a transcendental philosophy lies. 

First of all, although in some writings of Husserl, especially in the early *Idea oj Phenomenology* of 1907, there is a concern with epistemology, namely, with *how* knowledge is possible, this epistemological concern is not the predominant theme of phenomenology, even of the Husserlian sort. The concern is rather about what is *involved* in knowledge (as well as in other sorts of experience, moral, aesthetic) than in justifying any cognitive (or other non-cognitive) claims. The motive of "blocking scepticism" - that typical Anglo-Saxon concern - is 

*[p. 62]* ^pg91

conspicuous by its absence. There is no attempt to justify any particular historical scientific theory - Euclidean or non-Euclidean geometry, Newtonian or post-Newtonian physics, for example. This much of what Rorty says of Davidson's concept of truth holds good of phenomenology: "nature has no preferred way of being represented" (p. 300), no preferred theory of the world, for each such would be a noematic structure, and there is no route from the object to the noema. Phenomenology's concern, then, is with how these noemata are constituted in intentional acts. The intentional life of consciousness, as that through which theories or other sorts of noemata are constituted, is a historically developing life. As constituting the noemata through which the world is presented, this life of consciousness itself is not to be understood as one item in that world-picture. The constituting intentionality cannot then be understood in any "mundane" term. What Rorty says against a causal theory of reference on pp. 294-295, in fact against any non intentional idea of referring, is pertinent here: those relationships are parts of the theory of the world. It is in this sense that the constituting intentionality is *transcendental,* not in the sense of justifying any preferred theory, but in the sense of making possible any theory whatsoever in the course of history. It does set up, thereby, "a neutral matrix," but since this neutral matrix is, unlike Kant's, not committed to any *a priori* set of categories, it is as minimally structured as Rorty's matrix of the "conversation of mankind." 

In spite of Husserl's self-ascribed Cartesianism, his reinterpreted version of it is just as little Cartesian as possible without going over to physicalism. To be brief, I will make only two comments on this. For one thing, the constituting subjectivity is far from being mental. The mental, in transcendental phenomenology, is not a univocal concept. It admits of various interpretations, depending upon what discourse one is adopting in the pre-phenomenological stance. Each such concept of the "mental," as also of the world, is correlated to one such discourse. As the source of such constituting discourse, the ego is transcendental. The mental is essentially a mundane concept, not a transcendental concept. For another, intentionality, in phenomenology, does not define the mental as contradistinguished from the bodily. It defines a region that cuts across the body-mind dichotomy: the region of sUbjectivity. There are mental acts that exhibit intentionality, there are mental states that do not; there are bodily movements that do. As Scheler said, the bodily may be the most private and thought most public. Neither body-mind dualism nor Cartesian mentalism is a necessary part of phenomenology. They involve mundane discourses whose constitution phenomenology is after. 

*[p. 63]* ^pg92

This brings me to what I promised earlier in this review: to give at least one example of why rejection of Cartesian dualism need not entail physicalism. The Cartesian dualist and the anti-Cartesian physicalist share a common concept of the body: namely, of the body as a physical thing, the proper subject matter of physical science. But if the Cartesian *res extensa* and *res cogitans* define each other by contrast, then Austin's advice is pertinent: 

In philosophy it is often good policy, where one member of a putative pair falls under suspicion, to view the more innocent-seeming party suspicious as well. <sup>2</sup>

Rorty sees this point when he says that the line of demarcation between the "irreducibly physical" and the "irreducibly psychical" is not absolutely drawn (p. 242). Rejecting the Cartesian inner, if one swings back to the omnipresence of the Cartesian *res extensa,* one is still bewitched by Cartesian dualism. Phenomenology' 'overcomes" it truly when it rejects the primacy of the concept of body understood as *res extensa,* and introduces the concept of the lived body, of the body as intentional, of bodily subjectivity that is neither private and inner nor public and outer. In fact that distinction, if not spurious, is itself constituted historically, as well as - I should add - eidetically. 

If on the body/mind issue there is an option that Rorty does not consider, one that would have been closer to his point of view than physicalism as a theory of mind is, the same holds true of the philosophy of language and the theory of reference. In each case he has two options - the so-called pure and the so-called impure - carved out by analytical philosophy. Phenomenology, rightly understood, offers in each case, a third, more promising option, it also offers the promise of a nonfoundationalist, non-epistemological transcendental philosophy that recognises the validity of the historicist program, that is not committed to any specific theory of the world, and that posits an open-ended framework of intentional, intersubjective, historical consciousness, with a minimal *a priori* structure, within which any constitution is after all possible. In this sense, the new transcendental philosophy is committed to the constitution of *sense,* rather than to the justification of truth. If the early analytical philosopher's meaning-analysis looked foundationalist, that is because he was committed to an absolutely sacred stratum of meanings - the commonsensical or whatever else (e.g., those of physics). Phenomenology is not committed to any such.

IV 

I will now raise a few problems about Rorty's positive moral. First, if a history of ideas alone is to be the effective tool for overcoming philosophical mistakes (p. 33), then we need *a* history of the relevant ideas. Granting for the present that Rorty's account of Descartes is right (he acknowledges that the true history could be different (pp. 49- 51), our history, then, tells us that after certain conceptual requirements have been satisfied, the Cartesian concept of mind as the inner appeared. There is no question here of conceptual necessities. The story cannot tell us that only if a prior concept X is given, could a concept Y emerge. It can tell us only what in fact is the case. What sorts of connection, then, is Rorty showing among the doctrine of universals, the idea of grasping the universals, the idea of "glassy essence," the idea of the spirit surviving the bodily death, and the seventeenth-century notion of the mental? This order may in fact have been different from what Rorty and we *believe* it to have been. And, even if this order was surely not different from what in fact it was, it *might have been.* I ask not only about another possible world, but also that we consider other philosophical traditions than the western, that instead of "the conversation of the West," we *really* consider the conversation of mankind. Then we shall have a bewildering picture. The idea of the inner and the mental appeared in Hindu thought before the realism of universals, though the two coexisted later on; when they did coexist, universals were often regarded as being sensuously perceived rather than mentally abstracted. As early as the first century A.D., Buddhist nominalists also espoused an ontology of mental events. Consciousness, in the Indian tradition, was never confused with mind. And so on, so forth. How much of this global conversation constitutes *a* history, or *the* relevant history? Rorty has a nice historiography which, if true, would be so helpful: philosophy, according to this picture, first concerned itself with *things,* then with *ideas,* and finally with *words.* But it is true, if at all, only of the western conversation, not of the global. What to do, in that case? 

It may seem preposterous that in this review I should bring in such an extraneous point as oriental thought. For me, it is of course not extraneous. For philosophy, in general, it can be *made* extraneous by restricting philosophy to the conversation of the West. Whitehead says that "the intolerant use of abstractions is the major vice of the intellect.,,3 He also observes that our powers of explanation are unlimited - if only we exclude from consideration everything that does not

fit into our preconceived scheme. What I want to draw attention to is simply this. Rorty alternates between "the conversation of the West" and "the conversation of mankind." In any case - and this is my next point - the question of a historical possibility ("How did the concept *X* come into being?") can be answered only by the best available historical knowledge one has; and the more restricted domain of history one considers the more certain one is of the validity of his answer. This is why I said that the historical account should be supplemented by an eidetic, i.e. by an eidetic account of what process of thinking one *must have* gone through - in any possible world - before one could arrive at such a concept as *X.* Phenomenology seeks to combine both, the historicist and the eidetic. Husserl's account of the origin of Galilean physics is an illustration of this. 

My second anxiety concerns the idea of "community," in which the supreme authority lies. How large is it, or how small? In the case of physical theories, we know that it is the international community of physicists. What about such plain everday concepts as "body," "mind," "goodness," "truth"? Are we to consider, in morals, the "little" community of the village or the "great" community of intellectuals, artists and creative writers? Where do we draw the temporal and geographical boundaries? Don't we have here a fascinating hypostatisation, another of the many transcendental-philosophical constructions? Are we not extending the concept of "conversation" beyond its legitimate application - an application grounded, not in *a priori dicta,* but in our ordinary, socially accepted language-games? Isn't, then, "our conceptual scheme" to be understood simply as "the collection of views which make up our present-day culture" (even if "our" is restricted to the western world) - a motley crowd of jarring beliefs and ideologies? You can then save the idea of an ongoing conversation by making it minimally structured, in which case you approximate to the Habermas-Apel notion of an ideally communicating society as the transcendental foundation. 

I should add even a third point. It appears as though Rorty has gotten hold of the essence of man as a *conversing,* speaking, communicating being. His essence lies in *social praxis.* But we have thereby overcome neither essentialism nor transcendental philosophy. For me, not to have done so is not a fault. 

Finally, a remark about holism. It appears to me that there is a tension between the different components of the holism that Rorty develops. One component, borrowed from Davidson, is the claim that *meaning* pertains not to the components of a discourse, but to a discourse (from a sentence to a text to an entire discourse) as a whole (p. 303). A second component, derived from Quine, is that *justification* is a matter, 

*[p. 66]* ^pg95

not of components, word by word or sentence by sentence, but of a theory as a whole (pp. 170, 181). Another component, added to the others, centers round the idea of "social practice," which goes back to Rorty's Deweyan reading of Heidegger. Then there is a relativistic implication (beautifully stated and elegantly defended as perfectly harmless as far as our ordinary notion of objectivity is concerned): namely, that ontological questions "have a sense if we give them a sense" (p. 196) or that "explanatory power is where we find it" (p. 209). There are also the historicist, the behavioristic and the materialist components that, in different degrees, jar with the rest. One can speak of "a network of practices," of "social practice" as a whole, only if "behavior" is interpreted intentionally as Merleau-Ponty and Wittgenstein would have it. Rorty would free empirical psychology of these vestiges of mentalism, leaving his readers to puzzle how he can continue to speak not only of a whole of social practice, but also of such practice as the source of sense and justification - inspite of his having renounced the only basis for making such references. His strategy would be the one he adopts in the book: imbedding intentionality within a functional system: "following Wittgenstein, we shall treat intentionality as merely a subspecies of the functional," where" functional" is "the sort of property whose attribution depends upon a knowledge of context" (p. 32). But this larger context is itself describable only intentionally. If the behavioristic physicalistic component jars with the idea of "social practice," the historicist component jars with the idea of a whole. A discourse, a language, a theory can reasonably be said to be a whole; but an ongoing, open-ended process of conversation, in the course of which discourses, theories, and such wholes are generated and overcome ("discourses" are "strands in a possible conversation," (p. 318» one such strand is not, in the strict sense, a whole. In this ongoing conversation, itself a reified construct, the emerging wholes, however incommensurable amongst themselves as such, coexist as stages in one historical process. That re-establishes a certain continuity, despite incommensurabilities and discontinuities. Again, to my mind, none of this is a fault. 

Holism is not the only alternative to epistemological atomism. Between the theory that we learn or assign meanings word by word or even sentence by sentence and the theory that we learn (and thereby know in a peculiar sense of" know") the meaning of a discourse as a whole, there is a large area of middle ground. There is no empirical reason, derivable from either language-learning or communication, why a philosopher should not stake out some portion of that middle ground. \*

INTENTIONALITY, CAUSALITY AND HOLISM\* 

Recognition of the phenomenon of intentionality is threatened from two sides. From one side, Nature as an interconnected seamless order of causality leaves no room for intentionality. From another side, holisms of various kinds - the Hegelian absolute spirit as much as Richard Rorty's "conversation of mankind," with many intermediate forms in between - present the same threat though with less abruptness and with more tolerance. The thrust of this paper is to argue that not only do the causal order of Nature and the various holistic systems fail to eliminate intentionality, but they in fact presuppose it. Whereas such a thesis is reputedly a form of transcendental philosophy, an exact and satisfactory formulation of the thesis is a matter of utmost difficulty. This is the task I have set for myself in this lecture. 

I 

The relation between intentionality and causality has been thought of in various ways. At one extreme is (1) the naturalistic view that in Nature there is only causality and no room for intentionality. At the other extreme is (7) the idealistic view that intentionality is the constituting order, causality the constituted. In between these extremes, the following five seem to me to be significant alternatives: (2) that intentionality characterises the phenomenal level, whereas causality the underlying reality; (3) that one and the same event allows of a causal and an intentional description; (4) that it is one's intentional project

I will briefly consider the strengths and weaknesses of these seven positions before developing my own point of view. 

*(1) That nature is a causal order in which there just is no room for intentionality:* The truth of this position is that nature can be conceived as a seamless causal order in such a manner as not to leave room for intentionality. One may even strengthen this thesis by adding that this is how in fact science *does* construe nature to be. What is important is that we free the thesis under consideration from that *naive* scientific realism which generally goes with it when it is said that nature *is* a causal order. Nature may still be regarded as a causal order, not because that is what it is in the realist's sense, but because the *concept* of nature with which the scientists allegedly operate, is the concept of a causally ordered system. Such a system cannot leave room open for the emergence of intentionality, not because there would be no intentionality in the world, but because one who is committed to that pre-conception about what the world is like is obliged by virtue of that very committment to exclude intentionality, and in order to exclude intentionality he may take recourse to various theoretical strategies such as *translation, reduction,* or *replacement* whether for each token of intentional occurrence or for each type. Once such a research program is adopted, one can legitimately make an ontological assertion (which is in fact, in Carnap's language, an *internal* assertion) to the effect that there *is* no intentionality. But it would be a mistake to construe it at the same time as an answer to the external question, "Is there intentionality in the world?" 

*(2) That intentionality characterises phenomenal experience, whereas causality characterises the underlying reality:* My thoughts, beliefs, hopes and fears are, as phenomenally experienced by me, intentional, the underlying reality about them consists in bodily processes caused both by external and antecedent internal, organic, states. Intentional experiences are how these causal processes *appear* to the organism under appropriate conditions. Phenomenology is grounded in physics. Now, this view is not merely separating intentional discourse from the causal, but assigning to each a value - to the former the value of "appearance," to the latter, the value of "reality." Certainly, this assignment of value is not arbitrary, but is guided by certain specificable criteria, criteria such as "priority of physics," "desirability of truth-functional language," each one of which, faultless in itself, begs the issue. Moreover, it is not enough to say that a level of discourse or experience

In the case of intentional acts, there is no prospect of showing this for reasons I will give in the next section. 

*(3) That one and the same event allows of a causal and an intentional description:* The event that is my raising my arm to grasp a glass of water in order to drink *can be described* as a chain of muscular movements and biochemical processes. My "pure intending" such as intending to do something (without actually doing so) may be described in terms of neural processes within the cortex. *What is the one and same event* which allows of these two descriptions: the causal and the intentional? Is it "my raising my arm in order to ... " or "pure intending to do ... "? Then we should rather say that the intentional act can be described in causal language. Is it the muscular movement, the biochemical or the neural process? Then we should rather say that the physiological process can be given an intentional description. We do not however get hold of one and the same event that allows of botha causal and an intentional description, unless we posit an unknown *Ding-ansich* of which the two are alternate descriptions. The truth of the matter is that what is in itself an intentional act *can be explained as* - *not described as* - a physiological process *within* a causal-explanatory theory. The act however remains what it is in itself: an intentional act. 

*(4) That it is one's intentional project that confers the value "cause"* *on an objective state of affairs in respect of an action:* on this view, intentionality is to causality, what freedom is to facticity.! The truth in this view is that in the case of human actions, free intending is under the constraint of given situations, but what precisely is to count as the constraining situation depends upon what one's intentional project is. H one is climbing a mountain, the steep elevation is the opposition to be overcome, if one is mowing a lawn, the slight slope plays the same role. But none of these constraints can be said to be "causes" of my project, they may sometimes be said to "motivate" one. The view under consideration may be regarded as confusing between intentionality and free spontaneity. It is important to recognise that intentionality may be passive or even if it is active, may contain a component of passivity.

II 

There are two sorts of causal accounts with regard to intentional experiences: perceptions, beliefs, thoughts, hopes, desires and frustrations. One sort is to *explain* them by causally correlating them to states of the body, the latter are then easily shown to be caused by environmental stimuli and precedent internal states of the organism. The other sort purports to give a causal account of an intentional act by relating it causally to whatever happens to be its object. Thus a causal theory of perception understands perceptual experience as being caused by its object; a causal theory of knowledge likewise understands knowledge as being caused in a certain privileged manner by whatever is known. There are causal theories not only of meaning and reference, but of all intentional acts whatsoever. The following series of remarks are directed against both sorts of causal theories. 

(1) Phenomenology has since its beginnings recognised that the object of an intentional object is not *eo ipso* its cause. This is not only because the object may not exist and a non-existent entity cannot exercise causality, but also because of the fact that even when the object is an existent

*[p. 74]* ^pg103

physical entity as in outer perception one should distinguish (i) between that entity *qua* object and the very same entity *qua* cause, and, more importantly, (ii) between all the various causes or causal conditions which bring about a perceptual experience and that particular cause which is to be the object being perceived. One seeks to respond to the difficulty suggested by the second of these distinctions by speaking of "appropriate" causal chain, but this use of "appropriateness" only conceals the problem, instead of resolving it. 10 

(2) Leaving perception aside, the case for a causal theory of intentionality has hardly any merit. In the case of future-oriented acts such as hopes and desires, resolutions and expectations, the object is not yet at hand, it is something yet to be reached or realised, and could not therefore act as cause. Of emotional acts, what may be regarded as causing them is not quite the same as the object as intended in those acts. A shadow may cause fear of ghost, the object of fear is nevertheless a ghost and not the shadow. A paper tiger may cause fear whose object is not the paper tiger but a real one. 

(3) The cause of one's religious beliefs may not be the same as the object of those beliefs - it may be argued - but a stone is believed to be sacred and a book holy because of a set of other beliefs that one has either inherited or acquired. So even if the object is not the cause, this object is the intentional object because of other specifiable circumstances such as tradition, education and upbringing. With this, it is to be noted, one has left behind a physicalist idea of causality, and is appealing to the way the web of one's beliefs, i.e. one's cultural background, determines the intentionality of an isolated act. This is much more to the point, but one deludes onseself if he thinks that one is thereby still assigning a *cause* to the intentionality of an act. One is giving an explanation, no doubt, but the explanation appeals to motivational connections amongst beliefs rather than to either a *causal* efficacy or a causal-nomic law.

(5) An intentional act not only has a sense or noema, it is also characterised by temporality and horizontal character. The temporality that is a component of the causal relation is the simple relation of after-and-before. The cause precedes the effect. The two are events occurring at given moments in the objective time-series. The temporality of an intentional act is structured in a very different manner. An act, in the first place, carries within itself its immediate ancestry in the just-no-more past, it also contains anticipations of the not-yet future. Thus spread out on its "spacious present," an act carries the burden of history, and though sinking into the past (memory), takes its entire horizon with it and retains it as though in a sort of (Whiteheadian) "objective immortality." What physical events, the neural traces not excluding, could *cause* such temporality, not to speak of exhibiting it in its own nature? 

(6) Lest the purport of these negative criticisms may be misunderstood, I will conclude this section with a few remarks on how they should be taken. I am not suggesting that within the causal order of nature, in the very bosom of causality, the intentional *erupts* with its claim to be a *causa sui.* That indeed would be miracle, and I have no fascination for miracles. Scientific Nature, I concede, is a causal order, nothing can belong to it and yet not be amenable to causal explanation of some sort or other. Furthermore, it belongs to the very sense of material reality that it can bring about causal changes and can itself be causally affected by others. If mental life be conceived as a part of this nature, nothing can stop it from being regarded as subject to the same process of causal action and interaction. Whether one needs to accept identity or interaction would be a subsequent issue to be decided. But the very same mental process may be regarded as what is *in itself,* i.e. as intentional, as intending objects in the world via correlative ideal meanings, as characterised by temporality, historicity and horizonality, and, once this is done, mental life cannot any longer be inserted into the causal order of nature. It stands apart as the dispenser of sense, as constituting and not as constituted, as transcendental and not as mundane. This may be regarded as an extension of the Kantian thesis that:

To all this it may be objected: how can our regarding an intentional act as such and such so radically transform its nature as to lift it from the mundane to the transcendental realm? I have two replies to such a wonder. In the first place, it is *not* by a suitable interpretation of mental life that one claims to transform it from the mundane process it is to the transcendental that one wants it to be; it is rather by de-interpreting it, by removing the natural, naturalistic and scientific interpretations that one penetrates into, or rather lays bare, what is its true nature as intentional life. Again, no miraculous transformation is achieved by philosophical "regarding." At the same time, one should emphasise that if anywhere it is in the realm of consciousness that appearance and reality coincide. A state of consciousness is what it appears to be. A new attitude directed upon a state of consciousness transforms the state; we have indeed a "new" state. Such "transformation" does not take place in the order of nature. A state of consciousness stripped of its naturalistically transformed state is also the old state regarded now with a fresh innocence. 

III 

Even if intentionality is insulated from the intrusion of real, nature causality from outside, it still continues to be threatened by the spectre of holism from within itself. Consider the act - sense/noema - reference structure. Each of these three components, as it belongs to one singular intentional act, can barely stand on its own. Acts merge into acts, first in the mental life of one individual, but then, breaking open the bounds of a solipsistic ego, in the intentional life of a community of individuals. But how can one stop with a community of minds, short of mankind as a whole? or, short of the total historical experience of spirit in its totality? Turn to senses or noemata, and one is in no better situation. An isolated, possibly simple sense, looks like being an abstraction from a whole nexus of meanings - historically deriving from a *tradition,* logically entailing and being entailed by a *theory.* Just as acts hang together with acts, and senses with senses, so do the referents. A referent is an abstraction from a totality called the "world." Then we have three sorts of holism: a holism of intentional life, a holism of senses, and a holism of the world. There are many other holisms that are not easily identifiable in terms of these three, but which, so it seems to me, rest on one or the other. Thus the holism of language or discourse rests on the holism of senses, just as the historicist holism rests on the holism of acts. Where are we to locate a holism of the Hegelian sort? 

*[p. 78]* ^pg107

It is not a nexus of meanings, but a self-developing and self-differentiating spirit or notion which expresses itself in a hierarchy of such nexuses. I should add that a holism is not monism. Hegelian holism is monism, it recognises only one whole which satisfies the theoretical requirements of the system. Holisms that are pluralistic, i.e., recognise many alternative wholes, abound. Any holism of language is bound to be such. 

The Fregean Context Principle may very well serve as a starting point for a holism of senses. If a word has a meaning only in the context of a sentence, as Frege urged, one may want to radicalise Frege's point and insist that a sentence has a sense, in the long run, only in the context of our entire language. To understand a word is to understand how it functions in an entire language, and - as Davidson has emphasised - "the approach to language must be holistic." Or, one may start with the Frege-Husserl thesis that reference is *via* a sense, and then argue, plausibly enough, that a sense does not stand alone by itself, but is inseparably connected with other senses, all of which together, in the long run, may be said to constitute a *theory.* Thus although on the surface level a singular act has its own sense through which it refers, at a deeper level there is indeed a whole theory, a complex noematic structure, which determines what entities we take as belonging to our world. Change meanings anywhere in the language, and changes need to be made in other parts of the system. If this be the case, then two radical changes have to be made in our understanding of intentionality. It is not the singular act which by *its* sense determines the reference. It is rather the underlying, comprehensive theory that does so, the importance of any singular act in fact fades into insignificance, perhaps psychologically interesting or significant for the biography of an individual thinker or user of the language, but of no significance at all insofar as the correlation between the theory and the world is concerned. Secondly, the singular act, even if not determined by the causal order of nature, is determined by the underlying theory whose constraints it, in its intending, cannot overstep. 

I will make brief comments on this position in order to bring out its excesses. But it would be best to begin by recognising its truth which lies in the rejection of atomism, be it psychological, semantic or epistemological. Neither mental acts nor their senses nor their referents stand, each by itself, as autonomous unities. A mental act belongs to the mental life of its owner, just as a sense points to other senses, and a referent to other entities in its world. But rejection of atomism need not entail holism. Truth must lie in between these one-sided abstractions. Phenomena themselves do not speak for either. 

*[p. 79]* ^pg108

Frege, it should be recalled, not only laid down the context principle to the effect that words have meanings only in the context of sentences, but balanced it by the so-called composition principle to the effect that the senses as well as the referents of sentences are composed of those of the constituent expressions. If a word or expression had no sense of its own apart from its role or functioning in a sentence, one could not be said to understand the meaning of a word or an expression apart from knowing the meaning of that sentence. But *which* sentence? There are in fact infinite number of possible sentences in which a word or an expression may function. These infinite number of sentences would contain other words or expressions which would refer likewise to other groups of infinite sentences in which they are constituents. To ascribe to the speaker or (correct) interpreter of a sentence a knowledge of those sentences or, as the holist does, a knowledge of an entire language, is, to say the least, to use "knowing" in a most unusual sense. And, yet, it is, in an important sense, true that to understand a word is to know the role it plays or can play in a sentence in which it occurs or can occur. The context principle, then, does not amount to saying that words or expressions do not have senses of their own; it only specifies the conditions under which they have their senses. [^13]

Even if in order to know the sense of a word or expression it is necessary to know the sense of other words and expressions and of sentences in which it can function, that does not require that one should know an entire language. Knowledge of a fragment of the language is all that is required, and this much does not require a holistic conclusion. The stricter holistic requirement that all possible *senses* are entailed or implicated by a given sense would render both introduction of a new sense and changes in a given sense impossible without changing the entire language. Consequently, as Dummett has rightly insisted, holism would render it impossible to come up with a theory of meaning after all. [^14] A satisfactory theory, I want to contend, must be able to account for *both* the fact that words and expressions as well as sentences have meanings *of their own* - meanings that can be grasped without knowing large fragments of the language *and* the fact that words have their homes in sentences and sentences in discourse, language or theory (which ever whole you prefer). Phenomenology has given us two concepts which can be of help to us here: one is the distinction between dependent and independent meanings, the other is the concept of horizon.

While agreeing with this, one may still be under obligation to recognise that there is an important difference between the *sort of completion* names require and the *sort of completion* predicate expressions require, thereby *saving* the truth in the Frege-Husser! thesis that names are radically distinct from predicate expressions. At the same time, one must also emphasise that *while* the sort of incompleteness or dependence which meanings of names have is different from the sort characterising the meanings of predicate expressions, the *sort of* completeness or independence which the meanings of names may be taken to have is also very different from the sort of completeness or independence we shall ascribe to sentential meanings. Both Frege and Husser! recognise that a predicative sentence expresses a thought which can be grasped in itself, even if to interpret the meaning of the sentence one may need to know the meanings of other parts of the language. In other words, as against holism, we must distinguish between the *evidence* we have for ascribing a thought to a speaker or for interpreting his utterance in the manner we do, and the thought that we ascribe to him or the sense we attach to his utterance. The *evidence* goes beyond hearing him speak, a theory of such evidence may be quasi-holistic, but a theory of sentential sense itself needs to be "molecular." The recognition of the primacy of apophantic or predicative thought as the unit of discourse, out of which the totality of discourse is progressively built up, is not only historically a basis of western logic and epistemology, but, phenomenologically, an incontrovertible thesis which any holism can deny only at the risk of its own credibility. The principle of composition is still valid, not in the sense that the sentential sense is built out of sub-sentential senses as "building blocks," but in the sense that it is *founded upon* the incomplete and dependent senses of the sub-sentential expressions. Thus the sentential sense is both independent and founded; the sub-sentential senses are both dependent and yet founding. We thus have a means of reconciling the two principles - context and composition - which both Frege and Husserl appear to have accepted. 

But this is not to succumb to a semantic atomism. What we need to 

*[p. 81]* ^pg110

do is to avoid both the extremes: atomism and holism, and, for this purpose, the phenomenological concept of horizon is readily at hand. We grasp a sense, be it sub-sentential or sentential (now we can say) within a horizon, containing pointing references to what lies beyond, and arousing intentional expectancies (the Sanskrit grammarians called it aklink~a) which demand the movement of thought beyond what is thematically grasped. But a horizon is a horizon *insofar as it lets the thematic figure to stand apart.* Holism cannot account for this thematic grasping of atomic or molecular sense. The totality to which holism appeals is a metaphysical construction, while the horizontal character of all thematic apprehension is a phenomenological feature of our experience. 

After saying why holism in theory of meaning has to be rejected, I need not pause for long to consider a possible holism of intentional acts. A typical version of this is what Rorty calls "the conversation of mankind." Conversation is a dialogue, an ongoing process that takes place between two or more participants. The holist however is talking about *the* conversation of mankind, or even of the west. This is a metaphysical totalisation of what is, in a more apt sense, true dialogue or conversation. What we would have at most is an ongoing, open-ended process of conversation in course of which discourses, theories and such wholes are generated and overcome. In this ongoing conversation, itself a reified construct, the emerging wholes, however incommensurable amongst themselves, coexist as stages in a historical process. 

Two thoughts come to mind with this. In the first place, the sorts of entities that hang together to form wholes have to be intensional entities: propositions, senses, theories. Or, they have to be intentional acts, as in the ego logical life of one individual, or the mental lives of egos in a community, or "the conversation of mankind". Behavior, construed extensionally or naturalistically, can be linked to behavior only causally or as a mere temporal succession, but such a series cannot form a whole. The idea of social *praxis* as constituting a whole is incurably intentional. Rorty's holism contains two mutally incompatible elements: a preference for behaviorism which wants to exclude everything intentional and mentalistic and yet an appeal to wholes such as "conversation of mankind," "social praxis," "community," both as constitutive of sense and as the justifying authority. 

My contention, then, is that holism presupposes the concept of intentionality, rather than being able either to eliminate it or to explain it. 

By way of concluding, I will briefly summarise what I have been leading up to. The intentional acts performed by individual egos have a primacy which needs to be preserved, in my view, as against both 

*[p. 82]* ^pg111

causal and holistic explanations. This is not to say that any and every individual ego is the primal source of constitution of all those meanings it intends. On the contrary, most of the meanings that an individual ego intends are taken over from a tradition within which it finds itself. It may also be that in intending a sense, (or, what is its correlate and reverse achievement, in interpreting a speaker) one makes use of a fairly large, already available, network of senses and shared background beliefs, but those other senses *had* their "primal institution" in intentions of other egos, and those beliefs are beliefs shared by a community of egos. Though there is more to the intended sense than a particular act intending it may be aware of, what we are referred back to are themselves other actual or possible acts of individual egos: beliefs, expectations, thoughts, and the like. 

In this sense, intentional life is self-explanatory, but the causal order is not.

It was recognised by Brentano that mental phenomena are objects of inner perception. If this is what Husserl and Brentano mean, then it is worth asking what precisely is the nature of their thesis. They are in fact *defining* the concept of "knowledge" in terms of "evidence," and for botha knowledge, as implying evidence, may have the same noematic content as a blind judgment. The latter made without evidence may still be regarded as true, but only insofar as it is a possible component of an identifying synthesis with a fulfilling experience. To say that knowledge is knowledge of truth and further that it is characterised by evidence is only successively to elaborate the very concept of knowledge, and not to add anything to it. They are analytic truths, although the concept of knowledge is the concept of an identifying synthesis. 10 In this sense, then, all knowledge of truth is characterised by evidence - which is not to say that truth is intuited in some peculiarly nonrelational manner. The relational structure "closes up" into an identifying synthesis: this synthesis is immediately given to consciousness as an identifying synthesis. Truth is then a possible object of an identifying consciousness of the form "It is this," and in this sense possesses self -evidence2 . 

One has, however, to take into account two distinctions in order to be able to place the analysis given above on a sound basis: one, the distinction between "seeming evidence" and "real evidence"; the other, that between truth as the noematic correlate of our consciousness of truth and truth in itself. The former contrast is what gives scepticism its foothold: If some evidences are only seeming evidences, what guarantee could there be that any given evidence is genuine? Now this argument, Cartesian in form, is worthless. The concept of seeming *cp,* where *cp* is a predicate, is possible only if there are valid applications of *cp.* The possibility of error about evidence only proves that there are real evidences, and that the evident is the true, though the seemingly evident is not.
