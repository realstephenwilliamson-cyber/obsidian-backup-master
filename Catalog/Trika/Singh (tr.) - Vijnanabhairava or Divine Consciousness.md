## About

A short Sanskrit Shaiva tantra of the Kaula Trika tradition of Kashmir. Bhairava (Shiva) answers the goddess Bhairavi's question about his true nature by teaching about 112 methods of meditation (*dharanas*).

- **Form:** 163 stanzas in the *anushtubh* meter, framed as a dialogue. Abhinavagupta called it the *Shiva-jnana-upanishad* (Wikipedia). The University of Virginia's Mandala bibliography files it under the *Rudrayamala Tantra*.
- **Date:** Jaideva Singh suggests the 7th to 8th century CE and says an exact date is hard to establish.
- **Practices:** attention to the breath, bodily centers, mantra, visualization, the senses and emptiness. Christopher Wallis notes a strong Buddhist influence; Mark Dyczkowski sorts the practices by type (breath, kundalini, void, heart).
- **1918 edition:** edited by Mukunda Rāma Shāstrī in the Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies, printed at Bombay. It gives a commentary partly by Kshemaraja and partly by Shivopadhyaya, and Ananda Bhatta's *Kaumadi*. HathiTrust records it as two volumes in one, notes that no. 9 of the Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies is the volume with the *Kaumadi*, and dates Kshemaraja to the 11th century and Shivopadhyaya to the 18th.
- **Translations:** Paul Reps's poetic version in *Zen Flesh, Zen Bones* (1957) brought it wide notice. Wikipedia, following Wallis, names Jaideva Singh's (*Vijnanabhairava or Divine Consciousness*, 1979) and Dyczkowski's as the two reliable English translations. Singh's later SUNY Press version, *The Yoga of Delight, Wonder, and Astonishment*, is dated 1991 by Wikipedia and 1990 by the Mandala record. Lakshman Joo's *The Practice of Centring Awareness* appeared in 2002, and Vladimir Ivanov's Russian translation (part 1, 2024) has notes based on the commentaries of Kshemaraja and Shivopadhyaya.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says references to it appear throughout Kashmir Shaiva literature, which indicates that it was considered an important text in the tradition; following Wallis, it adds that many English versions are poetic or free-form renderings that fail to communicate the practices.
