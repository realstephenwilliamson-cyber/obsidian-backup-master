2.5.4 

##### 2.5.4 Etymology as an Interpretation of Reality

A name does not stand for a thing: instead it is much more the case that the thing stands for the name. A thing is there because a name for it exists. And this means that the name is always more real than the thing. This fundamental axiom that “reality is defined via language” also determines the heuristics of the Brāhmaṇas. The “nature” of a thing is described by the context of its creation, or its Arché, being described. But because the construction of reality by the Brāhmaṇas rests primarily on language, there are no primary relationships between things made of the same materials. In this way an early etymology is born, whose applications always function according to a simple scheme: if the noun x can be derived etymologically from the verb y (due to phonetic similarity), then y is an archetypal action of x and also constitutes its nature — at least according to the laws of mythical thought. 

For example, according to the Aitareya-Brāhmana™! the true nature of the “pouring in”, āhuti (of the food offerings to the gods which are poured into the fire) is the “calling hither” ahūti (the recitation of the hotr priest). The etymological operation aims to make it transparent that there is an esoteric connection between the sacrificial gift and the invocation of the gods. So etymology is understood quite in the original meaning of the word, namely as “the study of what is true, of the actual meaning” of the words. First and foremost it is a hermeneutic study. 

Etymologies can be combined with one another, embedded in broader contexts, and used in such a form, e.g. to interpret stages of the ritual. The etymologies reveal the esoteric correctness of the temporal and spatial order of the individual ritual elements. Such passages are mostly embedded in the context of creation stories. The origin of a noun from the verbal root corresponds here to the process of creation. #### 2.8 OM in the later Upanisads

The earlier Upaniṣads are an integral part of the Veda of a certain school, but not the later Upaniṣads. These are texts that simply exist somewhere, associated with the Veda only because of the high regard in which they are held. Most of them are formally allocated to the rather controversial Atharva- Veda, which was not recognized as a Veda until a later date, although they do not necessarily have anything to do with its content. These texts can articulate quite a range of different religious ideas. Some of them are concerned with transcendental philosophy; some aim to popularize new rites, many Upaniṣads are associated with new Lord Gods; and a few are even concerned with natural philosophy. 

2.8.1 

01 as Tool for Contemplation (Muṇdaka-Upaniṣad) 

While the earlier Upaniṣads are addressed exclusively to practicing Brahmins seeking to reflect on the sacrificial cult from within and to resolve it, some of the later Upaniṣads originated in religious groups who had abandoned the Vedic sacrifice completely and had their own rites. The Mundaka-Upanisad, for example, is addressed to the new religious group of the renunciates (sannyāsin), i.e. to those had left the old sacrificial religion, who lived out true “denial of the world” (in Max Weber’s sense) as itinerant begging monks. According to the later Brahmanic tradition, such a stage of life (āśrama) is obligatory as the last stage of life for the topmost three social classes. Munda means “shorn,” so in a literal translation we are talking about “instructions for bald heads,” meaning “those who renounce the world,” who give up all their possessions (including their hair). They totally reject the sacrifice and its symbols (as such), with the exception of OM. 

The ascetics thus renounce not only their material possessions, but also their spiritual possession, the Veda. With the cosmic mantra “bhūh \[= earth\] svēhā,” they mentally throw their Veda into the water, which means that they sacrifice it symbolically.” The mantra OM is the only mantra that is retained, because it does not bring any reward for performing a sacrifice. This notion can be directly derived from the ritual exegesis of the Brāhmanṇas. Even there OM was regarded as an esoteric mantra that relates only to the other world, while the three cosmic calls bhūh bhuvah svah (“earth, air, heavens”) designate the three parts of this world.
