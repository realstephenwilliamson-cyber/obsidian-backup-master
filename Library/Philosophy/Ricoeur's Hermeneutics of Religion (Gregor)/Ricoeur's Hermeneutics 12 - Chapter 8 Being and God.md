## Chapter 8

## Being and God

*Reflections on Transcendence,* 

*Immanence, and Divine Personhood* 

Then Nisus: “Is it that the Gods inspire, Euryalus, this fever of the breast? Or make we gods of but a wild desire?”

*—Aeneid*, Book IX, lines 219–20

The question of this chapter is Ricoeur’s changing view of transcendence. It is a question that arises in his earliest work—namely, his dissertation on the problem of God in reflexive philosophy. It is also present in his work on Marcel and Jaspers. It is present in his work on the Philosophy of the Will, where he argues that “Transcendence”<sup>1</sup> is the power that liberates the self. This idea becomes the core of Ricoeur’s later hermeneutics of religion; yet, in keeping with his allegiance to the reflexive tradition, Ricoeur also wants to preserve the integral role of immanence in the encounter with transcendence. We saw this in Ricoeur’s treatment of the call and response, and in his idea of revelation as a nonviolent appeal to human capacities of reason, conscience, imagination and desire. But questions remain regarding Ricoeur’s understanding of the origin of this revelation. At times Ricoeur writes of the Word addressed to us by God, and at other times as though transcendence is the manifestation of some deeper, more mysterious, sacred dimension of the world. What kind of transcendence does Ricoeur have in view? Is it the transcendence of God who is creator and redeemer of the world, or is it more of an immanent transcendence—a depth dimension, the sacred, the mystery of the world? And to what extent is there some kind of personal dimension to transcendence? Are the varieties of religions reducible to a variety of manifestations of the Absolute as an immanent process within the world, to put it in more Hegelian terms? These are questions we will explore over the next two chapters.

*[p. 162]* ^pg179

Always one to spot a false dilemma, Ricoeur does not see the need to pit a dualist transcendence against a monist immanence. Ricoeur resists this opposition and attempts to reconcile divine transcendence, as exemplified in the biblical figures of God, with divine immanence, as represented by Spinoza, and even Whitehead. This would seem to be a difficult fit insofar as the former is deeply personalistic while the latter is an impersonal substance. In this chapter we will examine a series of key texts that span Ricoeur’s career to see his changing approach to this question. In his late work Ricoeur ultimately suggests that the biblical God and Spinoza’s *substantia actuosa* might be compatible. These are merely suggestions that Ricoeur never works out, leaving questions about how this compatibility might be possible. I will suggest that a Trinitarian conception—which should be central to any hermeneutics of Christian religion—provides the interpretive key for reconciling the personal transcendence of God with the immanent manifestation of God in the world. There are hints that some kind of Trinitarian theology may be operative in the background of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, but he is not clear enough on the question for us to draw conclusions regarding the place of the Trinity in his thinking.

### The Transcendence of God and the Immanence of Reflection

This concern with transcendence and immanence is already present in Ricoeur’s earliest work, his thesis *Reflexive Method Applied to the Problem of God in Lachelier and Lagneau*. Ricoeur cites this thesis as his initiation into the reflexive tradition, and credits it with advancing his thought regarding the relation between philosophy and God. He was particularly impressed with the way Lachelier and Lagneau remained committed to the autonomy of philosophical reason while at the same time allowing “a place for the idea of God, for God as such, in their philosophy” (IA 6). How, then, does God enter into philosophy? Ricoeur was not satisfied with the approach of these reflexive philosophies because they end up turning thinking into a closed circle, such that the transcendence of God is reduced to the immanence of reflexive consciousness. For Lachelier, it is through a reflexive movement that thinking recognizes that God is not external to itself, but rather the “absolute foundation” of thinking consciousness itself. God is “thinking itself in its absolute power of desire and of knowing” (MR 91).<sup>2</sup> Thus the *I think* is really a *God thinks*, and this God is “radically immanent” and “totally interior to the ego” (MR 99). Similarly, Lagneau argues that God cannot be demonstrated theoretically, but must be discovered as “always already immanent in thought.”<sup>3</sup> 

*[p. 163]* ^pg180

What both thinkers have in common, Ricoeur writes, is the reflexive aim of becoming conscious of the very soul of all thought: “God himself” (MR 30).

Ricoeur concludes that the immanence of reflection is necessary as a philosophical method, but that it goes astray when this method turns into a philosophical doctrine. The problem with a philosophy of immanence is that it remains “radically incomplete,” since it does not recognize the irreducibility of transcendence. Ricoeur thus argues for a “radically realist” sense of alterity, as “really distinct” from the self and prior to self-consciousness. This is true for material reality, for the other person, and for God. Reflection should be supplemented with a strong emphasis on receptivity, since “philosophy is not only a reflection, but a welcome” (MR 233).

According to Ricoeur, the fullest sense of transcendence is found in personhood (MR 240). This is true for the other person, and most fully God, who transcends both the world and human thinking alike. Ricoeur therefore opposes reducing God to an immanent monism. This also entails a rejection of the univocity of being (MR 223). If all reality ultimately reduces to a monism in which divine thinking coincides perfectly with human thinking, then there is nothing genuinely *new*, no genuine creativity, only the progressive deepening and enrichment of that which already is. Thinking is just becoming aware of our “prepossessions” (MR 222). Ricoeur insists to the contrary that the “discovery of the absolute” is not merely the outworking of that which we already possess, an explication of that which is already implicit; rather it is “an expansion of our being by the intrusion of some alterity” (MR 233).

Ricoeur also suggests that God’s transcendent personhood is plural rather than singular. This is a condition of love, which requires a genuine diversity rather than the ultimate unity of all being. Lagneau recognizes this, writing that love requires a “multiplicity within unity,” and thus “presupposes a duality, even a trinity.” Is this a confession of the Triune God? Not so fast, cautions Ricoeur. For Lagneau, the diversity of Being is not the real story of the universe but merely a symbolic understanding that has not yet grasped the absolute unity of Being. The symbols of transcendence are ultimately just immanence that has not yet been recognized as such (MR 220–21). Otherness is ultimately reducible to the unity of the Same. 

Note Ricoeur’s point that the symbols of transcendence are not merely epiphenomenal manifestations of immanence. If Ricoeur ultimately becomes a Spinozist in his later work, we might level the same critique of him. Yet in the early text, at least, he seems quite at home with the idea of a unity in plurality of divine persons; if he stops short of naming the Trinity as such, he nevertheless rejects Lagneau’s tendency to reduce this transcendence in terms of immanence.

### Jaspers: Ciphers of Transcendence

The relation between transcendence and immanence is also a central concern in Ricoeur’s critique of Jaspers. As we noted in chapter 5, Ricoeur is critical of Jaspers’ impersonal concept of transcendence and appeals instead to Marcel’s personalist concept of God as personal transcendence, God as “*the supreme Thou*” whom we encounter in prayer*:*<sup>4</sup> “Who talks *to* God as Thou can talk *of* God as Transcendence, so far as that is fitting for man” (RJPR 640). 

Ricoeur was also critical of Jaspers’ notion of ciphers. For Jaspers, ciphers are the means by which transcendence manifests itself. Anything in the world can be a cipher of transcendence: a fish, a flower, a birth, a meal, and so on. Transcendence “comes to mind as a *cipher*,” but it cannot be put on display as an object for a thinking subject. If the cipher is identified as the objective presence of transcendence, it loses its genuine presence as cipher.<sup>5</sup> Religion often mistakes the symbolic reality of ciphers for the finite, sensible presence of transcendence, but Jaspers sees the cipher as the anti-thesis of the Incarnation, which affirms the unique historical event of God made flesh (RJPR 623–24). The cipher “is the language of transcendence, not transcendence itself.”<sup>6</sup> 

Jaspers thus attempts to develop a kind of philosophical faith that operates between religion and atheism, following Spinoza, Schelling, and Hegel by reintegrating “religion into philosophy by means of myth” (RJPR 625). This strategic reintegration tends to dissolve religion more subtly than outright atheism, since it transforms religion into aesthetics, giving poetry and painting a privileged role as ciphers of transcendence (RJPR 625–26). Ricoeur was heavily indebted to Jaspers’ account of ciphers, which shaped his vision for a poetics of transcendence, but at this stage in his work, Ricoeur also maintains that freedom can only be liberated through a gracious act of transcendence. For Jaspers, by contrast, transcendence does not have this liberating power but is more like “an obscure ground in the self” (RJPR 639). Jaspers does not articulate “the inner drama of freedom” in Christian terms of grace and election, but instead gives a “philosophical analagon of redemption” in the reading of ciphers (RJPR 638).

For Jaspers, the cipher of Transcendence is likewise the analogue of, or substitute for, the mediating Logos in Christianity. Likewise, conscience takes the place of prayer. Prayer, for Jaspers, is typically an attempt to secure peace and contentment on the cheap. He prefers instead a contemplation of a silent divinity, a hidden transcendence, but Ricoeur questions the status of this transcendence, whether it is truly transcendent: “Separated from the context of invocations, the tension between Transcendence and *Existenz* can be resolved into an inner tension between the self who asks and the self who answers, between the hidden depth of my subjectivity and the acts 

*[p. 165]* ^pg182

and experiences which give currency in everyday life to those riches which are inaccessible to the spirit of lucidity” (RJPR 639). The contrast between ciphers and the Christian Logos is instructive here: Jaspers’ immanent transcendence is a counterfeit of divine transcendence, which is not preserved by an empty, formless void beyond ciphers, but by the God whose transcendence is personal and to whom one can pray. 

Given this critique of Jaspers, it is surprising that Ricoeur will later make arguments that are not that different from Jaspers. It is possible to read Ricoeur’s later work, much like Jaspers, as offering a “philosophical analagon of redemption” in the reading of ciphers—that is, religious texts—and developing a kind of philosophical faith by integrating “religion into philosophy by means of myth,” and reducing religion to aesthetics, poetics, or hermeneutics. When Ricoeur starts to talk about vague ideas like “Essential” and the “fundamental,”<sup>7</sup> we might suspect his concept of transcendence is something like “an obscure ground in the self.”

This question is similar to the one we asked regarding Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of revelation: Who is speaking in the biblical texts? Here we have a related question about the nature of transcendence as a Thou, as personal. Ricoeur increasingly moves toward a phenomenology of the sacred and a hermeneutics of the symbols that manifest the sacred. Is this a shift toward a more impersonal idea of God? Let us examine some more texts. 

### Heidegger, Heraclitus, and Job

Just as Ricoeur’s hermeneutics shifts to a more impersonal focus on the text, so his ontology shifts toward a more impersonal conception of transcendence. Some of this is due to the increasing influence of Heidegger. We see this with Heidegger’s idea of the *logos* as an impersonal event or gathering of Being. In Ricoeur’s essay “Religion, Atheism, and Faith,” he sketches a post-metaphysical account of faith and God. This sketch is based on a Heideggerian reading of the Book of Job. When all the speeches have concluded, God shows up and speaks out of the whirlwind. But rather than giving reasons to justify Job’s suffering, God points to a created order that is quite other than human beings: “Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Speak, if you have understanding” ([[Job 38#^38-4|Job 38:4]]). God addresses Job, but what Job encounters—what is operative in this revelation—is an impersonal display of being, with an “absence of personal concern” (RAF 89). Ricoeur compares this passage with Heidegger’s idea of “the disclosure of Being as the Logos which gathers together all things” (RAF 93), as well as the pre-Socratic understanding of logos as the power of gathering together and making manifest, and *physis* as “the Overpowering” (RAF 94). Given these 

*[p. 166]* ^pg183

similarities, Ricoeur suggests that “the Book of Job and the Fragments of Heraclitus say one and the same thing” (RAF 95).<sup>8</sup>

Ricoeur’s identification of Job and Heraclitus would surely startle Heidegger, who makes such a big deal of the fact that Heraclitus and other pre-Socratics saw *logos* as an impersonal unfolding of being, an impersonal event of gathering. For Heidegger, the history of *logos* goes astray when it turns ontic—that is, when it is identified with a being, Christ, the second person of the Trinity, through whom and for whom all things came into being ([[John 01#^1-3|John 1:3]]; [[Colossians 01#^1-16|Col 1:16]]).<sup>9</sup> These biblical texts suggests a Christological ontology in which all things are gathered and hold together in Christ. Ricoeur alludes to something like this in another essay from the late 1960s, “Freedom in the Light of Hope,” where he explains his reasons for maintaining the autonomy of philosophy vis-à-vis theology: “If there is only one *logos*, the *logos* of Christ requires of me as a philosopher nothing else than a more complete and perfect activation of reason: not more than reason, but *whole* reason” (CI 403). In other words, there are Christological reasons for doing philosophy as philosophy. Such an appeal to Christology is rare for Ricoeur, but it does suggest that at this point he still has an ontology rooted in transcendent personhood.

Note as well that the word that gathers in Job is also an interpersonal event of revelation. Ricoeur draws on Heidegger to elaborate on how this revelation occurs: “The word-event is a word-becoming of Being,” but this word is a word addressed to Job by God. “For Job the revelation of the whole is first not a sight but a voice. The Lord speaks; that is the essential. He does not speak of Job; he speaks to Job, and that is sufficient” (RAF 90). Ricoeur’s use of Heidegger here is therefore less a shift to an impersonal ontology, and more about overcoming a purely anthropocentric view of creation. This concern reappears three decades later in *Thinking Biblically*, where he writes of the need for a fuller theology of creation, in which we see “a divine solicitude that extends to the whole of creation” (TB 137).

The problem with identifying the Heraclitean logos with the word of God the creator is that, for Heraclitus, this gathering is violent, marked by *eris*—strife, discord—something Heidegger picks up on in his *Introduction to Metaphysics*. There is a world of difference between this and the way Job depicts the Creator’s loving care for the creation. God gives rain to lands “where no one lives, on the desert, which is empty of human life, to satisfy the waste and desolate land, and to make the ground put forth grass” ([[Job 38#^38-26|Job 38:26–17]]). Why tend grass no human may ever see? Out of the superabundance, the gratuity, of God’s love and delight for his creation.<sup>10</sup> The gathering of God’s word is love, not strife.

The challenge of “thinking creation” is bound up in these questions of transcendence, immanence, and divine personhood. How should we understand 

*[p. 167]* ^pg184

the relation between creation and the sacred dimension of being? And how should we understand this fundamental sacred order, especially in relation to the Word? We get the beginnings of an answer to the questions when we turn to our next text, Ricoeur’s essay “Manifestation and Proclamation.”

### Revelation as Manifestation and Proclamation

We have seen that Ricoeur’s early philosophy of religion bears the deep imprint of the theology of the Word (as found in Barth, Bultmann, Bonhoeffer, and the New Hermeneutic). This emphasis shifts to some degree when Ricoeur undertakes a wider examination of the phenomenology of the sacred (especially as found in Mircea Eliade) (FP 526). But if Ricoeur moves away from his earlier dependence on Barth,<sup>11</sup> he does not abandon his emphasis on the theology of the Word. One of the distinctive features of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion is his attempt to mediate between these two lines of thought, incorporating both the proclamation of the kerygma and the manifestation of the sacred, as these are “the two poles of the religious” (FS 56).

Ricoeur uses the term “manifestation” to describe the way fundamental reality manifests itself as the sacred, the numinous. This sacred dimension of reality is not given immediately, but in symbols. The elements of the cosmos—earth, sky, water, trees, stones—become symbols of the sacred, and these symbols are the language in which the holy is manifest (FP 7, 14). In itself, water does not manifest the holy, but through the mediation of discourse it acquires a symbolic significance. A word of consecration, invocation, or “mythic commentary . . . *declares* the cosmic expressiveness, thanks to the double meaning of the words earth, heaven, water, life, etc. The world’s expressiveness achieves language through symbol as double meaning” (FP 14–15). The religious symbol is also not a subjective imposition on reality, but is responsive to different aspects of being, so that the symbol discloses new possible meanings about the cosmos and our place therein.

Ricoeur then uses the term “proclamation” to describe the pronouncement of a prophetic or kerygmatic word, which is not a manifestation of being or a fundamentally sacred dimension of reality. In the New Testament, for instance, the parables of Jesus have an essentially profane rather than sacred character, involving thoroughly mundane rather than mythological figures and settings. Rather than appealing to the sacred orders of the cosmos, these parables redescribe reality, opening the new reality that is the kingdom of God (FS 57–58). The same is true for the proverbs and eschatological sayings of the New Testament: They are hyperbolic, paradoxical, limit expressions that “burst” open the universe (FS 60–61).

*[p. 168]* ^pg185

The original instance of proclamation is ancient Israelite religion, especially in the genre of prophecy. From the prophetic perspective, the sacred of Ancient Near Eastern religion was idolatrous: YHWH is not to be identified with anything in creation, but only in YHWH’s covenant with Israel. This covenant requires an all-out rejection of neighboring Canaanite cults (FS 55). The world is not nature or cosmos, but the gift of *creation*. The prohibition against worshiping the sun, moon, and stars ([[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^4-19|Deut. 4:19]]) is based on the fact that they too are creatures, called into being by the word of the Creator ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-14|Gen. 1:14–15]]).<sup>12</sup> Thus, in Hebraic faith, word takes precedence over the numinous. There are still manifestations like the burning bush addressing Moses, or YHWH’s revelation at Sinai, but in these accounts “the numinous is just the underlying canvas from which the word detaches itself” (FS 56). Israelite religion is organized around discourse, around the word of revelation, so that hierophanies withdraw as “the instruction through the Torah overcomes any manifestation through an image. A theology of the Name is opposed to any hierophanies of an idol” (FS 56). This effects a reorientation from aesthetics to ethics, and likewise from cosmic time to historical time. In Hebraic faith, myth and ritual are not imitations of cosmic patterns or the rhythms of nature but instead repetitions and reactualizations of YHWH’s saving actions in history.<sup>13</sup>

This critique of idolatry is renewed in the New Testament, in the epistle to the Romans, where Paul writes:

That which is known about God is manifest (*phaneron*) within them; for God made it manifest (*ephanerōsen*) to them. For since the creation of the world his invisible attributes, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly seen, being understood through what has been made, so that they are without excuse. For even though they knew God, they did not honor him as God or give thanks, but they became futile in their speculations, and their foolish heart was darkened. Professing to be wise, they became fools, and exchanged the glory of the incorruptible God for an image in the form of corruptible man and of birds and four-footed animals and crawling creatures. (1:19–23, translation slightly altered)

This critique of idolatry is significant here because of its intensity—this idolatry is the basis for divine wrath against humanity’s unjust suppression of truth (1:18); but it is also significant for Paul’s introduction of the idea of manifestation, the phenomenal showing of the divine. This passage is sometimes presented in terms of a natural or general revelation, or in the Reformed tradition, common grace. As Werner Jeanrond observes, Ricoeur has a prominent conception of general revelation.<sup>14</sup> Ricoeur also has a distinct sense of the danger of idolatry, though his critique of it is adapted from Barth, Bultmann, and Bonhoeffer and is direct as an internal critique of his own Christian tradition, not outward toward other religions. 

*[p. 169]* ^pg186

The prophetic critique of the sacred is also central to the philosophy of Levinas, who draws a sharp distinction between the sacred (*sacré*) and the holy (*saint*). The sacred is characteristic of paganism, which is susceptible to violence and cruelty, as evidenced by its rituals of sorcery and blood sacrifice. The pagan sacred recurs in various strands of Romanticism, including Heidegger’s philosophy and its sense that rootedness in place gives insight into Being. Levinas saw Heidegger’s ontology as complicit in Nazism and its mythology of blood, folk, and soil.<sup>15</sup> The sense of the sacred is not always a benign wonder and reverence. As he writes in *Difficult Freedom*: “The mystery of things is the source of all cruelty towards men.”<sup>16</sup> Against this immanent sacred order, Levinas points to the wholly other Good in Judaism—in the prophets, and especially the prohibition from Sinai, which issues the commandment “thou shalt not kill.” The Good disrupts the sacred and its violence. It “destroys without leaving souvenirs, without transporting into museums the altars raised to the idols of the past for blood sacrifices, it burns the sacred groves in which the echoes of the past reverberate” (OBBE 18). Levinas is thus not one for trying to re-enchant the world. In fact, he takes a favorable view of modern technology and its disenchanting, desacralizing effects because they overthrow the pagan sacred and its conception of Being. You can’t get more un-Heideggerian than that.

Ricoeur, on the other hand, is less sanguine about this desacralization. He is also less confident that religion can fully uproot itself from the logic of manifestation. A certain iconoclastic perspective argues that we live in a “desacralized” world, and therefore religious faith can only survive by accommodating the decline of the sacred. As a case in point Ricoeur cites Bultmann, who maintains that authentic modern faith requires that we “free the kerygmatic kernel from its sacred husk” (FS 61–62). Ricoeur also cites Bonhoeffer’s musings regarding a “non-religious” interpretation of Christianity as a descralizing move. Ricoeur takes this interpretation as more than a merely exegetical approach to texts, but as a full-scale liquidation of the sacred from the life, observances, preaching, and public engagement of the church community (FS 63). This is actually a misreading of Bonhoeffer,<sup>17</sup> but Ricoeur’s larger point is that radical models of proclamation are flawed insofar as they exclude the logic of manifestation. Ricoeur is sympathetic to Levinas as well as these Protestant theologies of the Word, but he is not satisfied with a purely iconoclastic view of proclamation. There are two main reasons for this:

First, Ricoeur does not accept that the world has been thoroughly desacralized or disenchanted. A purely profane life is impossible. Humans cannot live “without some originary orientation” to privileged places, rites of passage and initiation, and times, such as festivals (FS 64). 

*[p. 170]* ^pg187

Second, Ricoeur thinks a purely profane faith is impossible. Religion needs some rooting in manifestation—biblical religion included. Ricoeur argues that the biblical texts are not simply kerygma cut off from the sacred. Instead, the kerygma dialectically resituates the sacred. In the context of Hebraic faith, proclamation issues a deep polemic against the sacred, as is evident in the Hebraic prophets and their attack on the cults, idols, and myths of Israel’s neighbors (FS 55). At the same time, Israelite religion is still attuned to the numinous (e.g., the burning bush or the revelation at Sinai), there are still sacred spaces (the temple) and times (Sabbath, festivals), and the heavens declaring the glory of God. The Israelite innovation is that all these are reoriented by the proclamations of covenant, law, and the prophets. 

Something similar is true of Christianity. In the prologue to John’s gospel there is a reconciliation, even identification of manifestation and Word. John testifies that “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we saw his glory” ([[John 01#^1-14|John 1:14]]). The sacraments of baptism and the Lord’s Supper are also Christian forms of manifestation. The point is not that water, bread, and wine are intrinsically sacred, but that the Word constitutes them as elements of manifestation. In this, Ricoeur sees a kind of “repetition or reenactment” that mediates the sacred and the kerygmatic. There is no viable “Christianity without the sacred.” The kerygmatic Word abolishes the sacred, but it also dialectically reaffirms, repeats, refigures it. The address of the Word radically reconstitutes us, but it also has a transformative power that is grounded in our “essential relation” to the sacred (FS 65). This is what the sacraments do, accomplishing “the mutation of sacred ritual into the kerygmatic realm.” Similarly, the symbolism of death and rebirth returns in “kerygmatic discourse regarding conversion.” Ricoeur notes that it is possible to speak of conversion in more profane, existential or ethical terms, such as limit situations and decisions in the moment of crisis, but unless this decision reactualizes the “older symbolic depths of death and resurrection, of the return to the maternal womb and rebirth,” our understanding of conversion will fade into profane banalities, with little existential significance or vitality. Without these symbols of “regeneration, new creation, and the advent of a new being and a new world,” the word “conversion” would cease to mean much of anything (FS 67). 

Ricoeur agrees that faith must work through the disenchanting logic of modernity, which is necessary for Christian faith to be mature rather than immature and idolatrous. But the language of this “mature” faith cannot be reduced without remainder to the language of ethics, social justice, or existential concern. The life of faith is founded by a Word, which the community encounters in the biblical texts. The life of faith is nourished by these texts, and this nourishment comes from its roots in the experience of the sacred and the fundamental mysteries of being. This nourishment feeds the imagination and discloses a new way of being, for the self and for the community.

### The God of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Spinoza?

The tension between transcendence and immanence is also present in Ricoeur’s attempt to reconcile his philosophy of the transcendent word with the immanent metaphysics of Spinoza. In a late interview with Richard Kearney, Ricoeur observes that he “no longer subscribe\[s\] to the typically anti-metaphysical Protestant lineage of Karl Barth,” and that he also feels greater freedom to mix philosophy and theology in a way he previously did not.<sup>18</sup> Through most of his career Ricoeur takes the line of Pascal, who famously distinguishes the God of the philosophers from the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Ricoeur also echoes Heidegger’s famous critique of ontotheology, arguing that when we speak of God in terms of the idea of Infinity, of Being, and Perfection, this philosophical God “is not somebody to whom we can pray, it is not somebody with whom we can enter into a personal relation, but a concept.”<sup>19</sup>

Ricoeur reflects this idea in his commentary on [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-14|Exodus 3:14]], where he considers the translation and interpretation of YHWH’s self-naming: *‘ehyeh* *‘ašer ‘eheyeh*. Through the Septuagint’s translation as *ego eimi ho ôn* and then the Latin *sum qui sum*, this name is often taken as an identification of God with Being: *I am that I am* (TB 331). Ricoeur argues that this name is not an ontological statement or abstract identification of the divine essence—a “metaphysics of the Name”—but instead “belongs within the ethical orbit of the mandate” (TB 337). That is, this name is revealed in a vocation story (TB 335–36). This story is a preeminent instance of the call and response, as YHWH calls Moses out of the burning bush: “Moses! Moses!” to which Moses responds, “Here I am.” God then identifies himself as “the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob” ([[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-4|Ex. 3:4–6]]). 

God names himself, but this self-identification is not enough for the newly commissioned Moses. If the Israelites ask him for the name of the God who has sent him, what should he say? André Lacocque points out that in this Ancient Near Eastern context, this question is less innocent than it appears to us, as it could betray an agenda “to appropriate ‘the unknown name of power’ of God for the sake of using it as a shield to protect himself from adversity in Egypt.” It is a request for “a Name of power.” In response, God gives a name from which no magical power can be derived: *I will be what I will be*; *I will become what I will become*. This is not the name of a magical incantation, an idol, or an abstract ontological essence. This is a God who is “personal and invocable; one can call upon his name, one can pray and address him as a ‘thou’” (TB 309–11). According to Lacocque, the name of this personal God is therefore a promise of God’s “active dynamic presence” (TB 314, 

*[p. 172]* ^pg189

316). God is committing to a “shared history between God and humanity, to a ‘becoming’ of God with Israel” (TB 323). God thus makes himself vulnerable by binding himself to the human. God ceases to be impassible, but is “‘emotional,’ changing, affected.” He salvages the “risky wager” he lost at creation through his relation with Israel, which has an eschatological goal that the NT sees fulfilled in the coming of Christ (TB 324–25, 329).

Ricoeur offers his commentary on [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-14|Exodus 3:14]] as a complement to Lacocque’s essay, and follows him in reading the text in terms of this revisionary metaphysics: God’s Being as becoming, as dynamic opening toward the future. At the same time, Ricoeur is “doubtful” whether any modern translation can forgo the verb “being,” and furthermore observes that there are resources in medieval thinkers, who at their most “audacious” are completely opposed to ontological abstraction (TB 335–36). This is an apt rebuttal to the facile vilification of everything before Nietzsche and Heidegger as “ontotheological.” Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, and Aquinas read Exodus 3 as God’s direct self-revelation, and did not confuse this with philosophical speculation nor suppose that such speculation “would reveal to human reason the secret of the divine essence in the intimacy of its innermost nature” (TB 341–42). Instead of an idolatrous fusion of God and Being, Ricoeur sees “a convergence that respects the misalignment between the philosophical and the biblical names” (TB 356). Ricoeur suggests that “the Hebrews thought being in a new way,” and that the “enigmatic Hebrew formula”—*‘ehyeh* *‘ašer ‘eheyeh*—“continues to give rise to thought” (TB 360–61). In other words, there is a surplus of meaning given to thinking, for the believer and the philosopher alike.

Ricoeur wants to think God and Being in new ways, in contrast to Levinas, who sees God as entirely otherwise than Being in an ethics of the Good beyond Being, and Jean-Luc Marion, who seeks to return to the first name of God as love rather than Being, as a divine gift that “does not have to be, since it suffices that it be given.”<sup>20</sup> Ricoeur is not convinced that philosophy can think without some concept of being; the trick is to find one that is not totalizing or ontotheological. Thus in his essay “From One Testament to the Other,” Ricoeur attempts to trace a line of continuity from Exodus 3 to [[1 John 04#^4-8|1 John 4:8]]: “God is love.” This reading shows that instead of a God without being, “the Johannine formula becomes more significant when interpreted as an expansion of the Hebraic formula.”<sup>21</sup> In other words, “God is love” expands “I am who I am” by allowing us to “*think more*” about God: who God has promised to be is *love*.<sup>22</sup> This “love is a self-divestment of the self for another which confers on the first self a dimension it would not have if it had remained alone with itself.” Ricoeur draws this point from Jüngel, who describes love as the “unity of life and death for the sake of life,” something we see in God’s suffering and death in the narrative of the cross.<sup>23</sup> This love is not simple 

*[p. 173]* ^pg190

self-divestment, but also a greater self-relatedness within the persons of the Trinity, and in relation to the rest of creation. At the same time, this God *is* love—that is, God exists as love. What results is a “biblical ontology,” which is *sui generis*, not subjugated to an abstract philosophical ontology.<sup>24</sup>

The question that arises at this point is how this biblical ontology fits with Ricoeur’s Spinozism. Ricoeur later says to Kearney: “I am not sure about the absolute irreconcilability between the God of the Bible and the God of Being (understood with Jean Nabert as ‘primary affirmation’ or with Spinoza as ‘*substantia actuosa*’). The tendency of modern French thought to eclipse the Middle Ages has prevented us from acknowledging certain very rich attempts to think God and being in terms of each other.”<sup>25</sup> Ricoeur acknowledges the medieval resources untapped by a lot of Continental philosophy of religion, but then he also appeals to Spinoza. These are very different ways of thinking about God and being, and it is not entirely clear where this would lead Ricoeur.

Kearney himself has picked up and run with Ricoeur’s suggestions, developing his idea of God as *posse*—as the primacy of divine possibility over actuality—in works like *The God Who May Be* and *Anatheism*. In a late fragment Ricoeur himself cites Kearney’s view as a possible path: “Perhaps a God of the posse? (Richard K.)” (LD 82). 

There are also those who take Ricoeur in a more explicitly Spinozistic direction. The most notable example is Pamela Sue Anderson, who opts for “an ‘intellectual love of God’ synonymous with belief in ‘Nature’ as eternal, infinite, and active being,” as opposed to “a personal Christian God to whom human subjects (must) submit in suffering.”<sup>26</sup> Ricoeur appeals to Spinoza’s idea of God as *substantia actuosa*, which Anderson translates as “dynamic matter” to indicate Spinoza’s idea of *Deus sive Natura* as the active, infinite, immanent cause of all things. For Spinoza, there is no substance other than God, and “whatever is, is in God.”<sup>27</sup> Particular finite things are “nothing but affections of the attributes of God, that is, modes wherein the attributes of God find expression in a definite and determinate way.”<sup>28</sup> This *substantia actuosa* can be equally described as God, Nature, Life, in which we all have our being.

James Carter also reads Ricoeur in this way, arguing that instead of conceiving of God as “infinitely ‘Other,’” Ricoeur adopts Spinoza’s conception of God as Life, “a single dynamic substance in which we all inhere,” and which underlies our human capability. “In Life, to be human is to participate in this whole of which one is an inextricable and irreplaceable component.” On this reading, Ricoeur offers a “moral religion” of human capability—a thin, broad civil religion grounded in “reverence for *Life* as that which binds us all together as capable human beings, irrespective of particular religious traditions.”<sup>29</sup>

*[p. 174]* ^pg191

One of the merits of this reading of Ricoeur is that it makes sense of his comments regarding Spinoza, as well as comments in the 1990s on religion as a response to the “fundamental.” Ricoeur uses this term to describe the experience of fundamental mysteries of being like love, the birth of a child, battlefield experiences, loss of a loved one, and one’s own death (CC 170). Ricoeur also connects it to Fichte and Schelling’s idea of the *Grund/Abgrund*, the “groundless ground,” an abyss that is also a foundation. This is an infinite source that dwells within the finite capacity of the human being (RSV 3). We will return to Schelling below.

One of the difficulties with the thoroughgoing Spinozistic reading, however, is to show how it fits with Ricoeur’s biblical hermeneutics, including these late essays where Ricoeur emphasizes the idea of God as transcendent and personal.<sup>30</sup> That said, even if we recognize that the Spinozistic reading of Ricoeur is rather one-sided, there is still the question of how exactly Ricoeur sees the biblical God as compatible with Spinoza’s God. One possibility is to follow Spinoza’s hermeneutics and eliminate whatever biblical texts do not fit his metaphysics. Although Ricoeur tends toward rationalism in downplaying or demythologizing aspects of the text that he finds problematic, he never goes full Spinoza.

Another possibility is that Ricoeur sees these texts as expressions of the religious imagination, the human in response to the mystery of the fundamental. Garrett Green describes Ricoeur's hermeneutics as a kind of “semiotic mysticism,” which seeks to “apprehend anew ‘the manifestation of Being,’” and as a “kind of natural theology of symbols, one which presupposes a primordial ‘sacred’ reality that manifests itself symbolically in a variety of religious forms.”<sup>31</sup> This reading also makes sense of Ricoeur’s evasiveness about identifying God as the speaker who performs divine speech acts. That said, Ricoeur does allow for something like divine speech, as we discussed in chapter 5. The difficulty is that Ricoeur wants to have it both ways—the God of the Bible and the God of Spinoza. This is a trademark of Ricoeur’s thought, and one of his most attractive qualities as a thinker: He finds reconciliation where others see opposition. The danger of this kind of thinking, however, is that it just might overlook an insurmountable either/or.

I believe this is the case with Spinoza, whose impersonal monism simply cannot be reconciled with the God of the Bible. For Spinoza, God is perfect and therefore does not act with an end in view, for that means God desires something he lacks. Instead, God acts out of necessity. All things are “predetermined by God, not from his free will or absolute pleasure, but from the absolute nature of God, his infinite power.”<sup>32</sup> This stands in contrast to a God who created all things for his good pleasure ([[Revelation 04#^4-11|Rev. 4:11]]); the biblical God does not act out of necessity but from the abundance of love. God’s 

*[p. 175]* ^pg192

desire for humans is not a desire of lack but an eschatological desire, arising from the surplus of love. Created being is not the outworking of divine necessity but a gracious gift. In addition to God’s creative act, God speaks and acts in the world. Whereas Spinoza denies any and all contingency,<sup>33</sup> these revelatory acts are entirely contingent—unnecessary, gracious acts of love.

If Ricoeur wants to break with ontotheology, then Spinoza is hardly the philosopher for the job. Ontotheology is not simply thinking God with Being. It is the attempt to use God as the ground that makes the whole of Being an intelligible totality. This is why Heidegger writes that “the deity can come into philosophy only insofar as philosophy, of its own accord and by its own nature, requires and determines that and how the deity enters into it.”<sup>34</sup> Alongside Hegel’s system, Spinoza’s conception of God is the supreme ontotheological gesture, for there is no divine revelation or mystery that is not cut to the measure of immanent rational necessity. Nor is there any ethical command that undermines human autonomy. As Merold Westphal observes, for Spinoza “epistemically and ethically the human remains the highest point of reference.”<sup>35</sup> This is one point where Ricoeur and Spinoza don’t fit very well, since Ricoeur advances a model of theonomy that can account for the logic of superabundant love.<sup>36</sup>

This issue of love is also a key point, since all the participants in this debate want to see God as the source of love. But does the “love \[of\] God” arise from an immanent or transcendent source? On Anderson’s reading of Spinoza, “‘to love God’ is not to trust in the benevolent purposes of a transcendent creator—the purposes for which we await satisfaction. Instead, it is the mind’s joyful recognition of itself as corporate, that is, as part of the unified whole of nature that expresses an intellectual love of ‘God.’” The core of this love is “the joy of continued bodily existence,” and per Anderson, this embodied participation in *deus sive natura* “is a more sure grounding for our loves than the glorification of unsatisfied desire for a wholly transcendent and disembodied divine.”<sup>37</sup> This description may have some rhetorical force, both in its caricature of traditional theism and in its depiction of the warm glow of divine holism, but Spinozistic Nature is not ultimately a credible ground for love. This God neither loves nor hates nor suffers because he is fully actual and never experiences the passivity of the passions. This God is an impersonal, immanent ground for all those persons who love,<sup>38</sup> and if the love between persons is just an expression<sup>39</sup> of an impersonal ground, then what is most fundamentally real is not personal but impersonal—and thus it is not really love. This divine substance is governed by logical necessity, not the paradoxical logic of the gift. There are no persons, no real difference, and, lacking both, no real love in this substance.

### Glimpses of the Trinity?

Ricoeur’s efforts to maintain both transcendence and immanence, both personal and impersonal manifestations of God, would find surer footing in a Trinitarian conception of God: namely, as the Creator who calls into being through his creative Word and addresses this world through the incarnation of the second person of the Trinity (the Logos, the Son), and as the third person (the Spirit), who is present in creation in a general way as immanent source of preservation of creation as well as the sanctification of the ecclesial community.

Ricoeur’s writings will frustrate most any attempt to identify a strongly Trinitarian background to his thinking, but could it be that a Trinitarian theology is operative in the background, as the unsaid of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion? That is true of his essay “On the Entanglement of Speech and Writing in Biblical Discourse,” but in general Ricoeur is too guarded to present a robustly Trinitarian theology. I am suggesting that this theological framework would provide the most coherent way to understand his reflections on God and Being. Ricoeur points to the biblical texts, especially Exodus 3 and 1 John 4, where we see a God who requires a new thinking of being—one that must be understood in terms of covenant, relations, and promise. Yet Ricoeur also suggests this God might be compatible with the Spinozistic view of God as *substantia actuosa*. The only way these two could be compatible is if Spinoza’s divine substance is also rethought in terms of personal transcendence. We see something like this in *Oneself as Another*, where Ricoeur describes a relation to this “primordial power that Spinoza continues to name ‘God’” as a dependence that is both “vertical and immanent” (OA 316). The vertical dimension has perplexed some scholars, who recognize that the vertical is foreign to Spinoza.<sup>40</sup> This could be a mistaken reading of Spinoza. But it could also be an attempt to open Spinozistic substance to a higher—and thus deeper—source of love and personhood than purely immanent substance can sustain.

That suggestion may open *substantia actuosa* to transcendence, but what about personhood? Here we need to consider Schelling and his metaphysics of the divine, which Ricoeur also cites in describing his idea of the fundamental as the “groundless ground” of Being. Schelling is seeking the original ground of all things—the ground that is prior to all ground, an *Ungrund*. It is the Absolute, the undifferentiated ground prior to all oppositions of being and nonbeing, real and ideal, light and darkness.<sup>41</sup> This sounds like an impersonal pantheism, but Schelling insists that while that may be true for Spinoza and Fichte, he conceives of God in terms of personhood. God is “a living unity of forces,” but this unity acts freely rather than from necessity.<sup>42</sup> God is personal, Schelling insists, and human persons are free insofar as they participate 

*[p. 177]* ^pg194

in God. “Only man is in God and capable of freedom exactly through this being-in-God \[*in-Gott-sein*\].”<sup>43</sup>

How, then, does this personhood arise? Personhood is not originary, Schelling admits: “In the non-ground or indifference there is admittedly no personality.” “But,” he quickly replies, “is the beginning point really the whole?”<sup>44</sup> God becomes personal through the bond with nature. “The first beginning for the creation is the yearning of the One to give birth to itself or the will of the ground.” This is a primordial, unconscious desire to be.<sup>45</sup> The second beginning “is the will of love, whereby the word is spoken out into nature and through which God first makes himself personal.”<sup>46</sup> Personhood is therefore still originally derived from the impersonal.

Ricoeur could point to Schelling’s later speculations on the Trinity, but that is an interpretive tangle of its own, and, regardless, it is Schelling’s idea of the groundless ground that Ricoeur cites. Ricoeur’s late hermeneutics of religion does not give much of a doctrine of the Trinity. He has a fairly low Christology, which stops short of embracing the full scandal—and power—of the Word become flesh. Instead, he takes up the phrase the “humanity of God” from Barth and Jüngel, only to suggest a kind of adoptionism to describe the relation between the Father and Son: “God has shown his love in being identified with the man Jesus.”<sup>47</sup> This relation between Father and Son is a “process of differentiation and re-identification” that Christian theology later “hypostatised. . .by speaking of God as Triune.”<sup>48</sup> Instead of identifying the persons as hypostases of/within the Triune God, Ricoeur identifies the Trinity as the hypostatization of a process.

Ricoeur recognizes that the identification of God as love ([[1 John 04#^4-8|I John 4:8]]) gives a personal source for love, but he also identifies love as a process of self-divestment that is not explicitly attributed to God. Ricoeur finds this in the famous hymn to love in I Corinthians 13. The apostle describes love as patient, kind, that it keeps no records of wrongs, it endures all things. And of the three that remain—faith, hope, and love—“the greatest of these is love” ([[1 Corinthians 13#^13-13|I Cor. 13: 13]]). Ricoeur suggests that “it is not important to know who is the lover and who is the beloved” in this hymn, but that suggestion overlooks the fact that this personal source is named in I John 4.<sup>49</sup> 

Ricoeur returns to this love hymn in the Epilogue to *Memory, History, Forgetting*, observing once again that love is not named as a “someone, at least not in the first stage of thinking, but a ‘spiritual gift’—a ‘charisma’—granted by the Holy Spirit” (MHF 467–68). The Holy Spirit is traditionally conceived as a *someone*, as the third person of the Trinity. Ricoeur is not always clear on this,<sup>50</sup> so it could be that he conceives of the Spirit impersonally. It could also be that a subsequent, overtly theological “stage of thinking” would point to a conception of the Holy Spirit as the personal source of this more generally 

*[p. 178]* ^pg195

available *charism*, just as the general logos has its ground in the personal Logos who is Christ (CI 403).

Ricoeur makes a similar point in *Memory, History, Forgetting* regarding the question of “difficult forgiveness.” How is forgiveness possible? Most pointedly, how can we forgive the unforgiveable? This would seem to be a figure of the *im*possible, and yet, Ricoeur observes, *there is forgiveness*. This proclamation resounds “like a voice from above. . . . It is a silent voice yet not a mute one,” and its appropriate mode of discourse is the hymn, much like the hymn to love. The hymn is a “discourse of praise and celebration. It says: *il y a, es gibt*, there is . . . forgiveness—the form of the universal designating *illéité*. There is forgiveness as there is joy, as there is wisdom, extravagance, love. Love precisely.” Ricoeur’s allusion to the *il y a*, the *es gibt*, is significant because these are impersonal grounds of Being.<sup>51</sup> Is this true for love as well? And can forgiveness find a satisfactory ground in this impersonal source if there is not a deeper personal source of love and forgiveness?

We might return to I John 4, where we see a rich movement between love as grounded in person. It is ironic that Ricoeur, who typically states his preference for reading biblical texts over the high-level abstraction of theology, focuses solely on “God is love” (v.8) without reading the verse in the context of the pericope (vv.7–21) as a whole. Participation in human love is a participation in divine love. “We love because he first loved us” (v.19). This love is grounded not in impersonal substance but in personal being—namely, in the love of the Father who sent his Son as savior of the world (v.14). This sending would be a great expansion to Ricoeur’s commentary on the text, since it is the most concrete expression of what it means to say that God is love: “God’s love was revealed among us in this way: God sent his only Son into the world so that we might live through him. In this is love, not that we loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the atoning sacrifice for our sins. Beloved, since God loved us so much, we also ought to love one another” (vv.9–11). 

Phenomenologically, it makes sense to describe the experience of love as a relation in which we participate: *there is* love. Yet thinking biblically also entails an interpretation of what the texts give us to think, and in this case the text gives us a profound interweaving of human love with divine love. The text also gives us reason to think of the source of love not as impersonal *illéité* but as grounded in the interpersonal love of the Triune God—the Father who sent his Son into the world out of love. Ricoeur’s rather unenthusiastic comments about the Trinity as a hypostatization of a process, his low Christology, and his pneumatology that borders on the impersonal do not support the suggestion that Trinitarian theology is the hidden source of his thinking. What I am suggesting, however, is that a fully Trinitarian theology would provide the framework that would give coherence to his attempt to 

*[p. 179]* ^pg196

reconcile the transcendent personhood of God with more impersonal, immanent manifestations of the sacred. It would also give a more compelling hermeneutic of the biblical testimony that God is love.
