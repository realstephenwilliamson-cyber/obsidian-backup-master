*Abstract* 

This dissertation focuses on the conception of the self in the medieval Hindu traditions of non-dual Śaivism in Kashmir, which flourished from around the 9th to 13th centuries CE. Through a study of symbols and images connected primarily with water and to a lesser extent with the heart and drama found in scriptural texts and commentaries, this paper seeks to explore different aspects of the Kashmiri Śaiva self. The central doctrine is that one[illegible]s true identity is that of the all-encompassing unified consciousness, personified by Śiva. The diversity of life experienced is explained by a great cosmic play of consciousness which unfolds the manifest world through a process of emanation of itself. Through awareness of one[illegible]s innate authentic self, one can experience true human nature as that which is inseparable from absolute Śiva consciousness. Given the esoteric nature of these teachings, symbols and images play a powerful role in conveying how the traditions conceive the self. These literary devices have the power to express depths of meaning that may defy the limits of less poetic language particularly in dealing with paradoxical notions and a mode of being beyond the distinctions of subject and object, real and imaginary.

Introduction and Methodology 

Common to the non-dual Śaiva traditions of Kashmir is the concept that the self is ultimately identified with Śiva, which represents the one absolute reality (Flood, 2006: 147). Rich symbolism and imagery can be found in foundational texts of the traditions, expressing different aspects of the self. Through a study of select symbols and images, this paper seeks to explore the meaning of the self in these traditions: the nature of human existence, the relationship of man with the world and the experience of the true self. The primary sources of this study are the *Śivasūtra, Spandakārikā* and Kṣemarāja's *Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam* along with their commentaries.<sup>1</sup> While there are many images and symbols connected with the self of monistic Śaivism in Kashmir, this paper is primarily focused on symbolism of water and to a lesser degree symbolism of the heart and imagery of drama. Deification and mantra are two fundamental themes with great symbolic significance for the non-dual Śaiva self, but which are, however, outside the scope of this study. Water and the heart are important symbols figuring in earlier Vedic and Brahmāṇic texts, while images associated with drama may also have been inspired by earlier religious and cultural traditions, including ancient Indian drama. The non-dual Śaiva traditions of Kashmir

Non-dual Śaivism, comprising of a group of tantric traditions, rose in the 9\* century in Kashmir in opposition to the dualistic Śaiva Siddhānta traditions (Sanderson, 2005c: 8047). The non-dual traditions are also referred to non-Saiddhāntika traditions and often in general scholarship as Kashmir Śavisim (Flood, 2006: 55). The term ‘Kashmir Śavisim’ has been contested by Sanderson on two fronts. Firstly, other forms of Śaivism co-existed alongside the non-dual traditions, notably the aforementioned dualistic Śaiva Siddhānta flourished in Kashmir at that time and secondly, it gives the false impression that non-dual Śaivism comprised of a single unified tradition (Sanderson, 2009b: 123). According to Sanderson, in the 9\* century CE the more conservative dualistic Śaiva Siddhānta tradition dominated in the Kashmiri valley in terms of doctrinal following, while a tradition which worshipped Svacchandabhairava and his consort Aghoreśvarī represented the main Śaiva cult (2005d: 8047). Symbolism of Water and the Kashmiri Śaiva Self

The Symbol of Water

In mythical and religious narratives across cultures, images and symbols of water have been employed to express varying characteristics of creativity, fluidity, fecundity, purification in addition to darker and paradoxical aspects such as destruction, the void and death (Rudhardt: 2005, 9697-9704). A reoccurring theme is the symbolism of water as primordial source: life-giving and life-sustaining. Proferes, drawing from the work of Schlerath, identities several gods of the early Vedic texts, such as Soma, Fire, Varuṇa and Indra, which ‘reside in the water, generate rain, or set the cosmic stream free to flow to earth for the benefit of humankind’ (Proferes, 2007: 78, Schlerath, 1960:106, 148-152). Eliade sees the ocean as representative of the ‘great reservoir or sacrality’, pointing to the Upaniṣṣadic myth of the Goddess Devi, who, herself born in the sea, is both mother and principle of the gods and the universe (Eliade, 1969: 350- 351, Oppert, 1893: 425-426). As Rudhardt has shown (2005: 9686), a similar theme is found in the Rgveda, where water is seen to exist before the rest of the cosmos; the text reads:

Neither Non-Being nor Being existed then.

Neither air nor the firmament above existed.

What was moving with such force? Where? Under whose care?

Was it the deep and fathomless water? (Rgveda 10.121.1)”

The True Self as the Great Lake

For the non-dual Śaiva traditions, pure consciousness equates to the true self, the one ultimate reality. This essential doctrinal teaching is announced in the first aphorism of the Sivasūtra, which states: ‘The self is consciousness’ (1.1°). Explaining the perception of duality and a world of multicity is inevitably a complex task for the non-dual traditions. The Kashmiri non-dual Śaiva traditions teach that ‘things exist because they are perceived to exist’, an understanding which is also found in Advaita Vedānta and the Buddhist tradition of Yogācāra (Dyczkowski, 1992a: 3), albeit with differing views of why and how multicity is perceived. Dyczkowski identifies the distinguishing feature of non-dual Śaiva traditions as the understanding that the one ultimate reality, represented as Śiva, is a ‘universal egoity’, which one can experience directly (1992a: 3). Rich symbolism of water, particularly centred on images of lakes and ocean with their vastness and depth, plays a role in this task of explaining the concept of universal egoity. The imagery and symbolism of water found in authoritative texts of the traditions convey key features of the true self, this universal egoity. Notably, the qualities of purity, unity and infinity are expressed in addition to the fundamental nature of the true self as the source of all existence and all potentiality.

The image of pure consciousness as lake figures in two aphorisms of the Sivasūtra. Aphorism 110 1.23 reads ‘(The yogi) experiences the vitality of Mantra by contemplating the Great Lake’ +°, while aphorism 3.17 reads ‘(Comfortably) seated (the yogi) sinks effortlessly into the lake (of consciousness}. The Individual Self and the World of Diversity

The experience of the individual self is understood by the non-dual Śaiva traditions as caused by increasing levels of concealment of the true nature of reality brought about by the play of śakti powers. As Flood explains, these traditions adopted the Śaiva Siddhānta cosmology centred on thirty-six tattvas or categories of existence albeit with a non-dualistic metaphysical underpinning whereby there is only one reality and the diverse levels of reality perceived are brought about the manifestation of Śiva consciousness (2006: 127, 146). The tattvas form a hierarchy of all existence and experience whereby Śiva is concealed in increasing degrees from the subtle to the gross; at the top of the hierarchy Śiva is fully revealed as pure supreme consciousness (Flood, 2006: 126)?

. At the lower levels in the hierarchy one perceives ordinary awareness in contrast to the fullness and omnipresence of Šiva consciousness. As such, the limited individual self and one’s relationship with the world is a contraction of consciousness or a ‘modality of transcendence’ (Flood, 2004: 104).

The śaktis or powers, which are expressions of Śiva consciousness, bring about the manifest world and the experience of the individual self. According to Singh in his introduction to Kṣemarāja's Pratyabhijñāhrdayam, these śaktis are infinite in number, however, there are only five main ones (2011: 7) and three of the powers relate to one’s faculties of consciousness resulting in the experience of oneself as a temporal individual person. These three powers or faculties are: icchā, the will or desire, jāna, cognition and kriyā, action (Flood, 2003: 221). Bhāskara and Kṣemarāja show how wave analogies convey the operation of the śakti powers responsible for bringing about the experience of the limited individual self.

The Process of Perception

The metaphor of wave has also been used to explain the process of perception. The term ‘ābhāsa’ denotes any reflection or expression of consciousness which appears in one’s perception in a limited manner (Singh, 2011: 18). Singh explains the notion of ābhāsa in the Pratyabhijñāhrdayam by drawing on a wave analogy (Singh, 2011: 19). The concept of *svatantra,* found in the non-dual Śaiva texts, denotes the ultimate agency of Śiva; the term is translated as [illegible]absolute will[illegible] by Singh (2011: 169) and [illegible]creative autonomy[illegible] and [illegible]freedom[illegible] of consciousness by Dyczkowski (1992a: 57, 99). FIVE

Conclusion

The religious and philosophical teachings of non-dual Kashmiri Śaivism are complex and esoteric with one’s innate authentic nature being that of pure Siva consciousness, which projects forth the entire manifest world. The true self experiences no separation of self and other as all subjects and objects are contained within one’s ultimate identity of universal consciousness. Against the backdrop of non-duality, the abstract converges with the concrete and the imaginary with the real and as such poetic metaphors and analogies serve as powerful vehicles to give expression to the inexpressible. The image of the ‘Great Lake’ revealed to the Sanskrit commentators various features and characteristics, which could articulate the teachings of their traditions including paradoxical positions. The image captures simultaneously unity and diversity, revelation and concealment, disturbance and tranquillity and the various interpretations together show, reflecting Clifford Geertz’s theory, that a symbol, laden with meaning, has the power to synthesise the whole teachings of a tradition, its understanding of the cosmos and the nature of man in the world. The image of the individual self as actor can serve as a ‘model for culture pattern’, providing guidelines on behaviour, while the symbol of heart may express the ineffable, the state of Śiva consciousness. Symbolism speaks to the imagination of the listener or reader as the semantic depth, like the ‘Great Lake’ is unfathomable. Returning to Bakhtin, unearthing new meanings of what might lie behind an image, is a work that will never be complete (2005: 6) and future readers of the non-dual Śaiva texts will undoubtedly offer new interpretations.
