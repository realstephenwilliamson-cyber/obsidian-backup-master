**1.** In [the era of] the Second Temple,[^1] the Greek kingdom[^2] issued decrees against the Jewish people, [attempting to] nullify their faith[^3] and refusing to allow them to observe the Torah and its commandments.[^4] They extended their hands against their property[^5] and their daughters;[^6] they entered the Sanctuary, wrought havoc within,[^7] and made the sacraments impure.[^8]  
The Jews suffered great difficulties from them, for they oppressed them greatly until the God of our ancestors had mercy upon them, delivered them from their hand, and saved them.[^9] The sons of the Hasmoneans, the High Priests, overcame [them], slew them, and saved the Jews from their hand.[^10]  
They appointed a king from the priests, and sovereignty returned to Israel for more than 200 years, until the destruction of the Second Temple.[^11] ^3-1

**2.** When the Jews overcame their enemies and destroyed them, they entered the Sanctuary; this was on the twenty-fifth of Kislev.[^12] They could not find any pure oil in the Sanctuary, with the exception of a single cruse. It contained enough oil to burn for merely one day. They lit the arrangement of candles from it for eight days[^13] until they could crush olives and produce pure oil.[^14] ^3-2

**3.** Accordingly, the Sages of that generation[^15] ordained that these eight days, which begin from the twenty-fifth of Kislev, should be commemorated[^16] to be days of happiness[^17] and praise [of God].[^18] Candles should be lit in the evening at the entrance to the houses on each and every one of these eight nights to publicize and reveal the miracle.[^19]  
These days are called Chanukah.[^20] It is forbidden to eulogize and fast on them, as on the days of Purim.[^21] Lighting the candles on these days is a Rabbinic mitzvah, like the reading of the Megillah.[^22] ^3-3

**4.** Whoever is obligated to read the Megillah is also obligated to kindle the Chanukah lamp.[^23] On the first night, a person lighting [the lamp] recites three blessings. They are:  
Blessed are You, God, our Lord, King of the universe, who has sanctified us with His commandments and has commanded us[^24] to light the Chanukah lamp.  
"...who wrought miracles for our ancestors...."[^25]  
"...who has granted us life, sustained us...."[^26]  
When a person who did not recite a blessing [on his own Chanukah lamp] sees a lamp,[^27] he should recite the latter two blessings.[^28] On subsequent nights, a person who kindles the lamp should recite two blessings and one who sees a lamp should recite one, for the blessing *Shehecheyanu* is recited only on the first night.[^29] ^3-4

**5.** On each and every one of these eight days, the entire *Hallel*[^30] is recited.[^31] Before [its recitation], one should recite the blessing "...who has sanctified us with His commandments and commanded us to complete the *Hallel*." This applies whether the recitation is individual or communal.[^32]  
Even though the reading of the *Hallel* is a mitzvah ordained by the Sages, one may recite the blessing [stating] "who sanctified us with His commandments and commanded us," as one recites a blessing for the reading of the Megillah[^33] and for the erection of an *eruv*.[^34] A blessing should be recited for every definite obligation established by our Sages.[^35]  
In contrast, if an obligation was established by the Sages because of a doubt - e.g., tithing *d'mai*,[^36] - a blessing is not recited.[^37] [This principle invites a question:] Why is a blessing recited over the second day of a festival; its observance was ordained only because of doubt?[^38] [This was ordained] lest it be treated with disdain.[^39] ^3-5

**6.** It is not the recitation of *Hallel* on Chanukah alone that is a Rabbinic ordinance,[^40] but rather, at all times[^41] - i.e., on all the days that the complete *Hallel* is recited, [the obligation to do so] is a Rabbinic ordinance.  
There are eighteen days during the year when it is a mitzvah to recite the entire *Hallel*.[^42] They are: the eight days of Sukkot, the eight days of Chanukah, the first day of Pesach,[^43] and the holiday of Shavuot. *Hallel* is not recited on Rosh HaShanah and Yom Kippur, since they are days of repentance, awe, and fear, and are not days of extra[^44] celebration.[^45] The [Sages] did not ordain the recitation of *Hallel* on Purim, because the reading of the Megillah [serves the purpose of *Hallel*].[^46] ^3-6

**7.** In places where the festivals are celebrated for two days, *Hallel* is recited on 21 days: On the nine days of Sukkot, the eight days of Chanukah, the [first] two days of Pesach, and the two days of Shavuot.[^47]  
[In contrast,] the recitation of *Hallel* on Rosh Chodesh is a custom and not a mitzvah.[^48] It is observed [only] communally. [To emphasize that it is a custom,] passages are skipped when it is read. A blessing should not be recited over [this reading], since a blessing is not recited over a custom.[^49]  
A person [praying] alone should not recite [the *Hallel*] at all [on Rosh Chodesh].[^50] If, however, he began its recitation, he should complete it, skipping the passages the community would skip as he reads it.  
Similarly, on the other days of Pesach, [the *Hallel*] is read while skipping passages.[^51] ^3-7

**8.** And how should one skip?[^52] One recites from the beginning of the *Hallel*[^53] until the phrase *chalamish lema'y'no mayim*.[^54] One then skips and [begins] reciting *A-donai z'charanu y'varech*,[^55] [continuing] until *Halleluyah*.[^56] One then skips and [begins] reciting *Mah ashiv lA-donai*,[^57] [continuing] until *Halleluyah*.[^58] Afterwards, one skips and [begins] reciting *Min hametzar karati Yah*,[^59] [continuing] until the conclusion of the *Hallel*.[^60]  
This is the common custom.[^61] Others skip [passages] according to a different pattern.[^62] ^3-8

**9.** It is appropriate to recite *Hallel* throughout the entire day.[^63] A person who reads *Hallel* in improper sequence does not fulfill his obligation.[^64] If a person reads and pauses, reads and pauses, even if he pauses for a time long enough to complete the entire [*Hallel*], he fulfills his obligation.[^65]  
On the days when the entire *Hallel* is recited,[^66] one may make an interruption between chapters.[^67] Within a [single] chapter, however, one may not make an interruption.[^68] On the days when *Hallel* is read while skipping portions,[^69] one may make an interruption[^70] even within a chapter.[^71] ^3-9

**10.** On all the days when the complete *Hallel* is recited,[^72] a blessing should be recited before *Hallel*.[^73] In places where it is customary to recite a blessing afterwards, a blessing should be recited [on these days].[^74]  
What blessing is recited?  
God our Lord, all Your works will praise You, and the righteous and Your pious ones, who carry out Your will, and Your nation, the House of Israel, will joyously praise Your name. For it is good to praise You, O God, and it is is pleasant to sing to Your name. From the [spiritual] worlds to the [physical] world, You are the Almighty. Blessed are You, God, the King who is extolled and praised, who is glorified, living and enduring. May He reign at all times and for eternity.[^75] ^3-10

**11.** There are places which follow the custom of repeating[^76] each verse from *od'cha ki anitani* ([[Psalms 118#^118-21|Psalms 118:21]] until the conclusion of the *Hallel*.[^77] Each verse is read a second time. In places where this repetition is customary, the verses should be repeated. In places where it it is customary not to repeat, they should not be repeated.[^78] ^3-11

**12.** This is the custom according to which *Hallel* was recited in the days of the early Sages:[^79] After reciting the blessing, an adult[^80] begins reciting the *Hallel* and says, *Halleluyah*. All the people respond *Halleluyah*.  
He then reads, *Hallelu avdei A-donai*, and all the people respond, *Halleluyah*.[^81] He then reads, *Hallelu et shem A-donai*[^82] and all the people respond, *Halleluyah*. He then reads, *Yehi shem A-donai mevorach me'atah v'ad olam*, and all the people respond, *Halleluyah*.  
Similarly, after every bar [of the *Hallel*, the people respond *Halleluyah*]. Thus, they respond *Halleluyah* 123 times throughout the entire *Hallel*; a sign to remember this: the years of Aaron's life.[^83] ^3-12

**13.** [It is] also [customary that] when the reader reaches the beginning of each and every chapter, the people repeat the line he recited. What is implied? When he recites the line *B'tzeit Yisrael miMitzrayim*,[^84] the people repeat the line *B'tzeit Yisrael miMitzrayim*.  
The reader then recites *beit Yaakov me'am lo'ez* and all the people respond, *Halleluyah*. [They continue to respond *Halleluyah* after each bar] until the reader reads, *Ahavti ki yishma A-donai et koli tachanunai,*[^85] to which the people all respond, *Ahavti ki yishma A-donai....* Similarly, when the reader reads *Hallelu et A-donai kol goyim*,[^86] the people all respond, *Hallelu et A-donai kol goyim*. ^3-13

**14.** The reader should read, *Anna A-donai hoshi'ah na*, and [the people] should repeat *Anna A-donai hoshi'ah na*, although it is not the beginning of a chapter. He [then] reads *Anna A-donai hatzlichah na*, and they repeat *Anna A-donai hatzlichah na*.[^87] He reads *Baruch haba*... and they respond *Baruch haba*....[^88]  
If the person reading the *Hallel* was a minor, a slave, or a woman, [the people] should repeat the entire *Hallel* after them word by word.[^89] The above represents the custom followed in the early ages and it is fitting to adhere to it. At present, however, I have seen different customs in all places with regard to the reading of [the *Hallel*] and the responses of the people, not one of them resembling another. ^3-14

[^13]: The commentaries raise the question: Why was the miracle of the cruse of oil necessary? With regard to communal offerings, in which category falls the kindling of the Menorah, we follow the rule that "the requirement for ritual purity is suspended," if it is impossible to bring the offering in purity (*Hilchot Bi'at HaMikdash* 4:7-17). Thus, if the cruse of pure oil had burned out, the Jews could have used impure oil. Why did God perform a miracle when there was no absolute necessity?  
    Among the explanations for this is that God saw the dedication of the Jewish people as evidenced by their search for pure oil, and, in a greater sense, by their entire struggle against the Greeks. This commitment which extended beyond the limits of their intellect evoked a miracle from God that transcended the limits of nature.
[^14]: This fact also provokes a question: Why did it take so long to produce pure olive oil? Seemingly, it would have been possible to produce oil from olives in the Jerusalem vicinity in less than eight days.
