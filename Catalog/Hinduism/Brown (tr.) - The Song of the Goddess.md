## About

A Sanskrit Shakta text, the Song of the Goddess, in which the Great Goddess (Mahadevi) teaches King Himalaya (Himavan). It forms the last ten chapters of the seventh book (skandha) of the *Devi-Bhagavata Purana* and often circulates on its own.

- **Size:** 507 verses in ten chapters (Wikipedia). Britannica gives the whole Purana as 12 sections and 318 chapters.
- **Author and date:** tradition ascribes it to Vyasa. C. Mackenzie Brown, as Wikipedia quotes him, finds it hard to place before the 13th century and allows that it may be as late as the 16th; SUNY Press's description of his translation says about the 15th century. For the Purana as a whole, Britannica reports scholarly dates from the 6th to the 14th century and a probable origin in Bengal.
- **Setting:** chapter 1 frames the dialogue with questions from King Janamejaya. The gods, defeated by the demon Taraka, take refuge in the Goddess, who promises to send Gauri (Parvati) to Himalaya. She appears as a blinding light, then as the four-armed Bhuvaneshvari, and later in her terrifying cosmic form, Virat.
- **Contents:** two models of creation, one from Samkhya and one favoured by Advaita; how individual souls arise through ignorance and karma; the oneness of the soul with Brahman as liberating knowledge; and the paths of yoga, devotion and knowledge, with supreme devotion (para bhakti) ranked highest. It often explains its teaching by quoting the *Bhagavad Gita*; SUNY Press says it was meant to supplant Krishna's teaching there from a goddess-centred view.
- **Translations:** Brown's annotated translation and commentary (SUNY Press, 1998), and his translation without the scholarly notes, *The Song of the Goddess* (SUNY Press, 2002); Wikipedia also lists versions in English, Tamil, French, Catalan and Russian.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia counts it among the sixty-four Gitas commonly cited in Hindu scripture and says it presents the Goddess as all-powerful, all-knowing and all-compassionate: creator, pervader and protector of the universe.
