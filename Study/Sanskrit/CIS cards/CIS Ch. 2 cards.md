## The order of sounds (p. 19)

What three principles does the order of Sanskrit sounds follow?::It goes from simple to complex; it goes from the back to the front of the mouth; and it groups similar sounds together.

In the order of sounds, which come first, the vowels or the consonants, and why?::First come the vowels, then the consonants. Vowels are ‘simpler’ as they can easily be pronounced on their own, while consonants usually need the help of a vowel to be pronounced.

How are the vowels and the consonants ordered among themselves?::According to where in the mouth they are pronounced, going from back to front.

## Vowels (pp. 18–19)

Which are the simple vowels?::a ā i ī u ū ṛ ṝ ḷ (ḹ)

Which are the complex vowels, and why are they called ‘complex’?::e ai o au. At an earlier stage of the language they were \*ai \*āi \*au \*āu: all of them once actually were vowel combinations or ‘diphthongs’. All four count as long.

## Stops and the places of articulation (pp. 18–19)

What are stops?::Consonants for which we stop and then release the flow of air at some point in our mouth.

In what order do the places of articulation of the stops come?::Velar, palatal, retroflex, dental, labial (from the back to the front of the mouth).

What are velars?::Stops formed by briefly stopping the air in the back of our throats (at the so-called velum): k kh g gh, with the nasal ṅ.

What are palatals?::Stops for which the tongue touches the hard palate, or roof of the mouth: c ch j jh, with the nasal ñ.

What are retroflex stops?::Stops with the tongue ‘retroflected’, i.e. curled back and touching the roof of the mouth: ṭ ṭh ḍ ḍh, with the nasal ṇ.

What are dentals?::Stops for which the tongue touches the teeth (dent- = Latin ‘tooth’): t th d dh, with the nasal n.

What are labials?::Stops for which the lower and upper lips touch (labia = Latin ‘lip’): p ph b bh, with the nasal m.

In Table 3, in what order does each row of stops run?::Unvoiced unaspirated, unvoiced aspirated, voiced unaspirated, voiced aspirated, then the nasal (k kh g gh ṅ).

## Voice and aspiration (pp. 19–20)

What do ‘voiced’ and ‘unvoiced’ mean?::‘Voiced’ means that the vocal cords vibrate when the sound is pronounced (finger on the throat: you feel it with d, not with t). Sounds that have this vibration are ‘voiced’; those that do not, ‘unvoiced’.

What does ‘aspirated’ mean?::That a sound is accompanied by air: the puff of air with the p of pot is its aspiration; the p of spot is almost unaspirated.

How do you pronounce Sanskrit th and ph?::th is a t with a puff of air accompanying it, not the English th of the or thanks; for ph, say the p of pot and add a more noticeable aspiration. Each is one sound, though written with two letters.

## Nasals, semivowels, sibilants and h (pp. 20–21)

Why are the nasals so called?::Because the pathway from the oral to the nasal cavity at the back of our mouth is open when they are pronounced; otherwise they are parallel to their corresponding stops.

Which are the semivowels, and what else are they called?::y r l v, the sounds that have vowel equivalents; they are also called glides or liquids (semivowels: literally ‘half-vowels’).

Which vowels do y r l v correspond to, and when do those vowels turn into them?::y to i/ī, r to ṛ/ṝ, l to ḷ, v to u/ū; whenever these vowels come to stand before a vowel other than themselves (e.g. i + a –› ya).

Which are the three sibilants?::The palatal ś, the retroflex ṣ and the dental s, listed in the same order as the corresponding stops.

How is Sanskrit h pronounced?::More or less as h is in English. The sound is voiced (unlike English h, which is unvoiced), yet an unvoiced pronunciation is predominant now.

Which sign must not be confused with h?::The visarga (ḥ), in spite of how similarly the two are transliterated into English.
