Autonomy, Practical Identity, 

## 1 Introduction: Autonomy, Practical Identity, Self, and Character

1\. 

### 1 Existential Approaches to Personal Autonomy as Deep Responsibility

Analysis of personal autonomy has flourished in analytic philosophy for four decades, since Harry Frankfurt and Gerald Dworkin proposed “hierarchical” theories involving higher-order volitions that endorse certain first-order motives or aim to make them effective in action.<sup>1</sup> These theories have been critiqued in favor of a host of alternative positions emphasizing coherence among psychic states, interpersonal relations, responsiveness to values, and the development of personal competencies, among other criteria. But as these responses to the initial hierarchical models have proliferated, it has become less clear that a single phenomenon (or unified set of phenomena) in human life is the same target of analysis or object of dispute. While the concept of personal autonomy is very generally “self-governance” or some kind of authority, authorship, ownership, or control over aspects of one’s “self,” this is not enough to ensure that the main conceptions of it in analytic philosophy today are really discussing the same set of phenomena or presenting rival explanations of the same concept or human potential.<sup>2</sup>

Certainly by the time of its 20th-century revival, the notion of “autonomy” was no longer restricted to *moral* self-governance or control of one’s intentions through an overriding will to right conduct or fairness to each individual. While the Kantian “good will” could claim to be authoritative by expressing our shared humanity, the development of romantic individualism in the 19th century led to the idea that a wide variety of creative motives and more agent-relative reasons for action could express a particular self in its uniqueness and thus count as authoritative controllers of that self’s intentions.<sup>3</sup> This momentous change was arguably implicit throughout the liberal tradition at least from John Locke’s notion that individuals receive from their creator a kind of authority to rule their own life that entitles them to certain basic liberties to try to order their affairs, within the limits set by other obligations, including those arising from the same authority in others. But it was the romantics who conceived it positively in 

*[p. 2]* ^pg21

terms of the distinctive calling to a creative venture or vocation for a unique work found in the depths of an individual’s self-discovery. As Charles Taylor says, “The notion of authenticity develops out of a displacement of the moral accent in this idea;” since Augustine, it consisted of being true to the voice of conscience within us, but Rousseau and especially Herder reinterpreted it as being true to one’s own original “way of being.”<sup>4</sup> On this view, the significance of ordinary life depends on “an inner impulse or conviction” that is understood as the “voice of nature within us.”<sup>5</sup> Self-expression becomes part of our telos, part of what it is to be a self.

Although they do not all endorse the claims romanticism made for the intrinsic value of “sentiment,” even apart from the proper objects of our loves, every major analytic account of autonomy from Joel Feinberg, Bernard Williams, and Frankfurt on has worked within this new paradigm: the relevant “self” in “self-governance” is no longer limited to a set of reasons or motives that are essential to all persons as such, or distinctive of morally responsible agency in general. Instead, autonomous motives can also include projects or commitments that are distinctive to the identity of particular selves.<sup>6</sup> By illustrative contrast, John Rawls was still relying on the narrower notion when he linked his conception of justice to Kant’s belief that “a person is acting autonomously when the principles of his action are chosen by him as the most adequate possible expression of his nature as a free and equal rational being.”<sup>7</sup> Here Rawls sounds like Hegel or even more like Marx, who referred to autonomy as the expression of one’s “species-being” or shared human nature (with the “sense of justice” operating as the distinctively human motive). He is much closer to the classical liberal and romantic ideas of individual autonomy when he follows “Royce’s thought that a person may be regarded as a human life lived according to a plan,” or lived according to endorsed long-term ends expressing a particular “conception of his good.”<sup>8</sup> Notice how the “person” in this formulation is equated with the whole of an individual life-process (leaving aside types of freedom exercised in steering that process). Similarly, in all major contemporary analytic conceptions, the conditions of personal autonomy depend on the structure of individual selves and their capacity for “identity-conferring commitments,”<sup>9</sup> though the conditions may also include forms of libertarian freedom.

This brief summary should be a suffi cient basis for appreciating why both the existential tradition in the history of philosophy and contemporary narrative accounts of self-identity can make vital contributions to current debates about personal autonomy. The signature thesis of narrative theories can be stated in a summary (though somewhat loose) way as follows:

Signature Narrative Thesis: each person’s individual’s identity *is*, or *depends on*, an understanding he has of his life in narrative form, as a development from his past towards his future prospects, ending in his death.

*[p. 3]* ^pg22

So phrased, this thesis is similar to Dan McAdams’ statement that identity “is itself a life-story”<sup>10</sup> and to Marya Schechtman’s statement that “the lives of persons are narrative in form.” The signature thesis leaves open how this form gets there, and thus it is weaker than causal theses such as her claim that “a person creates his identity by forming an autobiographical narrative.”<sup>11</sup> It is certainly open to causal claims like Peter McInerney’s thesis that “People consistently represent themselves as *enduring entities* that have specific types of bodily, psychological, and social characteristics,” and that such representations of persons (including themselves) have multiple direct and indirect effects on “the unity through time of persons’ psychological makeup,” usually by strengthening links between psychic states over time.<sup>12</sup> The signature thesis holds that such (mostly tacit) self-representations that have such unifying psychological effects tend towards narrative form: attitudes and dispositions of thought, judgment, emotion, and other motives that make up a distinctive self are connected and develop together with a coherence of plot, theme, and ethos much like that recognizable in the development of a complex character in a novel. And this has implications for autonomy: the authority of a motive to guide decisions and actions in the name of the self—to make particular ways of being count as self-determined—may depend (at least in part) on its thematic “centrality” in our life-narrative.

Narrative theories may also clarify the very concept of personal autonomy that the debates are supposed to be about, helping us pin down the central phenomena in need of philosophical explanation. Narrative theories naturally direct us to think of selves with the potential for autonomy as *characters* who are both agents and recipients of action in the stories they live out. The notions of character and (derivatively) character-traits are originally narrative notions, ideas first developed in literary art that from ancient times have also been applied to real embodied psyches. And while personal autonomy is not the same as Kantian moral autonomy—choice according to the moral law, or giving priority to moral obligation—it is still a broadly ethical concept, analogous to “free will” as the type of control needed to be responsible for garden-variety actions and intentions. While “free will” is the name or label for the control-conditions of responsibility, in general (for intentions, actions, omissions, consequences, etc), personal autonomy denotes the control needed to be responsible for (some parts of) one’s *character*. In short, autonomy is the control-condition of responsibility for one’s self in the sense of character-features that can be assessed as virtuous or vicious, as high or low in moral worth, as weak or strong. In Susan Wolf’s phrase, the autonomous person is *deeply* responsible for motives and dispositions with moral significance, such as virtues and vices;<sup>13</sup> in the deepest sense, he is responsible for the core of his identity from which most of his particular free decisions flow.

Autonomy as Deep Responsibility (ADR): personal autonomy of the most fundamental kind is the set of conditions that constitute an agent’s 

*[p. 4]* ^pg23

control over her *volitional character*—the ethically significant features of her identity that are shaped by her central ends and priorities—sufficient to allow her to be ethically responsible for this character (if she also meets relevant epistemic conditions).

This definition does not deny that there are other weaker senses of “autonomy” in use; for example, sometimes autonomy is valued simply as an antidote to blatant physical coercion, and it may also be a useful label for the mental competencies necessary to manage one’s affairs, e.g., in making important medical and legal decisions. Instead, ADR is a hypothesis about the core notion of autonomy underlying such derivative senses. It says that the core form of autonomy, which we may simply label *existential autonomy*, is the freedom-condition of the deepest sort of responsibility that persons can attain (though not every person who is morally responsible for particular actions actually achieves this kind of responsibility for self<sup>14</sup>).

As a hypothesis about the function of personal autonomy or the basic nature of the phenomena it denotes, ADR is clearly inspired by two central themes in the tradition of existential philosophy. First, Søren Kierkegaard’s portrayal of life-stages ranging from “aesthetic” triviality to “ethical” commitment and culminating in “religious” faith implies that persons can be shallow or deep, vacuous or well-defined by deep commitments; only persons with lasting cares have the sort of volitional character that even make them candidates for autonomy. Second, writers such as Kierkegaard, Karl Jaspers, Martin Heidegger, Nikolas Berdyaev, Jean-Paul Sartre, and Viktor Frankl (as well as several existential novelists) have argued that persons are meant to become responsible for themselves as a whole—not because they control every aspect of personality (or the situations and upbringing that form personality) but because they can shape these psychic materials in different ways through cares or devotions that give lasting purpose to their lives.

These connections should not be surprising. Existential writers from Kierkegaard and Nietzsche onwards were both responding to and developing the romantic paradigm that turned Kant’s concept of moral autonomy into the concept of governance by the deep self of a unique individual (which may be more than Lockean self-governance by the desire for happiness and judgments instrumental to it). Beyond the immediate social level where political liberties, legal competence, and monetary means may enable one to carry out parts of a life-plan, they sought psychological insight into the sort of self that could form such a plan or articulate its own understanding of its final ends, and the ultimate sources of its inspirations. Thus, they helped complete the paradigm-shift begun by the romantics that has set the agenda for contemporary analytic eff orts to analyze autonomy, even if mainstream analytic practitioners rarely recognize this heritage. Thus, also, the existential writers helped forge the idea that, with eff ort, a person can fashion a self that is more than the raw materials of birth and chance make her to be, and thereby become responsible for her very self—the 

*[p. 5]* ^pg24

concept of autonomy specified by ADR as the ultimate *explanandum* or highest target for autonomy theory. For that reason, if we could show that some existential conception of the individual self and its social relations stands up to rational scrutiny, this would reassure us that the notion of deep self-governance is not a mere fantasy or romantic illusion (as naturalizing reductionists suspect). Similarly, if some existential conception can be reconstructed in a way that is phenomenologically adequate, fitting with more of the evidence and cases we now have than do rival accounts, this could go a long way towards disclosing some of the determinate conditions of personal autonomy, or giving us a partial conception.

This book does not review the main existential accounts to determine the most likely candidate, but instead focuses on one existential model that is especially promising because of its connections with narrative theories of identity. While Nietzsche and Sartre have inspired several varieties of the view that life-stories are fictions and real lives are more fragmented, episodic, and discontinuous, Kierkegaard’s rich portrayal of a person’s freedom developing concrete identity over time arguably implies a narrative process in free choice itself. Because his philosophical anthropology has more in common with Aristotelian and other traditions of virtue ethics, his account also influenced (through Heidegger) contemporary hermeneutic conceptions of historicity. Paul Ricoeur, Charles Taylor, and Alasdair MacIntyre, who are among the most influential 20th-century sources of narrative theories of selves, rarely acknowledge Kierkegaard as a direct influence. But if Heidegger had more clearly acknowledged his debts, the extent to which Kierkegaard provided essential starting points for their accounts would be more evident.

It should be clear now why the main themes of this book are more closely connected than they might at first appear. The task of explaining personal autonomy can be advanced both by arriving at a clearer and more rigorous narrative conception of selves and by developing the concept of volitional character. Narrative theories of identity provide a bridge through which we can connect proposals from the mainstream analytic literature on autonomy and existential conceptions of self. In particular, I will focus on ideas from Kierkegaard’s conception of “ethical” agency that indicate how a certain kind of narrative conception can be extended to yield a partial account of agent-endorsement conditions in personal autonomy, although source conditions ruling out manipulative influences are also needed. My main goal in this book is not Kierkegaard exposition (though his multifaceted work is a fascinating interpretative challenge) but rather to show how a narrative approach inspired by Kierkegaard can be defended and—when connected with a Kierkegaardian conception of striving will or volitional commitment—can yield narrative conditions for personal autonomy. In the process, I will also defend and nuance my past interpretations of Kierkegaard, but hopefully readers interested in this aspect will also be drawn to recent work on narrative theory and autonomy in general.

*[p. 6]* ^pg25

One could start from a different point and arrive by analysis at the same interconnection of themes in moral psychology. For example, (i) one could start from familiar problems with hierarchical conceptions of autonomy in analytic scholarship and move directly to narrative accounts of extended action and planning to help explain the special authority that certain motives or reasons for action seem to have in our lives.<sup>15</sup> From there, one would face the problem of explaining what personality and character are, and of isolating that part of character for which we can be responsible. Or (ii) one could begin from existential diagnoses of inauthenticity, lack of integrity, *acedia*, and absence of robust life-meaning, and discover that avoiding these dissolutions of self requires a strong form of narrative unity that is correlated with experiences of autonomy, which the constitutive conditions of selfhood make possible but do not ensure. Alternatively (iii) one could set out from several points in the widely varying literature on narrative theories in general and, by grappling with important objections to such theories, come to a refined account that naturally links up with what Kierkegaard has said about the structure of “ethical” agency in contrast to the “aesthetic” modes of carelessness, detached speculation, or superficial busyness (his response to the 19th-century romantics). My approach will mainly follow this last route (iii), starting from issues that have been specifi - cally raised in the context of Kierkegaard scholarship but that have a wider relevance for the general debates about narrative theory and autonomy. Yet at a few junctures, I will at least outline how journeys (i) and (ii) would go as well. For, when different lines of analysis seeking to explain what first look like distinct phenomena converge on the same conclusions, that provide stronger phenomenological warrant for the conclusions; the resulting philosophical explanation is shown to have a unifying power.<sup>16</sup>

Still, my argument will be far from exhaustive. There are many other paths through the labyrinth of moral psychology that may arrive at the same theoretical center. For example, (iv) the problems of action theory have led some theorists to narrative conceptions and to the question of how to distinguish autonomous acts from acts that are less than autonomous in a variety of ways (e.g. akratic, self-deceptive, conflicted, wanton, or even coerced). Similarly, (v) attempts to resolve thorny conceptual problems about weakness of will might be aided by some of the hypotheses I will set out. And (vi) eff orts to explain the ultimate source of moral obligation in the constitutive conditions of morally responsible agency may also require some variety of narrative unity in individual identity as a *telos*. These approaches argue, contra some romantics, that personal autonomy also requires moral autonomy.<sup>17</sup> (vii) Others start from the traditional problem of explaining personal identity over time, which has been a perennial theme in Western philosophy since Descartes’ mental substance account, Locke’s psychological connection theory, Hume’s bundle-self, and Kant’s Paralogisms, up to contemporary discussions of split-brain cases and possible branching selves. The intractable aporias of this debate have inspired the recent focus on practical 

*[p. 7]* ^pg26

dimensions of identity. (viii) In continental philosophy, phenomenological studies of consciousness, when they move beyond consciousness in general to the forms of consciousness distinctive of persons, may find phenomena that require a robust and multilayered narrative account involving a kind of willing that sets final ends and sustains eff ort over time.

In order to keep this book focused on a manageable set of issues and interlocutors, and to facilitate a linear development that makes for easier reading, I will largely leave paths (iv–viii) untraveled here, though §4 below considers the continuity of consciousness over time, returning to issues in genre (vii), but with a focus on Galen Strawson’s recent arguments. The relation of personal autonomy to moral obligation arises in Chapter 3, but I focus on Kierkegaard’s contribution to this question. Similarly, I must largely leave aside some fundamental questions about the structure of time, the nature of meaning, and the relation of consciousness to embodiment that underlie my topics—though at appropriate junctures I note where my account may depend on contestable assumptions about these questions.

2\. 

### 2 An Overview of Main Themes and the Structure of the Analysis

The problems this work seeks to answer specifically arose for me in the context of debates about Kierkegaard, virtue ethics, and the apparent problem of “radical choice.” Several authors have recently argued that narrative models of selves and Alasdair MacIntyre’s concept of the unity of a life help make sense of Kierkegaard’s existential “stages” or series of life-views; in particular, narrative continuity helps explain the transition from “aesthetic” to “ethical” modes of life.<sup>18</sup> But John Lippitt, Michelle Kosch, Patrick Stokes, and others have raised diffi cult questions both for these readings of Kierkegaard and in general for narrative accounts of identity that draw on the work of MacIntyre. While some of their objections concern a strong kind of unity or “wholeheartedness” among an agent’s long-term goals or cares, the fundamental objection raised by Lippitt and Bernard Williams is that personal identity cannot *be* a narrative, because stories are artifacts made by persons. This book defends the narrative approach to practical identity and autonomy in general, and to Kierkegaard’s stages in particular, in six main steps.

In this chapter, I defend and develop the working distinction between *practical* and *theoretical* identity that is sometimes explicitly invoked, as in works by Marya Schechtman and Christine Korsgaard, but more is often implicit in widely read work on moral psychology in the last four decades. The indispensability of this distinction is especially evident in the concept of *character*, as a brief overview of recent accounts will be suffi cient to show. I also try to clarify the relation between practical identity and traditional questions about the unity of consciousness over time in response to 

*[p. 8]* ^pg27

Strawson, who has argued for the possibility of “episodic” rather than “diachronic” selves. Thus this chapter focuses on setting out the main themes of the book and laying out the framework within which they will be analyzed.

These clarifications make it easier to state the main objections to narrative theories of selves that have emerged in recent articles and collections on narrative identity, and in recent work on Kierkegaard. Chapter 2 begins with these objections and then focuses on the fundamental objection noted above, namely that selves of some sort must exist before their autobiographical narratives, which are derivative products of self-interpretation. My response is a form of *realism* about life-narratives that arise even in prerefl ective experience, which contrasts with various constructivist narrative theories that implicitly concede to the objection. I first distinguish different *levels* of narrative continuity that are relevant for practical identity. For example, Anthony Rudd’s argument that some narrative connection is essential to the intelligibility of responsible rational agency applies at a level that includes most human agents even if they are not autonomous, and even if they would not count as “ethical” agents in Kierkegaard’s sense. At this constitutive level, a wide variety of important work in action theory strongly supports the view of MacIntyre and Rudd that intentional action has a recognizably narratival form.

Second, I argue that action is one of several types of experience that form narratival connections as an agent lives her life largely *prior* to autobiographical reflection and other narration concerning that life. Drawing on David Carr, Paul Ricoeur, Charles Taylor, and others, I argue that one’s personal identity is that of a protagonist in one’s personal *narravive*<sup>19</sup> or living thread of cumulating meaning-relations that is *like* told narratives in diachronic form. Narravives will explain why some biographies are truer than others, and why some stories are more adequate accounts of historical events. The most basic level of narravive emerges prerefl ectively or prior to stories one tells oneself or others about one’s actions, thoughts, emotions, and intentions, although reflective self-interpretation and narration by others adds to it and alters the felt meanings among many of its existing connections. This account shows that there is a coherent “narrative realist” alternative that can answer the main problems facing narrative approaches to practical identity.

This realist version of the signature narrativist thesis can be formulated either with a wide or narrow sense of a person’s “life.” If we prefer to think of a person as a character *within* a life-thread, and distinguish her from elements of that story that are literally parts of the external world or other persons (the actions of others, a change in the weather etc.), then we can say that a person’s identity is constituted *within* her unique narravive. However, because a narravive strictly consists in the layers of meanings that different elements of a life have for its protagonist (from her viewpoint), including relations between elements that affect their significance to her—much like a person’s “world” in Heidegger’s sense—we may say that in the wide sense, a person’s identity *is* his unique narravive without absurd 

*[p. 9]* ^pg28

implications (such as that his favorite chair or his sister’s face are, qua physical objects, part of him). While it is notoriously diffi cult to say what counts as ‘part of one’s life,’ the relation ‘being part of a narravive’ is clear, though it may be hard to know in practice whether certain events meant anything to an agent. This allows me to minimize the apparent distance between my narrative account and a view like Richard Wollheim’s that distinguishes between a person, her life (a kind of “product”), and the process of “leading a life” by which the person produces this product.<sup>20</sup> I am not committed to the claim that everything we might consider part of a person’s “life” *is* that person, or that a person is “a collection of events spread out over time,” i.e. a four-dimensional object.<sup>21</sup> I can also distinguish a “person” as present acting consciousness from the process of leading a life, though the narravive realist account says that the person’s identity grows with, and is constituted by, the narrative structure which that process realizes. These issues are briefly clarified in a section of Chapter 5.

Proceeding from the account of narravives, Chapter 3 argues that personal autonomy involves higher levels of narrative unity beyond the narravive connections that are necessary for planning agency in general. These higher levels of unity result from certain kinds of volitional eff ort and reflective choices that agents make. At these levels, narrative unity involves wholeheartedness and an ethical framework for caring, as modifications of Frankfurt’s hierarchical model help to explain. To form a volitional character for which we are deeply responsible at least requires continued caring based on loyalty to objective values and, in its strongest form, essential coherence among one’s cares and a search for pragmatic coherence as well. Although Lippitt and other critics rightly distinguish several different kinds of Kierkegaardian aesthetes with diff erent limitations, they each fail to meet different conditions of the thick narrative integration achieved in ethical wholeheartedness. Kierkegaard’s contrast between “stages” can thus be explained in narrative terms.

These arguments in the second half of Chapter 3 defend and clarify the interpretation of the aesthetic–ethical distinction in Kierkegaard’s pseudonymous writings that Rudd and I have offered in previous essays. But beyond issues of Kierkegaard interpretation, they also directly support the position (against Williams) that full personal autonomy involves ethical consciousness. I describe this position as the result of a series of progressively more demanding theses that operate as *extensions* of the basic narrative realist account set out in Chapter 2. One could coherently accept the basic narrative realist theses while rejecting some or all of the further theses concerning autonomy and ethical agency, though the latter build naturally on the former. This is important because critics have often misconstrued certain objections to the ethical claims of narrativists as if they also challenge the basic ontological and structural intuitions behind narrative accounts of identity in general. Instead, the ethical claims should be understood as part of stronger narrative positions that pertain particularly to autonomous agency.

*[p. 10]* ^pg29

Chapter 4 argues that Kierkegaard’s signed writings present a narrative conception of mature practical identity, which confirms my interpretation of the aesthetic–ethical distinction in terms of robust narrative unity among cares. In particular, the ideal of ethical agency that Kierkegaard calls “purity of heart” is an especially strong form of narrative unity; it does not dispense with care for finite earthly goods, but incorporates the attitude of “infinite resignation” into the structure of ethical identity. The resulting volitional continuity is illuminated by a sustained contrast with diff erent forms of “double mindedness,” each of which corresponds to a type of aesthete found in earlier pseudonymous writings. The cure for double mindedness, division of the heart, and volitional weakness is a devotion to an ideal that regulates other ends, a standard of perfection in one’s eff ort or striving that is not dependent on actual results in the world. This brings us to the border of Kierkegaard’s “religious” stage.

Chapter 5 explores the religious extension of this Kierkegaardian conception of selves by addressing the two remaining ontological problems for narrative realism, which are closely related. First I consider Patrick Stokes’ arguments that a real life-narrative would be a four-dimensional object, and that such a view does not do justice to the sense that our identity is entirely “there” in the present—a sense that Kierkegaard recognizes as important. Second I address the problem that human *mortality* is said to pose for narrative accounts of practical identity. Again the skeptics have underestimated the resources of Kierkegaard’s account. In conjunction with Stokes and others, I argue that in his discourse “At the Graveside,” Kierkegaard shows how our life-story can become a “whole” that even includes our death by way of anticipating the point when we will no longer be free to change the meaning of our life. This limiting case of responsibility for character is of general interest because it shows how a narrative conception of autonomy is consistent with the possible significances that mortality may have in one’s life. This problem is one that all narrative theories must address, though not all narrativists will follow Kierkegaard in regarding the final self one becomes as having eschatological significance. For him, this is an inspiration to faith in a completion of our worthwhile endeavors, and improvements to our self, that we cannot achieve by our own eff orts. The point is not that we attain “purity” in our striving to become a better person, but that the striving must come first and live on within the faith that recognizes our ultimate dependence. Autonomy, paradoxically, enables us to recognize the limits of self-determination and our need for God.

3\. 

### 3 Practical Identity as the Issue: Parfit, Williams, Korsgaard, and Schechtman

This book addresses two central questions: first, whether the “practical identity” of a human person is a helpfully understood as having a narrative 

*[p. 11]* ^pg30

structure; and second, whether a narrative approach can shed light on conditions of personal autonomy. But several preliminary steps are in order before we turn to these main themes. First, I need to explain the concept of practical identity developed in recent moral psychology to clarify the standard goals of narrative theories of identity and the way they differ from closely related philosophical issues. Second, it will facilitate later stages of the investigation if I outline the relation between practical identity and other senses of “self” based on self-awareness, sameness of consciousness over time, and present agency (e.g., in choice). This analysis will help clarify the importance of “character” as a phenomenon that mediates between a pure phenomenal subject and a “whole life.” It will also ready us to confront the main objections raised against narrative accounts in recent critical responses, including those in the Kierkegaard literature.

Turning to the first preliminary task, the disputes about “identity” on which this work focuses do not mainly concern theories of “self-consciousness” or the identity of consciousness over time. The challenges I will address by Lippitt, Williams, and other critics of narrative unity as either a constitutive condition or an ideal of personhood all concern something more than the unity of “apperception” in Kant’s sense, which I interpret as the formal continuity of a subject that is conscious of itself as the same subject that experienced other earlier events. The sort of identity they concern is closer to the “forensic” or social sense that was the initial topic of John Locke’s account emphasizing memory,<sup>22</sup> but more recent “Lockean” accounts have a different goal than most narrative theories. The phrase “personal identity” is systematically ambiguous; it can refer to a person’s publicly recognized identity and character, but in its pure theoretical sense, it involves the idea that there is something that makes a given person the same *entity* over time, independently of any beliefs that he or others have about his identity. In this sense, it is an instance of the same question applied to any type of objective entity (same rock, same ship, same painting, or same animal).<sup>23</sup> Familiar metaphysical accounts of what makes someone “the same person over time” in this theoretical sense fall generally into three camps. They focus on

 (i) a suitable set of relations among the *contents* of consciousness, as Lockeans have held;

 (ii) the endurance of a distinct something that *contains* conscious experiences, e.g., some substance that has these experiences, as Descartes, Leibniz, and their heirs have held, and a physical system, such as a brain (according to most physicalist or dual-attribute theories<sup>24</sup>);

 (iii) something else that is neither the contents of thoughts nor that which thinks, but instead the first-personal *form* that thinking takes (as Kant and his heirs have tried to argue).

There are two closely related problems with these approaches, or with their theoretical preconception of the phenomenon to be explained. First, 

*[p. 12]* ^pg31

the target of such metaphysical analyses would seem to cover all possible intelligent, conscious, and reflective beings—including those that lack the power to make decisions, to form higher-order volitions, or even to experience motivation of any kind. This is why Harry Frankfurt complained that an analysis of “the concept of a person” as an entity with mental and physical properties (as proposed by Peter Strawson, A.J. Ayer, and others in the mid-20th century) “is not actually an analysis of that concept at all.”<sup>25</sup> We might enrich the conditions of embodied self-consciousness with other rational or reflective powers to distinguish human beings from other kinds of animals, but this still would not yield an analysis of a “person.” Instead, the most important concept of a “person” corresponds to “those attributes which are the subject of our most humane concern with ourselves . . . ,” and which would generate the same kinds of self-concern even if animals of some other species counted as persons in this sense.<sup>26</sup> In other words, the personhood we want to explain is not a natural kind, but an essentially *practical* concept, or a set of closely related practical concepts. For example, persons may be defined as beings capable of moral responsibility, or (more widely) as beings who deserve a distinctive sort of moral consideration because it is normal for them (or part of their function) to develop the capacities suffi cient for moral responsibility, even though disabilities can block this development. Or (more narrowly), persons are beings who can “achieve” a distinctive identity or form an autonomous “self.”<sup>27</sup> If we seek to understand the continuing identity of a person in this sense, we are not asking about the metaphysical conditions of identity over time for one kind of animal.

The second problem is that, while such metaphysical conditions of diachronic identity will doubtless differ for different categories of beings, including beings of different natural kinds, when the issue is understood this way as a purely metaphysical one related to the notion of a substance, it may have practical implications only derivatively. That this should not be assumed at the start of analysis emerged in an early series of papers by Bernard Williams, who argued that bodily continuity is probably a condition of “personal identity” over time.<sup>28</sup> In the best known essay, he uses our natural fear at the prospect of torture to show that we should have a personal interest in whether we would be identical with our current body or instead with our current thoughts and memories if the latter were transposed with the thoughts and memories of someone else currently manifested in another body. Williams concludes that a third-personal perspective on the question is more likely to lead to a mental-continuity view, while the “first-personal issue” arising from fear of future pain no matter what psychological changes precede it suggests bodily continuity as more fundamental.<sup>29</sup> Because the theoretical question of diachronic identity is essentially third-personal, seeking a metaphysical truth that holds independently of anyone’s perspective, a different concept or target of analysis is already implicit when Williams privileges his practical question.

*[p. 13]* ^pg32

For these reasons, it is important that not every theory of “personal identity” that is described as a “narrative” theory (whether proposed or critiqued) is a theory of practical identity. Daniel Dennett’s well-known account and David Velleman’s variant on it, for example, seem to be offered *first* as answers to the theoretical question about the unity of consciousness over time, though they connect this with other diffi cult issues in the philosophy of mind.<sup>30</sup> Other antinarrative accounts, such as Derek Parfi t’s famous argument for scalar identity-relations, also seem to be offered first as answers to the theoretical question about what makes something the “same consciousness” earlier and later.<sup>31</sup> He speaks of “qualitative identity” in properties as including a “person’s character,” but he says that “When we are concerned about our own future, it is numerical identity that we are concerned about,” since we are not worried about many types of qualitative change; these changes will worry us only insofar as they may impact numerical identity.<sup>32</sup> Yet he hopes this analysis will help resolve fundamental ethical problems, e.g. by showing that the “separateness of persons” has less moral importance than is commonly thought.<sup>33</sup>

Thus Williams is also subtly changing the topic (or exploiting ambiguities in Parfi t’s account) when he emphasizes against Parfi t “the ordinary idea of a self or person which undergoes changes in character,” rather than being dissolved by them into a series of selves.<sup>34</sup> Williams argues that, given the possibility of suicide, we should have reasons or motives for going on living that are “categorical” in the sense that they do not depend on the hypothesis that we are still alive<sup>35</sup> (e.g. not like, “*if* I live into my nineties, I would like to spend some time in Florida . . .”). Williams calls such categorical motives “ground projects,” and says these are projects we should be willing to die for—given that if violating such a project is the price of staying alive, we have insuffi cient reason to do that.<sup>36</sup> What this shows, in Williams’ view, is that our basic interest in having a future is practical; it is explained by the authority that our *present* projects, life-goals, and other personal commitments have in giving us reasons to go on living. Thus “\[t\]he correct perspective on one’s life is from now.”<sup>37</sup> However, what Williams means by this is very different from the attitude that only the present matters. Though he does not fully spell it out, his argument seems to be that (1) our “ordinary sense of self” depends on having ground projects and other particular goals and activities that “constitute a character,”<sup>38</sup> and (2) that the main elements of character function as *pre*commitments to future motivation, because self-perpetuation is essential to their structure (e.g. to be committed to my friend involves being committed to *remaining* loyal to him). Thus (3) in present practical reasoning, I could not rationally try to accommodate the interests of a predicted future stage of me whose projects would contradict my current highest priorities; instead I should want to hinder that future me.<sup>39</sup> This is true even though we know our cares can change. On Williams’s view, it is primarily for the sake of our existing commitments, which are essential to our life having robust meaning for us, 

*[p. 14]* ^pg33

that we care about our numerical identity (even if we might be intimately concerned that a future version of us who has lost all memory in total senility to be treated decently enough, e.g. not be tortured).

For similar reasons, Alasdair MacIntyre also responds to Parfi t that one’s identity as a “character” in a shared history, or loss of such identity, cannot be founded “on the psychological continuity or discontinuity of the self” in a Lockean sense alone.<sup>40</sup> For practical identity depends on relations of meaning; it involves what we may call the “existential” meaningfulness of a life *to the agent* living it—that is, a sense of his activities and relationships as worthwhile rather than pointless.<sup>41</sup> As David Carr says, when we ask about narrative identity, the issue is not whether the physical world is meaningless apart from the reality of persons: “it is human reality, including the very activity of projecting our concerns, which is portrayed in stories, and against which narrative must be measured. . . .”<sup>42</sup> In other words, we want to know whether our life-narratives (and fictional cases) are true to our experience of human practical identity.

Of course, this is not yet enough to establish that practical identity is an essentially narratival phenomenon. It is especially due to Christine Korsgaard, who rarely refers to narrative, that the sense of “self” so closely associated with having “character” has come to be called a “practical identity.” Korsgaard conceives this kind of identity as arising from practical reflection, and she contrasts it with a “metaphysical self” or noumenal entity in which thought inheres.<sup>43</sup> In response to Parfi t, she says that when we deliberate and choose, we have a sense not only of active agency but of a kind of identity that we are expressing, which is like a “law” for us, or a sense of commitment to various roles and social memberships. Following Frankfurt and Williams, she says that this kind of identity is not an issue of scientific fact, but rather

a description under which you value yourself, a description under which you find your life to be worth living and your actions to be worth undertaking. So I will call this a conception of your practical identity. . . . You are a human being, a woman or a man, an adherent of a certain religion, a member of an ethnic group, a member of a certain profession, someone’s lover or friend. . . . <sup>44</sup>

Korsgaard immediately connects having a practical identity in this sense (including affi liations that matter to one) with the importance of “integrity” in the sense of living up to one’s own standards: to violate the most unconditional standards, laws, or ends involved in one’s sense of oneself is to lose a “fundamental part of one’s identity,” which is like being “dead.”<sup>45</sup> This echos Williams’ definition of “ground projects” and suggests that my practical identity involves (at least) my sense of what I care about, what I am doing in light of these cares, how my devotions were formed, and what values they respond to. And when Korsgaard speaks of coming to a 

*[p. 15]* ^pg34

coherent “conception of \[your\] practical identity” and inhabiting a resulting way of life, she implies that diachronic coherence in identity is necessary for integrity.<sup>46</sup> This is an extension of the idea that intentional action in general commits us to *consistency over time*. For choice to form intentions (particular means-end connections) requires reasons for them that would apply again if we faced similar circumstances in the future; these reasons are thus public or shareable.<sup>47</sup> However, unlike Williams, Korsgaard also thinks that a practical identity formed by adopting such norms involves an implicit commitment to the moral law; thus revising one’s cares to make them cohere with moral respect for other persons would count as an increase in self-integration on her account.<sup>48</sup>

Korsgaard further develops the idea of a unified practical identity in her more recent work on the constitutive sources of moral obligation. Again, the argument starts from a notion of chosen action: “to regard some movement of my body or mind as my action, I must see it as an expression of myself as a whole, rather than as the product of some force that is at work *on* me or *in* me.”<sup>49</sup> This formulation, like several others in Korsgaard’s work, is in danger of obscuring the distinction between fully autonomous action and intentional action of persons in general,<sup>50</sup> but it remains useful for two reasons. First, it suggests that to think of ourselves as *agent-causes* of our intentions and acts guided by our intentions requires a sense of an identity that our choices can express. For this, “the identity of the human animal on whom the person normally supervenes” is not enough.<sup>51</sup> Instead, as self-conscious beings who are aware that our choices are causes, we need a coherent sense of the reasons we take as justifying actions: “It is as the possessor of a personal or practical identity that you are the author of your actions, and responsible for them. And yet at the same time it is in choosing your actions that you create that identity.”<sup>52</sup>

Second, Korsgaard’s account suggests that the agency we exercise in our significant acts is not (in Iris Murdoch’s memorable phrase) a “bare point of freedom;” it has a content that we both express and shape in acting. She sees forming a practical identity in this sense as part of our natural telos,<sup>53</sup> and she has also linked sticking to personal norms or commitments we make to having a “diachronic” self.<sup>54</sup> Still, “practical identity” in Korsgaard’s sense is an imprecise notion; it lumps together many different aspects of a person’s life and character that may have varying types of authority for us or varying relations to our agency (because some of them may even be features that we actively resist). Practical identity can even be broadened to include virtually everything about us that transcends our shared animal form and bare continuity of consciousness over time. For example, Owen Flanagan says

. . . there is also the problem for each and every person (not that it is mandatory that it be faced) of what kind of person he or she is.  .  .  . Identity in this thick, rich sense—let us call it actual or full identity—is 

*[p. 16]* ^pg35

constituted by the dynamic integrated system of past and present identifications, desires, commitments, aspirations, beliefs, dispositions, temperament, roles, acts, and actional patterns, as well as by whatever self-understandings (even incorrect ones) each person brings to his or her life. It is this sort of identity on whose basis such goods as self-esteem and self-respect arise and are maintained.<sup>55</sup>

This formulation recognizes how much complexity practical identity involves. Theories of existential “authenticity” ask why it is important for us to consider such features of our identity and try to shape them in some distinctive way.<sup>56</sup> Theories of responsibility and autonomy help to sort through this array of personality aspects and traits by asking whether any of them are more basic to moral responsibility for actions and character. For example, it is plausible that moral responsibility for our practical identities normally depends on moderate reasons-responsiveness in some psychological processes that influence the formation and continuation of one’s cares, commitments, or second-order volitions involved in cares (by which we identify with certain motives towards that which we care about). By contrast, a creature lacking rational and volitional powers that are necessary conditions of responsibility in general, or of responsibility for character in particular, might still have types of memory and self-awareness that make possible unity of consciousness over time; but it would not be a “person” in the sense of an agent with ethically significant practical identity who may also be capable of achieving autonomy.

Given that most narrative theories of persons aim to explain practical identity, it is unsurprising that they usually distinguish this from metaphysical unity of a substance over time and any of the three forms of diachronic unity of consciousness typically offered as theoretical accounts of being the “same mind” over time (as listed at the start of this section). More than MacIntyre and Carr, Marya Schechtman emphasizes this distinction in her well-known argument for a narrative approach. In the mid-1990s, Schechtman found the post-Parfi t literature on identity focused on what she calls the “reidentifi cation question” rather than the “characterization question:”

The former is the question of what makes a person at time *t*2 the same person as a person at time *t*1; the latter \[is\] the question of which beliefs, values, desires, and other psychological features make someone the person she is. The reidentifi cation question thus concerns the logical relation of identity, whereas the characterization question concerns identity in the sense of what is generally called, following Erikson, an “identity crisis.”<sup>57</sup>

This distinction corresponds to what I have called the theoretical question of metaphysical unity as opposed to the ethically grounded question of practical identity. Schechtman’s labels are helpful because they highlight 

*[p. 17]* ^pg36

the fact that something much less than character may be used as a basis for reidentifi cation. Yet reidentifi cation theories try in vain to capture “four basic features of personal existence” in the practical sense, namely “survival, moral responsibility, self-interested concern, and compensation,” which are not all-or-nothing relations.<sup>58</sup> I agree with Schechtman about this tendency to blur the explanatory tasks and issues; but the situation is even worse than she says, because concern about one’s identity is also ethically motivated through concern about the quality of one’s character, whether one’s goals and values have an authentic basis, and how autonomously one acts upon them in important contexts. These *three further features*—virtue, authenticity, and autonomy—are clearly relevant when practical identity is the focus, but they tend to be obscured in the literature that has focused on the reidentifi cation question.<sup>59</sup> I would add that reidentifi cation theories of identity go astray not only because they try to find criteria for reidentifying persons in a thick practical sense involving Schechtman’s four features, but also because they look to metaphysical accounts of identity for physical objects, animals, and artifacts as their models (thus the temptation to theories of persons as four-dimensional wholes made of instantaneous time-slice person-parts that Schechtman critiques).<sup>60</sup> This is why, as Schechtman argues in response to Parfi t, David Lewis, and John Perry, “psychological continuity theorists” have to offer either reductionist accounts (explaining persons in nonpersonal terms) or four-dimensionalist accounts to remain consistent, neither of which is a plausible result.<sup>61</sup>

In Schechtman’s larger analysis, this becomes a trilemma. Its root is that “\[o\]ur prephilosophical intuitions tell us that it is psychological continuation that underlies the importance that we attach to identity,” so we cannot retreat to a physical criterion (or even a soul-substance without psychological continuity) (horn 1).<sup>62</sup> But the criteria of psychological continuity that fit with the transitive form of logical identity are reductive or four-dimensionalist, and thus do not sustain the features we care about in practical identity (horn 2). Instead, (horn 3) “\[w\]e generally assume that psychological continuity implies the continued existence of the *same experiencing subject*, and it is with this understanding that we assume psychological continuity underlies personal identity,” or supports the four features of practical identity.<sup>63</sup> But the rich sort of psychological continuity needed for practical identity does not meet the desiderata for a metaphysical theory of diachronic identity (which disallows splitting). Rather than conclude with Parfi t that personal identity is not as important as we thought, Schechtman says that we should look instead look to psychological criteria for “characterization” that can explain the self-phenomena we intuitively recognize as important.<sup>64</sup> In other words, though reidentifi cation retains its intrinsic interest, we should focus on explaining the phenomena of practical identity instead.

Like Flanagan, Schechtman takes a wide view of these phenomena, which include the ascription to persons of “actions, experiences, beliefs, values, desires, character traits, and so on.”<sup>65</sup> But unlike Korsgaard, she 

*[p. 18]* ^pg37

also emphasizes that they include the differences between someone manifesting characteristic and their being internal to “a person’s true identity” as opposed to resulting from forms of manipulation, coercion, or false consciousness (such as Nora’s sham identity in Ibsen’s *A Doll’s House*)—differences that Frankfurt, Charles Taylor, and Gary Watson try to explain with their theories of autonomy.<sup>66</sup> Her view, which we will see Kierkegaard also supports, is that practical identity in the wide sense covering all we might consider in characterizing a person includes psychic states and acts that are literally attributable, those that are “truly” attributable, and the relation between them, which depends on their role in her history:

It is highly implausible to think of a person’s *true* identity as something completely distinct from his *literal* identity, or even as some sort of an island within it. Instead, all the characteristics that are part of a person’s history are presumed to contribute to making up her identity. Some, however, play a more central role.  .  .  . \[The characterization question\] asks which characteristics are part of the story of a person’s life, and what role do they play in that story.<sup>67</sup>

Schechtman recognizes that these questions are not entirely unrelated to reidentifi cation. Still, before she develops her “narrative self-constitution” theory as an answer to the characterization question, Schechtman shows that these questions are different in logical form: whereas reidentifi cation seeks “a relation between *two distinct person-time-slices* that make them slices of the same person,” characterization seeks “a relation between *a person* and *particular actions, experiences, or characteristics* that are hers.”<sup>68</sup> The trouble with this is that, as Schechtman’s own response to autonomy 

theories suggests, the relation(s) of belonging or ownership between a person and her characteristics may not be adequately statable in *static* terms: they may depend on patterns of development over time. The contents of a particular practical identity may be fixable at a given instant only by way of abstraction, and this may explain why the “features” of practical identity that intuitively concern us can seem to be about characterization *or* reidentifi cation. While mere continuation of the “same subject” of consciousness is insuffi cient to constitute a practical identity, development of a self normally *depends* on this basic kind of psychological continuity (even if it does not require a nonbranching version of sameness in consciousness). As Joel Kupperman argues, the basic unity of consciousness that Kant describes is the “framework” for “theoretical and practical connections among items” of experience, and it is because this “field” only occurs in distinct organisms for humans that we do not develop a “group-self:” a practical identity is initially “constructed” or built up by the one field of consciousness in a single growing human being.<sup>69</sup> Anthony Rudd draws the same conclusion in commenting on Lynne Rudder Baker’s account: “the first-person perspective, i.e. the ability to think of these experiences as all being mine, 

*[p. 19]* ^pg38

is necessary but not suffi cient for full selfhood,” which involves a coherent story of one’s life.<sup>70</sup>

Thus in Rudd’s view, Schechtman’s reidentifi cation and characterization problems are not neatly separable, and narrative accounts of “character and personality” must also address the “traditional debate” about identity over time,<sup>71</sup> which implies that something substantial must endure according to a successful narrative account. In his own alternative, this can be achieved by extending Wittgenstein’s notion of “expression” of psychic states in bodily behavior; in addition, “the pattern of those mental states is expressive of the person she is.”<sup>72</sup> Rudd suggests that there is a hierarchy of such expressive relations. A particular bodily act may express an emotion, which itself expresses one or more “character traits,” which in turn express a whole way of being: “The Self . . . is at the top of the hierarchy.”<sup>73</sup> Because “expression” in Rudd’s sense is a relation of significance that is broader than propositional reference or designation, we might also equally say that the meaning expressed by more particular and outward items is nested within the broader meaning of the more underlying psychic processes, with the Self as the most embracing set of meaning-relations (at least until we move on to historical relations among selves). While Rudd interprets these relations of expression in explicitly narrative terms,<sup>74</sup> he holds that they also provide “a way to think about the Self as a substance,” because the relations between an X that expresses a Y are “internal relations . . . that are constitutive of their relata.”<sup>75</sup> The sort of substance consisting of this network of meanings would avoid problems with mental substances as bare containers, remaining the same no matter what thought-contents go into them; it would also avoid problems with Lockean connections among atomic mental contents.<sup>76</sup> Since the expressive relations making it up are temporally extended, it would also include all the developments in the network that occur over a lifetime.<sup>77</sup>

I will return to this intriguing idea of expressive relations in the discussion of “narrative realism” in Chapter 2 §4; later in Chapter 5, I’ll take up the question of whether it can adequately capture the sense of movement through time that is essential to being a living person. First, however, we need to consider a few of the issues concerning continuity of consciousness in time. These issues are raised by a view that is on the other end of the spectrum from narrative realist approaches—namely Galen Strawson’s account of selves, which rejects *both* thick and thin psychological continuity conditions.

4\. 

### 4 Galen Strawson’s Episodic Self and Continuity of Consciousness

Given his essay titled “Against Narrativity,” Galen Strawson is naturally taken to be a key critic of narrative approaches to identity whose description 

*[p. 20]* ^pg39

of “episodic” selves may constitute a challenging counterexample to claims for the narratival structure of human lives. And this challenge is refreshing given Strawson’s apt concern that the “psychological Narrativity thesis,” which is the descriptive claim that humans typically “see or live or experience their lives as a narrative or story of some sort,” is in danger of becoming a dogma in the humanities and social sciences.<sup>78</sup> As we will see, there are several problems with simplistic invocations of narrative as a description of selves. However, Strawson’s critique of the descriptive thesis turns on the claims that

1\. there is a distinct phenomenon in which a human consciousness experiences itself directly “as an inner mental presence or self” that is given with present consciousness of anything else (for which Strawson uses “I\*,” “me\*,” and their cognates), but that is distinct from the human being that I am, which is a particular animal that lasts through time,<sup>79</sup> and that

2\. some human beings do *not* experience that first-personal “inner mental entity or ‘self’ of some sort”<sup>80</sup> as the *same* entity that was the subject of past experiences of the same human being, or that is anticipated to be the subject of future experiences in their life.

Strawson thinks it follows from these points that the “minimal form” of the “self-experience” described in (1) need not include any persistence beyond the lived present.<sup>81</sup> I will argue that it is hard to know whether this critique has much bearing on narrative claims about practical identity, for which “autobiographical memories” and anticipations that Strawson says are compatible with episodic consciousness may be suffi cient. For (2) is a phenomenological claim that some people do not experience their consciousness as enduring over long periods of time, although the human being to which it is closely related has psychological connection to past experience via memories. But if so, it is obscure what relation (if any) this short-lived self-experience has to their practical identity.

A full assessment of Strawson’s argument would require detailed answers to several sets of questions; but an outline of the issues will help clarify what kinds of continuity in consciousness may be important for practical identity. Here is the first set. Does claim (1) refer to a prethetic or not-reflectively thematized awareness (of) sentience that occurs within current thoughts or experiences (which for convenience, loosely following Sartre), I dub “translucence”? And is this nonpositional reflexivity a necessary component of *all* conscious awareness of anything (as opposed, say, to “blindsight” and similar unconscious registering of information), as held by Fichte, Sartre, and so-called Heidelberg School thinkers such as Dieter Henrich and Manfred Frank?<sup>82</sup> But if it always accompanies thetic awareness of any intentional object, why not think of it as part of the form of consciousness, which is probably the same in lower mammals that lack practical identities (of the 

*[p. 21]* ^pg40

complexity found in persons)? We might say that this experience has to occur in some mind, but that seems like a further step.

Strawson does not hold that consciousness is always reflectively aware of itself (or of a mental thing that is thinking),<sup>83</sup> though this may be an odd implication of introspective theories of sentience.<sup>84</sup> In his original work on the self, he argues that there is a cross-cultural universal “minimal form of genuine self-experience.” Following Anthony Kenny, he calls it an experience as if there is an “inner, mental self,” or “a mental presence, a mental someone; a single mental something or other,” which may develop from discovery of the privacy of consciousness, the sense of being “alone in one’s head,” or “mere awareness of oneself as thinking.”<sup>85</sup> He recognizes that this is very different than practical identity or “the sense of ‘self’ that is discussed in books about ‘personal growth’,” and he associates it with “constant background awareness of our own mental goings on”—though he adds that this notion of background awareness is “imprecise” and inadequate to the phenomenon, because it often flips into “some explicit sense of ourselves as minded.”<sup>86</sup> This seems correct to me, and there may be degrees or levels of ‘backgroundness,’ as well as some intermediate states between minimal prethetic awareness (of) experience when we are fully focused on something outside us and fully articulated reflection on one’s mental operations (as in Descartes’s famous mental exercises).

So Strawson’s thesis (1) is compatible with translucence, but does not assert that the mental entity we experience exists. As he knows, from Kant on, it has been argued that neither reflective nor prerefl ective self-consciousness amount to any proof that a mental substance exists, or any insight into the true nature of *what* is thinking. Yet he makes the phenomenological claim that we have a basic experience of being a “mental *subject* or inner mental presence,” something that has experiences and that acts, and that “nearly everyone has at some time experienced themselves as a kind of bare locus of consciousness, void of personality, but for all that a mental subject.”<sup>87</sup> It is plausible that such a minimal subject is part of the background (at least) in every intentional state *qua* present experience.<sup>88</sup> While this idea is closely related to (1), it may be useful to set it out as a distinct thesis:

3\. along with each conscious thought or intentional act is indirectly given a “subject” who is represented as having this experience (though it is not represented as being this way or that way in the content of the experience, but remains ‘behind the lens’).

We should distinguish this in turn from Strawson’s further metaphysical claim that there must be a subject of thoughts or experiences, which I’ll call (4).

Kant may agree with (3) when he writes in the Transcendental Deduction that “I think” (or “I experience,” as Strawson translates it) must accompany “all my representations”<sup>89</sup>—though no *so-sein* or mode of being is 

*[p. 22]* ^pg41

ascribed to this “I,” but only *da-sein*, existence.<sup>90</sup> As clarified in the Fourth Paralogism, the “I” seems simple because “its representation has no content,” and “it is the mere form of consciousness.”<sup>91</sup> Strawson takes related statements in the Paralogisms to mean that we have to regard thoughts as each time the thoughts *of* a particular mental subject, even though this gives us no proof that the subject is an entity.<sup>92</sup> Kant seems to hold it as analytic that (what I’ll call) a *virtual subject* is implied or traced for us in each experience,<sup>93</sup> although this subject is not like any entity that is directly represented as an object of thought.<sup>94</sup>

This claim is modest. It is more than what Stokes aptly calls the “bare quality of ‘first-personal givenness’ cited by traditional phenomenologists,”<sup>95</sup> because the sense that an experience is *for* a nondescript yet distinct subject is more than the translucency of all sentience (the immediate prerefl ective tie of awareness back to itself). In his Deduction, Kant refers to “pure” or “original” apperception, and he calls the unity it guarantees “transcendental unity of self-consciousness” to distinguish it from the “empirical consciousness, which accompanies different representations” and is as “diverse” as them.<sup>96</sup> While there is plenty of debate about Kant’s meaning, the idealist and analytic traditions have taken from his analysis the lesson that each discrete item’s being for some subject is not enough to explain our ordinary mental life; they have to be combinable in “one consciousness” for various kinds of thoughts (e.g. empirical judgments) to be possible.<sup>97</sup> This “synthetic unity” resulting from “original apperception” is the *form* of our consciousness and the basis of its ability to apply concepts to objects discriminated within the manifold of intuition.<sup>98</sup> Fichte and Sartre probably agree, and Heidegger comes close to this view when says that Dasein or personal being is “in each case mine,” making its being an issue for it. This gives us a first-personal perspective without any “so-sein” or particular properties, which sounds like Kant’s subject of apperception.<sup>99</sup> Strawson does not say that we can get directly from (3) to (4), but (3) still raises questions at the phenomenological level. The translucent reflexivity and experiential givenness of sentience do not *explain* the sense of “virtual mineness” (minimal as it is—for even motives or feelings that I know to be coerced are experienced as “my” occurrent psychic states in this sense). That is clear if some simple animals have conscious states that are translucent yet also lack the virtual sense of a distinct mental subject who is having sentient experience.<sup>100</sup> But if there is such a gap between synthetic apperception (an experience-unifying subject) and mere translucence, then why does our consciousness always come with the former? It cannot originate from the ability to reflect explicitly on one’s awareness, which instead depends on synthetic unity (and a continuing sense of sameness in virtual subject might exist in animals without reflective abilities). Could it result from human consciousness being connected to certain powers of agency and a resulting sense of a source of initiation that at least some lower animals lack? If so, then the minimal experience of a subject described in (3) would be tied to 

*[p. 23]* ^pg42

the practical side of the mind. And so might Strawson’s actual minimal self, if it is the entity veridically disclosed in the minimal experience.

Strawson rejects this on the basis that a “pure observer” consciousness is metaphysically possible.<sup>101</sup> We may eventually be able to find out. Suppose a highly sophisticated parallel processing computer became sentient, but remained incapable of intentional action. This seems possible (and let us hope that the capacity for intentional action does not follow necessarily from sentience, given that some level of nondigital computing power will probably produce sentience). I’ll wager that such a sentient computer would not experience itself as the same virtual subject across multiple thoughts or experiences. If it were capable of honest communication, we might be able to elicit this fact from it. Either way though, Strawson needs his own explanation for why we experience ourselves as a virtual subject, rather than as only a mere dimension of phenomenal givenness.

This brings us to a third, specifically Kantian, question. On one common reading, Kant thinks that unity of a virtual ‘owner’ is indispensible for any propositional thought; he implies that multiple elements have to be experienced as if by the *same subject* to be synthesized in a single judgment.<sup>102</sup> Strawson similarly affi rms that in “the conscious entertaining of a thought like ‘the cat is on the mat’ .  .  . the elements *cat*, *on*, and *mat* are bound together in a single thought.”<sup>103</sup> He follows Kant’s argument in the Second Paralogism that elements of a single cognition at a given instant must be the objects of a single consciousness, and he adds that such a “strongly experientially unified or hiatus-free period of thought” may extend for several seconds.<sup>104</sup> We may grant this Husserlian extension to the “lived present”<sup>105</sup> as within synchronic synthetic unity of apperception. Yet Kant makes clear in the B-edition that he sees all four paralogisms as on a par; in each, “that which cannot be thought otherwise than as subject,” namely the “form of thought” necessary for complex cognitions to be possible, is taken by rationalists to disclose a mental “substance” or entity, when this “unity of consciousness” is actually related to no intuition that could disclose a “substance” (because “in inner intuition there is nothing permanent .  .  .”).<sup>106</sup> However, there is a valid transcendental version of the second premise in each paralogism from which no mental entity can be inferred. So, re the Second Paralogism, at the level of direct nonsensible experience of thought, the unity of the “I” that thinks is analytic.<sup>107</sup> Likewise, in the Third Paralogism, we cannot infer “the identity of the person” or “one’s own substance as a thinking being” through time;<sup>108</sup> but the identity of the *subject* as form of thought at different times is analytic, even if (given Kant’s doctrine of phenomenal time as merely the form of inner sense) the distinct times are strictly represented only “in me.”<sup>109</sup>

Although this is only a phenomenological claim about how we experience consciousness in time, it may not be consistent with Strawson’s claim that there is an episodic way of experiencing this. The problem is clearer if we read the Transcendental Deduction and Second Analogy as implying 

*[p. 24]* ^pg43

that we must also be able to experience real alteration and thus the asymmetric flow of time for causal judgments to be possible.<sup>110</sup> Strawson seems to recognize the force of this argument when he explains that the experience of qualitative change in *oneself* “presupposes that one is diachronically single” in the sense that the same minimal subject experienced the two phases of the alteration.<sup>111</sup> We might then ask: how could Strawson’s episodic person experience himself\* now as *different* than the past subject who (using the eyes and brain of the same human being) experienced some event long ago, unless the underlying form of consciousness linked these two subjects, making the comparison possible? Maybe Strawson could respond that episodics experience no qualitative change in the subject of consciousness then and now, but only a change in numerical identity. But how does one *experience* difference in purely *numerical* identity? Since by definition there is no difference in content to experience here, it seems this could only be experienced as part of the form of our consciousness. And if difference from moment to moment in the minimal apperceptive subject given with the synchronic unity of an experience were in the form of consciousness (contra Kant’s Third Paralogism) then it would be a mere *tautology* that experience of this minimal subject is “episodic” (just as it is analytic that one time-slice of a person is not identical with another time-slice of that person). This is certainly suggested when Strawson says he is following William James’s idea that each “perishing pulse of thought” is a kind of self when he defines “SESMETs” as “subjects of experience that are single mental things,”<sup>112</sup> each of which lasts exactly “\[a\]s long as the experientially unitary period of experience of which it is the subject,”<sup>113</sup> though this is a metaphysical hypothesis.

Yet Strawson denies that the self\*-experience is *necessarily fleeting* when he says that “Some people have a strongly narrative or (more neutrally) *Diachronic* way of thinking about themselves, a strong sense that *the I that is a mental presence now* was there in the past and will be there in the future,” and he contrasts this with the opposite “*Episodic* way of being in time.”<sup>114</sup> But my questions support Patrick Stokes’s critique that Strawson’s account makes it “unclear just what Diachronic experience could possibly consist in, other than some sort of artefact produced by *reflection upon* the thematic continuity of our thoughts across time.”<sup>115</sup> Indeed on Strawson’s view that synchronic unity of apperception is formally necessary but diachronic unity of apperception is not, Stokes’ proposal seems to be the easiest way to explain the sense that a much earlier experience X that I\* now remember is mine\*, while another remembered experience Y of the same human being is not mine\*. Note that this is a point about how we experience ourselves in time, rather than about what selves really are. Owen Flanagan presents the same hypothesis as the idea that the apparent unity of a “stream of experience” over time results from its “unique and distinctive” content, including the way that “thought can create complex models of the stream in which it occurs.”<sup>116</sup>

*[p. 25]* ^pg44

Whether this is a plausible explanation of diachronic experience depends on answers to a fourth line of questions concerning Strawson’s description of memories. In his examples, Strawson is clearly referring not just to memory *that* a given event happened (so-called “semantic” memory of a fact) but to remembering an event *through* remembering a direct experience of it. Diachronic unity of apperception is often assumed in describing this kind of memory: the experiences that depict the recalled fact “are presented as ones that the subject had at the time of the episode” (episode-memory).<sup>117</sup> In commenting on this subset of memories, Richard Wollheim argues that “not only must the event that I remember be an event that I experienced \[in the past\], but I must remember it as I experienced it,” or at least largely so (without large “structural deviations”).<sup>118</sup> Usually episode-memories recall events “from a point of view” that is “represented in the memory,” which he thinks entails the further feature of “my remembering it *as* something that I experienced.”<sup>119</sup> Wollheim calls this “experiential memory,” which he defines as “centered event-memory in which the point of view is that of the rememberer.”<sup>120</sup> And this may be what Strawson means by diachronic experience: one “naturally figures oneself, the self or person one now experiences oneself to be, as something that was there in the (further) past and will be there in the (further) future.”<sup>121</sup> This just seems to be a long temporal extension of the virtual subject or “me\*” of apperception that unites parts of a present experience. For example, suppose, I have the “sense” that it was same subject months ago who saw an advertisement for a vacation and who is now seeing the mountains while on vacation: this may just be synthetic unity of apperception over time. Similarly, when Strawson writes that he has a clear memory of a past event in the life of the human being he is but “no sense that I\* was there” in this past experience,<sup>122</sup> he implies that it is the minimal “me\*” given in the present is missing in the recalled experience (but found in recalled experiences of a diachronic person). Here this seems to be the sense of ‘ownership’ or “lived identification with the subject of the remembered experience” that Strawson says is lacking in most of his autobiographical memories, despite their “‘from the inside’ character” or perspective and the multiple ways their content relates to the life of the “same human being” that he\* knows he still is.<sup>123</sup>

I have assumed that Strawson equates the virtual subject whose apparent identity allows parts of present experience to be thought together and the “me\*” that can be stripped down to its essence in the “minimal form of genuine self-experience,”<sup>124</sup> as several passages imply. This suggests that this minimal me\* is the subject of synchronic apperception, which in episodics can be experienced as “transient” or lasting only for a few seconds at most.<sup>125</sup> On Strawson’s view, then, this true form of the subject seems to explain why episodic selves\* limited to the extended present are the minimal form of self-experience.<sup>126</sup> Yet Strawson also says that the ontology of a SESMET lasting up to three seconds does not limit the experience of I\* or me\* to that duration, that it may often 

*[p. 26]* ^pg45

extend 30 minutes into the past, and that he anticipates his death and recalls a few long-past experiences “as involving Me\*.”<sup>127</sup> But then, the sense of being the *same subject* at distant points in time must be something *other* than diachronic apperception—an extension of the virtual subject that belongs to the *form* of present consciousness; the relevant sense of ‘ownership’ is then more than simply finding *that subject* in long-past experiences.<sup>128</sup>

Equivalently, the surplus of temporal extension in diachronic self-experience over the minimal self-experience cannot be part of the form of consciousness in these cases. Instead, the explanation is that “the apparent continuity of experience, such as it is, and the consistency of perspectives across selves \[as fleeting SESMETs\] derives from the fact that SESMETs ‘appropriate’—in James’ word—the experiential content of the experiences of their predecessors.”<sup>129</sup> In other words, diachronic identification with a distant past experience is constituted by *shared content*, just as Stokes concluded,<sup>130</sup> rather than by diachronic unity of apperception. Over time, the same-subject relation is either a construct of self-interpretation or some more spontaneous part of the content of multiple experiences separated in time.

Strawson does not see this discrepancy or accept that there is any ambiguity in his definition of diachronicity as “the sense that the self I now am was present in the past.”<sup>131</sup> But there is. He writes that experiencing a past action as “one’s own” is the same as experiencing “a present action as one\*’s own action.”<sup>132</sup> If ownership of present experience is due to synchronic unity of apperception, this statement implies that recalling a past action as owned by the recaller would be explained by the diachronic unity of apperception (or identity of the virtual subject then and now). Yet we saw that for Strawson, this apperceptive virtual subject is essentially fleeting; only present experience is unified by the *form* of human consciousness. So the sense of “self” there in some recalled past events, and “the sense that it is I\* who is going to die,” must be based instead on some relation of similarity in content. To clarify this, maybe he should say that it seems to me\* (the virtual subject) now that “I+” \[my term\] experienced past embarrassment,<sup>133</sup> where “I+” and its cognates stand for *content–* *connection-based* sense of self that (in his view) often extends up to 30 minutes even for episodics and much longer for diachronics, and that is in general more elastic than apperceptive unity. I suspect the root of the ambiguity is that I\* and I+ overlap in present experience, but Strawson’s account entails that they can come apart (more evidently in the past and future, but even in the present).

Given this way of resolving the ambiguity, a fifth series of questions follows. If this kind of continuity in I+ is natural for most of us, is something wrong with episodics who do not experience it beyond (say) an hour for most memories? Do they suffer from a kind of dissociative disorder like “somatoparaphrenia, a pathology in which mental states can be conscious 

*[p. 27]* ^pg46

even when they are represented as belonging to someone else?”<sup>134</sup> If it is shared content that constitutes the felt sameness in the subject of experience across time, then why isn’t the “from-the-inside” perspective enough, as Wollheim’s analysis suggested? Are there other added *qualia* involved in (say) a moment of intense shame several years ago that are also in each experience *as present* now (including the remembering of shame)? If it is vividness of the experience that creates a sense of mineness+, as Strawson’s descriptions suggest, then this quality could be lacking in some of my current experiences, e.g. now thinking of a very boring or almost empty object. And if a quality underlying the sense ‘ownership’ stays in the content of certain memories rather than others because of the “charge” with which they were felt *then*, how does it get into the content of an anticipated future experience (is it only *qua* scary that it seems like my+ death?). Moreover, is a memory of a past experience with whose past subject I now identify always this way? Or could I remember my high school graduation today as if I\*/I+ (the subject now reminiscing) was also the one who graduated, but remember the same graduation tomorrow as if it wasn’t me+ whose hand received the diploma?

Finally, what about *time-relative* me\*s? (In order not to beg any questions, I’ll limit myself to Strawson’s notation here.) Here we have a dilemma. If at time *t*2 (now) I have the experience *e*2 of remembering an earlier experience *e*1 that took place at time *t*1, won’t the content of *e*1 as I now remember it have to include it belonging to me\*-at-*t*1, just as *e*2 belongs to me\*-at-*t*2 (though the time-relative me\*s may or may not seem identical)? If we say yes (horn A), then how would the sense that *e*1 happened to the same me\* who is now remembering it at *t*2 work for a diachronic memory? On this view, it seems that the sense that me\*-at-*t*2 = me\*-at-*t*1 would be an extra part of the *content* of *e*2—in addition to the usual experience of me\*-at-*t*2 (now recalling *e*1) being part of *e*2 (which by hypothesis I get even when *e*1 seems non-owned)? Call this the Content thesis. It generates a forward regress: in diachronic persons, the content of later thoughts will expand geometrically via their normal psychological connection to past experiences.

Strawson is likely to say no (horn B), and thus avoid the Content thesis. But then it looks like the relation between me\*-at-*tx* and the relevant events at *tx* is back in the form of human consciousness, not in any contingent part of its content (for if it were in the content, then it belonging to me\*- at-*t*1 *would* be part of *e*1). Then we have a subdilemma: either (B1) diachronic identification with a remembered past conscious episode as mine\* is impossible (which is a *reductio*), or (B2) such ownership of a memory-content is essential to the form of human consciousness after all. On B2, diachronic unity of apperception would always hold in each experiential memory, making thesis (2) false. What this shows is that either way Strawson resolves the ambiguity about “ownership” described three paragraphs up, his account has problems. We need more detail from him about how ownership of a recalled episode occurs.

*[p. 28]* ^pg47

I favor the horn B2, i.e. that minimal recognition of the same virtual subject is a necessary transcendental condition of first-personal episode-memory and anticipation,<sup>135</sup> in which case its apparent absence in “episodic” memories would be a mis-description of something else in their *content*—perhaps a sense of how far away that past seems, or how much we have changed in character since then. For example, an old Ellis Boyd (played by Morgan Freeman) in *Shawshenk Redemption*<sup>136</sup> says that when he thinks of the boy he was, who committed a wanton murder out of sheer stupidity, he remembers him as if he were someone else. He clearly means that the motives of his earlier self are entirely alien to his present character, and not that the synthetic unity of apperception has failed in relation between himself as a present parolee and that unthinking youth. Ironically, on this diagnosis, it seems that episodism might be more a phenomenon of practical identity; in some cases, perhaps it could even result from cultivating a “no-self” theory.<sup>137</sup> But we cannot be sure; the meaning of thesis (2) remains murky.

Until proven otherwise, then, I will assume that psychological connections of memory and its future-oriented analog require diachronic unity of apperception as a formal condition.<sup>138</sup> The connection this implies between me\*-now and me-then is so formal and minimal that it might not be noticed. I will also operate with this assumption while conceding that synchronic unity of apperception does not entail diachronic unity. And I will largely set aside concerns that Strawson’s episodic person or thesis (2) is a challenge to the “psychological Narrativity thesis.” This often seems to be part of Strawson’s argument, because he entertains the hypothesis that “being Diachronic is at least necessary for being Narrative” in one’s self-understanding, and he moves straight from the description of himself as episodic to the claim that “I have absolutely no sense of my life as a narrative with form. . . .”<sup>139</sup> Yet he actually *rejects* the hypothesis that narrativity requires diachronicity, because he argues that a narrative or “form-finding” construal of one’s life can be combined with largely episodic experience of oneself\* over time.<sup>140</sup>

These two points are closely related. If the capacity to understand one’s life in narrative form is important to practical identity, then in Strawson’s view, episodics are not necessarily barred from it (though he clearly thinks some episodics are people for whom narrative self-construal is unnatural). Similarly, if I am wrong that memory and anticipation depend on diachronic unity of apperception, this would simply mean that other forms of connection involved in agency, such as planning via long-term intentions, are possible *without* strong diachronic unity, as Strawson has suggested.<sup>141</sup> Indeed, he has recently added that “When I have a clear sense of my\* moral responsibility with respect to the past actions of GS,” he has all that is needed for moral agency.<sup>142</sup> But if so, then even if thesis (2) can be made coherent, it would not rule out narrative connection as essential to a sense of responsibility (both regular and deep). Thus (2) would not even begin to justify the separate normative thesis that

*[p. 29]* ^pg48

5\. “one’s development as a person” (i.e. a practical identity) need not involve “any significant amount of conscious planning, any need for studied reflection on where one has come from or where one is going, any pre-occupation with one’s life considered specifically *as one’s own life*.”<sup>143</sup>

Given Strawson’s other arguments, episodic agents can reflect on their past and plan for their future as continuing human beings; thesis (2) simply implies that the practical relevance of a sense of diachronic identity in consciousness *as such* is vastly less than we thought. If (2) were true, then I would simply restate the argument of the next chapter using memory-connections without unity of apperception as their basis. Thus Strawson’s ethical claims (e.g. that most types of reflection on one’s life are vanity or indulgence<sup>144</sup>) have to be considered on their own merits—and I will consider some of his objections to the “ethical Narrativity thesis” later (Chapter 3 §4.2).

### 5 Finishing the Preliminaries: The Present Self and the Concept of Character

I have argued that Strawson’s psychological objection to narrative accounts of persons fails both because it is not clear what it means to have episodic versus diachronic experience, and because episodic experience may be compatible with rich forms of narrative continuity in one’s practical identity. However, the attention Strawson draws to the minimal self is useful in three ways for the account to come in the following chapter. First, as noted, it clarifies that arguments for unplanned, “automatic and unpondered”<sup>145</sup> ways of living cannot follow from a theory of consciousness alone. Second, it clarifies that diachronic unity of apperception is normally taken to underlie the work of episode-memory and related types of psychological connection in making “reidentifi cation” (in Schechtman’s sense) possible for us when looking back into our past or forward into our future. Third, it highlights that there is an important sense a person as a *present* subject of consciousness—and a related sense in which we think of ourselves as agents acting right now by deliberating, forming intentions and enacting them, or acting spontaneously in line with our habits and past decisions that helped shaped them. I have suggested that these two senses of present self may be closely related (if the virtual “mineness” of human consciousness depends on our sense of potential for agency); but they are also metaphysically distinct, given that a conscious animal might have continuity in virtual subject of consciousness without robust forms of free will attributed to personal agents on many accounts.

Thus narrative theories face the objection that your present self (in both these senses) is not identical with your whole life-history, though you now 

*[p. 30]* ^pg49

recall past events happening to the same subject of consciousness that you would have called your present self *then*. Patrick Stokes phrases this as an “apparent bifurcation of the self as a narrative whole and the self as present, conscious agent” (notice the “agent” part).<sup>146</sup> I doubt Strawson’s view that this “core or minimal self, \[as\] the present locus of consciousness” is ever given (even indirectly or virtually) as pure subject or bare noetic pole of experience; but as noted in connection with Wollheim earlier, I accept a *prima facie* distinction between our sense of present agency and “the narrative or autobiographical self” as a whole life or the process of leading that life.<sup>147</sup> As Stokes notes, Dan Zahavi has also emphasized this distinction and invoked it to explain how identity-formation can get going.<sup>148</sup> However, it is useful to distinguish empirical questions about generation or *initiation* of practical identity from the basic *bifurcation* objection, as I’ll call it, which concerns the appearance that you (as present subject) are moving through time *and* building up a life-history through this movement—giving us two distinct senses of “self.” I will argue later (Chapter 5) that this bifurcation is less deep than it first seems: for the perspective from which we experience and choose as present agent includes the practical identity we have developed through our history *thus far*, which (if suffi cient narratival connections hold) is an *extension of the same* life-story we had at earlier points in time. What the objection shows is that narrative theories have to capture the experience of flowing time (the moving “now”) as essential to a living personal identity.

The inseparability of the self as present conscious agent and a growing practical identity is indicated in a preliminary way in the complex phenomena of “character.” Returning briefly to this topic will complete the groundwork for the analysis in the next chapter. In most of its common uses, “character” has an *intermediate* status between the poles of pure phenomenal subjectivity in the present moment and the patterns of a whole life. Character, by definition, has a lasting quality; its relative stability is why Aristotle thought it was so crucial for noble friendship. But one’s character can still change over time, even radically, as the earlier example from *Shawshenk Redemption* illustrates. And the choices of the self as present agent can be involved in such changes of character. If there were no essential connection between that present self and the identity one forms throughout a whole life, it would be impossible for a person’s character to play such an intermediate role. Thus, if character-traits are real phenomena rather than a convenient fiction,<sup>149</sup> then the Stokes/Zahavi bifurcation cannot go ‘all the way down.’

There are many analyses of “character” in contemporary moral psychology indebted to the traditions of virtue ethics (both western and eastern), but four accounts are enough to show how the concept suggests complex forms of continuity in motivation. A representative wide notion of “character,” which Zahavi attributes to Paul Ricoeur, includes “the totality of our enduring dispositions and habits, those distinctive traits that enable others 

*[p. 31]* ^pg50

to recognize and reidentify us” as the same person.<sup>150</sup> Ricoeur emphasizes “the distinctive marks which permit the reidentifi cation of the human being as the same,” or “the set of lasting dispositions by which a person is recognized,” especially in interpersonal contexts.<sup>151</sup> Though he also rejects “characterology” (personality-type theory) as an attempt to specify an objective set of character-traits in a person, or a “portrait painted from the outside,” Ricoeur did initially see character as an immutable “finite, unchosen perspective,” a complete way of seeing “the world of things, ideas, values, and persons.”<sup>152</sup> “Character” in this inclusive sense is like one’s overall personality, given that it includes aspects of temperament and attitude fixed by earliest upbringing in combination with innate predispositions that pervade all other aspects of one’s personality, some of which may be more under one’s control. In this maximal sense, the “permanence of character” is a tautology that tends to erase the distinction between “*idem*”-identity (or continuity of the same substance in space-time) and “*ipse*”-identity (or self as responsible for action), in contrast to the promise-keeping self of “self-maintenance” which emphasizes the *idem–ipse* distinction because it is only by continued choice that *ispe*-self maintains a practical analog of *idem*-identity.<sup>153</sup> Such “character” is the polar opposite of the minimal subject of present consciousness. However, once Ricoeur reinterprets character according to the more familiar medieval notion of “second nature,” it regains intermediate status as a stable but historically developing “what” of the one “who” acts, giving character a “narrative dimension.”<sup>154</sup>

As this theory indicates, the sort of patterns sought in “trait-type theories” in empirical psychology,<sup>155</sup> vary widely: as Flanagan says, they may be “cognitive, affective, or behavioral;” they may be more or less “situation-sensitive;” they can refer to motives or only to patterns in outward action (e.g., “liberal” with money, “dry” or alcohol-avoiding); they can also be vague or specific (e.g. “friendly” versus “immediately at ease”).<sup>156</sup> Moreover, a few traits seem to refer to basic casts of “temperament” that condition many other traits: “Warm and cold are central in this sense, polite and rude are not.”<sup>157</sup> But these different types of disposition may all be insuffi - cient to reveal character on most conceptions, which are narrower in focus than Ricoeur’s. For similar patterns of behavior, attitude, and emotion can result from quite different values, convictions, motives, and standing intentions, while differences in demeanor and basic temperament can also mask deep convergences in basic commitments or ground projects. As Candace Upton argues, our best reason for attributing character-traits or ongoing states of mind to persons is that they are the appropriate targets of ethical appraisal that goes beyond particular acts but that does not directly assess the whole person: evaluation in terms of virtue-concepts looks to this intermediate level.<sup>158</sup>

Similarly, on Williams’ view, it is a subset of motive-dispositions that constitute character, namely those that are closely related to a person’s sense of what makes life worthwhile or gives it basic meaning; and, as 

*[p. 32]* ^pg51

commitments, these motives are self-perpetuating (or involve a reflexive affi rmation of their priority over conflicting desires and the importance of their continued pursuit). In this sense, a person’s character cannot change upon a whim, or diametrically invert for expediency. If someone apparently devoted to political causes changes political party merely for higher pay, or someone with long service in a church suddenly converts to another religion just to gain favor with a potential romantic partner, we would normally judge that the character we believed to be underlying their prior patterns of behavior, emotion, and judgment had been an illusion (and not that a real character had suddenly changed). We might attribute certain vices to such an agent, but they would be marks of shallowness more than of any definite character in Williams’s sense. Still, these agents might be just as cheerful or as dour as another agent who would never change authentic commitments for such reasons of convenience.

Given its narrower focus, we may say that Williams off ers a conception of *volitional* character, which underlies those personality traits that are more immediately evident to others (and usually ascribed to a person by others in the normal course of social interaction). I have attributed such a conception to Kierkegaard, whose pseudonymous work on freedom suggests (as in Duns Scotus) that there are *dispositions of will* and choice that are distinct from mere habits of intentional action.<sup>159</sup> Ricoeur also moves in this direction when he distinguishes dispositions that result from “*acquired identifications*” with “values, norms, ideals, models, and heroes, in which the person or community recognizes itself.” In this sense, character is volitional because it involves an ongoing eff ort to be loyal to the objects of identification, and thus also “fidelity . . . towards maintaining the self.”<sup>160</sup> A character-self in this sense involves stabilized “preferences, evaluations, and estimations” with ethical import. Yet even when someone’s volitional character is very robust and well-defined, we can still distinguish between *what* someone’s character is and *who* she is as the agent of a particular choice, which can be out-of-character.<sup>161</sup> I would add that we could distinguish her ongoing current volitional character from an arc of development we might discern throughout her whole life (either up till now, or looking back after her death). Character lies in between these limits; for, as Ricoeur says, the person’s existing (volitional) character itself “has a history” that will in turn have a “narrative” form, but one that is not finished.<sup>162</sup>

A similar result follows from Stanley Hauerwas’s detailed analyses of character as an “orientation” of will and related faculties that “provides a proper transition from our past to our future.”<sup>163</sup> While Hauerwas holds with MacIntyre that this concept can be clarified by drawing on the Aristotelian tradition, he also holds that it is indispensible to Christian theology, even though in this tradition, freedom of choice means that an individual transcends the “sum total” of his actions up to any given point.<sup>164</sup> By developing accounts of voluntary action and agent-causation, Hauerwas argues that character is a “the qualification of man’s self-agency .  .  . by which 

*[p. 33]* ^pg52

a man acquires a moral history befitting his nature as a self-determining being.”<sup>165</sup> This is volitional character in a strong sense, i.e. character over which we have control, for which we are responsible—or what I have called autonomous character, which goes with a normative thesis claiming that we should make an eff ort to shape our character rather than simply letting our nature and nurture rule in us.<sup>166</sup> While character is shaped in multiple ways by our social relationships, predefined social roles, and shared vocabularies for interpreting and explaining action, our responsibility requires that our (volitional) character not be entirely determined by these factors.<sup>167</sup> Like Kant (and Korsgaard), Hauerwas thinks that motives do not explain our actions without our involvement; appetites, inclinations, and other forms of desire become our “motives” through our incorporation, which is also an implicit endorsement of one kind of identity rather than another: “in moving ourselves as agents we embody the motives that give our action and ourselves their peculiar unity and form—in a word, their character.”<sup>168</sup> The larger character of a self is a kind of “direction” seen in the character of our important actions. Thus, for Hauerwas, character emerges from the very nature of free intentional activity:

Character is the qualification or determination of our self-agency, formed by our having certain intentions (and beliefs) rather than others. Once it is clear that character is but the concrete determination of our agency we can understand why no ultimate distinction can be made between acquiring character and having character. . . . Since it is born only in intentional behavior, it exists only as a qualification of that continuing \[voluntary\] behavior. . . .

 .  .  . character cannot be thought of as a kind of outer manifestation that leaves a more fundamental self hidden; it is the very reality of who we are as self-determining agents. Our character is not determined by our particular society, environment, or psychological traits; these become part of our character . . . but only as they are received and interpreted in the descriptions which we embody in intentional action.<sup>169</sup>

There are two key ideas to explain here. First, Hauerwas is suggesting that character is implied in the narrative structure of intention and action themselves: the beliefs, values, and reasons that inform our long-term ends or more embracing intentions (including ground projects) make a pattern that is properly called a character. Character is not an epiphenomenon supervening on many discrete actions, but rather a dynamic tendency emerging in but also shaping the process of choice: “it is the particular direction our agency acquires by choosing to act in some ways rather than others.”<sup>170</sup> Second, while he thinks this volitional character underlies more superficial traits and personality-features, he rejects the idea that leeway-libertarian freedom (to which his religious starting point commits him) implies a “true self” that is hidden behind volitional 

*[p. 34]* ^pg53

character or separate from it.<sup>171</sup> Although he denies that “a man’s action can be explained wholly in terms of his will, motives, desires, or character,” because our freedom allows us to transcend the inertia in these patterns,<sup>172</sup> the presently choosing agent is never the “naked self” that Stokes finds in some of the writings of Kierkegaard’s pseudonym Judge William—a self momentarily standing outside all its acquired dispositions.<sup>173</sup> Our agency is not “that of a formal or transcendental ‘I’;” instead, the self is inherently “relational” both in the sense of requiring social context and of being “open to change and growth.”<sup>174</sup> Hauerwas thinks of the freedom exercised by a moral agent in the present moment as intelligible only within the same narrative process in which we find character. This view is also close to the notion of dispositions in free will itself that I have attributed to Kierkegaard, as noted above.

However, here the notion of volitional character as an intermediate structure encounters a set of hard questions that may have no precise answer. As Hauerwas notes, it is unclear “exactly what kind or what level of generality” the direction of agency that constitutes character has. Some particular acts and choices have “deeper or wider significance” for expressing or shaping our character than do many others.<sup>175</sup> What course I decide to teach probably says more about my goals than what cereal I pull off a supermarket shelf, for example; whether you decide to be a parent is usually more momentous for the shape of your life than how neatly you tend your garden (though of course such aesthetic choices can reveal personality traits and sometimes reflect deeper facets of one’s identity). This suggests that character is not maximally embracing like one’s whole life-history; the direction that is one’s character is not “our most general intention under which the greatest number of actions can be grouped” (if we have one), or what we spend most of our time doing; it may be a smaller subset of our actions that we feel “reveal who we really are” or that most fully express what we care about.<sup>176</sup> Nor does a person need “one all-consuming purpose” or “one all-dominant description . . . embodied in all his activity;” instead, “most people’s character” consists in a “more of less consistent set of intentions and descriptions variously interrelated in some hierarchy of priority in a way that provides a general orientation.”<sup>177</sup> I think this description aims at roughly the right level of thematic connectedness that we pick out when articulating someone’s volitional character (for example, saying that Y is a very loving, trustworthy, nonjudgmental person without a bigoted bone—which says nothing about whether Y is shy or gregarious, a maven or someone who prefers to have just one friend, comical or serious, etc.). It must be part of the function of a theory of personal autonomy to clarify these issues about the level at which volitional character-descriptions operate, because it is this sort of character for which autonomy is supposed to make us responsible. Thus we will see in Chapter 3 how a narrative account of autonomy may help answer Hauerwas’s questions while synthesizing his insights.

Finally, in his influential analysis, Joel Kupperman aims at something similar to Hauerwas’s notion of direction or orientation, but with a new twist. Like the three other accounts we have briefly considered, Kupperman’s distinguishes between character and such widely varying types of personality features as distinctive quirks that make someone stand out, charisma or persuasiveness, charm or wit versus being a bore, being insecure or confident, behavior patterns such as stuttering or speaking fluidly, or habits of thought like a tendency to obsess.<sup>178</sup> By contrast, “the word character has moral overtones that the word personality lacks.”<sup>179</sup> Having “character” in general is associated with moral reliability that is incompatible with strong conformist or conventionalist tendencies, while “good character suggests the presence of virtues and absence of major vices.”<sup>180</sup> But character is not simply constituted by a disposition for choices to be guided by ethical values; Williams is right, in Kupperman’s view, to say that personal “responsibilities one takes on may well engage one’s life more deeply” and reveal one’s character more than a single performance in a moral test-case. Nevertheless, one’s character is not simply identical with one’s “projects and commitments;” for “\[i\]f Bludgeon, who has been a dogmatic and unreflective Communist suddenly becomes a dogmatic and unreflective extreme right-wing conservative, we might well say that Bludgeon’s character has not changed at all.”<sup>181</sup>

While “at all” might be an overstatement, there is surely an insight here. Kupperman’s point is that some aspects of character may continue across changes in life-goals or projects, even if these are quite stark, and be expressed in *the way* they form, pursue, or modify the cares or activities that give meaning to their life:

*how* someone maintains or fails to maintain commitments and responsibilities normally counts heavily towards character, as do the sort of commitments and responsibilities someone takes on. Character also is displayed in the day-to-day quality of relationships with people to whom we have responsibilities or who have responsibilities to us.<sup>182</sup>

Character-traits such as dogmatism or self-critical inquiry, ambitiousness or is absence, sensitivity to people in relationships versus insensitivity, or being authentically present versus withdrawn or distant all illustrate this point: they color how people develop and execute the patterns of intention that seem volitionally central to their identity, so that two people (A and B) might have very similar commitments and relationships at present but very different ways of forming them, living them out in particular actions, and being open to amending them. As these *adverbial* traits modify how we manifest our willed projects and relationships (perhaps partly by bringing other features of personality to bear on them), these traits are ethically important. For example, Kupperman thinks “strength” of character is revealed in the ability to resist at least some significant “pressures, temptations, and 

*[p. 36]* ^pg55

diffi culties” out of loyalty to standing goals and relationships—or the tendency to refuse to violate or abandon them for the wrong sorts of reasons.<sup>183</sup> Such an adverbial trait as strength of will can apply to many different commitments, but it operates as a formal trait requiring some volitional “matter” on which to work: “it is not possible to have what we would call a strong character and to have no ongoing concerns or commitments whatsoever,” being ready to abandon current ends whenever the winds of fortune or appetite change direction.<sup>184</sup> In other words, what Frankfurt has called wantonness is a dearth of character in Kupperman’s sense.

Notice that such adverbial aspects of character involve patterns that may only be recognizable in a process that extends at least from the genesis of a ground project or other commitment through its particular embodiment in concrete activities, relations, and the manner of carrying them out (e.g. sticking to them or betraying them), and on to possible voluntary alterations in them. In other words, to recognize all these aspects of character, a short-term snapshot of a person (even in tough circumstances) over a month or even a year will probably be insuffi cient. We need something more like the *story* of their development; we need to know how they got like that and how they are prepared to respond to new challenges. Thus character in Kupperman’s expanded sense “unifies a person’s life through time” even through significant changes in purposes, though not necessarily throughout their entire life-history.<sup>185</sup>

To appreciate this insight, return to Parfi t’s “Russian Nobleman” example. The Nobleman’s social ideals move him to a precommitment: he asks his wife to promise to enforce a legal document giving to the poor the lands he will inherit in later years, even if his future self abandons these ideals for other ends that require the property.<sup>186</sup> As we saw, Williams views this kind of precommitment as essential to volitional character (to lack it, as a utilitarian must, is to be wanton). While this explains how present commitments have deliberative priority for Williams, the Nobleman case appears confusing because Parfi t suggests that commitments can change autonomously, and that seems right.<sup>187</sup> Could the older Nobleman then be regarded as the same person in a practical sense? While “same” may misleadingly imply bivalence, if the opposition is between earlier and later *volitional* states (rather than alienated desires, as when an unwilling addict anticipates being overcome by cravings), the older Nobleman’s goals may have *prima facie* authority later in time. But we do not know whether the Nobleman’s commitments have *autonomously* changed: Is there a development from earlier to later by which the change is made intelligible in terms of deeper principles or an appreciation of new values that grew out of his earlier views—e.g. perhaps a love of children that developed from the same root that led him to care about the poverty of peasants? Or are his new ideals a result of brainwashing, or weak-willed conformance to the expectations of peers whose contempt he could not stand? Obviously these factors could matter to his wife in deciding whether she should 

*[p. 37]* ^pg56

keep her earlier promise. Kupperman’s suggestion that character may not wholly alter even in radical changes in projects points towards these questions. A full narrative conception of the development of character would help us know what further information would be required to decide the Nobleman’s case.

If that is convincing, or even sounds plausible, then a successful theory of autonomy as the control over character needed for deep responsibility should start from a narrative conception of practical identity in general. That is the task to which we now turn, bearing in mind the lesson that a self as present choice-maker and identity as a gestalt of one’s whole life-history are connected through the mediation of character. If autonomy is possible, that which controls character cannot be entirely distinct from that which it controls, and a life-path cannot be entirely distinct from the person traveling it. We will see how well narrative accounts can make sense of this.
