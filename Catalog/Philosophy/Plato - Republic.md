## About

Plato's dialogue in ten books on justice, asking whether the just person is happier than the unjust. To answer, Socrates builds an ideal city in speech, on an analogy between the city and the individual.

- **People and setting:** Socrates narrates. He and Glaucon go down to the Piraeus for the festival of the Thracian goddess Bendis and are brought to the house of Polemarchus, where they talk with his aged father Cephalus. The other speakers are Polemarchus, Thrasymachus, Glaucon and Adeimantus.
- **Dramatic date:** during the Peloponnesian War. The SEP (Nails and Monoson) says it is set loosely throughout the war (431–404 BC); Wikipedia reports Nails's view that any single date between 432 and 404 produces anachronisms.
- **Composition:** Wikipedia gives about 375 BC. The IEP's chronology puts Book I among the early dialogues and Books II–X in the middle period (c. 380–360 BC), and cites Nails's finding that writing and re-editing may have gone on over a very long period. Wikipedia notes that Book I is often thought a separate earlier work.
- **Contents:** Book I tests definitions of justice; II–IV build the city, the guardians' education, the myth of metals and the three-part soul; V sets out common families among the guardians and rule by philosophers; VI–VII the Form of the Good, the sun, the divided line and the cave; VIII–IX the decline of regimes down to tyranny; X the critique of imitative poetry, the immortality of the soul and the myth of Er.
- **Place among the dialogues:** generally assigned to the middle period (IEP, Republic entry; Wikipedia).
- **Why it matters:** the IEP says it has been Plato's most famous and widely read dialogue since the mid-19th century. Wikipedia lists Aristotle's *Politics*, Cicero's *De re publica*, Augustine's *City of God* and More's *Utopia* among works it shaped. Readings differ: Popper (1945) called its city totalitarian, while Strauss and Bloom took it as an exercise rather than a blueprint.
