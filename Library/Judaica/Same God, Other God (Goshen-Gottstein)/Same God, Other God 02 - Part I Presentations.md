## Part I Presentations

 Overview 

 Part I consists of three introductions, presenting the project as a whole. The first introduction is the “classical” book introduction. The book’s problematics and key strategy are spelled out. The book seeks to engage two interrelated questions—the status of Hinduism as idolatry, Avoda Zara in Hebrew, and a conceptual revisiting of the very category of Avoda Zara , with Hinduism as its test case or, if you will, its dialogue partner. The four parts of the book and the unfolding of the thesis are also presented, without entering into the details of the argument. 

 The second introduction offers some pointers concerning the author’s background and experiences. A scholar of Jewish studies, the author has had a deep personal interest in Hinduism for decades and has cultivated numerous relationships with Hindu religious leaders and teachers. The author’s voice is accordingly that of a thinker and educator, as well as a dialogue partner, who draws on his personal experiences and encounters as foundations for the present project. The author does not shy away from complementing the theoretical analyses of this book with his own first-person voice. 

 The third introduction leads us into the subject matter by profiling a particular event, which can serve as a test case or a model for the entire project. The event that occurred in 2004 concerns a rabbinical ruling on the status of wigs worn by ultra-Orthodox Jewish women. These wigs were made of natural human hair, whose provenance is in Hindu temples, where the hair is shorn as part of a vow or offering to the deity. Leading rabbis made a pronouncement on these wigs, based on their view of Hinduism as being idolatrous. The sight of public wig-burning made international headline news. After presenting this model case, a series of questions is posed concerning appropriate procedures for studying another religion—in our case, Hinduism. How do we go about understanding ritual? What is the relationship between ritual and religious practice and theological and philosophical understandings of the religion? Who holds the keys to interpreting the religion correctly? Who speaks for Hinduism? In short, what can be learned from this incident in terms of how to *not* go about 

*[p. 2]* ^pg13

studying another religion and how might a different methodology have yielded better understanding and consequently a different ruling concerning Hinduism’s status as Avoda Zara. 

 While this book does not seek to present a systematic view of Hinduism that is an alternative or complement to how rabbinical authorities went about issuing their ruling, it assumes such an understanding and refers to it throughout. Based on a more philosophical view of Hinduism, on encounters with leading Hindu figures, and on an appreciation of Hindu spiritual life, it seeks to problematize the ruling of these rabbis, even as it problematizes the very application of the category of Avoda Zara. The book is not an argument for Hinduism *not* being Avoda Zara. Rather, it is an argument for applying a richer and more nuanced method and for rethinking the very category of Avoda Zara. In the process it provides the building blocks from which an argument for Hinduism not being Avoda Zara could be made.

### 1 Book Presentation

 This book grows out of a classical Jewish perspective and seeks not only to address a question that arises out of that perspective but also to revise that 

very perspective. The perspective is that of viewing other religions through the lens of idolatry, foreign worship, Avoda Zara . It is a deep-seated perspective that is thousands of years old, continuing biblical and rabbinic attitudes down to the present day. The question that arises out of this perspective concerns a particular religion, Hinduism, and its status as Avoda Zara. In the crudest way: Is Hinduism Avoda Zara? Rather than offer a “yes” or “no” answer, this book seeks to revisit the very perspective of how other religions are viewed, leading us to rethink the very category of Avoda Zara, even as it tries to work through the very complex reality of Hindu religious reality, in light of that category. Whether at the end of the day a “yes,” “no,” a question mark, or some combination of these elements of the terse formulation of the question emerge as the book’s conclusion may itself depend on the reader and what she takes away from what is more an exercise in thought than a guide to action. <sup>1</sup> 

 Hinduism provides a wonderful opportunity to reengage the subject of Avoda Zara and examine how we apply it. Judaism’s encounter with Hinduism is relatively new, <sup>2</sup> not encumbered by attitudes that are thousands of years old and by the painful history that did much to reinforce such attitudes. This allows us to make the encounter with Hinduism not only an occasion to assess that religion but also a context for thinking through in fresh ways the very categories that have furnished our attitudes to other religions and that are consequently applied, at times carelessly, to a Jewish view of Hinduism. Accordingly, in what follows I shall not only consider the various angles from which Hinduism may be considered in terms of the classical Jewish concern for Avoda Zara, but also explore how we apply that category. Posing the question of how we apply the category of Avoda Zara is not driven by an interest in reaching one position or another in relation to Hinduism. It grows, rather, from my own assessment of what is required from a contemporary Jewish theology of religions and is informed as much by my contact with Hinduism as it is by my contact with other religions, primarily Christianity. Therefore it is not an argument for Hinduism *not* being Avoda Zara. Rather, it is an exercise in how to think about Avoda Zara and, relatedly, how to view Hinduism through this lens. While it provides building 

*[p. 4]* ^pg15

blocks for a position that exonerates Hinduism from the charges of Avoda Zara, it leaves the final verdict to the reader. Better yet, it makes the determination of Hinduism’s status as Avoda Zara a function of so many variables that at the end of the day the reader must reflect and decide, rather than rely on a facile answer provided by me or anyone else. 

 The novelty of the encounter with Hinduism and how partial our knowledge of it still is lead to the inevitable recognition that all issues that are raised in the present work must be considered as work in progress, items requiring further thought and deliberation. In fact, one of my main contentions is that it is too early for us to conclude with any finality anything with regard to Hinduism. Its reality is simply too complex and our knowledge of it too recent to be able to resolve the complicated issues involved in addressing idolatry in relation to Hinduism. The issues raised in this book require more than one or two high-level meetings of religious leaders, the reading of a handful of books, or even the studied reflection of one or more specialists devoting time to exploring these issues. The questions raised require time, process, and a degree of comfort and familiarity that we may not be able to attain even within the scope of a generation. Perhaps it is awareness that, no matter what, we are only taking first steps and there is a long journey ahead that emboldens me to publish the present volume, despite my feelings of inadequacy where insider knowledge of Hinduism is concerned. 

 To see Hinduism through the lens of Avoda Zara is probably the central governing perspective through which most halachic Jews (those following the body of Jewish law) would approach an examination of Hinduism. Let me illustrate how much of a knee-jerk response to Hinduism this emphasis on idol worship has become for common Jews by sharing an email exchange I had, prior to one of my trips to India, with my uncle Gershon. I had written him, excusing myself for not having enough time to engage some of his ideas, as I would have liked, saying: “I’m off to India for nearly a month on Sunday and under lots of pressure.” His response was telling: “Good luck in India, where the avodah zarah must be fascinating.” Avoda Zara is the primary halachic category through which Jews consider the permissibility and legitimacy of other religions. It literally means “foreign worship,” connoting both the worship of foreign gods and foreign means of worshipping God. It is considered the gravest of sins and a clear identity marker. Avoda Zara is what Jews fear most about their association with other religions. The worship of images has been the major expression of Avoda Zara throughout the generations, and the means by which Jews recognize, intuitively even more than by means of study and reason, the problems associated with another religion. When my uncle therefore refers to India in terms of Avoda Zara, he is conjuring up a range of attitudes and perceptions that governs Jewish views of other religions. To say they are Avoda Zara is to claim they are off limits for Jews, problematic in and of themselves, and likely devoid of spiritual value. None of this is stated or worked out, nor is the balance of positive and problematic dimensions of another religion considered in a manner particular to that religion. The mere appeal to Avoda Zara simply conjures up all these associations, effectively defining a religion as beyond the pale and hence not an appropriate subject of association. What is interesting, however, in this brief and brilliant summary of an entire Jewish mind-set is that Indian religion is not simply 

*[p. 5]* ^pg16

categorized as Avoda Zara, but that it is described as fascinating. Fascination is coupled with suspicion and rejection, by the very appeal to Avoda Zara. An entire complex worldview and cultural attitude are summed up in this pithy comment. 

 Almost every contemporary Jewish writer on Hinduism acknowledges the challenge of Avoda Zara. <sup>3</sup> None, however, has attempted to deal with the subject extensively, in a way that is commensurate with the place it occupies in the Jewish psyche and with how Jews conceptualize their relations with other religions. My decision to devote a monograph-length study to this subject stems from my appreciation of its centrality to any normative Jewish view of Hinduism and the complexity of the challenge involved. More than solutions, I seek to lay out the issues and to bring to light the unexamined assumptions we carry, the problematics of how we approach other religions, and the challenge of what it means to understand another religion on its own terms and how that might impact our view of another religion as Avoda Zara. 

 The instinctive response of my uncle became public policy ten years ago when the subject of Hinduism as Avoda Zara made headline news. The event that occurred in 2004 concerns a rabbinical ruling on the status of wigs worn by ultra-Orthodox Jewish women, made of natural human hair, whose provenance is in Hindu temples, where the hair is shorn as part of a vow or offering to the deity. Leading rabbis made a pronouncement on these wigs, based on their view of Hinduism as being idolatrous. The sight of public wig-burning made international headline news. After presenting this model case, a series of questions is posed concerning appropriate procedures for studying another religion—in our case, Hinduism. How do we go about understating ritual? What is the relationship between ritual and religious practice and theological and philosophical understandings of the religion? Who holds the keys to interpreting the religion correctly? Who speaks for Hinduism? I bring this case as an introduction to the book because it raises so many cardinal questions. We will have recourse to these questions throughout the book. 

 Let me now offer an overview of the book’s structure. The book is divided into five parts of unequal length. The book’s flow is best captured by identifying the key question that is addressed in each part. The methodology of this work is largely one of framing and reframing the leading question concerning Avoda Zara. Introducing the leading question of each of the book’s parts is therefore a helpful way of following its argument. I will not enter now into a more detailed presentation of the breakdown of each part and the unfolding of the argument in each section of the book. This has been done at the beginning of each part, where an overview of the following chapters, their key arguments, and contributions are offered. Let us at this point appreciate the project by zooming out and following its unfolding, by means of its leading questions. 

 The first part answers the question of what is this book, what is this project. It serves as the book’s introduction. It comprises several introductions. One is the present chapter, introducing the book. The second is a brief introduction of myself, my experiences, and the resources I bring to this project. The third is a detailed presentation of the just-mentioned wig-burning incident, which takes us to the heart of the concerns of this book. 

*[p. 6]* ^pg17

 The second part answers the question: What is Avoda Zara? It offers two introductions to the category. The first is conceptual-historical and addresses the historical evolution of the category, explaining what is at stake. Avoda Zara is a central category through which Jewish law and Jewish thought evaluate other religions, their adequacy, and their legitimacy. It has multiple dimensions of meaning and the discussion explores these, as well as the meaning of their being subsumed under one category. The second introduction speaks of the psychological, emotional, and attitudinal implications of the charge of Avoda Zara. The pronouncement of another religion as Avoda Zara is not only a statement of permissibility of worship or legitimacy of a spiritual path; it also conjures up a range of attitudes that includes, or can include, scorn, contempt, exclusion, delegitimation, and invalidation. There are inevitable psychological and attitudinal implications to the determination that another religion is Avoda Zara, unless these are kept at bay through educational strategies, or through some of the ways of viewing Avoda Zara that will be discussed in the third part. 

 The third part of the book is the longest and in many ways the hardest part of the book, certainly for the reader who is not familiar with rabbinic discourse and reasoning. This part answers the question of what makes a religion Avoda Zara. If the second part introduced the very category, the third part poses the question of what it is that leads to the determination that another religion is Avoda Zara. Here we draw on the work of medieval Jewish authorities and present multiple understandings of what constitutes Avoda Zara. The different views, many of which were formed in response to Christianity, provide different, at times conflicting, perspectives on what it is that makes a religion Avoda Zara. With reference to each of these we ask how this criterion, how the view of this particular authority, could be made to address the case of Hinduism. We need to follow this procedure because Judaism has had next to no contact with Hinduism. Consequently, until recent years there has never been a serious evaluation of Hinduism as a religion, and certainly no well-informed and considered rabbinic discussion of its status as Avoda Zara. Individual travelers did encounter Hinduism and brought to their personal encounter the attitudes by which they had been shaped either in their Christian milieu or through exposure to Judaism’s fundamental Scriptures. <sup>4</sup> But there is almost no meaningful rabbinic, halachic discussion of Hinduism’s status until the twenty-first century. In this state of affairs we lack one of the fundamental tools for holding a rabbinic discussion—precedent. We therefore have to rely on other precedents and to transpose their findings to the case of Hinduism. The precedent that comes most readily to mind and concerning which we do have ample literature is that of Christianity. While both Hindus and Christians may be unhappy about being placed in the same halachic boat, from a Jewish perspective the case is quite similar, allowing us to draw lessons from one to the other. Both are religions that profess faith in one God in some meaningful way. Both also exhibit a faith in God that does not conform to the simple affirmation of divine unity as taught in Judaism. Both use images in worship, and both have incarnational understandings of the most important personalities in the respective religious system. While there are meaningful theological differences between them, one could argue that what they have in common, from 

*[p. 7]* ^pg18

a Jewish perspective, outweighs their differences. And whatever differences the Jewish observer may note, making Hinduism appear stranger, less familiar, more idolatrous etc., could in theory be discounted as expressions of the novelty of the encounter and lack of familiarity with Hindu reality, rather than fundamental differences that distinguish one religion from the other. Therefore, if we require precedents to advance in our evaluation and lack such precedents in relation to Hinduism, we can draw on precedents from Judaism’s view of Christianity as a foundation for the present discussion. 

 In seeking precedents we do not seek to simply transfer a ruling from one domain to another. Rather, we seek models for how other religions are viewed, different answers to the question of what makes another religion Avoda Zara. References to Christianity, whether they affirm Christianity’s status as Avoda Zara or absolve it from the charge, will tell us what it is that makes another religion Avoda Zara. In seeking an answer to this question we shall turn not only to explicit references to Christianity but also to medieval authors, specifically Maimonides and Nachmanides, who offer their own theories of the formation and evolution of Avoda Zara, thereby allowing us to get a handle on what it is that makes another religion Avoda Zara. 

 With reference to all the authors and precedents brought in Part III , we shall attempt to make those voices speak to the case of Hinduism. The application of these principles to Hinduism is not necessarily straightforward and I shall attempt to bring to light what is at stake in a given application, what is possible, and what are the implications of applying a given position or precedent to the case of Hinduism. The third part of this work will give us much food for thought, multiple perspectives, and also some important voices and approaches that lead to the view that Hinduism should not be considered Avoda Zara. 

 Part IV reframes the leading question as a way of moving the discussion forward. In Part III we asked what it is that makes another religion Avoda Zara, with special reference to Hinduism. In Part IV we revisit the same sources but now with another question framing the discussion. Here we move from the “other god” question to the “same god” question. The discussion in Part IV assumes that the criteria by which we declare Avoda Zara and the criteria by means of which we affirm that the same God is worshipped in two religions are not the same. It is possible that even authorities who declare a given religion to be Avoda Zara might still be able to affirm that it is the same God that is worshipped in both religions. This recognition has huge implications. Affirmation of a common God changes the tone and attitude toward another religion, neutralizing one important component of the myriad of positions and attitudes that are involved in the view of another religion as Avoda Zara. It would also make us consider more readily whether we wish to affirm the declaration of another religion as Avoda Zara or whether we might be more inclined to alternative views and criteria for what constitutes Avoda Zara, once the same God has been recognized through both religions. Furthermore, if we are able to affirm that the same God is worshipped in two religions, this creates a space for a better hearing of that religion, including those parts of it that are particularly strange, even offensive. If we do recognize that the same God is worshipped in Judaism and Hinduism, this recognition 

*[p. 8]* ^pg19

makes room for thoughtful interrogation of practices that would have otherwise simply been dismissed as Avoda Zara. These include the approach toward Hindu deities and the worship of idols. Once we recognize deep commonalities, there is more room to listen to how what seems strange at first, and even tenth, sight, can itself provide testimony to a form of spiritual life that must be recognized as valid, as it ultimately turns to the same God. 

 Part V concludes the discussion by returning to the question of Avoda Zara. With lessons learnt from the rich history of approaches to other religions, Christianity in particular, and with a new openness provided by reframing the question in terms of the “same God,” we can now return to the category of Avoda Zara. The question changes once again. Now we are in a position to ask not *what* is Avoda Zara, but rather *when* is a religion Avoda Zara. This question grows out of the application of the criteria discussed in the earlier parts and from the growing recognition that Avoda Zara is not only a view of other religions; it is also an internal category that describes aspects or qualities of the religious life. Our religions are complex entities. They all have higher and lower aspects. And they are all informed by multiple factors, the multiple factors that in the hands of various authors provide each in turn a definition of what is Avoda Zara. In Part V we move toward a broader synthetic understanding of Avoda Zara, taking into account the multiple factors that can define Avoda Zara. These, it is argued, operate in tandem, rather than in isolation. There is a calculus where the different factors converge, leading to the pronouncement that another religion is or is not Avoda Zara. The discussion seeks to make this calculus explicit and to make it address Hinduism. But here Hinduism is no longer viewed in a global way. Rather, corresponding to the greater nuance brought to Avoda Zara by the application of a calculus of multiple factors (turning to another god, image worship, morality, spirituality, overall quality of spiritual life, and more) is the application of this calculus to different expressions of Hinduism and for different purposes. The calculus will be applied differently if we seek to legitimate doing business with Hindus or if we seek to share spiritual wisdom with them. And it will be applied differently to different configurations of Hinduism, in light of the different principles represented in the calculus. Thus, what begins as a straightforward “yes or no” question concludes with a more nuanced approach, best summed up under the question of *when* , rather than the question of *if* . Answering this question is often a question of spiritual discernment, rather than of halachic ruling. Significantly, this very same question and this very process can now be applied internally to Judaism as well as to another religion. 

 Our project is therefore one of mapping complexity and getting a handle on multiple criteria by means of which an inherently complex reality is addressed. It is imperfect because of the complexity of the subject we are studying, the difficult application of internal Jewish principles, and the novelty of the effort. It is an exercise in thought, a study in method of how to approach a complicated subject. For this reason, the process is in many ways more important than the outcome. The outcome is above all the realization that the process must continue. We require more study, more thought of how we go about discerning, and more careful weighing of the options available for the guidance of the varying 

*[p. 9]* ^pg20

circumstances and situations in which Jews are faced with the challenge of Hinduism as Avoda Zara. If this study has provided building blocks, a method and precedent for how to go about thinking of Hinduism in particular and Avoda Zara in general, such as would allow future students to improve upon the foundations it lays, my efforts are well worth it. 

 Having spelled out what this project is, I would like to say a few words about what it is not. It is neither an introduction to Hinduism, to Judaism or, to the history of their encounter. The encounter is the subject of another book, published simultaneously with the present one, titled *The Jewish Encounter with Hinduism* . I believe the present book has benefited from the suggestion of the publisher to make it a stand-alone volume, and not, as it was originally, part of the presentation of the Jewish-Hindu encounter. This has allowed the questions of method and the manner in which we go about studying Avoda Zara to come to the fore, using Hinduism as the test case, but offering a thesis that is of broader significance than a Jewish view of Hinduism. Because this book was initially part of a larger project, the writing assumes some of the subject matter that is now in the complement volume. While the argument stands on its own, it is better appreciated when both books are read in tandem. 

 This book does not present either Judaism or Hinduism. It takes for granted a certain knowledge of both traditions. I do realize, however, that most readers will come to this book with knowledge of one or the other of the traditions; few will have a command of or even decent exposure to both. I see no way of addressing this situation other than recommending more reading. My own assumptions and understanding of both religions are made apparent in the course of the discussion, but they are not the subject of systematic exposition. While I hope this book will be read by both Hindus and Jews, as well as others, I recognize that most of the potential future readership is Jewish. In view of the fact that the discussion makes repeated assumptions concerning a Hindu view that is different from what appears to eye-witnesses, such as observed Hindu hair offering, I would like to make some brief points toward a sketch of what that other Hinduism (true Hinduism?), or an alternative view of Hinduism, consists of. <sup>5</sup> There are several keywords that I would like to employ in order to draw this sketch. They are as follows : 

 *God* —Hinduism recognizes ultimately one God. I shall deal momentarily with the relationship between this recognition and the apparent counterevidence. For devout and dedicated Hindu practitioners, God is a total reality, to which all of life is dedicated. Within Hinduism we find model lives of total dedication, offering, renouncement, and a living that is completely God-focused. 

 *Spirituality* —The approach toward God conditions an attitude, a spirituality. Hindu spirituality highlights God’s readily available presence and makes the quest for the Divine a conscious and ever-present aspect of the spiritual life. In this, it has become a model for seekers from other faiths, Jews included. *Wisdom* —There is a strong component of wisdom in the tradition. There are two foci to this wisdom, corresponding to the two points above: a metaphysical 

*[p. 10]* ^pg21

understanding of the Divine and a detailed understanding of human consciousness and spiritual processes. 

 *Morality* —Hinduism teaches an ordered moral life. This will be the subject of discussion in chapter 10 of the present work. 

 Taken together these elements form religious types that are often models for emulation and inspiration. If one adds *devotion* as a further characteristic, Hindu life, as it is expressed in the lives of great masters and ordinary practitioners, can be, and has been, inspiring to members of other faiths. 

 These are all commendable aspects of a religion. In one way the argument of the present book is that if these aspects are taken as characteristic of the religion, rather than the worship of multiple gods and their images, a different view of Hinduism will emerge. How then does one reconcile such an ideal view with the seemingly incongruous patterns of worship that Hindus follow? 

 In the briefest way, Hindus affirm not only that there is one God, but that the one God manifests in everything. Therefore, everything is an expression of the Divine and is potentially worthy of worship. This includes forces of nature, holy individuals (even unholy individuals), and, in short, all of existence as it is seen (in the physical world) and imagined (in the celestial realm). There are various ways of stating this. All these are seen as manifestations of God or, as some would say, God manifests or comes *as* all these things. In other words, it is not a hierarchical view of nature, of creatures being distinct from God, occupying a lower position on a metaphysical scale, but a view of God manifesting, and therefore as approachable, through these various beings. When God is approached through these manifestations, it is God, ultimately God the absolute, that is approached, rather than these other beings. Put in more philosophical terms, God’s being pervades all, making all of life divine. 

 I do not intend to debate these views or to consider what they might mean to a Jewish dialogue with Hinduism. <sup>6</sup> I have provided this brief sketch mainly so that Jewish readers who are completely unfamiliar with Hinduism would have some starting point from which to enter the conversation. I hope the discussion itself will provide more nuance and evidence for this brief sketch. As a concluding thought exercise, if the rabbinic emissary who was sent to India to determine whether wigs may be worn or must be burned had had significant personal exposure to the factors of which the above sketch is made up, would his report have looked differently? To some extent, the present book tries to work out this hypothetical question.

### 2 Author Presentation

 Writing this book requires multiple skills, some of which I possess only in part. I think it is appropriate to introduce not only the book and the sub-

ject matter, but also the author. For all its objectivity, this is a book that requires great personal investment. As the reader will see, the book draws on my encounters and experiences, but even more fundamentally on my capacity to discern and to make sense of spiritual phenomena. It is therefore appropriate to provide some information regarding myself, as relevant for this particular project. 

 Viewing another religion as Avoda Zara and calling into question how that category is constructed requires familiarity with halachic literature. Years of formation in rabbinical academies, leading to eventual rabbinic ordination, allow me to work through this literature from within, understanding its logic, its nuances, as well as its limitations. Engaging the broader implications of this subject requires working with the world of ideas and thinking in light of the formative conceptual categories of Judaism, as well as internalizing its ethos. As a scholar of rabbinic thought and Jewish thought I believe I come adequately equipped to the task. In recent years I have increasingly found myself “thinking on behalf of Judaism,” so to speak, in various interreligous think tank and academic and theological collaborative projects, many of which I myself organized under the auspices of the Elijah Interfaith Institute that I founded. I think it fair to say that working across religious lines and speaking for a tradition in this way has allowed me to synthesize the different bodies of knowledge, methodologies, and the various approaches that previously existed in a less integrated way, when I was working purely within the Jewish academic or religious setting. As my friend Peter Ochs once commented, it has allowed me to find my voice. 

 Jewish scholarship(s) is not all that this project requires. It requires knowledge and understanding of Hinduism. It also requires some “storehouse” of experiences, encounters, and opportunities; in short, it requires circumstances that spur on the kind of thinking that this book gives expression to, not to mention the inclination and the capacity to think constructively about and between religious traditions. I don’t believe this project can be undertaken in the comfort of one’s study or library, simply based upon readings, no matter how good or authoritative they are. The project of evaluating Hinduism in light of our most significant category requires experience, exposure, and engagement, which in turn lead to 

*[p. 12]* ^pg23

critical and constructive thinking. It is likely that someone who embarks on this project without any engagement will reach different conclusions than the ones I do, and I do not believe his or her vantage point, based on *lack* of meaningful exposure, provides any significant objectivity. It is simply a lack of firsthand knowledge and experience. So, in addition to some degree of authoritative knowledge of Judaism, this project also requires an understanding of Hinduism, both in its theoretical aspects and through some lived engagement with it, representative and extensive enough to allow this project to come to fruition. 

 I would like to say a little more about the impact of experience and how it feeds into such a project. I think I am only able to undertake the present exercise because I feel I have attained a degree of comfort and of feeling at home in the Indian religious reality. This is more a matter of psychology than of philosophy, but I consider it significant for purposes of the present undertaking. In many ways, I no longer feel that sense of strangeness toward Hindu religious reality that the outsider does. Clearly, I remain an outsider, continually learning and in many ways repeatedly surprised by the wealth of Hindu religious life. But my learning now takes place in the context of a basic comfort with Hindu religious reality. It is different, but it is no longer strange. I consider the breaking down of psychological barriers an essential aspect of deep interreligious engagement. It is as true of Hindu-Jewish relations as it is of any relationship between different religions and their practitioners. As long as we are governed by our sense of strangeness and otherness, especially as this sense of strangeness feeds the sense of distance and even revulsion that has characterized the history of our tradition in relation to another, we have not yet reached the level playing ground from which our engagement with another religion should take place. If we seek to genuinely understand and work our way through an issue as fundamental as idolatry, we cannot bring with us a psychological attitude that is governed by the sense of strangeness of the other. One must come to the discussion from a place of psychological or mental comfort and of acceptance of the other’s religious reality as something “normal,” at least not shocking, bizarre, or off-putting. How can we seriously study another religion if our sense of its strangeness and our feeling of being put off by it dictate what we see and how we interpret it? 

 How do I know I have crossed a threshold of comfort that allows me to undertake the present project? Because I no longer feel surprised, outraged, or shocked. I am able to move into a certain kind of mentality that characterizes those Hindus with whom I spend time (it is likely there are many other Hindus with other states of mind who would no doubt continue to surprise me or to make me feel like the extreme stranger, unable to build bridges of understanding). I can begin to see things their way, rather than just being told this is how they see it, without it necessarily becoming my viewpoint. To a large degree I can anticipate and predict, from within so to speak, how my Hindu friends would respond and react, how they would see religious reality, and how this could make sense, given their premises, and particular vantage point. This, I submit, is an appropriate point from which to conduct the kind of reflection that this book undertakes. 

 Having shared something of where I am, let me backtrack and share something of the story of how I got here, and in a more basic way about my credentials for 

*[p. 13]* ^pg24

writing about Hinduism. I must state up-front that my knowledge of Hinduism is far inferior to my knowledge of Judaism. I am at best a learned amateur, with very keen senses, sharp intuition, a capacity to pose questions, and an ability to see the larger whole, where some others only see details. But none of these can substitute for the erudition I wish I had. Whether my lack of knowledge of Sanskrit and other Indian languages, or whether various other kinds of limits of my knowledge have led to false conclusions, only someone with a fuller knowledge of the religious literature of both religions can tell. I am comforted, however, by the fact that many Hindu friends, scholars, and religious leaders have read the manuscript and expressed approval of how Hinduism is presented in it. Having so many Hindu friends who are scholars and leaders is in itself a part of the journey that led me to write this manuscript, and I would like to share some pointers from this journey. This journey makes up in small part for some of the knowledge I wish I had. 

 I hope I am not wrong in stating that I do bring to this project a unique personal story of continuing interest, exploration, study, engagement, and dialogue with Hinduism. I am not familiar with another author who would possess the Jewish halachic and philosophical tools to engage in this study, who has enjoyed the kind of extensive engagement with Hinduism that I have. <sup>1</sup> If I am an authority on anything, it is on my own process, my own story. And this story consists of thousands of small questions, asked over decades, that amount to larger questions, through which I seek to get a grasp over religiously complex realities and their possible relations. If I have what to contribute to others, be they spiritual seekers, halachically minded authorities, or theologians seeking to reflect upon another religion, it lies in formulating the questions, conceptualizing the issues, and tentatively offering my own responses, based on my personal experiences and understanding. 

 I have had an interest in Hinduism in some form or another since my teenage years. Interest in things Indian prompted me to practice Transcendental Meditation in my late teens and to delve into the philosophical tenets of the system, as expounded by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi. This form of practice was complemented by academic studies. Beginning in the mid-70s, and over several years, I took quite a number of courses in Hinduism with David Shulman at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. My own personal spiritual quest continued with many years of training with a teacher, who was herself ordained by a great Indian sage, Swami Sivananda. Understanding Hinduism remained a staple of my spiritual and intellectual diet. 

 Interest in and engagement with Hinduism entered a new phase with the foundation of the Elijah Interfaith Institute, in 1996 in Jerusalem, as the Elijah School for the Study of Wisdom in World Religions. The original name of the school provides the rationale for including Hinduism in interreligious dialogue in Jerusalem, despite the fact that there is no significant Hindu community in Israel. The vision of the school sought to make the wisdom of all religious traditions the subject of academic study as well as of interfaith dialogue. Our annual summer school gathered a community of scholar-practitioners from different faith traditions. Scholars of Hinduism were regularly invited to Jerusalem as part 

*[p. 14]* ^pg25

of the Elijah summer school program. This provided me with the opportunity to learn from, and to dialogue with, some of the leading Hindu figures in the field of religious studies in the United States. The annual meetings of the American Academy of Religion and of the Society for Hindu-Christian Studies also provided more than a decade’s worth of contact and engagement with a broad range of scholars working on theological issues from within Hinduism, or in dialogue with it. 

 As Elijah grew, so did my relationship with Hinduism deepen. With the formation of the Elijah Interfaith Academy in 2002, working on collaborative thought projects with Hindu scholars became an ongoing reality. And beginning in 2003, with the founding of the Elijah Board of World Religious Leaders, I gained access to some important Hindu leaders, some of whom have become friends and whose thought-prints are to be found in this book. The mix of leaders represented at Elijah has allowed me to cultivate relationships with leaders and scholars representing different streams of Hinduism. While *Advaita Vedanta* probably has the most representative voice in this work, as well as in Hinduism’s public face in the West (and possibly in India as well), I have appreciated the opportunity to have continuing exchanges with disciples of Madhavacharya (Swami Sugunendra Theerta of the Udipi Math) and Chaitanya (Shrivatsa Goswami), as well as scholars who identify themselves as disciples of Ramanuja (Vasudha Narayanan). While I am sure I have not yet adequately integrated the fine points that distinguish one school from the other, I do think that working across so many schools in collaborative settings, where scholars and leaders are called to speak for “Hinduism”, even as they speak for their own subtradition, has allowed me to internalize a perspective in light of which I too dare address “Hinduism,” even as I recognize the existence of nuances and variations and even as I am aware of the possibility that some generalities I make might in fact be the projection of one particular school. 

 It was Sri Sri Ravi Shankar who invited me to India in 2003, paving the way to a series of extended visits to India, on an annual basis, since then. These visits focused on religious communities and centers of learning and worship. They provided me with the opportunity to think through the issues of this book through ongoing dialogue with some outstanding personalities, as well as through a process of continuing observation, listening, and reflection. I have spent months in some of the leading ashrams. I should mention in particular the Sivananda Ashram in Rishikesh and the Sadhana Kendra Ashram near Dehradun. Both ashrams have become home, in different ways. In both I gained immensely from the wisdom and spiritual perspective of the resident sages. Swami Yogaswaroopananda, of the former ashram, has spent dozens, well over a hundred I imagine, of hours with me, discussing issues that are seminal to a Jewish understanding of Hinduism, particularly issues related to the worship of idols and how these are viewed in Hinduism. Personal friendships and relationships have played an important role in allowing me to enter the mind space, understanding, and feeling of Hindus, how they live their spiritual life, and how they approach God. Of the many important friendships with which I was blessed over the years of my travels to India, the one that was formed with Swami Atmapriyananda is particularly significant 

*[p. 15]* ^pg26

in the context of the present work. This senior monk of the Ramakrishna Order, who has become an active partner in the work of the Elijah Institute, opened for me the gates of understanding through a unique combination of mind, heart, and spirit that provides continuing inspiration to me as a person, as well as to the present project. Through him, I have come to appreciate the Ramakrishna Order, its vision, and above all the spiritual testimony of its founder. Many other sages, ashrams, and temples, in different parts of India, north and south, form part of the story, and it is a story that is far from having reached its final chapter. 

 Because this book grows so much out of my own personal intellectual and spiritual journey, I have decided to not mask these components, but rather to expose them. I know what I think I know because of experiences, encounters, dialogues I have had, and a process that has been personal, even while based upon academic foundations. There is no point converting the discussion to a completely academic, philosophical, or halachic mode of discourse. It seems to me much truer to the process and to its potential import for future readers to feature the first-person voice as well as the impressions of dialogues and exchanges. The reader will not only hear my voice, but also those of some of above-mentioned swamis, all of whom have shaped my thinking. Accordingly, I have tried to incorporate personal experience and reflection into the more neutral and objective discourse one might expect of such a work.

### 3 Model Case Presentation: The Sheitel Crisis

 Avoda Zara has never been a purely theoretical issue; nor is the view of Hinduism as Avoda Zara a purely hypothetical matter. In fact, one of the 

most public and well-publicized instances of Jewish response to Hinduism revolves around Hinduism’s status as Avoda Zara. And the case we discuss in this chapter is only a decade old, a product of the early twenty-first century. The impact of the case was global, making headlines in international news, appearing in the *New York Times* , not to mention in a multitude of Jewish media. This became a defining moment that illustrates just how much present-day engagement with Hinduism is a contemporary reality that cannot be sidestepped. It also illustrates the problematics associated with learning about another religion and how to, or how not to, go about doing it. It raises the question of who is responsible for the interpretation of a religion in a powerful way. In short, it touches upon some of the key issues that a discussion of Avoda Zara involves and, as such, serves as a model case by means of which to introduce us to the topic, its urgency and vitality. 

 So here is the story. Orthodox Jewish women cover their hair after marriage. Some do so by wearing a kerchief or a hat. Others by wearing a wig, known as a *sheitel* in Yiddish. Sheitels can be made of synthetic or human hair. Clearly the latter is aesthetically preferable, but the wig-manufacturing industry requires a steady source of human hair to keep up with the demand. While there are different sources for natural human hair, apparently the most convenient and largest source is Hindu temples. In South Indian temples there is often a custom among devotees, both men and women, to shave their heads as an offering to the deity. This is undertaken as a vow, either in order to fulfill a particular request or as an act of pure devotion. South Indian temples are thus a major source of human hair for the international wig industry. Particularly prominent is the temple of Venkateshwara, Balaji, in Tirupati (actually Tirumala). Thousands of men and women offer their hair at this temple, and temple trustees gather it, sort it according to grade, treat it, package it, and then sell it on the international market. This is one of the sources of Tirupati’s great wealth, considered by its devotees to be second only to that of the Vatican, and taken by them as an indication of the great power of the local deity. <sup>1</sup> 

*[p. 18]* ^pg28

 Jewish tradition dictates that one is not allowed to derive any benefit or pleasure from Avoda Zara. This comes into potential conflict with the use of hair offered to the deity in Hindu temples. That the wigs of ultra-Orthodox women had their provenance in Hindu temples had been known for a long time. It was addressed in a responsum by Rabbi Yosef Shalom Elyashiv, the protagonist of the present story, over 30 years ago. At the time, Rabbi Elyashiv ruled that shaving of the head does not constitute an offering proper, and therefore use of that hair is not forbidden. <sup>2</sup> It is not clear what led to the change of ruling. Ultra-Orthodox media claimed that a change had taken place in Hindu practices, necessitating a change in halachic ruling, but this is just one more indication of how poor the effort was to understand Hindus on their own terms. Jews would construct imaginary rituals and transformations in those rituals only to make sense of the internal problem of a major rabbinic figure changing his ruling. 

 In any event, the question came up again in 2004. Was the offering of hair by Hindu devotees an offering of Avoda Zara that should be forbidden to Jewish women? The natural thing would be to study the matter firsthand. And so, a prominent London rabbi was sent as an emissary of Rabbi Elyashiv, by now the leading figure of ultra-Orthodox Jewry and its halachic figurehead, to Tirupati to study the matter personally. Of course, the emissary, Rabbi Donner, did not go to the SOAS (School of Oriental and African Studies) of London University for a course on Hinduism. <sup>3</sup> He made his way to Tirupati, and was able to return home after 48 hours, mission accomplished. He was able to provide the needed information, based upon which Rabbi Elyashiv ruled that wigs that originated in Hindu temples could not be worn by Jewish Orthodox women. What Rabbi Donner did, according to reports that followed the visit, was to engage locals in an enquiry as to the nature of the act involved in devotees shaving their head. When they responded that they were offering their hair to the deity, he was able to reach his conclusion that this was an offering to an idol, and hence should be forbdden for use. <sup>4</sup> 

 The upshot of the story was that Rabbi Elyashiv issued a ruling that wigs should be burned. Idolatrous offerings may not be enjoyed in any shape and the only way to dispose of them is through burning. This reflects the Torah’s strong concern with idolatry and how it seeks to avoid it at any cost. This cardinal value found one of its most recent expressions in relation to the devotional practices of young Hindu women, with whom the prospective Jewish wig bearers would never have any contact. The burning of the wigs made headline news, as Jews in Israel and New York were pictured burning wigs on street corners. <sup>5</sup> It also deeply offended the Hindu community in ways that the rabbis never imagined, and perhaps to date have not realized. It further reflected badly on hassidic and ultra-Orthodox Jews in general. I recall how appalled the local hassidic rabbi in Anchorage, Alaska, was when I happened to visit him during that period. His wife and he, emissaries of the Lubavitcher Rebbe and sole representatives of Orthodox Judaism for a great state, found themselves having to apologize and explain something they did not believe in. It was, to them, a *hillul hashem* , a desecration of the Holy Name, a term used to designate problematic and negative portrayal of Jews in the public eye. 

 As an aside, it is worth considering how Jews of this very same branch of the hassidic movement dealt with the issue of wigs. It is perhaps worth noting that 

*[p. 19]* ^pg29

the response to the wig issue divided along the lines of whether the proponents of the individual rabbinic position were generally in favor of Jewish women wearing wigs or not. Observers have noted that the battle was driven by factions in the ultra-Orthodox world that oppose all forms of wig wearing as an inappropriate solution to the need to cover women’s hair. Lubavitch Hassidim have long upheld wigs not only as an acceptable but as the preferred way to cover their women’s hair. One Lubavitch rabbi, Rabbi Shalom Dov Wolpe, composed a book devoted to legitimating the practice of wig wearing, including the continued use of wigs that had their source in Hindu temples. <sup>6</sup> While most of his book is devoted to a halachic consideration I shall shortly share, his introductory remarks are particularly interesting from a broader religious perspective. Rabbi Wolpe noted that it seems a priori inconceivable that Indian wigs should constitute a problem. His reasoning is as follows. The late Lubavitcher Rebbe was known to be a man gifted with great supernatural, perhaps prophetic like, knowledge. His ministry was unique on the scene of Jewish leadership and included traditional learning, community organization, and supernatural knowledge of facts, individuals, etc., in ways unparalleled in the present generation and known only from the descriptions of former generations. The Lubavitcher Rebbe would stand for hours every Sunday, receiving thousands of devotees—men and women— and handing each a dollar, to give in his name to charity (rather: to keep as a blessing, and to give another dollar in its stead as charity). Now, had there been anything spiritually wrong with these wigs, it is inconceivable, argues Rabbi Wolpe, that the Lubavitcher Rebbe would have not “smelled” it. If it did not affect him, that means there is no problem. Having established this on spiritual grounds, he then proceeds to argue the matter halachically, that is, on the same battleground used by those who forbade the wearing of these wigs. 

 How were these wigs deemed permissible for use? Rabbi Wolpe, along with a long list of respectable contemporary rabbinic authorities, <sup>7</sup> argued that the act of cutting the hair in Hindu temples did not constitute an “offering” in the technical sense. That is, it is not sufficient to associate the word “offering” as used by individuals with the formal act of “offering,” as defined by halacha. For various reasons, the act of hair offering was deemed too removed from the deity, the center of offering, to be considered a forbidden offering. Hence the wigs were deemed permissible. There was a lot of common sense in this position, as it was supported by some key facts on the ground, such as the distance between where the hair cutting took place and the deity’s abode and the fact that the cut hair is considered impure and hence cannot be brought into the presence of the deity, making it an unlikely offering. So, one could argue that ultimately common sense and healthy intuition, supported by a certain amount of knowledge, were able to balance the radicalization of ruling, as practiced in Rabbi Elyashiv’s court. However, this would be to ignore the deeper faults in procedure, common to both sides, and representative of how Jews have gone about viewing Hinduism and Avoda Zara more generally. 

 Let us begin by noting the unexamined assumption of the entire process. No one ever stopped to ask the question of whether this worship was indeed Avoda Zara, or considered what the status of Hinduism in this respect is, and whether the worship in the major temple under examination, Tirupati, conforms or does 

*[p. 20]* ^pg30

not with a broader view of Judaism, if such a view does indeed exist, on Hinduism as a religion. <sup>8</sup> There did not seem to be a need, even for those who sought to permit the wigs, to enter into that most fundamental question. At the time, I read dozens of responsa on this issue. I was struck by the fact that not a single halachic decisor felt the need to raise the question of whether Hinduism, or the worship in Hindu temples, should be considered Avoda Zara. <sup>9</sup> There seems to me only one possible explanation for this. The power of images and their worship is so great, and their impact upon Jewish memory and imagination so complete, that it leaves no room for querying this fundamental assumption of the discussion. It is possible, of course, that halachic rulings were influenced by some rabbinic precedents of great decisors of previous generations, who, in similar vein, ruled or assumed that Hinduism is Avoda Zara. <sup>10</sup> But that would only be pushing the matter backward in time. The dynamics that would have led two or three hundred years ago to such a position would have been the same as those described above. The power of the immediate vision is so great as to determine unequivocally that the specific form of Hindu worship under discussion ought to be considered as Avoda Zara. But applying the same procedure today is actually far more problematic. Centuries ago there was no contact with India, and decisors had to rely on the current state of knowledge. In today’s world knowledge is more readily available and the stakes for intercommunal living are much greater. In fact, the financial stakes of the whole story are of staggering proportions. It has been estimated that the wig industry is worth a billion dollars a year. Thus, despite the huge responsibility of making a billion dollar decision, nothing more was needed than a 48-hour eyewitness report and the application of a series of principles, whose end result was that wigs are forbidden and should be burned. 

 The story of Tirupati and the sheitels serves as an introduction to the present work because so many questions converge around this one incident. First and foremost, it raises the question of what is Avoda Zara. What do we mean by Avoda Zara and who does it refer to? Is it still something to contend with? Specifically, should Hinduism be considered Avoda Zara? The present work assumes that answers to these questions involve more than a simple “yes” or “no.” The question is not simply whether Hinduism is or is not Avoda Zara, but more significantly, what do we mean by Avoda Zara and how do we define it. Hinduism therefore serves in the present discussion in two capacities. It is the object of a specific inquiry, in terms of Avoda Zara, but it is also the catalyst for a more theoretical discussion that seeks, a propos of and in relation to Hinduism, to revisit the conceptual foundations of Avoda Zara. 

 A rabbinic report on the sheitel crisis sums up the interest in the story. It is the occasion to engage in theoretical halachic speculations that one rarely has the possibility of engaging. <sup>11</sup> Avoda Zara is, for those engaged in taking a stance on the sheitel issue, not a matter of relations with the other, but some esoteric and out-of-the-way domain for halachic speculation. I take just the opposite view. Avoda Zara is a fundamental instrument in defining relationships, and it is precisely for this reason that the import of the sheitel crisis does not lie in the pros and cons of one position or another but in providing an opportunity to think through seriously and systematically what Avoda Zara means, as a concept, as a 

*[p. 21]* ^pg31

worldview, and as a pronouncement and judgement placed upon Hinduism, as well as other religions. <sup>12</sup> 

 The sheitel story opens a window to many questions. The reason Rabbi Donner could be so sure of Hinduism’s status as Avoda Zara is because he not only spoke to some devotees, but also saw images with his own eyes, or spent time in an environment that takes image worship for granted. This leads us to one of the key issues that we shall tackle—the power of the image, the icon, or the idol as determining what is Avoda Zara. One must be aware of the deep Jewish biases against Avoda Zara and of how easily these resort to the power of the visual as a first step in applying a more critical methodology to the Jewish study of Hinduism. Can we make the immediate and facile leap from image worship to the declaration of another religion as Avoda Zara or should the leap from foreign worship to foreign god be examined? <sup>13</sup> Of course, if we do not recognize the image of the god and the stories told of him or her, or if we encounter the worship of elements of nature that have been prohibited for millennia, it would seem there is little to discuss. Still, the religion of India confronts us precisely with the challenge of separating ritual from philosophy and theology. This could allow us to reframe the question. Rather than asking how do we determine the otherness of the god that is worshipped (answer: by means of ritual), we would be asking how we could determine, by means other than ritual, that the God worshipped by two religions is the same God. <sup>14</sup> The contribution of the present work lies in shifting perspective from a ritual-only means of discernment to a broader view of the religion <sup>15</sup> and from framing the question in terms of confirming otherness to seeking to establish means of affirming God as one and the same in two religions. <sup>16</sup> 

 The alternative means of establishing the identity of God, recognizing him as the same God, are philosophy and religious understanding. <sup>17</sup> It is these that provide meaning to the rituals, to the modes of worship, and to the entire religious life. To relate to ritual only without the superstructure of meaning could be akin to how the proverbial Martian or, for that matter, the Hindu off the street might understand Judaism when he first encounters it. Would the gestures of individuals make sense in and of themselves, or do they require an interpretive framework and a class of interpreters to make sense of them? What does it mean to carry the Torah scroll around, to kiss it, and to show it respect? <sup>18</sup> In whose mind is this distinguished as fundamentally different from similar gestures practiced toward objects of adoration in other religions? I once met someone who left a synagogue, quite upset, at the sight of the lions that were sewn onto the synagogue *parochet* , a cloth cover of the ark, where the Torah scrolls are kept. Now, I am not suggesting that Jewish use of images is identical to Hindu use. But I do consider it important to ask whether there is really a neutral descriptive view of religious and ritual realities and whether any description does not beg an interpretive framework. Once we are in the realm of interpretation, we are forced to recognize the authority of the interpreter and opening ourselves up to authoritative voices of another religion could block the facile move from foreign worship to foreign god. <sup>19</sup> 

 The exclusive appeal to worship, while ignoring philosophical understandings of what a religion means, is the root problem in the sheitel story. It points to a weakness in the classical Jewish approach. It also reminds us of how difficult it 

*[p. 22]* ^pg32

is to come to a balanced understanding of Hindu religious life, because it raises the questions of what is considered knowledge of the other and who represents it. This is both an internal and an external problem. Internally, on the Jewish end, can such knowledge be gained in 48 hours? By several years of travel? Who is qualified to report to rabbis what the religion means? But even more serious than this is the external problem, which can be briefly summed up by the question: “Who speaks for Hinduism?” <sup>20</sup> Let us consider the options in the case of Tirupati. If we ask the people standing in line to have the *darshan* of Balaji, they may not be able to articulate a coherent theology in light of which Balaji is to be appreciated, which could provide enough of a counterpoint to the worship of images to legitimately open the question of Avoda Zara for discussion. Some may; many won’t. The question I continue to struggle with is what would the majority think. I have, as a matter of fact, repeatedly engaged local worshippers at Hindu temples on the question of whether they ultimately worship one God or a multiplicity of gods. <sup>21</sup> I have also posed this question repeatedly to swamis and spiritual teachers. There is strong evidence that the public at large is indeed aware of one God and of a higher teaching. Many would claim that broader popular consciousness is not so far from the recognition of one God or divine life beyond, behind, or through the multiplicity of deities and images. But on second thoughts, does the majority really matter? Maybe what a religion means is not determined by majority understanding. Perhaps in this case we should ask the priests, who receive gifts for the deity. Are they the voice of the temple? Or perhaps the heads of various spiritual schools and dynasties, who honor the site and frequent it on regular pilgrimage. <sup>22</sup> If so, the meaning of it all might be found outside the temple, among a narrow section of its users. 

 There is still one further possibility that is relevant. The temple at Tirupati was dedicated, actually rededicated, by one of India’s greatest philosophers, the eleventh-century philosopher Ramanuja. The present-day rituals were instated by him. In a situation in which the meaning of an action is unclear, it would make sense to turn to the founder of the institution and learn of his intentions. Now, Ramanuja was a proponent of a school of philosophy called modified nondualism, *Vishishtadvaita.* <sup>23</sup> What is at stake in the differences between the different philosophical schools is just how extreme the monistic vision of reality is and how real one considers the relationship of deity and devotee, as well as of this world, to be. This school is not identical with Advaita Vedanta, the brand of Vedanta that has increasingly come to represent Hinduism, associated with the eighth-century teacher Sankara. According to Advaita Vedanta all is ultimately one, and the unity of the supreme divinity, Brahman, is affirmed beyond as well as through various deities, nature, and all of being. But even if we do not hold to ideas of extreme monism, Ramanuja’s brand of philosophy also recognizes the ultimate unity of the Godhead or supreme being. In other words, it is a sophisticated philosophical school that represents anything but a simplistic view of gods, images, and worship. This school still enjoys great prominence and remains one of the major philosophical and religious schools in India. Thus, in turning to Ramanuja we are not simply turning to a founding figure of old. Rather, we are turning to a figure whose teachings continue to have broad, if not universal, impact and 

*[p. 23]* ^pg33

whose legacy is mediated through this specific temple as well. If so, it would seem appropriate to consider how Ramanuja himself would have viewed the worship of idols and to what extent his own practice, seen in the light of his own religious teaching, could provide a frame of reference for assessing the religious meaning of what goes on in Tirupati today. 

 But then, assuming we have provided a response for one institution or one school, can that answer hold true for all of Hinduism? Hinduism is a federation of faiths and practices, exhibiting enormous diversity. Beyond the diversity of schools there is also diversity of Hinduism as practiced in different localities. In my travels I have come across different forms of Hinduism in different localities. <sup>24</sup> Nepalese Hinduism is one context in which I had great trouble identifying traces of the recognitions of the higher spiritual teachings with which I was so familiar from Indian ashrams. <sup>25</sup> If the case is, as Swami Yogaswaroopananda maintained when we discussed this, that in Nepal too there are pundits who know the teachings of the Upanishads and Vedanta, then this provides us with a contextualized instance of the broader question under discussion: Who holds the key to interpreting the religion? If only a handful of people are aware of the “true” meaning of their religion, do we look to those handful or to the masses? And should we look to the authorities wherever they may be, regardless of local reality? And perhaps the meaning of the religion should be found in the writings of great figures of the past, regardless of the current state of understanding. 

 At the end of the day, the diversity of perspectives might force us to move away from a global response to the question of Hinduism and Avoda Zara. Perhaps we need to relate to schools, movements, and spiritual centers, rather than to “Hinduism” as a whole. <sup>26</sup> In the extreme, we would be forced to answer on a person-by-person basis. In that case, one person’s idolatry would be another’s true religion—identical actions, radically different understandings. This seems rather paradoxical and highlights the difficult choices we are forced to make as we undertake an understanding of Hindu faith and worship from a Jewish perspective. 

 Facing a complex reality can lead to one of two reactions. The first is to attempt to simplify it and to make a pronouncement on that simplified reality. Those who simply dismiss Hinduism as idolatry do just that. At the other extreme, those who celebrate statements, declarations, or other materials that allow us to once and for all table core questions are similarly engaged in simplification of complex realities. <sup>27</sup> If we do not wish to err on the side of simplification, there seems to be only one appropriate response: recognition of the complexity of the phenomenon at hand. Such recognition need not be paralyzing. Even if some practical course or another must be charted in an attempt to address practical concerns, awareness of the complexity of a phenomenon amounts to an invitation to stay open—to further study, future reconsideration, new insights. The response to complexity is thus educational, the need to learn more. If there is any conclusion to be drawn from the present study, it is that more work, more study, and more reflection are required. There seems to me nothing wrong with the possibility that a process of learning, involving traditions that for thousands of years have not had extensive contact between them, might take a generation or more. Rather 

*[p. 24]* ^pg34

than the facile pronouncements that characterized the sheitel incident, I envision a lengthy, laborious, and complex process whose results may be much less unequivocal, but much truer to the complex problem at hand. At the end of the day, my own views on Hinduism as Avoda Zara may be so nuanced as to not be useful to those decisors. But they will surely be useful on other fronts. They will be useful for those who seek relationships and understanding, and not simply permission to wear wigs of human hair. They will be useful for those who want to enter into deep dialogue with Hinduism, and not merely have commercial relations. And they will be useful for those who recognize that what Judaism needs today is a thorough thinking through, maybe even a revision, of its views on other religions, and not simply a mechanical application of well-established principles. For me, getting to the root of things and recognizing their complexity, even at the cost of our ability to make final pronouncements on some matters, seems preferable. It is preferable because it paves the way to a fuller understanding that will emerge tomorrow, when conditions have ripened and understanding has deepened. The alternative leads nowhere. <sup>28</sup>
