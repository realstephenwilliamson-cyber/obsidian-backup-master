> [!note] The Beatitudes and Antitheses *(p. 18)*
> The expression “blessed are” (Gk *makarioi*) appears 68 times in the LXX, usually for the Hebrew *ashrei* (“happy are . . . ”); see e.g., [[Psalms 084#^84-5|Ps 84.4]], “Happy are those who live in your house, ever singing your praise. *Selah.* ” The Beatitudes also draw upon scriptural precedent. For example, “Blessed are the poor in spirit” (5.3) and “Blessed are those who mourn” (5.4) may be dependent upon [[Isaiah 61#^61-1|Isa 61.1–3]], which also speaks of the poor and those who mourn. “Blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth” is a near-quotation of [[Psalms 037#^37-11|Ps 37.11]] (LXX 36.11; see also LXX [[LXX Isaiah 61#^61-7|Isa 61.7]]). The Hebrew of the psalm speaks of inheriting “the land” (*ha*ʾ*aretz*), which should be taken as a reference to the land of Israel. The LXX and hence the New Testament reads *gē*, which could be translated as either “land” or “earth”; the translation of “earth” has been read by some Christians as universalizing a text originally associating the people Israel with the homeland Israel. The concern for those who “hunger” or “thirst” evokes [[Psalms 107#^107-5|Ps 107.5–6]],[[Psalms 107#^107-9|9]] (see also [[Psalms 022#^22-27|Ps 22.26]]); LXX [[LXX Proverbs 14#^14-21|Prov 14.21]] (see also LXX [[LXX Proverbs 17#^17-5|Prov 17.5]]) underlies the concern for the merciful. “Pure in heart” or “clean of heart” recalls [[Psalms 024#^24-3|Ps 24.3–6]] (see also [[Psalms 073#^73-1|Ps 73.1]]), another ref- erence to the land; the expression also echoes [[Psalms 051#^51-12|Ps 51.10]] (Heb 51.12; \[LXX 50.12\]).
>
> Chapter 5 concludes with a series of six teachings known as the “antitheses,” named for the form “You have heard that it was said . . . But I say to you” (5.21–48). Some interpreters incorrectly understand the antitheses to be abrogations of Torah. This is not the case, for Jesus has just stated that he has not come to abolish the Torah (5.17–20). Rather, the antitheses *intensify* the Law, or in Jewish terms, “build a fence around the Torah” (see *m. Avot* 1.1), that is, mandate observing a law well beyond its minimum requirements to ensure that the law itself is observed. The fences in the antitheses concern attitude as well as action: to prevent murder, do not be angry; to prevent adultery, do not lust; to avoid swearing falsely, do not swear at all since all one’s statements should be honest. In two cases, the antitheses veer from legal issues to behavioral guides. First, Jesus changes the issue of maiming presupposed by “an eye for an eye” (5.38) to non-physical injury. Second, there is no commandment to “hate one’s enemy” (5.43); Prov 24 and 25 instead mandate fair treatment. Jesus again intensifies Torah by mandating not only righteous behavior toward but also love for the enemy.
