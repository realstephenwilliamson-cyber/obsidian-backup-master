**1.** The term "the frond of the date palm" employed by the Torah[^1] refers to the branches of a date palm[^2] as they sprout, before their leaves separate[^3] and spread out in various directions.[^4] Rather, they should appear as a scepter.[^5] This is called a lulav.[^6] ^7-1

**2.** The "fruit of the beautiful tree" mentioned in the Torah[^7] is the etrog.[^8]  
The "boughs of covered trees" mentioned in the Torah[^9] refer to the [species of] myrtle whose leaves surround its branch;[^10] i.e., there will be three or more leaves in each ring.[^11] However, if there are two leaves on one level, with a third leaf slightly higher than them, that is not considered to be "covered."[^12] Rather, it is called a wild myrtle.[^13] ^7-2

**3.** The term "willows of the brook" mentioned by the Torah[^14] does not include just any plant that grows by a brook, but rather a particular species, which is called the "willows of the brook."  
Its[^15] leaf is extended as a brook,[^16] its edge is smooth,[^17] and its stem is red. It is called a willow. The majority of this species grow near brooks. Therefore, it is called the "willows of the brook."[^18] Even if this species grew in the desert or on a mountain, it would be kosher.[^19] ^7-3

**4.** There is another species which resembles the willow. However, its leaf is rounded, its edge resembles a saw,[^20] and its stem is not red.[^21] This is called a *tzaftzefah*. It is unfit [to be used for the mitzvah].[^22]  
There is another type of willow, whose leaf[^23] does not have a smooth edge, but it is not like a saw. Rather, it has tiny juttings, like the edge of a small sickle. It is kosher.[^24]  
All the above definitions were explained according to the oral tradition transmitted by Moses, our teacher.[^25] ^7-4

**5.** These four species are considered to be one mitzvah,[^26] and[^27] each one[^28] is required for its performance.[^29] All of them [together] are called the mitzvah of lulav.[^30] One may not diminish them[^31] or add to them.[^32] If one of the species cannot be found, a similar species[^33] may not be substituted for it.[^34] ^7-5

**6.** The most desirable way of performing the mitzvah is to bind the lulav, myrtle, and willow together, thus making a single, unified entity from the three of them.[^35]  
Before one takes them to perform the mitzvah, he should recite the blessing[^36] on the mitzvah of taking the lulav,[^37] for all the others are dependent upon it.[^38]  
Afterwards, he takes this bound entity in his right hand and the etrog in his left hand.[^39] He must take them as they grow[^40] - i.e., their roots below towards the earth, and their heads upward towards the sky.[^41]  
If a person did not bind them together, but rather took them one by one, he has fulfilled his obligation, provided he possesses all four species.[^42] However, if he has only one species or he is lacking one species, he should not take them until he acquires the remaining species.[^43] ^7-6

**7.** How many does one take of each of them? One lulav,[^44] one etrog,[^45] two willow branches,[^46] and three myrtle branches.[^47] If one would like to add more myrtle[^48] branches so that the bundle will be larger, he may. Indeed, it is considered to be an adornment of the mitzvah.[^49] However, it is forbidden to add to or reduce the numbers of the other species.[^50] If one adds to or reduces their number, it is not acceptable.[^51] ^7-7

**8.** What is the required length of each of these species?[^52] The lulav may not be less than four handbreadths.[^53] [Beyond that,] regardless of its length, it is kosher.[^54] Its length is measured only from its *shidrah*[^55] and not from the tips of the leaves.[^56]  
The myrtle and the willow may not be less than three handbreadths. [Beyond that,] regardless of their length, they are kosher.[^57] Even if each branch has only three fresh leaves on it, they are kosher, provided the leaves are at the top of the branch.[^58]  
If one has bound [the other species together with] the lulav, the *shidrah* of the lulav must extend beyond the myrtle and the willow a handbreadth or more.[^59]  
The minimum size of an etrog is the size of an egg.[^60] [Beyond that,] regardless of its size, it is kosher.[^61] ^7-8

**9.** Once a person lifts up these four species[^62] - whether he lifts them up together or one after the other[^63] - whether in his right hand or in his left hand[^64] - he has fulfilled his obligation.[^65] [This applies] only when he lifts them up as they grow.[^66] However, if he does not lift them up as they grow, he has not fulfilled his obligation.[^67]  
The fulfillment of the mitzvah as the law [requires is as follows]: One should lift up the three species as they are bound together in one's right hand and the etrog in one's left hand.[^68] Then, one should pass them back and forth, up and down,[^69] and shake the lulav three times in each direction. ^7-9

**10.** What does the above entail? One passes the lulav forward[^70] and shakes the top of the lulav three times,[^71] brings it back[^72] and shakes the top of the lulav three times.[^73] One follows this same pattern when lifting it up and down.[^74]  
At what point [in prayer][^75] does one pass the lulav back and forth? During the reading of the *Hallel*,[^76] at the first and final recitation of the verse \[[[Psalms 118#^118-1|Psalms 118:1]], 118:29]: *Hodu[^77] Lado-nai ki tov...* and at the verse \[[[Psalms 118#^118-25|Psalms 118:25]]]: *Ana Ado-nai hoshi'ah na*.[^78]  
It is acceptable to take the lulav throughout the entire day.[^79] However, it is not taken at night.[^80] ^7-10

**11.** If one wraps a cord of silver or gold around [the three species] as they are bound together,[^81] or wraps a [decorative] cloth around them and takes them, one fulfills his obligation.[^82] Taking the lulav through another medium is still considered to be taking it,[^83] provided [that medium] is one which gives honor and beauty [to the mitzvah, because]: "any entity which makes a substance more attractive is not considered to be a separation."  
However,[^84] if one placed these species in a vase or a pot[^85] and took them, one has not fulfilled one's obligation.[^86] ^7-11

**12.** If one binds the lulav together with the myrtle and the willow and separates between the lulav and the myrtle with a cloth or the like,[^87] it is considered to be a separation.[^88] If one separates between them with myrtle leaves,[^89] it is not considered to be a separation, because an entity does not separate between its own kind.[^90]  
One may bind the together with a string, a cord, or with any substance one desires,[^91] since binding it together is not a required element of the mitzvah.[^92] ^7-12

**13.** The mitzvah of taking the lulav in every place,[^93] during every age[^94] - even on the Sabbath[^95] - applies only on the first day of the festival, as \[[[Torah 31 - Emor#^23-40|Leviticus 23:40]]] states: "And on the first day, you shall take..."  
In the holy place alone,[^96] it is to be taken on each of the seven days of the festival, as [the above verse] continues: "and you shall rejoice before God, your Lord, [seven days]."  
When the Sabbath falls during the [later] days, [the lulav] is not taken on the Sabbath.[^97] This is a decree[^98] lest one carry it four cubits in the public domain, as decreed regarding the shofar.[^99] ^7-13

**14.** Why was this decree not put in effect on the first day of the festival? Because [taking the lulav on that day] is a mitzvah from the Torah even outside of Jerusalem. Thus, the laws applying to it are not the same as those applying to the remaining days,[^100] because on the subsequent days of the festival a person is obligated to take the lulav only in the holy place.[^101] ^7-14

**15.** When the Temple was destroyed, [the Sages] ordained that the lulav be taken everywhere for the entire seven days of the festival, as a remembrance of the Temple.[^102]  
On each day,[^103] one recites the blessing on it:  
*[Baruch Attah Ado-nai...] asher kid'shanu b'mitzvotav v'tzivanu al netilat lulav.*  
because[^104] it a mitzvah ordained by the Rabbis.[^105]  
This enactment, like the other enactments instituted by Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai when the Temple was destroyed [is only temporary]. When the Temple is rebuilt, these matters will return to their original status.[^106] ^7-15

**16.** While the Temple was standing,[^107] the lulav would be taken [in the holy place[^108] even] when the first day of Sukkot fell on the Sabbath.[^109] The same applies in other places[^110] where they were certain that this day[^111] was celebrated as a holiday in *Eretz Yisrael*.[^112] However, the places which were distantly removed from Jerusalem[^113] would not take the lulav on this day because of the doubt[^114] involved.[^115] ^7-16

**17.** When the Temple was destroyed,[^116] the Sages forbade even the inhabitants of *Eretz Yisrael* who had sanctified the new month[^117] to take the lulav on the Sabbath on the first day of Sukkot.[^118]  
[This was instituted] because of the inhabitants of the distant settlements, who were not aware of when the new month had been declared. Thus, a uniform guideline was established, rather than having some take the lulav on the Sabbath and some not.[^119] [The guiding principle was] that the obligation [of taking the lulav] on the first day applies in all places,[^120] and there is no longer a Temple to use as a point of distinction.[^121] ^7-17

**18.** At present, when everyone follows a fixed calendar,[^122] the matter remains as it was, and the lulav is not taken on the Sabbath in the outlying territories or in *Eretz Yisrael*[^123] even on the first day [of the festival].[^124] [This applies] even though everyone knows the actual day of the month.[^125]  
As explained,[^126] the reason for the prohibition of taking the lulav on the Sabbath is a decree lest one carry it four cubits in the public domain. ^7-18

**19.** Whoever is obligated to fulfill [the mitzvot of] shofar and sukkah is obligated to take the lulav. Whoever is not obligated regarding shofar and sukkah is not obligated to take the lulav.[^127]  
A child who knows how to shake [the lulav][^128] is obligated[^129] regarding the lulav by Rabbinic law, in order to train him in the performance of mitzvot.[^130] ^7-19

**20.** It is a halachah conveyed by Moses from Mount Sinai[^131] that[^132] - in addition to the willow of the lulav - another willow branch was taken in the Temple.[^133] A person does not fulfill his obligation with the willow branch in the lulav.[^134]  
The minimum requirement [to fulfill this mitzvah] is one branch with one leaf. ^7-20

**21.** The Willow Branches Placed Around The Temple Altar  
How was this mitzvah performed?  
On each of the seven days [of the festival], branches of willows were brought[^135] and stood upright near the altar[^136] with their tops bent over the altar.[^137] When they would bring them and arrange them [near the altar,] a series of [shofar blasts][^138] - *teki'ah, teru'ah,* and *teki'ah* - were sounded.  
When the Sabbath fell in the midst of the festival, the willows would not be arranged [near the altar][^139] unless the seventh day fell on the Sabbath.[^140] [On such an occasion,] the willows were arranged [near the altar],[^141] to publicize the fact that [taking] them is a mitzvah.[^142] ^7-21

**22.** How would they fulfill [this mitzvah on the Sabbath]?  
They would bring [the branches] to the Temple on the Sabbath eve and place them in golden containers, so their leaves would not dry out.[^143] On the following morning, they would be placed next to the altar and the people would take them in the same manner as they did each day.[^144]  
Since the willow is not explicitly mentioned in the Torah,[^145] it is not taken on each of the seven days of the festival as a remembrance of the Temple.[^146] Rather, at present it is taken only on the seventh day.[^147]  
What is done?[^148] One takes one[^149] branch or many branches[^150] in addition to the willow of the lulav, and hits the ground[^151] or a utensil with them two or three times.[^152] No blessing is recited, because this practice is a custom instituted by the prophets.[^153] ^7-22

**23.** On each day of the festival,[^154] they[^155] would walk around the altar once, carrying their lulavim[^156] in their hands, reciting: "Please, God, save us. Please, God, grant us success"[^157] \[[[Psalms 118#^118-25|Psalms 118:25]]]. On the seventh day, they would walk around the altar seven times.[^158]  
It has become universally accepted Jewish custom to place the ark in the center of the synagogue[^159] and walk around it each day,[^160] as they walked around the altar in remembrance of the Temple [service]. ^7-23

**24.** The following custom was observed in Jerusalem:[^161] A person would leave his house in the morning [carrying] his lulav in his hand.[^162] He would enter the synagogue with it in his hand; pray while it was in his hand;[^163] go to visit the sick and comfort the mourners with it in his hand.[^164] When he entered the House of Study, he would send it home with his son or servant.[^165] ^7-24

**25.** During the time the lulav was taken on the Sabbath,[^166] a woman was allowed to receive the lulav from her son or her husband[^167] and return it to the water on the Sabbath.[^168] On the festival, a person might add to the water.[^169] On Chol Hamo'ed,[^170] one might change the water.[^171] ^7-25

**26.** It is forbidden to smell the myrtle in the lulav.[^172] Since it is useful only for smelling[^173] and it has been set aside for the performance of the mitzvah, it is forbidden to smell it.[^174] However, it is permitted to smell an etrog, because setting it aside for the mitzvah [prohibits it from being] eaten.[^175] ^7-26

**27.** It is forbidden to eat the etrog[^176] throughout the seventh day [of the festival];[^177] since it was set aside for a portion of the day, it is set aside for the entire day. However, on the eighth day it is permitted to be eaten.[^178]  
At present, when we celebrate [the festivals for] two days[^179] - even though the etrog is not taken on the eighth day[^180] - the etrog is forbidden on the eighth day,[^181] since it was forbidden on the eighth day[^182] during the time [the festivals] were celebrated for two days because of the doubt whether [the eighth day] was, in fact, the seventh.[^183]  
When a person sets aside seven etrogim, [one for each] of the seven days [of the festival], each one of them can be used for the mitzvah on its day and eaten on the morrow.[^184] ^7-27
