## About

A short dialogue on prayer, traditionally ascribed to Plato and now generally considered spurious. Socrates meets Alcibiades on his way to pray and argues that it is unsafe to pray for anything without knowing whether it is good or bad for oneself.

- **People and setting:** Socrates and Alcibiades only. Alcibiades is about to offer prayer to Zeus. None of the sources consulted gives a place or a dramatic date.
- **Argument:** madness and ignorance are both kinds of the opposite of wisdom; rulers such as Archelaus of Macedon were murdered or driven out after getting what they wanted; knowledge of particular things without wisdom can do harm. Socrates commends the Spartans' simple, private prayer and advises Alcibiades to put off his sacrifice. Alcibiades gives Socrates his garland.
- **Authenticity:** the IEP lists it among works doubted already in antiquity, though sometimes defended today. Wikipedia cites defective arguments, lack of humor and style against it. Jowett, introducing the translation (made, he says, by his secretary Mr. Knight), thought no modern critic would maintain it and judged the dialectic "poor and weak".
- **Date of composition:** those who reject it place it in the 3rd or 2nd century BC (Wikipedia). Jowett says it may be attributed to the second or third century BC, by a writer who seems to have known Plato's *Laws*. The IEP says that, if genuine, it would go with the early or early transitional dialogues.
- **Place among the dialogues:** Diogenes Laertius records it in Thrasyllus's fourth tetralogy as *Second Alcibiades, or On Prayer*, classed "obstetric", beside *Alcibiades I*, *Hipparchus* and the *Rivals*. Stephanus pages 138a–151c.
- **Why it matters:** it is one of the dialogues that test where the Platonic canon ends: accepted into Thrasyllus's arrangement, yet doubted then and now. It also quotes the pseudo-Homeric *Margites* and an oracle of Ammon praising the Spartans' silent worship.
