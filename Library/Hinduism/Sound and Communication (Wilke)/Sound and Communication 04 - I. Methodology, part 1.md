### 1 Hinduism as a Culture of Sound

If you visit the holy city of Banaras (Varanasi) you will not need an alarm clock to get up early in the morning. It is quite sufficient to take a lodging close to the Mumukshu Bhavan, between Assi Ghat and Lanka. At 5 a.m. precisely, any chance of sleep is dispelled by the loud calls of “Raam Raam Raam” emanating from the loudspeakers of the Ashram, and for the next half hour a man’s voice enthusiastically sings Godď’s praise, while no-one in the vicinity can possibly get away from the sound. The enthusiastic devotion to God is expressed not least by the fact that the man praying turns up his amplifier to maximum volume. Should you step out onto the Lanka Road after breakfast, you will witness another pan-Hinduistic phenomenon. Even if the roads are not completely blocked with the usual congestion, the cars drive through the streets sounding their horns, and as for the numerous cyclists, you would be forgiven for thinking that Indian bicycles were powered solely by their bells. Even Indian street life appears to prove the widespread Tantric philosopheme of the connection between sound and cosmic Dynamis. In the alleys that have hitherto been quiet in the district of Godowlia, the traders have now appeared. Just like birds in the forest, they mark out their territories with long, drawn-out and piercing calls and try to attract custom by means of constantly repeated calls such as “steel plate.” “Chai, garam chai” (“tea, hot tea”) is probably the most omni-present sound phenomenon in India, used by the tea-sellers at railroad stations to make their presence clearly audible. They intone their loud and monotonous call with a characteristically long drawn-out interval of a third, like a cuckoo, without which this secular mantra apparently cannot have its effect of selling tea. Religious sounds, too, are not limited to the early morning, but also occur throughout the day. If one lives in a Brahmin family, at 11 a.m. one hears resonant mantra recitations and the bright ringing of small bells coming from the room housing the domestic altar — a ritual act, used to dedicate a freshly cooked meal to the deity before it is eaten as a gracious gift. In the afternoon it is common for one to hear loud chanting from a side-street:

“Rām-Nām satya hai? (which is Hindi, pronounced something like, “Raam Naam Satye hey”). This is not a demonstration and neither has India just won a cricket match against Pakistan. In fact this is the sacred mantra of deliverance, which is typical of Banaras: “Rām-Nām satya hai” (“the name of Rāma is Truth”) which, according to the myth, Śiva himself whispers into the ear of the deceased. This “mantra that leads beyond death” (tāraka-mantra) is continuously called out at funerary processions by the men carrying the bier bearing the body of the deceased, who is wrapped in gold brocade. If finally you take a walk at dusk, when the streets have become quieter again, to the ghats along the banks of the Ganges, or through the narrow alleyways of the town center, it will not take long to find a temple, for you will certainly hear one before you can see it. The intense ringing of small bells announces the evening ritual and even more vigorous bell-ringing, small gongs, hand-drums and cymbals mark its climax. After sunset loudspeakers start to broadcast religious singing, or else groups gather spontaneously in the temples to enthusiastically and unceasingly intone “Rāma Rāja jay Sītā Rām” to a simple melody CD || accompanied by the sounds of cymbals and a harmonium. At 10 p.m. quietness descends and one only hears the dogs that wander the streets, taking over the territories of the steel-plate sellers. However, it is quite possible that a pious neighbor will be fulfilling an oath by intoning the call “Rām Rām Sīyā Rām” at regular intervals throughout the night, rather like a Western clock tower chiming the hours. A single day in India illustrates just how much a cosmos of sounds pervades the whole of everyday activity and religious life. 

Our impressionistic ‘tableau’ will probably ‘ring true’ in the ears of everybody who has visited India' — and not by chance. There is some justification in calling Hinduism a highly sound-based culture. The audible and recited word is of major importance. Our extended methodological approach to text emphasizes the performative aspects and with them the readers’ imagination and the ever-changing flow of tradition. Texts are not static entities with a fixed meaning, and neither is their meaning exhausted in their semantic sense. This applies particularly to religious texts. In libraries and in academics’ studies they may well be the object of rational analysis, and when bound between the covers of a book they may suggest closed (or complete) systems. During transmission and reception, however, they are exceptionally fluid constructions. Texts — even as part of a canon - are not ‘finished,’ but are the subject of cultural discourses. During their transmission and their reception by various cultural actors, new codings are superimposed on them, and they are “re-glossed”? and re-interpreted. In fact, such openness and fluidity are essential to the life of a text in order to safeguard its vitality and cultural significance. Our main emphasis, however, will be on the sound material. When texts are read out, they are aesthetic events. Language as expressive sound has its own independent validity of meaning, its own ability to communicate, and its own aura. It unfolds its effects not only in the sphere of the terminological and logical, but also in the sphere of the sensory and emotive. We are therefore focusing on the event character of religious texts in a double sense: on the one hand relating to their potential ability to produce sense in an unfinished, ever new, fashion and on the other, relating to their aesthetic quality, i.e. audible physical nature.

Sanskrit Hinduism is characterized by a plurality of the most varied approaches to language and sound — a plurality that begins as early as the Veda, the most important “myth(os)”? (in the sense of guiding cultural image and great narrative) that was to leave its mark on Indian culture, and the sonic paradigm par excellence. The Veda itself exhibits the fluidity of texts particularly well. Whereas the Vedic hymns were still evocative calls to the gods and magic poetry at the time that they were composed, in late Vedic times they became sacred language material in which one no longer tried to find a visionary meaning, but was much rather seeking mysterious numeric combinations. In epic times, the mere recitation of the holy text acquired an importance equal to that of the late Vedic fire sacrifice. The Rāmāyana depiction of the “holy noise” (brahma-ghosa) of the resonant three-tone Veda recitation, as it emanates from the simple straw huts of modest-living Brahmins, already suggests completely the Vedic aura and solemn sacredness, which even today is linked to Veda recitation. Just the sound of the Veda (CD -10| imparts a meaning associated with one particular cultural “habitus”: when a Hindu hears the characteristic three intervals of a Veda performance, this transports him to the peaceful, pure and strictly vegetarian world of ideal Brahminhood. The Veda has kept its sacred power although it has altered substantially in its reception over the course of time. One and the same text initially was understood as a “normal semantic text,” then as an esoteric secret formula, and finally, essentialistically, as sacred sound substance.

This discussion leads the reader directly to the next section, since the linguistic philosophy of the Brahmin Bhartrhari was an important source of inspiration to the mostly non-Brahmanic Tantrics and has had particularly great influence on the linguistic philosophy of Abhinavagupta. 

Chapter 5, entitled “Performance and Sensuality,” is primarily given over to Tantric mantra rites, the “science of mantras,” and sonic and linguistic cosmologies. The Tantric spectrum is broad and its “sound universes” are manifold, ranging from the heterodox Tantra of the Siddhas, Sādhakas and Bhairava worshipers with its occult noises, onomatopoetic and ghostly sounds and wild songs, to the domesticated and Brahmanized Tantra of the Smārtas, Śaiva Siddhāntins and Śāktas, who develop many and varied (including graphical) ritual uses of mantras, expressive symbolic interpretations of the potent “seed-sounds” and whole sonic cosmogonies. The Tantric linguistic speculations, alphabet cosmologies and mantra rites culminate in the teachings of the great

Kashmiri theologian and aesthetic theoretician Abhinavagupta, who drafts his non-dual philosophy of a “cosmic language” primarily based on the experience of sound and is probably the most notable proponent of what we call “reflected universal myths.” But sound is also the central medium of expression, communication and perception in the devotional bhakti traditions, although here not in the form of subtle interpretations of the world, as is the case with Abhinavagupta, but instead in the form of music as an expression of pure participation and devotion. Our examples range from the musical language of a famous hymn to Durgā and a highly artistic poem individually set to music, to the group singing of simple songs and purely instrumental music. 

By way of conclusion we analyze how all these threads become intertwined in the term Nāda-Brahman (“sonic absolute,” more literally “Sound-Brahman”) discussed in chapter 6. The Nāda-Brahman was “invented” by the Indian musicologist Śārṁgadeva in the early thirteenth century and historically effective within popular Tantric and devotional traditions. By fusing the Tantric primordial sound that pervades the universe (Nāda) with the Vedāntic non-dual absolute (Brahman), Śārṁgadeva gave Indian music a metaphysical basis and claimed listenting to music was a form of Yoga for everybody. The Nāda-Brahman is not only the most recent transmutation of the Brahman and the latest systematic conception in the Indian assessment of sound, but has also left musicians’ circles and gained transcultural significance, e.g. in Western Yoga movements. The jazz historian Joachim Berendt was one of the transmittors in this process. His New Age assessment of the Nāda-Brahman and record of the reception of Indian modal music in the 1960s and 1970s is given special attention because Berendt mirrors processes of religious change going beyond Max Weber’s thesis of rationalization. Alongside a historical reconstruction of the little-studied Nāda-Brahman, and an exploration of the aesthetics of classical Indian music and the dovetainling of music and religion, the chapter discusses the impact of India in forming modern Western spirituality. It ends the treatise with some theoretical conclusions on this subject.

Music in particular creates strong group identities and ties through synchronization by collective chanting and hearing, and social effervescence, the “swelling up” of moods, reactions etc. by joining in and becoming part of a common experience. Music can also elicit a very personal reaction of melting away, bringing about peace of mind, intense devotion and satisfaction in self-forgetful absorption. This dissolving is the reiteration of a cultural pattern of psychological “blending” and non-dual “fusing,” which has occurred again and again since the time of the Upaniṣads in new and very different forms — cognitive, emotive, ritual, aesthetic, controlled and restricted, and ecstatic forms. 

Some things suggest that the Indian predilection for holistic world-views correlates directly with the sound-centered life-world and was promoted by the paradigm of sonic perception. In Sanskrit Hinduism language and sound are among the major means of identification and the most significant holistic symbols of non-dual reality. We find, for instance, an identification of deities based on the phonetic similarities of their names. The acoustic piety and sonic theology that are typical of Hinduism appear to be founded not least in the fact that the experience of sound can easily bring about immediate absorption and allows a sensory experience of non-duality. If philosophical theological (Tantric) and musicological speculations are also considered, sound even creates a physical participatory link with the whole cosmos. Here sound, and space/ether, are regarded as “everlasting” elements and the emergence of the world is traced back to rhythmic sound. But sound also has a special position in less esoteric, non-Tantric discourses. Sound is the most “ethereal” object of sensory perception and the Indian classification of the senses correlates hearing, acoustics and space directly and always viewed them as the most subtle elements of this world, coming closest to the supernatural. Therefore sound was especially suitable for representing and embodying the immaterial, transcendental and supernatural. Furthermore, sound is particularly suitable as a symbol of wholeness and cosmic unity. Sound’s very own domain, that is, to be accessible to sensory perception and at the same time to be inaccessible to it as vibrations and sound waves, was apparently persuasive evidence for the linkage and continuity of the internal and external worlds.
