## About

Martin Heidegger's (1889–1976) essay *Die Frage nach der Technik*, and the 1977 English collection named after it. The essay argues that the essence of modern technology is nothing technological: it is a way of revealing that orders nature and people as standing-reserve.

- **Origins:** it grew out of "Das Gestell", the second of four lectures Heidegger gave to the Club of Bremen on 1 December 1949 under the title "Insight Into That Which Is"; the others were "The Thing", "The Danger" and "The Turn". Babich reports that he gave three of them again at the Bavarian Academy of Fine Arts in Munich on 6 June 1950.
- **Publication:** first printed in 1954 as the opening essay of *Vorträge und Aufsätze*. Babich notes a version published by Neske in 1962 as *Die Technik und die Kehre*.
- **Argument:** Heidegger starts from the usual view of technology as a human means to an end and traces it back to the four causes (with a silver chalice as example), to bringing-forth (poiēsis), and to truth as unconcealment (alētheia). Modern technology instead challenges nature forth and sets everything up as standing-reserve; his examples include the Rhine and a forester. Its essence is Gestell, rendered "enframing" by Lovitt. The danger lies in that essence, which Wikipedia says also holds the possibility of rescue, with art pointed to as one way.
- **The 1977 collection:** *The Question Concerning Technology, and Other Essays*, translated with an introduction by William Lovitt (New York: Harper & Row, Harper Torchbooks; xxxix + 182 pages). It contains "The Question Concerning Technology", "The Turning", "The Word of Nietzsche: 'God Is Dead'", "The Age of the World Picture" and "Science and Reflection". Wikipedia's infobox names Garland as the English publisher; the Penn catalogue and Babich give Harper & Row.
- **Why it matters:** the SEP says Heidegger's later thought was increasingly occupied with "the dangers of our technological age". Babich notes that Mitchell's later translation renders Gestell as "positionality".
