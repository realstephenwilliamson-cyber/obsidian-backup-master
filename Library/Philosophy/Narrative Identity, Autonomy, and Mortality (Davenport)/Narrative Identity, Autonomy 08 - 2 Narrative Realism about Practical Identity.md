## 2 Narrative Realism about Practical Identity

1\. 

### 1 Key Ontological and Practical Objections to Narrative Theories

As outlined in Chapter 1, there are several different lines of philosophical inquiry that converge towards a narrative conception of selves. The problems we face in trying to understand personal autonomy, practical identity, and character all leads in this direction. My route into the narrative approach begins specifically with issues that arose in explaining and defending Søren Kierkegaard’s ideas about self-choice in ethical agency and the transition from “aesthetic” to “ethical” life-views (or “stages”) in the development of personhood. This proves to be an especially useful point of entry to the debates about narrative theories of identity, as we will see, because Kierkegaard’s ideas encourage us to distinguish different *levels* of narrative connection.

In recent articles, John Lippitt has raised important questions about the notions that human selves have a “narrative” structure and that the natural development of our capacity for robust selves (including autonomy, authenticity, and even ethical maturity) involves achieving “narrative unity” in the stories of our lives.<sup>1</sup> His questions connect with and deepen other recent critiques of narrative models raised in the wider and growing literature on this topic, such as Galen Strawson’s defense of “episodic” identity as an ethically respectable alternative.<sup>2</sup> Lippitt also forces us to reconsider claims that Anthony Rudd, I, and others made in *Kierkegaard After MacIntyre* that (i) MacIntyre’s famous account of narrative unity as part of the telos of human life<sup>3</sup> sheds light on Kierkegaard’s conception of selfhood, and that (ii) insights from Kierkegaard can help us develop and defend such a narrative model. For example, Lippitt argues against the view that progress through Kierkegaard’s existential “stages” of life—especially from an “aesthetic” to an “ethical” attitude—can be explained by increasing diachronic integration of a narrative sort, or that the contrast between these outlooks has to do with narrative unity of a life.

In three recent essays, Anthony Rudd has addressed several of these objections by Lippitt and other critics of narrative identity theory in the 

*[p. 39]* ^pg58

mainstream literature.<sup>4</sup> In the next section, I will build on Rudd’s argument that the lived experience of human action naturally has a narrative shape. While Rudd addresses several of the key objections to extant narrative theories, we will see that there is a remaining ontological objection to MacIntyre’s account of self-identity that cannot be answered on the basis of action theory alone. Answering this objection will require ontological theses that theorists have often hoped to avoid by focusing on the phenomena of practical identity. Although practical identity remains distinct from what I have called theoretical identity of consciousness over time, an ontology of narrative is needed to defend the idea that the practical identity of a human person *is* a kind of narrative identity—or (a bit more weakly) that it is usefully understood on the analogy of narrative structures. I begin in scholastic fashion with a summary of Lippitt’s objections, several of which dovetail with objections from others. Simply for ease of later reference, I give each of them a short label (which do not fully capture their nuance):

1\. *Triviality*: Defenders of narrative accounts are not suffi ciently clear about what constitutes the type of narrative that is said to form practical identity (or to be an essential aspect of it).<sup>5</sup> We need to know more about what narratival connection is. The relevant sense(s) of narrative cannot be trivial, or the narrativist thesis becomes vacuous.<sup>6</sup> 2. *Artifact*: How can one’s “whole life” consist of (or even be much *like*) 

a single unified story, given that stories are human artifacts (e.g., biographies, historical accounts, or fictional novels) and their unity results from *selecting* among many possible plot-developments, details of settings, thoughts, and actions etc. in order to present one interpretation of a set of events?<sup>7</sup>

3\. *Selectivity*: Given the ever-present temptation to rationalize events or aspects of our life that may conflict with others or make less sense to us, and our propensity to retouch the more unsavory or less interesting features of our life, isn’t any tale we tell about our practical identity bound to be full of convenient omissions, exaggeration, half-truths, and other types of self-deception?<sup>8</sup>

4\. *Fact-value*: If practical identities consist of, or require, a narrative structure in order to be intelligible to the agents whose identities they are, how can they have normative implications for human life? How can it be *better* to live a unified life if narrative unity is a constitutive condition of having a life at all?<sup>9</sup>

5\. *Flexibility*: Even if types of narrative unity that go beyond the constitutive conditions of agency can be distinguished as normative ideals for practical identities, they may exaggerate the level and/or types of coherence that are good for us, cutting us off from kinds of novelty or tension that are necessary for personal development.<sup>10</sup> We need to know what *kinds* of unity are important for a fully developed practical identity or robustly meaningful life.<sup>11</sup>

*[p. 40]* ^pg59

6\. *Mortality*: Given its lack of conclusion, a person’s life cannot be a “complete” story for her while she is living it; at best, it can only become a unified narrative for others after her death. Thus the analogy between lives and stories breaks down completely at the fatal end-point.<sup>12</sup>

Several of these objections are closely related. For example, (2) and (4) are combined in concerns that selves are described as “products” of narration but some sort of self must exist first to form a narrative self-conception.<sup>13</sup> The objections can also be stated as theoretical questions. In responding to Strawson, Marya Schechtman distinguishes “three basic questions for the narrative theorist: . . . What counts as a life-narrative? . . . What counts as having a narrative? . . . What are the practical implications of having (or failing to have) a narrative?”<sup>14</sup> Her first question corresponds to (1) and (2) in my list, and her second and third questions concern the higher types of unity critiqued in Lippitt’s objections (3) and (5). There are also further worries about claims made for the importance of narrative unity that are not neatly subsumed under these headings, including Lippitt’s arguments that narrative unity as a condition of intelligibility cannot explain the supposed superiority of a distinctively ethical life-view in Kierkegaard’s work:

7\. *Aestheticism*: Given the diversity of “aesthetic” ways of life in Kierkegaard’s texts, the distinction between them and “ethical” selfhood is not most perspicuously explained in terms of greater narrative unity.

Such critiques of “MacIntyrean” readings of Kierkegaard cannot be answered until we develop systematic answers to the more general objections (1)–(3). In this foundational task, the defense of what I call a “narrative realist” response has two parts. The positive argument for narrative realism begins by abduction in light of contemporary action theory, and develops in the next chapter drawing on considerations in autonomy theory. Once we answer objection (1) by distinguishing different levels of narrative unity, we will see that a narrative approach off ers a better explanation of planning agency and personal autonomy than its atomistic rivals. To answer objection (2), in this chapter I build on past sketches of narrative realist ideas to argue that we can account for the persistent gap between real lives and stories about them using the concept of “narravive” in place of primary life-narratives. The negative part of this argument, given in §3 below, focuses on a particular fallacy that underlies the artifact objection (2), which motivates fi ctionalist versions of narrative theories that undermine narrative conceptions of autonomy. The positive part off ers a detailed account of narratival connections in real life that supports a better response to the problem of self-deception than its constructivist rivals (see §4 below).

Finally, we must consider a new objection that arises specifically in response to narrative realism as an answer to objections (1)–(3). In Chapter 

*[p. 41]* ^pg60

1, I briefly noted Patrick Stokes’ argument that equating a person with the narrative of his “whole life” leaves out the self as present subject of consciousness and agent (choosing and acting).<sup>15</sup> Recently, he has further specified this as a metaphysical objection that equating one’s practical identity with a life-narrative makes us into four-dimensional objects, with all the problems that four-dimensionalist Lockean theories face:

8\. *4D*: If the narratival unity of a whole life is a real structure of meaning-relations, it is a *four-dimensional entity* that cannot be wholly present at any one time. Yet there are important senses in which “self” apparently refers to an entity that is fully present in the now-moment of experience and action.<sup>16</sup>

A plausible narrative account of practical identity must address this concern, and an existential conception of autonomy must preserve the self as an existing source of free choices rather than an ersatz structure in which each made choice is essential to the whole (like a Leibnizian complete individual concept).<sup>17</sup> This aspect of the four-dimensional objection is closely related to the sense of incompleteness in presently living selves that motivates the mortality objection. Both of these objections will be considered together in Chapter 5.

### 2 Three Levels of Narrative Unity

#### 2.1 Rudd and the Narrative Implications of Contemporary Action Theory

In this section, I start from Rudd’s response to Lippitt’s objections (1) and (2). He begins by emphasizing that the type of narrative that MacIntyre associates with actual (rather than fictional) human life has to do with the intelligibility of *intentional actions*: unlike a scientific explanation in terms of effi cient-causal chains, a MacIntyrean narrative “is essentially teleological—it provides reasons, not just causes.”<sup>18</sup> Moreover, the reasons that make present movements intelligible as actions depend for their signifi - cance on their complex relationships to other events, actions, and reasons that were considered at other times and places by this agent and others, as well as their perceived relation to future prospects. Thus the intelligibility of discrete intentional actions, or short series of actions in the extended present, is usually nested in a much wider teleological context. As Rudd puts it, if we reject atomistic accounts of action and recognize that reasons-explanations will make reference to a “mini-narrative” even in cases like Strawson’s example of making a particular cup of coffee,<sup>19</sup> we cannot stop there. For these mini-narratives do not make sense to us without “at least implicitly thinking of them as embedded in and deriving intelligibility from 

*[p. 42]* ^pg61

the person’s whole life.” Thus “normal human agents do have a sense of themselves as characters in a developing story,” in which current choices are understood as responses to situations shaped by the past and to future possibilities.<sup>20</sup> Even though we experience our story as incomplete while still living it, we encounter important life-decisions as potentially affecting our long-term prospects and the meaning of many aspects of our life; the significance of past episodes is also revised by later developments.<sup>21</sup> 

This kind of temporal *weave* of significance, as I’ll call it, strongly resembles the meaning-networks we find in fictional narratives, although the weave is more complex in real lives (see §4.2 below). Thus my status as a temporally continuing, self-aware intentional actor involves a narrative sense (however inchoate) of my entire practical identity *and* the social relationships with which it is bound up. The intentional actions of persons “do not exist in their own right, prior to and independently of their narration,” but rather as parts of larger narrative wholes.<sup>22</sup> As Charles Taylor says, “any situated action, . . . however trivial” involves a sense of what we have done and experienced and what our future options are: actions move from an earlier state towards what we will become. This dynamic sense has to extend to the “direction of our lives” in general, according to Taylor, because of our inevitable orientation to goods and awareness of temporal change. Thus it is a basic condition of “making sense of ourselves, that we grasp our lives in a *narrative*.”<sup>23</sup> Taylor is following Heidegger’s description of lived time here; as Charles Guignon summarizes, “Heidegger makes it clear that each action gains its meaning from its place within the agent’s entire life story.”<sup>24</sup> Taylor’s positions is also close to what MacIntyre meant in his well-known argument that the “primary intention” of an action is favored over descriptions emphasizing other aspects of a given bit of behavior because of its intelligibility in the agent’s “setting” and its “history,” including the relationships with others it involves, and its fit with “longer term intentions” that relate multiple aspects of a setting.<sup>25</sup>

In my view, Rudd, Taylor, and MacIntyre are correct, though I will argue that we have to distinguish different types of narrative connection. Recent work in action theory by Carlos Moya, among others, strongly supports MacIntyre’s insights about the reasons that make action intelligible as intentional. Moya thinks this is most easily seen in “meaningful” acts like signaling that involve commitment to follow norms shared in the communication-community relating these acts to intended future acts.<sup>26</sup> And this capacity for commitment to future action, which is intrinsically intentional and irreducible to mere physical happenings, “is an essential part of our agency and our consciousness of being agents.”<sup>27</sup> More generally, simple actions like turning a steering wheel become intentional only as part of a coherent plan involving future actions leading to the further end of the action-series.<sup>28</sup> We individuate actions out of such a process as a heuristic device for various explanatory purposes, focusing on larger or smaller bits of activity depending on the explanatory context. Intentions 

*[p. 43]* ^pg62

are also not inherently distinct atomic phenomena; they “can exist only in the wider context of a mind” with multiple intentions connected by coherence requirements. Of course sometimes our intentions, beliefs, and current actions are not coherently related, but “Cases of incoherence can arise only against a background of coherence.”<sup>29</sup> The mental life of agents in general is pervaded by a normativity that is based on holism: “Intentional states are essentially a network, a whole system. They are not discrete, separate items.”<sup>30</sup>

This analysis fits with several other recent theories of responsible agency. Moya’s claim that coherence by normative guidance is a constitutive basis of intentional action, relative to which incoherent patterns of action and violation of norms we have implicitly accepted are only a deficient mode of intentional action, is well supported by Gary Watson’s argument for an internal connection between intention and practical reason.<sup>31</sup> But following Thomas Pink and Michael Bratman, Watson also recognizes the so-called “executive function” of decisions in forming plans and coordinating an agent’s actions over time.<sup>32</sup> In turn, summarizing his 20th-century work, Bratman writes that “Our purposive activity is typically embedded in multiple, interwoven quilts of partial, future-directed plans of action,” which “typically have a hierarchical structure” with means and “preliminary steps” embedded in ends and “proximate ends embedded in further ends.” Such embedded partial plans provide a background within which practical deliberation typically takes place, and impose end-neutral rational requirements “for the coherence and consistency of plans.”<sup>33</sup> Intentions taking the form of concrete plans or schedules of particular actions on “specifi - cally characterized occasions” interface with “personal policies” that are more general intentions.<sup>34</sup> In an essay on autonomy, he explains that these “distinctive norms of plan consistency, plan coherence, and plan stability” allow plans and policies to provide “cross-temporal organization,” because “in filling in and/or executing prior plans one normally sees oneself in ways that refer back to those prior plans.” Thus plans contribute to the psychological “connections” emphasized in “Lockean accounts of personal identity over time.”<sup>35</sup> And this kind of persistence is associated with agential authority: “the relevant plan-type attitudes . . . claim to speak for the temporally persisting agent.”<sup>36</sup> Although Bratman does not refer to this as narrative continuity, he says that his analysis adds “forward-looking connections” to memory in a generally Lockean approach: “one and the same agent—me—begins, develops, and completes temporally extended and coordinated activities and projects.”<sup>37</sup> These are connections of *meaning* rather than cause or mere similarity: as Bratman notes, an initial intention refers implicitly to its later execution in the right sort of action, just as the latter refers back to the former, whereas “mere continuity of a desire” may consist only or similarity in earlier and later desire.<sup>38</sup> And this is the beginning of a plausible distinction between specifically narrative connections and sub-narrative forms of psychological continuity. Notice that an agent’s 

*[p. 44]* ^pg63

explanation of the referential connections Bratman finds in planning would naturally take the form of a story—e.g. “I was initially interested in biology, but as I learned more about environmental issues, I decided to switch my major and to take extra courses next summer to finish the Environmental Policy program.” As Flanagan puts it, not only do we live in time and “appropriate” memories of our past; we “navigate” towards the future with “attentiveness to the long-term,” employing a sense of how our desires and beliefs have already developed.<sup>39</sup>

Similar points about intentional action are made by David Velleman, although with a significant variation: following Grice and Harman, he hypothesizes that intention consists in predictive belief about our own future action combined with a desire to be right in such self-predictions.<sup>40</sup> Citing Bratman, Velleman notes that current motives and instrumental beliefs are insuffi cient by themselves to settle a future course of action, as required for many aspects of our lives; only intentions can meet this need for coordination over time.<sup>41</sup> This leads to a view that practical reason is based in a desire for a coherent understanding of one’s actions (including agreement between “what one accepts that one will do” and what one actually does), which is based in the constitutive aim of intentional action—namely conscious control of behavior.<sup>42</sup> It is then no surprise that Velleman has adopted a narrative account of practical identity. His account of the need for narrative unity is developed in terms of one’s need to act so as “to maintain correspondence between his story and his life.”<sup>43</sup> This is a narrative version of his proposed constitutive aim of intentional agency. Velleman’s and Bratman’s views notably resemble Korsgaard’s conception of practical unity as a constitutive aim (as I described in Chapter 1). Korsgaard thinks that acting intentionally involves seeing selves as causes of action, which in turn requires accepting rules or principles as ongoing bases for adopting particular intentions.

It should be emphasized that almost all these accounts argue from the phenomenology of intention and the normative role that plans and policies play in our organization of our lives; they do not depend on metaphysical theses concerning free will. As we saw, Korsgaard follows Kant in holding that, in the standpoint of agency, we have to regard ourselves as agent-causes (which she regards as something theoretically unknowable yet compatible with our metaphysical knowledge). MacIntyre may agree; yet the diachronic structuring role of intentions and adopted reasons for action does not seem to depend directly on this conception of the freedom required for responsibility. To see this, we can return briefly to Hauerwas, who argues that “self-agency” commits us to believing that we can be an “uncaused cause” of our choices.<sup>44</sup> He also defends the idea from Aristotle and Aquinas that our ends are usually determined along with a plan partly specifying means for achieving them;<sup>45</sup> he suggests that “intention” broadly understood involves “everything that goes into our descriptions and envisionment of our act (our project)—i.e. beliefs, motives, reasons, etc.”<sup>46</sup> 

*[p. 45]* ^pg64

Intention makes sense as part of a process and can include anticipating side effects that are not (in the narrower sense) intended. Following Anscombe, Hauerwas argues that intention depends on the “description under which the agent understands himself to be acting,” but that such a description must be in a language that is publicly intelligible. What I am doing “must in principle at least be communicable to other people.”<sup>47</sup> Although not all our intentions are fully articulate in reflective consciousness, what they are depends on how their plausible descriptions fit within a broader explanation of our activities. Thus, before MacIntyre, Hauerwas defends a social and narrative conception of intentional action. We can always

. . . check the declaration of intentions in terms of circumstances \[setting\], the agent’s further actions, his further avowals and disavowals, his interests, etc . . . because having an intention is a matter that pertains not to one and only one incident of the proceedings but to the whole character of the proceedings that surround the action performed . . . <sup>48</sup>

though our longer-term or most general intentions are harder to check in this way, because they operate as background for the rest. This is quite similar to Moya’s holistic conception, though Moya’s and the later accounts defend this more on the basis of the normative commitments involved in intention. Thus, while Hauerwas follows Charles Taylor and R.S. Peters in arguing that for action to be intentional, it must be caused by agents through their adoption of motives and intentions, it seems that diachronic holism as a condition of the intelligibility of intentions and their practical function could be compatible with diff erent accounts of how intentions are caused (e.g., teleological causation or agent-causation<sup>49</sup>). So far then it seems that narrative accounts of identity building on action theory can remain agnostic about agent-causation.

#### 2.2 Levels 0 and 1: Consciousness and Planning Agency

The examples in the prior section show that work in contemporary action theory continues to bear out the insights of *After Virtue* (ch.15) that Rudd defends.<sup>50</sup> Yet it is crucial that the diachronic holism of practical reasons and means-to-end relations implied by these theories is *constitutive* of extended intentional action or “planning agency” (Bratman’s term). Though it is not required for all purposive movement, e.g. a mouse’s instinctive running or subintentional human facial expression, the relations of reference and expression among elements in personal plans and policies,<sup>51</sup> which place intentions in a network of multiple kinds of significance-relations, describe a level of unity that is found in the lives of all agents with responsibilities ranging over extended plans. As shorthand, I’ll refer to this level of planning agency as *unity-1.* Although its exact conditions are debated, it is clear that to lack this basic kind of coherence in self-understanding is not to have 

*[p. 46]* ^pg65

an *ethically bad* life; it is rather to lack what (building on Susan Wolf and John Fischer) I have previously called “moral sanity,” which is a key part of the control conditions for full moral responsibility.<sup>52</sup> Unity-1 is thus significantly stronger than diachronic unity of apperception (as described in Chapter 1 §4), which a conscious being without motives connected to long-range practical reasoning could enjoy. We see this distinction at work when Lippitt contrasts “complete” narrative unity (which he thinks is impossible for human agents because of the openness of the future and mortality), with a weaker form of continuity in consciousness:

However fragmented my life, it is a unity in the most minimal sense that it is in some sense “mine.” Even the most radical schizophrenic, one of whose multiple personalities seizes the reins every ten minutes, can probably still give a name when his psychiatrist’s secretary tries to book him in for the next session.<sup>53</sup>

It will help to flesh out this example. A subject who suffers from a dissociative identity disorder (DID), as it is now called, will suffer “inability to recall important personal information, the extent of which is too great to be explained by ordinary forgetfulness,” and which is not caused by substance abuse or other illness.<sup>54</sup> If severe enough, one persona remembers little of what the (some) others remember and each displays different emotional dispositions and attitudes; then complex long-term planning may become impossible. Even if there is a “primary identity that carries the individual’s name,” it tends to be “passive, dependent, and depressed” rather than agentive, while more “hostile, controlling, or ‘protector’ identities” tend to have more complete memories of this human being’s history and conscious knowledge of the other personas.<sup>55</sup> Such an agent would lack unity-1 and thus could not be held responsible for keeping promises or for any kind of temporally extended activities that depend on planning. As Owen Flanagan suggests, in such DID cases, there are two or more “narrators who cannot grasp the connection between or among the narratives or narrative segments.”<sup>56</sup> Lippitt’s suggestion is that such an agent could retain a more minimal unity of consciousness; though it is unclear if this is true in severe DID,<sup>57</sup> we can imagine such a human being.

Whether this minimal continuity in consciousness is due to diachronic unity of apperception or to robust connections in memory-contents (e.g. as in the recall powers of Strawsonian episodics—see Chapter 1 §4), I will refer to it as *unity-0* for short: this consciousness is able to recognize experiences across time as belonging to the same animal. Certain higher mammals, such as apes and dolphins, probably have this kind of diachronic unity in awareness, even though they are assumed to lack the full powers of planning agency. Stated this way, unity-0 and thus unity-1 presuppose embodiment. Though I leave them aside, there are interesting metaphysical questions about whether the diachronic unity of apperception and planning 

*[p. 47]* ^pg66

agency necessary for narrative identity could be achieved by a nonembodied mind that experiences its endurance through time.<sup>58</sup>

These different kinds of unity in experience may be understood as thresholds (even if their edges are fuzzy). For example, consider Rudd’s suggestion that “Insofar as I’m not in a zombie-like state of automatism, I am aware of myself (even if only implicitly) as acting in a certain way in order to bring about certain results, which I want because they fit in with certain plans or ambitions.”<sup>59</sup> This case actually contrasts a zombie or somnambulist that lacks even unity-0 with an agent who enjoys at least unity-1. Human beings in early stages of life may normally live in between these thresholds, as when a child of 2 years smiles, laughs, and walks towards the cat he remembers seeing yesterday but is not yet capable of more complex planlike intentions, like getting revenge on his older sister next week for a trick she played on him four days ago. This child enjoys a prerefl ective form of unity-0 but does not yet have unity-1.

These cases suggest that unity-0 is a necessary but not suffi cient condition for unity-1. When unity-0 is lost between two phases of life, normally the psychological connection between them is cut, and contents of the earlier phase can play no practical role in conscious planning during the later phase. The most familiar way in which this occurs is total amnesia. For example, the character “V” in the film *V for Vendetta* has no memory of his life before the bizarre biological experiment that gave him extraordinary physical and mental powers, though his life from then on becomes unified in senses much stronger than unity-1 (indeed, it is too bent on a single finite end, as he finally discovers).<sup>60</sup> Likewise the sort of person who Schechtman cites as lacking “weak” narrative unity because of “Korsakoff syndrome or advanced dementia” may suffer partial loss of unity-0, and thus have significant diffi culties in exercising planning agency as well.<sup>61</sup> There is a reason that real brainwashing strategies try to make their victim forget her past: her planning agency is then not conditioned by past values, relationships, and intentions.

Yet unity-0, as Lippitt rightly says, is too weak to support further claims about ethical life made by “MacIntyrean Kierkegaardians.”<sup>62</sup> In fact, the same goes for unity-1: I suggest that to be an “aesthete” in Kierkegaard’s sense, an agent needs both unity-0 and unity-1.<sup>63</sup> Like a normal adult human “wanton” in Frankfurt’s sense (who *could* form higher-order volitions but does not), an aesthete is capable of complex instrumental reason, awareness of an accumulating past, and a sense of future potentials; even if he tries to live in the stream of consciousness, he is at least omitting use of his capacity for planning and personal policies. By contrast, a human subject who is incapable of planning agency is largely *incompetent*; she is incapable of full responsibility for most of what she does. So the values of unity-0 and unity-1 are *metaphysical* rather than ethical; these types of diachronic coherence are essential to the functioning of humans as responsible agents, whatever their variation on measures of ethical worth; they constitute a human being 

*[p. 48]* ^pg67

as a person subject to contrastive judgments of ethical worth.<sup>64</sup> Ordinarily we do not blame someone for lacking unity-0 or unity-1 (unless the lack is their fault, due to negligence or intentional self-harm committed when they did enjoy moral sanity). By contrast, Kierkegaard’s pseudonyms use the label “aesthete” pejoratively as well as descriptively; it usually implies that the aesthete could do better (or at least that there is a better way to be, once it becomes accessible to him). Though the aesthete’s failing is not an ordinary ethical failing, it is a failure to enter into the deeper sort of responsibility associated with autonomy (according to ADR).

If Kierkegaard’s aesthetic–ethical contrast is explained (partly) in narrative terms, then, this requires a higher form of narrative connectedness that builds on unity-1 as its precondition. In past work I have argued that for Kierkegaard, at least aesthetic agents who are “awakened” to the need for ethically grounded serious commitments are *capable* of a stronger type of narrative coherence (call it *unity-2*) that they could build using their volitional power to form such commitments. This unity-2 that is missing in wantons and most types of aesthetes is associated with the familiar though hard-to-explain concepts of autonomy and authenticity in one’s practical identity, and it involves more explicit concern about what Charles Taylor calls “the shape of my life *as a whole*.”<sup>65</sup> If we can specify a type of narrative linkage that is stronger than unity-1 and that is necessary for, or partly explanatory of other key features of autonomous or authentic identities, the *Fact-value* objection (4) will be answered, because unity-2 is a more evaluative concept, whereas unity-1 describes a constitutive condition of human agency according to narrative theories. Some narrativist accounts also associate unity-2 with well-being or flourishing, which is also an evaluative issue.<sup>66</sup> An account of unity-2 will address objection (5) as well—for the questions it raises about “flexibility” versus rigidity in our agendas and openness to new considerations concern the diachronic structure of autonomous character and the relation among the commitments that define an authentic identity. The level-distinctions also help answer the *Triviality* objection (1): while there is a level of continuity in consciousness that is subnarratival (unity-0), it becomes inseparable from narrative links early in normal human lives due to the emergence of planning agency. It is at this level that we have to explain what distinguishes “narrative” connections in their most basic forms (unity-1) from other types of sequences or trajectories through time.

As we will see in Chapter 3, narrative approaches to personal autonomy that give accounts of unity-2 also find support in contemporary analytic work, in particular from “coherence” theories of autonomy and from volitional conceptions of “caring.” For example, consider Laura Ekstrom’s argument that autonomy resides in “uncoercively formed preference\[s\]” that express the agent’s synchronically coherent and lasting strong evaluation of her desires.<sup>67</sup> Such accounts clearly offer conditions that go beyond planning agency in general. LITERATURE VERSUS LIFE: THE HARD 

### 3 Literature vs Life: the Hard Problem for Narrative Theories

3.1. Williams’s Flawed Critique of MacIntyre

While narrative theories of practical identity developed from several sources in the last three decades, MacIntyre’s brief discussion in *After Virtue* is one of the most influential. For that reason, and because he directly compares lives to literary genres, MacIntyre is the most frequent target of criticism that narrativists confuse literature and life.<sup>68</sup> In the early 1990s, Paul Ricoeur argued that MacIntyre does not address this problem: real life-stories are incomplete and their “author” is ambiguous, especially since our stories are interdependent—though Ricoeur thinks these problems can be overcome if we adopt a more dialectical (less “naive”) conception of the mimetic relation between life and story.<sup>69</sup> A much tougher critique is offered in Bernard Williams’s posthumous essay titled “Life as Narrative,” which responds to MacIntyre’s claims that “stories are lived before they are told.  .  .  .”<sup>70</sup> Williams says that according to MacIntyre, beyond the level of intelligible action, the whole process of living is social storytelling: “the narrative I *construct* for myself has to be part of a larger narrative enterprise, reaching beyond myself. This is one reason why I am only coauthor of the narrative” of my life.<sup>71</sup> But Williams complains that this is ambiguous: “When MacIntyre says that the narrative structure of actions is prior to people’s narrations, does he mean that it is prior to fictional narration, to any artful narration, or to any telling at all?” Let us call this third option the maximal priority thesis. Williams maintains that it must be false, for in

 . . . the essentially narrative understanding of complex human actions and the living of lives . . . what makes a given story a good story cannot be altogether prior to any telling at all, even if it is prior to the telling of that particular story. One respect . . . in which the narrative structure of complex human agency could not be entirely prior to narration, in the sense of mere telling, is that it could not be entirely prior to telling by agents themselves. This, surely, is what MacIntyre means. So when 

*[p. 50]* ^pg69

he says that narrative structure is prior to narrations by novelists and \[poets or dramatists\], he does not mean that narrative is prior to any telling at all, but that artless telling is prior to artful telling, or factual telling is prior to fictional, or both.<sup>72</sup>

But then, given that we cannot identify narratives that are about a person’s life without a prior concept of personhood, our ability to pick out a person and his life is prior to any narrative about him, Williams insists: some “idea of the coherence of a person’s life” has to precede even artless narration for such narration to have a subject matter. So he rejects the response proposed by MacIntyre and Rudd that narrative structure and personhood are mutually interdependent phenomena, neither being reducible to the other. Because narratives are perspectival and constructed, incommensurable stories may both be convincing.<sup>73</sup> But a real person only has one life; therefore that life is prior to stories about it.

As a result, “at the level of narrative interpretation of a whole life,” MacIntyre’s account of practical identity is subject to skeptical critique.<sup>74</sup> For MacIntyre thinks that “the unity of an actual life is like the unity of a fictional life.” Call this *the Analogy thesis;* it holds that narrative unity “is found first in life, and is carried over from life to the construction of fiction. But this conception must be wrong,” Williams says, because fictional characters are artifacts; having no future, “fictional characters are not living at all.”<sup>75</sup> Moreover, the “character in fact I am” on MacIntyre’s account is not entirely determined by my social relations or unchosen attachments before I live my life;<sup>76</sup> I am not identical with any fully determinate character until my life is done. Here Williams is rejecting what I have previously called the narrative essentialist thesis that at any time in their life (or even before they live, if we allow possible persons), each person has an individual essence that includes the style and character-traits of the protagonist of a historically unique and complete life-to-death narrative.<sup>77</sup> It might seem that the signature narrativist thesis equates an individual’s identity with a maximally concrete life-history in this sense (though see Chapter 5). Williams concludes that “the idea of a completed, unified, or coherent narration is of no help in leading a life. The idea of living a life as a quest for narrative is baseless.” For, even if a biographer can retroactively fit such a narrative to my life once it is over, my aspiration should hardly be to make it easy for biographers.<sup>78</sup>

While that much is surely right, there are three serious problems with Williams’ critique of MacIntyre. First, unlike Lippitt, Williams slides quickly from the Artifact objection (2) to objections (4) and (6) concerning Mortality, which risks obscuring the central ontological issue in (2). Second, MacIntyre’s account of personal identity is a response to Parfi t that starts from the same insights about the continuity of life from the practical standpoint that informed *Williams’ own* response to Parfi t, as we saw (Chapter 1 §3). MacIntyre’s view is meant to explain that it is because of 

*[p. 51]* ^pg70

the narrative structure of agency that our present projects have a practical priority that is incompatible with utilitarian discounting for changed future preferences. Third and most importantly, Williams treats the maximal priority thesis, Analogy thesis, and narrative essentialist thesis as if they were logically inseparable when they are not. I have twice critiqued narrative essentialism as incompatible with libertarian freedom while defending the view that leeway-liberty is schematized in narrative form.<sup>79</sup> Below I will reject the maximal priority thesis, because our reflections on our lives, including our formation of “self-images” and the ways we interpret relations among episodes in our history, inevitably affect the diachronic weave of meanings-relations with which we always prerefl ectively operate as we go on. Moreover, our sense of identity develops from our birth within the matrix of language. However, there is no obvious way in which these points threaten the Analogy thesis *at all*. But of the three, it is only the Analogy thesis that is crucial for robust narrative conceptions of practical identity.

Against the analogy thesis, Williams is dogmatic. In insisting that the narrative unity of a fictional character cannot be significantly like that of a real person’s practical identity, he presupposes that narrative identities can only be constructs resulting from explicit acts of telling, or artifacts of narration. That is why, in his view, we are unlike fictional characters who can be “a given whole” which is “always already there” in their beginning.<sup>80</sup> Even as a claim about literary characters, this is dubious; in reading a good story that follows the codes of its genres fairly closely, we often have the sense that characters could (in a relevant sense) go either way at key junctures. It is not psychologically necessary for Lear to let Cordelia go; Angel Clare could stay with Tess and devote himself fully to her; Gol-lum could repent his betrayal as he watches Frodo sleeping on the stairs to Cirith Ungol.<sup>81</sup> If we really get *inside* what Tolkien called the “subcreated” world of a good story, it feels very much as if time flows through forking paths, just as it seems in real life. Still, recognizing the fabricated nature of such fictional characters is compatible with the dramatic tension we feel as their story unfolds for us (the audience) being similar to (and even derivative from) the way real practical identities actually develop in time. The Analogy thesis can be true even if narrative essentialism and maximal priority are false.

A residual worry remains. Williams means that in themselves, literary characters are usually finished products: their story is set there in the work, whereas that of a living person is not. This is related to Lamarque’s point that literary characters “come into being only through being described.”<sup>82</sup> Moreover, they are described to a reader or other *living* audience; but does my real life-story only develop because I (and others) witness it? Wollheim argues that living a life is partly like theatre in this respect, but we are more active participants; similarly, Johan Brännmark says that we are “a mix of author and protagonist” in our life-story because we “really can make a difference” in how it goes.<sup>83</sup> If we are an intimate audience of our lives 

*[p. 52]* ^pg71

even when no one else is around, then setting aside Berkeley’s omniscient observer, we are mostly an *implied* audience; events unfold *as if* there were a single inner witness to them, even when that perspective is not most salient to us. It might be objected that a story cannot be its own audience; instead, a consciousness that is constituted prior to narratival structuring would have to be the audience. This is what Zahavi means when he argues that “it takes a self” in the sense of a first-personal “experiential dimension” in order “to experience one’s life as a story.”<sup>84</sup> I have partly conceded this by saying that unity-1 rests on unity-0, but unity-0 is insuffi cient for (existential) personhood. However, I hold that it is only artifact-narratives that strictly require an audience distinct from them. In real lives the separation is weaker: once a narravive structure has begun, our consciousness experiences from the perspective of a practical identity, however rudimentary.

However, even if the concept of an “audience” poses no deep problem for narravives, these questions point back to the underlying issue of objection (8), which does suggest a difference between fictional and real persons: the narrative connections making up the identities of real persons actually accumulate as time flows on, and the resulting dynamism makes the asymmetry between their past and future more radical than in the more “spatialized” time of an imaginary figure whose story is all there in a book. An adequate defense of the Analogy thesis will explain this key disanalogy (see Chapter 5).

#### 3.2 Lippitt, Sartre, and the Logos Fallacy: Four Basic Theses of Narrative Realism

Now the narrativist can offer an error-theory of Williams’ dogmatism. That a real person’s life is like the story of a literary character in ways relevant for psychology and ethics has seemed implausible to many critics because of an underlying assumption about narrative that is rarely questioned. The heart of Williams’ skepticism about “life-narratives” lies in his assumption that anything like a story must be “told”—if not by others, then by the agent herself. Similar points are better articulated by Lippitt, who rightly focuses much of his discussion on this issue. He says that “by taking literary narratives as the default kind of narrative, MacIntyre off ers a misleading guide to understanding human life,” while also forgetting that our existence in time makes it impossible for us at any point to have a “whole life.”<sup>85</sup> To support this claim, Lippitt cites Ricoeur’s plausible view that with their clear beginnings and endings, novels (even of the most elaborate sort) always have a simpler temporal structure than human life as experienced from the inside, where your memory fades off in recalling the far past and anticipation reaches uncertainly towards your death and even to possible effects after that.<sup>86</sup> Lippitt also emphasizes his agreement with Ricoeur and Stephen Mulhall, who say that because I’m not there to grasp my life as a whole when it is over, “the one person whose unified 

*[p. 53]* ^pg72

‘life narrative’ I can’t in principle have access to is my own.”<sup>87</sup> However, it is important to keep the special problem raised by mortality (which I address in Chapter 5) distinct from the more basic issue in objection (2) which arises even for the claim that my life is now an unfinished narrative, or relevantly like a character’s story *in medias res*. Against this claim, Lippitt approvingly cites Mulhall’s Sartrean argument that, because the self as object of reflective consciousness does not coincide with the subject doing the reflecting, any narrative it can “tell” about itself will fail to include this very act of narration.<sup>88</sup>

This particular argument that the self transcends narrative structure can be set aside. I agree with Sartre that (i) the subject of consciousness always transcends the contents of its thetic awareness, and that (ii) this also applies to reflective consciousness (which cannot get the subject doing the reflecting into the focal point of its attention). These arguments were developed from Kant and Fichte to show that certainty of our own existence must involve an immediate prerefl ective awareness of sentience (see the discussion of translucence in Chapter 1 §4). Beyond the extended present, the binding together of past events and future possibilities in lived time goes on constantly at this prethetic level: though they often contribute, reflective acts are not needed for this kind of continuity, and the possibility of self-recognition in reflection is based on prethetic self-awareness.<sup>89</sup> But this important argument only applies to the basic level of consciousness for which unity-0 is the issue. As a result, Sartre’s point (i) should apply to the consciousness of sentient animals that lack reflective capacities, and his point (ii) applies to any reflective self-consciousness, including the simplest subhuman kinds (e.g. that of an intelligent ape or dolphin), and including a conceivable self-conscious but motiveless computer. They imply nothing about the structure of *practical identity*, except that the morally responsible agency constituted by unity-1 and developed in unity-2 will always include prerefl ective levels of awareness that we can never get entirely into reflective view. The illusion that Sartre’s analysis threatens the narrative unity-1 of a practical identity arises from thinking that anything like a narrative structure must be *recounted, composed, or articulated*, as if its entire contents must be the result of reflection. This is largely true of biographies, historical accounts, and fictional stories, which consist primarily in explicit statements (though they also operate by engrossing us in their flow). But it is not true of self-narratives, in which most of the content does not result from rendition, speech, or explicit self-contemplation, and most of what does result from overt interpretation of one’s own acts and motives is absorbed into the nonrefl ective background that continues to frame new experiences.

This error underlying the Sartrean argument and Williams’s critique of the Analogy thesis is so prevalent and influential that it needs its own name: I call it the *logos fallacy* about narrative identity to indicate that it equates narrative structure with a rendition or account. As David Carr explains, “This objection derives . . . from the claim that narrative structure requires 

*[p. 54]* ^pg73

not only a temporal configuration of events but also a narrator and a possible audience.”<sup>90</sup> According to the objector, real life is lived as a stream of experience without

the authoritative, retrospective standpoint of the story-teller. Thus the real difference between “art” and “life” is not organization versus chaos, but rather the absence in life of that point of view which transforms events into a story by telling them. Narrative requires narration . . . not just a recounting of events, but a recounting informed by a kind of superior knowledge.<sup>91</sup>

This assumption that “narrative requires narration” from a knowing perspective is the root of skepticism about narrative identities of “whole lives.” For example, it leads Matias Dalsgaard to equate the narrative ideal with an objective theoretical self-description: “narrativism is fundamentally a theory of self-knowledge.”<sup>92</sup> Similarly, Lippitt quotes Lamarque’s claims that “Few people even attempt to *tell* the complete story of their lives . . .” and that “For most important sequences of events, most people have more than one narrative to *tell .* . . ,”<sup>93</sup> as if it follows from this that they have no unique life-narrative. Indeed Lamarque does think it follows from this that our identity is not made by “the narratives we tell about ourselves;” we can tell many, and their accuracy is only “constrained by factors independent of narrative” (i.e. non-narratival facts about us).<sup>94</sup> Lippitt concludes from Mulhall and Lamarque that “no narrative I could ever *tell* about my ‘whole life’ could ever .  .  . make it a ‘unity’ in any full and robust sense.  .  .  .”<sup>95</sup> Likewise Lippitt draws from Jeffrey Turner the lesson that it is the embellishment and omission involved in artful “telling” that make self-deception almost inevitable in “telling the tale” of our lives. Notice in these passages the repeated emphasis on telling a story, ‘spinning a yarn’ in words or thoughts, or presenting an account to an audience. This implies a dilemma for narrativists: our unedited stream of experience is non-narratival, but our stories about it must select from it and distort it, even if they appear to unify it by drawing explanatory connections between some of its contents.

Sometimes this logos-assumption is not presented as an objection but as a conceptual truth: “narratives” are directly defined as artifacts. For example, Gregory Currie says that “narratives are *intentional-communicative* artifacts” and “the content of a narrative is the story it has to tell.”<sup>96</sup> This means that “narratives are the product of agency” that aims to “represent their stories.”<sup>97</sup> Thus narratives cannot be fully understood without reference to the intentions of their authors, which include being “constructed to tell” a particular story (even if they hope no one will read or see their narrative).<sup>98</sup> As a matter of communicative theory, I agree with Currie against Lamarque’s view that pragmatics of communicative intention can be left aside.<sup>99</sup> “Narratives” in the ordinary sense of artworks are distinguished from other kinds of entities by the intention 

*[p. 55]* ^pg74

involved in their formation to communicate by representing a certain kind of content. However, by distinguishing between narratives as a “representational artifacts” or “corpora” and the “stories” that they represent or tell<sup>100</sup>—a distinction I normally waive—Currie opens a way to question the logos-assumption. Must all “stories” understood as the content of narratives also be artifacts of human invention, or dependent on artifact-narratives?<sup>101</sup> Nothing in Currie’s analysis seems to justify this further claim, especially as he recognizes that much content is implicit and sometimes belies what is said in “unreliable narration.”<sup>102</sup> He recognizes that episode-memories and dreams may have something *like* narrative structure without being intentionally constructed as communicative devices, even though on his account, “no life is a narrative since no life is a representational artifact.”<sup>103</sup> But this is compatible with a person’s life being a “story,” or something quite like a story, before an author has taken it up with an intention to communicate (some part of) it in a narrative. While in Currie’s view this is less mistaken than calling life a narrative, he thinks it leads to errors such a believing that we can adequately represent our lives in narratives we construct.<sup>104</sup> As we will see, such errors can be avoided without insisting that story-structure can arise and exist only on the basis of an artifactual narrative whose content it is.

The main weakness in the logos-assumption should be apparent from Lippitt’s own version of objection (3), which notes that some stories that others tell about us, or that we tell about ourselves (via “narratives” in Currie’s sense) are “closer to the truth” than other versions, even if the former are less interesting or beautiful. Implicit in this objection is that tales about actual persons can have ‘truth-making’ correspondents in their lives. But Lippitt seems to assume that what makes one story more accurate and another more deceptive are simply some discrete facts about actions and events, or physical and psychological states of affairs, that stand in no *irreducibly plotlike* relationship with one another. Currie implies the same when he says that a “biography of Churchill may get the facts wrong and leave out a lot of important stuff ,” but he denies that this constitutes a mistaken version of “the Churchill story.” Instead, he reserves that term for “whatever is so according to the narrative” of the particular biography,<sup>105</sup> as if “the facts” or “stuff ” of Churchill’s actual life stand in no narratival relations to each other, all such relations being merely imposed by a logos, telling, or similar authorial eff ort. But he has not defended that key assumption, and it is precisely what narrative theorists about practical identity should deny. To answer objection (2), we should defend the following version of the Analogy thesis:

(A) the truthmaker of a biographical narrative itself has something much *like* the multidimensional weave of temporally extended meaning-relations that we find in stories recounted, told, or made in human expositions—even though much of the cumulative web of significance 

*[p. 56]* ^pg75

that acts as truthmaker for a biography is not (in its first order) a result of any interpersonal or intrapersonal logos, communicative accounting, or reflective act.

For example, Guignon attributes a version of this thesis to Heidegger, for whom “life has a narrative structure before there is any explicit attempt to put that life into the form of a story.”<sup>106</sup> On this view, Currie is correct to distinguish the story-as-told in a given biographical narrative and the real life of the biography’s subject, but the biographical narrative and the story it tells (if we insist on distinguishing these) are *both about that life*. And the biographical story is truer or more false as it corresponds to the storylike structure of the life. Notice that thesis (A) is compatible with a narrower conception of a person’s real identity as the protagonist of a particular narravive, or a wider conception according to which her identity includes her entire narravive, understood as a developing set of significance-relations. The narrower sense of identity is needed if we think of a person’s narravive informally as including all the other entities with which she has meaningful relations. More formally, however, a narravive does not include these other entities themselves; rather, it is the history of relations of significance that entities have for a conscious volitional agent, and her self includes this entire history (for this distinction, see Chapter 1 §2).<sup>107</sup>

Building on this thesis (A), the narrativist should answer the *Fact-value* objection (4) by distinguishing the level of storylike structure that (A) says is essential to a life being the life of a person from more normatively ideal levels of narrative continunity:

(B) the real structure of diachronic meaning-relations that *constitutes* a unique practical identity includes as *constitutive conditions* a certain level of short- and long-term memory connections (unity-0), and the teleological relations of significance necessary for minimally effective planning agency, i.e. intentions that extend over long periods of time and coordinate multiple capacities (unity-1). But it need not include stronger kinds of narrative unity associated with personal autonomy; the agent whose structure it is must make volitional and cognitive eff orts to achieve these higher kinds of unification.

I’ll refer to (B) at the Level thesis. This realist version of narrativism about practical identity makes clear the strong ontological claim that is implicit in MacIntyre’s approach. But in my view, only by taking on this ontological commitment can narrative theory resolve the main ambiguity in the relation between literature and life, and be coherent and true to our experiences.

The Analogy thesis (A) rejects the logos-assumption by asserting that many elements in a person’s life acquire narratival meaning-relations prior to explicit reflection on them. This is not to claim that they acquire such significance independently of all language or conceptual thought. As John 

*[p. 57]* ^pg76

Christman explains, there is some consensus in empirical psychology that our episode-memories are not just passively replayed or “watched” in our minds. Instead, an episode-memory will “include and exclude elements of the original experience or contain details that you fill in” to make a coherent picture; memories recalled from the participant perspective are (at least partly) active reconstructions.<sup>108</sup> However, this does not make such memories into reflective *logoi*: the operation of linguistic thought, valuation, and selective attention to some details for their salience and relevance to our present circumstances does not constitute a *logos* of the sort we find in an explicit account of some series of events (e.g. your attempt to explain an upsetting experience to a concerned relative) or the intentional articulative eff orts of historians, biographers, and novelists. Hence thesis (A) is supported by Christman’s conclusion that “\[n\]arrative remembering is necessary for one’s sense of self; it verifies, structures, and constitutes one’s self-concept.”<sup>109</sup> I take no position here on whether meaning can be experienced prior to linguistic structuring (or whether there is a language of thought), but the twin linguistic theses that (i) all meaning in mediated by language and that (ii) language only represents and can never directly disclose reality<sup>110</sup> are together not enough to entail the logos thesis that all narratival connections result from reflective composition and explanation.

Thesis (A) also claims that a person’s whole practical identity is constituted by a *primary* narrative that is founded on prerefl ective or first-order meaning-connections, while *secondary* narratives tell about or try to make sense of the primary life-narrative of a person (or group of persons), and fictional narratives resemble secondary narratives but only attempt (to some extent) to explain or interpret facts of life, society, psychology, value, nature, and the transcendent as experienced in primary narratives in general (without representing real individuals). A primary narrative always includes unconscious influences that may never be consciously understood or told in story form. In these terms, we respond to Williams with a third basic narrative realist thesis (C): the basic human capacity to make secondary narratives, including nonfictional or broadly “historical” accounts and fictional stories, is *derived from* our experience in living out primary narratives—both those that constitute individual practical identities, and those that constitute the looser identities of interpersonal groups. This is what Guy Widdershoven calls the “continuity thesis:” “life is the basis for every possible story that can be told about it.”<sup>111</sup> For clarity, I will instead call this the Mimetic thesis: art tries to imitate life as its constitutive aim, and deviations from this basic mimetic telos are intelligible only against the background of the norm. If this were not so, how could we care so much, and feel such genuine emotions, about actions and events in fictional narratives?<sup>112</sup>

Thesis (C) is supported by some empirical studies in comparative anthropology. For example, Allen Tilley argues that “The general order of plot in 

*[p. 58]* ^pg77

\[fictional\] narrative arises from the sequence of major transitions in human life.”<sup>113</sup> He cites evidence showing that five stages of life, with transitions at adolescence, full adulthood, midlife transition, and preparation for death are cultural universals experienced in some form by people in virtually all societies.<sup>114</sup> While narrative realism is not wedded to this specific anthropological thesis, it illustrates why we might think that “the root of plot” in secondary narratives is a “life pattern” based in universal conditions of maturation.<sup>115</sup> Although the causal and inspirational origins of the first-told stories in human history remain shrouded in mystery, the hypothesis that they began as descriptions of our dreams remains a plausible one. But as Tilley suggests, the dreams we remember often have a narrative form that derives from that of waking experience: “The shape of human life may have informed the shape of our dreams before we were quite conscious that our lives had a shape at all.”<sup>116</sup> Similarly, the function of *point of view* in generating narrative structure in artifact-narratives seems clearly derived from the way that unity-0 of consciousness provides a basis for the developing relations of meanings-to-an-agent that we find in a person’s real life. Those who reject thesis (C) owe us some alternative explanation of how literary narration is invented without drawing on prerefl ective experiences of significant temporal connection between events, continuity of action, and senses of transition or development in life.

Yet the difference between art and life remains: as William Ellos says, the tendency of any narrative to move towards a “gathering summary” precludes any “precise and exact mimetic relationship of the narrative to any set of supposed facts.”<sup>117</sup> It is compatible with theses (A), (B), and (C) to hold that (D) even the best literary depictions of a fictional life, or biographical portrayals of an actual life, or historical works about groups of persons, necessarily fall short of the almost infinite detail of significance in actual lived experience, which is always charged with potential resonances between experiences in past, present, and the anticipated future. The layered relations of potential meaning affect each other in a kind of compounding: for example, an act that seemed like a transgression in youth may now appear to be an early sign of a possible career in chemistry. Such interactions of potential significance, some of which register only unconsciously, extend beyond anything that words can fully capture: a complete true biography is impossible. This is why the skeptics are right that actual lives do not usually follow conventional plot-structures, or exactly repeat stock fictional settings, or perfectly fit into interaction paradigms of stock characters in our literary traditions (e.g., no real person is a perfect romantic hero). Thus a narrative realist is not committed to thinking that the higher levels of unity worth wanting beyond unity-1 should be close to forms of literary unity realized by following codes of a genre, such as a comedy in which all loose ends are happily resolved, or an archetypal motif that connects diverse experiences;<sup>118</sup> as Schechtman says, a “meaningful, good” or worthy life need not be structured like a “quest.”<sup>119</sup> We often fall back on familiar narrative schemata for interpreting patterns in our lives 

*[p. 59]* ^pg78

because these motifs, character-types, and plots pass along a hermeneutic wisdom about life-patterns accumulated by storymakers down the ages. But maturity also requires recognizing the limits of such ‘paradigm scripts’ for identities, personality types, and emotional dispositions.

Thus we may call (D) the Incompleteness thesis. It helps to distinguish real lives from secondary narratives about these lives. The weave of actual and potential significances that are alive in normal adult experience, swelled by years of memory and thoughts of the farther future transcend any finite description, but that does not mean that their structure is unlike a narrative composed in some language. Moreover, it is compatible with (D) that an account of a real life that omits many details may be more insightful for highlighting the experiences and choices that were most important for higher levels of narrative coherence beyond unity-1 in that life. Thus (D) does not imply that a biography is better the more details it includes, for the purpose of most types of secondary narrative is not to chronicle as many points as possible in the weave of significance that gave the real person’s life its unity-0 and unity-1.<sup>120</sup> This point is obscured by the selectivity objection (3) because it fails to distinguish primary and secondary narratives.

#### 3.3 Constructivist Theories, Fictionalism, and the Logos Fallacy

Theses (A–D) outline a plausible kind of realism about life-narratives that will support Kierkegaard’s reflections on selfhood and the existential stages, as we’ll see in Chapter 3. But it should be admitted that many philosophers, psychologists, and social theorists who describe personal identity as a narrative phenomenon mean something weaker than theses (A–D) describe. They take different positions on the ontological spectrum short of narrative realism. Some seek to avoid the sort of problems that Williams and Lippitt raise by treating talk of primary “life-narratives” as merely metaphorical, or as a useful heuristic not meant to carry any serious metaphysical baggage. Others think that primary narratives and the practical identities they constitute are more than merely useful “as if” devices for thinking about our lives, but still hold that they are constructed by some kind of “narration” or quasi-artistic practice that amounts to giving a logos to what in itself lacks any meaningful structure, or at least lacks order and cumulative development. And because the idea of an identity as an artwork that we make has been popular since Nietzsche (and may be quite useful in explaining unity-2), many have found it attractive to think that our identity is made up of stories we tell ourselves about our life.<sup>121</sup>

This *simple constructivist* view is certainly the type of narrative theory that has become most popular across the humanities in general and in some branches of psychology today. For example, consider Thomas King’s intentionally enigmatic statement that “The truth about stories is that that’s all we are.”<sup>122</sup> It sounds like there is something deep here, but there is not: there is only ambiguity (which contrasts with the insights in King’s many 

*[p. 60]* ^pg79

poignant stories about people he has known). His view faces a dilemma: either it falls into the serious problems laid out by Lippitt, Williams, and other skeptics, or it is really a backhanded way of accepting the skeptical conclusion that constructivism implies *fi ctionalism*—that our identities are largely illusions, imaginary artifacts of language with little relation to deep psychological facts. This fi ctionalist alternative is the sophisticated version of constructivism that has become most influential in literary theory and the social sciences, so much so that Carr calls it “the standard view:”

Fictional stories are distinct from ‘reality’ or ‘real life’ not just because they are fictional . . . but also because . . . of the way those events are interrelated as fictionally presented. As for any discourse—like history, but also including biography, journalism, or even anecdote—which claims to represent the real: to the extent that it does so in narrative form, that form must alienate it from the reality of events that it relates. Such form is ‘imposed’ on reality. . . . It distorts life. At best it constitutes an escape, a consolation, at worst an opiate, either as self-delusion or . . . imposed from without by some authoritative narrative voice in the interest of manipulation and power.<sup>123</sup>

Donald Polkinghorne argues that such fi ctionalist constructivism is heavily influenced by the linguistic turn and “poststructuralists’ views on the relation between language and extralinguistic reality.”<sup>124</sup> The idea that everything we experience, think, and believe we know “are constructions of the conceptual network of our particular language system” leads to the view that personal identities are also “languaged constructions imposed on our lives by the social context in which we happen to live.”<sup>125</sup> For example, we typologize our identities according to familiar cultural scripts for different types of personalities and stock characters from our media. The most radical version of this approach says that the very idea of individual identity is a social construct; “the self is an artifact of language.”<sup>126</sup> A somewhat less radical version of this position holds that

.  .  . the literary form of narrative, with its linkage of life events and actions as contributors to a final denoument, is an imposed structure and is not descriptive of life as lived. . . . Life consists in a mere sequence of events in which one thing follows after the other; it is not the connected unity that narrative depicts. Narrated life-stories are distortions, not descriptions, of the life as lived.<sup>127</sup>

Polkinghorne and Carr attribute this view to Frank Kermode, Seymour Chatman, Louis Mink, and Hayden White, who all emphasized the artifact nature of *told* stories. As this summary indicates, they also seem to think that the *linguistic theses* (i) and (ii) about meaning entail the logos thesis—an error I pre-empted in the previous section.

*[p. 61]* ^pg80

Both the stronger and weaker versions of fi ctionalist constructivism are forms of error-theory about practical identity: our sense of who we are is embodied in a personal narrative that must always grossly falsify reality just because it is a narrative: there is no such storied identity at the noumenal level. The more radical variant implies that this illusion is nefarious, manipulative, and potentially escapable, whereas the less radical variant thinks that in practical contexts we need selves and must be contented with enlightened fictions. MacIntyre attributes the radical view to Sartre’s *La Nausée*, though his answer to Sartre’s version of the artifact objection (2) is too brief to map out the realist alternative.<sup>128</sup> The less radical version is illustrated by some analytic fi ctionalists who employ narrative concepts. For example, David Velleman summarizes Dennett’s idea of the self as the “center of narrative gravity” as follows:

. . . the autonomous person . . . is an illusion conjured up by the human organism. . . . In Dennett’s metaphor, the self is the nonexistent author of a merely fictional autobiography composed by the human organism, which neither is nor embodies a real self. So understood, the self has the status of an *abstractum*, a fictional object . . . <sup>129</sup>

that it is nevertheless highly useful to believe in. For selves are posited in the “intentional stance” by our brain’s eff orts to make a coherent story about who we are (a process without which we could not function, even if it is rife with rationalization and defense mechanisms). The protagonist of ‘your’ autobiography is a fictional character with your name, but the author is really a brain that belongs to no one (it is not really *your* brain, because there is no “you”).<sup>130</sup> Against the obvious objection that this credits human brains with most of the powers that we would normally attribute to “real selves,” Dennett suggests that an unconscious computer in a robot might be able to write or tell a good novel about its identity and history: then we would mistakenly think it has a self, when in fact this is only a useful way to predict what it will do.

Vellemen critiques this idea that selves are personifications of control centers in the brain and argues instead that when life-narratives are told, they exercise a kind of downward causal influence on the mental sources of the narration as well. Even though these sources are distinct from the protagonist of the story, they have to portray the protagonist as living this story, and thus portray her as conforming her future actions to forward-looking elements in the story as it stands so far (which agrees with Vellemen’s theory of intentions—see §2.1 above).<sup>131</sup>

This ingenious modification alters Dennett’s picture considerably, but as Velleman explains, it still leaves us with a quasifi ctional self. Now “the person’s autobiography” or “self-conception” takes over much of the control-function, as it heavily influences what the brain control centers do hour by hour and over the longer term: given this causal power, the partial story 

*[p. 62]* ^pg81

that is the self also comes to act as coauthor of its subsequent episodes. On this theory, the self is more than Dennett’s epiphenomenal but useful heuristic; rather, the self is constituted by the narrative coherence over time of various (mostly sentient) thoughts about a life, and this mental construct perpetuates itself by bringing it about that brain and body act according to its self-expectations and running themes, thus “maintaining the internal coherence of the story itself.”<sup>132</sup> For my purposes though, whether we see Velleman’s self as a fictional construct or a real process, the key point is that it still makes the narrative self arise from *logoi* or accounts that are mostly thought in words as verbalized or inner speech: a self is a *self-logos* developed and maintained by reflexive narration. But the familiar difference between how a person really is and his self-image is thereby obscured, and it is diffi cult to see how it can adequately be saved on this approach.

So it turns out that many theorists who at first appear to endorse narrative accounts of identity actually agree with Lippitt’s objections, and even push them to dogmatic extremes that he does not.<sup>133</sup> What Lippitt may share with these constructivists is the logos-assumption, which is fostered by the terminology in which the whole debate has developed: as Currie’s case indicated, given that “narrative” is a literary concept, it is natural to assume that a “life-story” must be something told or thought out, something that consists in accounts given to oneself or to others, something that only exists in virtue of communicative acts aimed at an audience, and done from a point of view that is somehow privileged for identity-fabricating purposes. If a narrative identity theorist also neglects to distinguish the different levels of continuity (claimed in thesis B), they will naturally make statements implying the logos-assumption even though they do not intend a fi ctionalist account. For example, in laying out the relation of narrative identity to embodiment (following Merleau-Ponty), Kim Atkins says that selfhood requires “that you are able to give content to your sense of self by giving an account of who you are,” as if a logos is necessary for one’s attributes to be “integrated into—and render intelligible—one’s overall sense of who one is from one’s first-personal perspective.”<sup>134</sup> Yet in fact such a “continuity in my sense of who I am” involving relations of meaning from different perspectives<sup>135</sup> can be largely implicit and unthematized at the level of unity-1. It is only in what Atkins (following Marcel) calls “secondary reflection” when I explicitly question my purposes, beliefs, values, or dispositions and reintegrate myself through endorsing or committing myself to some motives or values over others that a self-logos is clearly necessary. This is part of the movement towards unity-2, as is “self-constancy” in Atkin’s sense, which she rightly compares to Korsgaardian “reflective endorsement.”<sup>136</sup>

In connecting these forms of “constructivism” and fi ctionalism with the logos-assumption, I am not rejecting all senses in which a narrative self might described as “constructed during a life” rather than being innate, as Kupperman puts it.<sup>137</sup> In particular, narrative realists may accept his 

*[p. 63]* ^pg82

suggestion that personality and an awareness of it develop within a unified framework of consciousness as a precondition (see Chapter 1 §3), even if this consciousness never exists by itself without any “characteristics” or as a pure “range of possibilities.”<sup>138</sup> Some minimum of exposure to language and social relationships are also probably preconditions for the development of a self. We can even accept the formula that a self results from a “creative, integrative activity of thought”<sup>139</sup> as long as this is not limited to autobiographical narration and reflective self-portrayal. While these may be crucial in forming the sort of volitional states involved in unity-2, as Kupperman suggests, “a self begins to appear in early childhood” and is “constructed within the protoself \[or field of consciousness\] .  .  . by the formation of habits and attitudes and the emergence of characteristic ways of thinking and behaving.”<sup>140</sup> He rightly emphasizes that, even when intentional acts start to be involved, the process is not initially or for the most part self-conscious, as if the agent “repeatedly thinks of herself or himself as making choices that contribute to constructing a self.”<sup>141</sup> Awareness of such a goal is a later development, if it comes at all. This is compatible with holding that a self “constructed” in this prerefl ective way has something like narrative form, because the Analogy thesis allows us to deny that all narratival relations results from *logoi*, i.e. artful or artless explanatory conceptualization of acts and experiences. However, for simplicity, I will usually reserve the “constructivist” label for theories that at least implicitly endorse the logos-assumption (in accord with common usage).

A similar ambiguity affl icts Korsgaard’s and Schechtman’s use of “self-constitution” as a label for their theories of practical identity. It sounds as if Schechtman is endorsing a logos conception when she introduces her answer to the characterization question: “a person creates his identity by forming an autobiographical narrative—a story of his life.”<sup>142</sup> Her readers will assume that it is through some kind of reflection, even if it is not explicitly formulated in philosophical terms about identity, that “individuals constitute themselves as persons by coming to think of themselves as persisting subjects . . .” and “weave stories of their lives. . . .”<sup>143</sup> Yet it turns out that what she means by someone acknowledging “her personhood” and appropriating “certain actions and experiences as her own”<sup>144</sup> is not an autobiographical explanation or account; it is much closer to what Charles Taylor means by a self-understanding that remains mostly in the background. She allows that “there is no *single* form that the story of a person’s life must take” in their self-conception, but more importantly that this “construction of an identity-constituting autobiographical narrative does not have to be self-conscious.”<sup>145</sup> Otherwise few human beings would be persons, for few have composed an autobiography. Instead, our self-constituting narrative is a mostly unthematized way of being, “an idea of who were are and what kind of story we are living”<sup>146</sup> that is expressed in some explicitly self-interpretative thoughts and statements but that is much larger than their content. This becomes clearer the further we proceed in her account. She 

*[p. 64]* ^pg83

draws from Locke the idea that past events can impact present consciousness affectively even when they are not explicitly recalled. It is mostly by such prerefl ective effects on our “overall sense of self” that “\[o\]ur past give us our conception of who were are,” and anticipation of the future operates in largely the same way.<sup>147</sup> In summary, much

besides the explicit telling of one’s life story might be involved in having a narrative self-conception . . . the telling of an explicit story is, indeed, usually an inappropriate way of expressing one’s self-conception. The sense of one’s life as unfolding according to the logic of a narrative is not just an idea we have; it is an organizing principle of our lives. It is the lens through which we filter our experience and plan for actions, not a way we think about ourselves in reflective hours.  .  .  . To have an autobiographical narrative in the relevant sense is thus to have an implicit understanding of one’s history as unfolding according to the logic of a story. . . . <sup>148</sup>

Here Schechtman firmly rejects the logos-assumption. But then it can be confusing to use the phrase “autobiographical self-conception,” as that may connote some sort of telling or accounting. The idea of a person’s “*implicit* self-narrative .  .  . as the psychological organization from which his experience and actions are actually flowing” then needs further defense, as Schechtman recognizes, for it will be called contradictory by those who think narrative depends on a logos. Schechtman’s explanation depends on her “Articulation constraint,” which requires that a person be able to articulate key parts of her self-conception when called upon, so that “the narrative self-constitution view does not allow a person’s self-narrative to remain entirely subterranean.”<sup>149</sup> I agree with this condition and will return to it at the end of §5 below. But we must first explain how that which gets articulated can already have something like a narrative structure before it is articulated, so that an “*explicit* self-narrative” formed well or poorly can add to the implicit one that first constitutes personal practical identity.

### 4 The Defense of Narrative Realism: Towards a Viable Alternative

#### 4.1 Carr and Ricoeur

This preliminary explanation of the four basic theses, including the level distinction, Analogy thesis, and the distinction between primary and secondary orders of meaning, problematize the logos-assumption (or reveal it as a potential fallacy) and thus begin the defense of narrative realism. But a full defense of this narrative approach to practical identity, on which much recent work on Kierkegaard implicitly relies, requires a more fully worked 

*[p. 65]* ^pg84

out alternative to constructivist theories that can avoid the logos fallacy. This requires looking beyond MacIntyre’s literary analogies for a better way to develop thesis (A) and to define minimal conditions for narrative connections that can exist prior to reflective *logoi*. While Lippitt is right that MacIntyre’s short account does not suffi ce, more fully worked out conceptions of primary narrative offered by Carr and Paul Ricoeur are closer to providing what narrative realists (including Kierkegaardian narrativists) need. In particular, I will explore Polkinghorne’s view that Ricoeur’s later work supports conceptions like Carr’s, Bruner’s, and Olafson’s, which hold that before autobiography, real human lives exhibit the same sort of “synthesis of the heterogeneous” that we find in stories.<sup>150</sup>

Carr began by arguing that historical narratives need not falsify the lives and events they recount because (as thesis A holds) behind all literary products “narrative structure pervades our very experience of time and social existence, independently of our contemplating the past as historians.”<sup>151</sup> In his version of thesis (C), Carr adds that literary narrative “arises out of and is prefigured in certain features of life, action, and communication” that naturally lend themselves to narrative “configuration.”<sup>152</sup> Following Husserl, he argues that we have extended experiences whose parts correspond to the parts of temporally extended processes in the world. Stream of consciousness is thus “lived as a complex of configurations whose phases figure as parts within larger wholes.”<sup>153</sup> Like Rudd, MacIntyre, and Moya, Carr argues that familiar actions like serving a tennis ball are not composed of atomic parts or “basic actions” but consist rather in a single process that is both psychological and physiological with “interdependent” phases.<sup>154</sup> In passive experience and in activities, “the temporal span is configured into events, in the one case, and actions, in the other,” which are in turn “closed” or distinguished from the surroundings or background.<sup>155</sup> Thus something like the distinction between a “setting” and a plot unfolding across different scenes is already involved in prerefl ective lived time. Actions and events are experienced as temporal gestalts with beginnings, middles, and ends.<sup>156</sup> Given its means-ends structure, the completion of an action requiring eff ort is also a practical closure or solution to a preexisting problem. These features are all found in even short-term contexts.<sup>157</sup> This analysis clearly supports Rudd’s arguments for what I called constitutive narrative unity-1.

Like other critics, Carr thought that Ricoeur’s view was closer to White’s and Mink’s, although Ricoeur recognized that the “world of action” has a quasi-narratival form that lends itself to “narrative configuration.”<sup>158</sup> As Carr explains, according to Ricoeur,

Life is not alien to narrative structure, nor does it need to borrow from literature to achieve such structure. It has its own structure, which is “not reducible to simple discordance,” not simply chaotic, but is a kind of “inchoate narrativity,” \[or . . .\] “prenarrative structure.”<sup>159</sup>

*[p. 66]* ^pg85

On this view, life in the practical standpoint is already “schematized” (in Kant’s sense) in a way that makes it narratable, or that lends itself to narration, allowing us to represent it fairly using concepts such as plot, setting, goal, means, interaction, accident, convergence, resemblance, and so on that describe aspects of a coherent story. But Carr fears that Ricoeur’s way of resolving the basic contradiction between lived time and cosmic time in a “poetics” of narrative time conceives the latter as redescribing an extralinguistic reality that is inherently more discordant: “\[I\]n the end it seems that the gap between narrative and life is left open” by Ricoeur.<sup>160</sup> This is not implausible; though Carr’s essay preceded *Oneself as Another*, even here one finds ambiguous passages. For example, in one footnote, Ricoeur says that “self-understanding” is an “interpretation” which borrows from history and fiction, “making a life-story a fictional history or . . . a historical fiction . . .”<sup>161</sup> Similarly, Zahavi reads Ricoeur as saying that a self-narrative is not “a way of gaining insight into . . . an already-existing self. On the contrary, the self is first constructed in and through the narration.”<sup>162</sup>

Carr himself admits that there are important differences between lived experience and narratives that imitate it while leaving out much of the detail in real life. But the difference is not as sharp as is implied by critics who think that there is *no* selection in lived experience just because there is no narrator or logos-giver in the stream:

Narratives do select; and life is what the select from. But it hardly follows that in life, no selection takes place. Our very capacity for attention, and for following through more or less long-term and complex endeavors, is our capacity for selection. Extraneous details are not left out, but they are pushed into the background, saved for later, ranked in importance. And whose narrative voice is accomplishing all this? None but our own, of course. In planning our days and lives we are composing the stories or the dramas we will act out and which will determine the focus of our attention and our endeavors. . . . <sup>163</sup>

In agreement with contemporary action theory, Carr’s reference to planning suggests that it is through intentions that the stream of experience is narratively schematized. He adds that this is a kind of “storytelling” in which we explain our acts to ourselves and to others, so his formulation still risks encouraging a logos-conception of primary narrative. But his key insight is that patterns of salience, or immediate sensitivity to greater and lesser relevance of details in our current experience permeate our planning agency (just as they operate in memory); certain features of events and certain relations between them and past experiences or future possibilities stand out as relevant to our intentions. As we saw, episode-memory operates this way too, actively focusing on only some features of “the original experience” and hypothesizing in missing links.<sup>164</sup> If we did not prerefl ectively gestalt details and meaning-relations this way, then human attention could never 

*[p. 67]* ^pg86

avoid the so-called “framing problem” for artificial intelligence. This suggestion is a generalized version of Ronald de Sousa’s account of occurrent emotion as a prethetic framing in which we perceive our current situation as similar to paradigms for various affects,<sup>165</sup> and Peter Goldie’s related argument that “an emotion is structured in that it constitutes part of a narrative—roughly, an unfolding sequences of actions and events, thoughts and feelings—in which the emotion is embedded.”<sup>166</sup> The larger narrative is one in which changes in emotional episodes, other affective states, intentions, plans, and social relationships all “interweave.” In summary, emotions are intelligible in the same holistic way as intentional actions (see §2.1 above). This kind of holism does not require reflective self-inquiry, let alone that we perceive patterns in our development or personality as similar to paradigm plot-structures, character-types, and models of interaction familiar from stories and cultural scripts. Though this kind of script-fitting self-interpretation often errs, the vital point is that our narrative structure is not all imposed *ex post facto* by reflection on atomic events: “Narrative coherence does not impose itself on an incoherent, merely sequential existence, but is drawn from life.”<sup>167</sup>

At least some points in Ricoeur’s account suggest much the same relationship. Ricoeur is read in different ways, and I make no pretense of doing justice here to his monumental work, *Time and Narrative*, which is probably the most extensive treatment of relations between physical time, lived time, and history. But it suffi ces for my purposes to note places where he comes close to the basic theses of narrative realism. Early in the first volume, after analyzing Augustine’s paradoxes of time and Aristotle on “emplotment” as a way of organizing events, Ricoeur states his hypothesis that “between the activity of narrating a story and the temporal character of human experience there exists a correlation that is not merely accidental. . . . Time becomes human to the extent that it is articulated through a narrative mode.”<sup>168</sup> While narrating is still understood here as a kind of “refi guration” by a logos involving plot, there is a *fit* between the resulting story and the original temporal experiences it recounts. While pure time-lapse is not a narratival succession in itself, in human experience it has a form that “prefigure\[s\]” narrative “configuration”: “the composition of the plot is grounded in a preunderstanding of the world of action, its meaningful structures, its symbolic resources, and its temporal character.”<sup>169</sup>

Thus Ricoeur believes that Aristotle was on the right track in thinking that “whatever the mind constructs through its narrative activity . . . it finds succession in things before taking it up again in itself.”<sup>170</sup> Although this did not resolve the main aporia of time, it anticipated Dilthey’s insight that the “connectedness of life” involves an “ordered sequence of experiences” that is not (originally) an artifact of reflective recounting. What Heidegger calls the “self-constancy” (Selbständingkeit) of Dasein is a protohistorical endurance through time as “stretching along” through which the past and future are connected in the present<sup>171</sup>—and this occurs before any historical texts 

*[p. 68]* ^pg87

are written or historical stories are told. It is for this reason that “historical time” can mediate between the lived time of subjectivity and objective or cosmic time.<sup>172</sup> Historical explanation cannot be done accurately in terms of lawlike invariants because that is not how the agents in their time (immediately and then reflectively) experienced the significance of past developments, present events, and future possibilities in acting out their part in the sequence that a historical account aims to explain.<sup>173</sup> There would be little difference between fiction and history if there were not “a certain correspondence between our \[historical\] narrative and what really happened.” The correspondence, Ricoeur suggests, is one of *analogy*. This means that “what really happened” was itself *like* a narrative in form, though the historical account is a “reconstruction” of the actual “course of events.” This actual course prefigures the best narrative that can be told about it, making it possible to “do justice” to it, or to “render its due” to the past, as Ricoeur puts it.<sup>174</sup> Specifically, the relation can be understood in terms of the four basic tropes of classical rhetoric, especially metaphor: “ . . . between a narrative and a course of events, there is not a relation of reproduction, reduplication, or equivalence, but a metaphorical relation.”<sup>175</sup>

This may be the kind of statement that worries Carr, but I think Ricoeur means that what analytic philosophers writing about action and responsibility call the “actual sequence” of events is itself quasi-narratival before it is reflectively understood in any logos; thus it *lends* itself to such a logos. While a kind of “arbitrariness” will infect any linguistic redescription of actual events, feelings, and actions, and “the historian’s language” can never make this actual sequence “entirely transparent,” these imperfect refi gurings find “incitations” and limits in “the facts *as* they *really* happened.”<sup>176</sup> In short, reality as experienced from the practical standpoint is a “world” in Heidegger’s sense—temporally extended network of meanings-relations or understandings that condition our experiences prior to reflection, though they may be partially interpreted and thus refi gured in reflective narratives. Ricoeur’s complex account of the “interweaving of history and fiction” similarly implies that the contributions of imaginative “seeing-as” in writing history can reveal ethical truth (such as the horror of Auschwitz)<sup>177</sup> rather than functioning only rhetorically because the “real” past is not just a series of physical events but a developing web of actions and sufferings.

The impression that Ricoeur instead holds a logos-conception of narrative identity comes most strongly from points where he associates it specifically with ethical “self-constancy” or “soi-même” (“*ipse*”) which allows change within endurance

 . . . to the extent that its identity rests on a temporal structure that conforms to the model of dynamic identity arising from the poetic composition of a narrative text. The self characterized by self-sameness may then be said to be refi gured by the reflective application of such narrative configurations. . . . The subject then appears both as reader and 

*[p. 69]* ^pg88

writer of its own life, as Proust would have it. As literary analysis of autobiography confirms, the story of a life continues to be refi gured by all the truthful *or fictive* stories a subject tells about himself or herself. This refi guration makes this life itself a cloth woven of stories told.<sup>178</sup>

This is why Widdershoven sees Ricoeur as holding that the “prenarrative structure” of life is “changed into a narrative structure by the plot of the story told about it,” as if the former did not direct the latter.<sup>179</sup> This leads to a worrying endorsement of psychoanalysis as a process in which one’s primary life-narrative is seen as something we can fix or “repair” by telling new stories.<sup>180</sup> There is certainly potential for confusion here, because Ricoeur later describes narrative identity as mediating *between* self-constancy and “character” rather than equating it with the former (see Chapter 1 §5). Moreover, while he is plausible in endorsing Heidegger’s distinction between “the permanence of substance” and “self-subsistence”—which is similar to our distinction between theoretical and practical identity—he also uses “character” in an unusual sense that approximates to a substantial form. This leads to statements such as “\[t\]he continuity of character is one thing, the constancy of friendship another,”<sup>181</sup> which are facially counterintuitive, as we usually think of the loyalty or dispositions to keep promises involved in friendship as aspects of “character.”

These worries aside, however, when Ricoeur says that a life-story is continually refi gured, he may simply mean that our articulations of self-interpretations (including their errors, and even intentional misrepresentations of ourselves) become part of our life-narrative; an ideally complete biography would have to take their occurrences and effects into account. As I will detail in the next section, this should be understood as a point about *second-order* nodes in a life-narrative that are added by autobiographical reflection, partial adoption of others’ portrayals of us by explicit acceptance or tacit self-imagery, and acts of “identification” with various “values, norms, ideals, models, and heroes. . . .”<sup>182</sup> It is because he thinks of “self-constancy” or active faithfulness as involving this kind of identification, which is crucial for unity-2, that Ricoeur portrays it as involving a logos, as if the maintenance of commitment includes a reflective self-conception (even if it does not require reflection on morality). So it is the reflexive modifications of prethetic meanings which are especially required for unity-2 that Ricoeur has in mind when he writes that, given “the elusive character of real life . . . we need the help of fiction to organize life retrospectively” using plot-motifs and figures found in fictional and historical stories:<sup>183</sup> the point is that at the second-order, self-interpretation employs narrative schemata from literary sources.

Certainly in this sense, our narrative *logoi* both “express and shape” our practical character, and thus “narrative identity is the poetic resolution of the hermeneutic circle.”<sup>184</sup> This point is consistent with Ricoeur’s basic insight that the first-order level of experience that is reflexively taken up by 

*[p. 70]* ^pg89

its subject is already in a quasi-narratival form. “Events” mean something in our experience before explicit interpretative activity because they are already “configured” in significance-relations of several kinds with other events.<sup>185</sup> Thus it is crucial to isolate the protonarrativity of prerefl ective experi-

ence and activity (often involving language, but not a logos) before considering the many types of reflexive modification it undergoes (see §5 below). As Ricoeur said in a later roundtable exchange, human lived experience is more than “a biological phenomenon” because the interests that constitute the practical perspective of agency give rise to a “phronetic understanding” of life as a field of action and passion extending over time. “If indeed action can be recounted, this is because it is already articulated in signs, rules, and norms” even in our prerefl ective experience of it.<sup>186</sup> Just as action is a “quasi-text” that is already readable in narrative form, there is a general

. . . *prenarrative quality of human experience*. It is due to this that we are justified in speaking of life as a story in its nascent state, and so of life as an *activity and a passion in search of a narrative. . . .* It is not by chance or by mistake that we commonly speak of the stories that happen to us, or of stories in which we are caught up. . . . \[We must\] grant to experience as such a virtual narrativity which stems, not from the projection of literature onto life, but which constitutes the genuine demand for narrative.<sup>187</sup>

This is clearly a version of the Analogy thesis (A). In defense of it, Ricoeur gives several examples in which we want to say that a “chain of episodes” is like a potential story that “has not been told.” This is why a story can tell the truth about a person: “the story answers to the man.” While our existence and identities are lived stories, “narrating is a secondary process grafted on our ‘being-entangled-in-stories.’”<sup>188</sup> This is the key to narrative realism: the reflexive overlays and frequent regestaltings worked through various kinds of storytelling and explanation flow from and express a narrative-like structure that *already* exists before we represent it to self and others in a logos. Rudd puts this point in a pithy capsule form in response to Lamarque: “I don’t have to be constantly reciting the story of my life to date to have an essentially narrative conception of who I am as a temporal being. The existence of that self-conception is what gives meaning to what I am doing now.” Instead, it is an implicit story (satisfying a threshold level of unity-1) which is “the basis for my capacity to formulate explicit narratives about myself when I do so.”<sup>189</sup>

#### 4.2 Narravive, ‘Rosebud’ Elements, and Six Conditions of Narratival Connection

Whether or not Ricoeur is a full ally of narrative realism, it is clear that his account of narrative identity helps to avoid the logos fallacy: it indicates 

*[p. 71]* ^pg90

how practical identity can be understood in narrative terms without accepting strong constructivist or fi ctionalist models. I will now offer five further modifications or elaborations to strengthen this defense of narrative realism.

The first is a terminological suggestion in aid of a conceptual point. From Carr’s perspective it would surely help if we could clarify Ricoeur’s idea that a human life is an “inchoate narrative” with a “prenarrative” norm,<sup>190</sup> involving “quasi-plot, quasi-character\[s\], and quasi-event\[s\]” before it is told.<sup>191</sup> As we have seen, the problem is that “narrative” literally refers to an artifact in writing, film, or speech. One of the most comprehensive online glossaries of literary terms defines narration as “the act of telling a sequence of events, often in chronological order” and narrative as “the story or account itself.”<sup>192</sup> As Currie says, we could extend the term to complex episode-memories and dream sequences, but then we would need some other term to mark off “histories, biographies,” novels, and similar communicative works.<sup>193</sup> Instead, to avoid confusion, I suggest another term to replace Ricoeur’s on the side of living experience and activity: the combination “narra-vive” connotes a story that lives, the development of which is, for the most part, prior to its telling or rational interpretation. The term “narravive” signals that we are not referring to a told or written narrative in the familiar sense, given that the life of a responsible agent is not (in its primary level) an artistic artifact. But neither is its relation to literary narrative merely metaphorical: a narravive is *analogous* to a narrative, and though biography will always elide much of the detail of real life, a story can partly capture the multiply-layered dynamic of an infinitely complex actual narravive. Unlike written or spoken narratives, narravive is an “existentiale” in Heidegger’s sense—not a thing (substance) or a property that we “have” or possess, nor a category of such properties, but rather a fundamental way that we are—it is an ontologically basic mode of personal existence, definitive of the type of being who forms a practical identity in time.

In these terms, the narrative realist holds as part of thesis (A) that there is exactly *one basic narravive* for each practical identity at the constitutive level (unity-1), though higher levels of this identity (and unity-2 if achieved) alter within the narravive as it is lived.<sup>194</sup> Notice again that this holds whether we think of a person’s identity as *part* of her narravive (in its wide, informal sense as including figures and events that could exist without her)—namely, the part threaded along the protagonist’s point of view—or we think of her identity as the whole of her narravive (in its strict, narrower sense). Either way, there is a one-to-one relation between basic narravives and personal identities; “identities” distinguished within a single basic narravive are aspects of one personal identity, and identities without a single basic narravive are sub- or transpersonal. Groups, such as a family, a tribe, or a corporation may have narravives, but they are nonbasic, because they are built up out of the basic narravives that each constitute a unique personal identity. In this work I set aside the complex topic of group identities, so the “basic” qualifier is implicit wherever I refer to narravives, 

*[p. 72]* ^pg91

unless otherwise marked. But note that narrative realism has an easier time than weaker constructionist theories in explaining why there is a clear distinction between personal and group identities: the former depend on the unity-0 of a single consciousness as a thread along which the unity-1 of a narravive is built up.

The person’s narravive includes such existential changes and foregone possibilities of such changes. Unlike a narrative, a living narravive is incomplete and still growing, and modally extended into nonactual but possible continuations into the future, which are closed only at the agent’s death. Narravive is the measure of truth or accuracy in a biographical or historical narrative about the person either during her life or after her death. To take one of MacIntyre’s examples, there are several accounts of St. Thomas Beckett’s life, but some of them are closer to the truth—the narravive that was his true identity—though presumably only an omniscient being could know the full contents of such a narravive. And such narravives are essentially social; Beckett’s could not have taken the course it did without interlocking with the narravives of many others, such as King Henry.

My second addition to Ricoeur’s model is an argument that we already intuitively recognize connections in human life as narratival rather than merely causal in some cases where the meaning of a past event and the way it influences later relationships and informs action is never thematized by the agent or understood by others who interact with her. Even in fiction, such subconscious narratival linkage is sometimes noted and even shown as a distinctive feature of human life (at one remove from reality, of course). In Orson Wells’ famous film, *Citizen Kane*, we are shown three different interpretations of Kane’s life from his youth through his career as a media millionaire to his failed marriages and lonely old age—each told from the perspective of someone who knew him well. Yet in all, some piece of the puzzle is felt to be missing—a key to understanding ‘who he really was,’ a crucial aspect of his practical identity. The missing piece is something that he seems to have forgotten (or at most dimly sensed) until his end, when he looks at the miniature winter landscape in the glass bulb, remembers, and says “rosebud” with his dying breath.<sup>195</sup> I will not spoil the mystery; it suffi ces to say that in the film’s final scene, we see (from an omniscient viewpoint) what “rosebud” was and we are able to recognize what it meant in Kane’s life—or the many ways it may have conditioned the meanings he found in various pursuits, ambitions, and relationships with others. On my (fairly standard) interpretation, this key allows us to grasp a part of Kane’s *narravive* that is missing from all the *narratives told* about him in the film, all the attempts made to explain his identity. When seen with this new information, his life-story is regestalted before us; we can now recognize it as more of a tragedy.<sup>196</sup> Of course, this is not an actual narravive; the concept as I have defined it cannot be applied literally to a fictional character such as Kane. But the gap that this classic film brings to light applies to real people: all their narravives have secrets—“rosebuds”—that 

*[p. 73]* ^pg92

others never know, events and acts that deeply affect their practical identities, coloring the significance to them of many objects, words, and deeds in later episodes, in ways even they never explicitly articulate to themselves.<sup>197</sup> This fact, which *Citizen Kane* teaches, implies that the logos-assumption is false. For the significance of “rosebud” elements could only be explained by their part in something like a story—they are not bare facts, as described in a natural science. Yet by definition, they are not included in any narrative that the self or others has reflectively articulated. Thus they can only be parts of his narravive.

My third addition concerns the other hard problem for narrativists, namely the *triviality* objection (1), which asks what distinguishes narrative sequences from other temporally ordered sequences of events. It is obvious that there must be such a distinction, because the sequence “moment 1, then moment 2, then moment 3 . . .” is clearly neither a narrative nor a narravive. Likewise for a sequence consisting merely of two atoms moving between two positions relative to each other; it would be too much of a stretch to call this a “dance.” Defending the concept of narravive thus requires some deeper explanation of what narratival connection is. My tentative answer will draw together and develop suggestions introduced in prior sections.

It helps to begin with John Christman’s forceful statement of this question in his recent work about autonomy and social conceptions of selves. He thinks of the narrative approach as “conceiving selves as nothing but a sequence of experiences, acts, traits and judgments that have. . . . a narrative form.”<sup>198</sup> My formulation has been more modest in two respects, claiming only that a living analog of narrative form is essential to practical identity, and not that it is a suffi cient condition for it (because there may be other conditions of agency not explained by narrativity). Following Schechtman and the psychologists Kenneth and Mary Gergen, Christman also adopts a logos-version of the narrative thesis as holding that “a subject reflecting on memories and experiences” is a “single, unique personality” only if this reflection can construct a unified story from these elements.<sup>199</sup> But the central problem, in his view, is that it is hard to identify conditions for narrativity that are neither “implausible or otiose” as requirements for selfhood.<sup>200</sup>

Christman focuses on three types of conditions that some allege to constitute narrativity. First, “causal narrative sequence” proposals conceive plot as a series in which earlier events make later ones more probable or necessary. But we are now used to story-forms in which accidental events intrude and different causal sequences progress separately. Similarly, “many sequences of a person’s life proceed quite independently of one another,” at least in terms of causal ordering—their “being experienced by the same physical subject” is not a narrative link <sup>201</sup>(and I have agreed in characterizing unity-0 as subnarratival). In response, we might urge that even nontraditional novels with diff erent lines of development or subplots usually tie 

*[p. 74]* ^pg93

them together or causally interrelate them at some point. And to the extent that they remain causally unrelated, that is because the distinct plot-lines involve separate characters, distinct persons. In her account of narrative form, Schechtman refers to Jerome Bruner’s thesis that the “incidents and episodes in a narrative take their meaning from the broader context of the story in which they occur. . . . Their meaning is given by their place in the overall configuration of sequence as a whole—its plot or *fabula*.”<sup>202</sup> I refer to this as the *plot-connection* condition. However, Christman could rejoin that a story with parallel nonintersecting plot-lines can be unified by relations of similarity, contrast, foreshadowing, anagogical anticipation, or other forms of symbolic relation instead. The analogous (valid) point for a human life is that actions and events without prior causal connections can seem significantly related for us due to similarity or contrast in pattern, the way one prefigures the other, and many other forms of association. Thus at least effi cient-causal relations may not be necessary for narrative linkage (and we have already seen that they are not suffi cient)—though usually causally unconnected elements are related in part through other elements that *are* causally connected.

Teleological connections are more promising, as our prior examples from action theory have illustrated. Following Thomas Leitch, Christman objects that teleological linkage is not a necessary condition for narrative relation, because there are nonteleological stories without any summative point or ending. Moreover, in real lives as well, “people generally undertake entirely *separable* projects and goals, which, though each has an internal organizing aim, fail to interconnect in a grand scheme.”<sup>203</sup>Thus a requirement of *teleological connection* (as I’ll call it) through the same final end is too strong, in his view. He also worries that teleological conditions are often part of normative conditions that at best only apply to “flourishing lives,” rather than to all lives that recognizably include selves. Here three points are needed in response. First, the teleological sense that intention gives to behavior depends on prior networks of significance that establish the social and natural *setting* for action. As Guignon puts it, “action takes place against a backdrop of standards and conventions accepted by a social group:” thus explanation of action “always presupposes a background understanding of what things mean to agents” and of agency itself as understood in “a particular life world.”<sup>204</sup> Thus the teleological meanings in a particular line of action are not independent of settings that makes the agent’s other plans intelligible.

Second, Christman does not seem to argue that teleological connections are insuffi cient for narrative sequence (though it is only the kind of teleology involved in intention and the backgrounds they require that probably is suffi cient for narrativity).<sup>205</sup> Instead, he denies that they are a necessary condition. There are certainly teleological conditions that plausibly apply only to higher levels of narrative unity beyond unity-1, and I will consider a version of Christman’s separable-projects point as a potential objection 

*[p. 75]* ^pg94

to requirements of “wholeheartedness” in the next chapter. At the level of unity-1 in agency, however, there is a planning connection between distinct pursuits with unrelated final ends because one has to allot time and resources to each. Call this the *effective-agency* condition. If this seems too little to constitute a narrative linkage, consider how making a schedule for a period of time, or marking up a blank calendar, or just making a to-do list help to structure our activities, fitting them into larger rhythms of eating, sleeping, or bathing. It is not just that we apply the hypothetical imperative in dividing our time and our energies; these divisions shape our experience of time, giving urgency to finishing one sort of activity to make room for the next before night falls. There is also coordination with others in many activities.

Thus, having distinct final ends pursued in different settings does not thoroughly sunder even our most thematically disconnected activities, considered from the perspective of the second-order telos of effective agency in general (a regulative goal that we can and do fail to realize, but can only fail to *intend* when beset with perversity or depression that, if it persists, leads to loss of competence<sup>206</sup>). Schechtman makes a similar point when she says that we must accept as an ideal aspiration the coherence or intelligibility that defines narrative form: “To be a person one must realize that one’s beliefs, desires, values, and traits are supposed to support one another,” and thus one must recognize the force of questions about apparent practical contradictions in our actions.<sup>207</sup> Third, beyond minimal consistency in division of our attention, our diverse pursuits are more effectively engaged and fulfilling if they also fit together in several other ways, such as providing pleasant contrast; for example, consider how Christman’s cases of teaching philosophy and doing gardening likely complement one another by affording quite different challenges and engaging different skills, with each offering relief from the more tedious aspects of the other. Other types of “fit” include different activities being mutually supporting, facilitating comparisons and recognition of analogical relations leading to insights carried from one domain into another, and keeping our framework of values balanced rather than one-sided. Call these *complementarity* relations. While “fit” between diverse lines of means-to-ends must be further elaborated to answer objection (5), this is enough to suggest how, at the constitutive level of agency, teleological connections among intentional actions of the same agent tend to form a narratival structure, even if narrative sequence can also be formed in other ways.

Last, Christman considers “thematic unity” as a flexible relation in which elements are ordered so as to be “suggestive of a general idea, value, or moral.” This “hermeneutic” sense of narrative connection is the most flexible, and provides a way to explain why in narrative orders, “the ordering of described events matters crucially to the meaning of any of those events.”<sup>208</sup> Call this the *temporal-ordering condition*; I suggest that it is necessary but not suffi cient for narrativity, as the dependence of parts on a 

*[p. 76]* ^pg95

diachronic whole for their significance is found in many kinds of patterns. What Karen Jones calls “trajectory-dependent properties” all have this feature, as by definition they depend on “trajectories” that “necessarily unfold over time rather than being wholly present at a time:” such properties have “temporally extended truthmakers” and their ascription to something at any given time “depends on what happens else*when. . . .”*<sup>209</sup> Many trajectories, such as the process of “building . . . a house” or “being on a journey” have a narrative structure, but not all do. Thus, if lines of intentional activity constitute narrative sequences, this seems to be due to teleological connection rather than merely to their being trajectories. Similarly, the closely related concepts of *progression* or *development* are perhaps essential to anything that could be nontrivially called a narrative sequence, but they are not enough by themselves for narrativity. For a smart computer might meaningfully be said to progress as it learns to play chess better; a crystal might grow in a laboratory; and a set of school test scores might improve over time (we might ask why, and expect a storylike explanation, but the improvement might be accidental). Thus Christman considers thematic unity among the elements in the sequence to be the decisive feature. He also considers Velleman’s suggestion that narrative development “corresponds to the natural ebb and flow of emotional expectations, tension, and resolution, for which we are partly hardwired; thus narratives build towards an “ending.”<sup>210</sup> But he responds that “it is not clear that lives can be adequately defined as a sequence leading towards an ending;” and many lives are not thematically unified, if this means that “that there must be some *single* idea that provides the lens through which the experiences of a life can be understood.” For many people are still trying to decide on goals, merely coping day to day, or responding to random factors such as accidents that make no sense in themselves, but only as we adjust to them.<sup>211</sup>

While these points, which are closely related to the separate projects argument, are correct, Christman’s demand that elements express a single idea sets the bar too high for “thematic unity.” He implies that a weaker sort of thematic unity “achieved whenever the interpreting subject can make minimal sense of her experiences, where ‘sense’ is not specified in advance,” makes the concept too weak for narrativity. In his view, though people need the ability to understand stories and often think in narrative form, the reason narrative identity claims seem plausible is really that “What is truly necessary for a unified life . . . is the capacity for reflection on events (one’s own and those of others) in a spirit that attempts to render the events coherent within the categories of meaning available to the subject”—categories that as socially shared.<sup>212</sup> But, apparently as an alternative, he suggests in another work that we can simply *reduce* narrativity to such reflective intelligibility. Because we cannot get from “the form of a traditional story” any plausible way to “separate stories from nonstories,” he defines “narratives” simply as “those arrangements of events that result from the active project of (socially mediated) interpretation that makes 

*[p. 77]* ^pg96

the event sequent coherent”—a minimal definition in terms of a possible logos.<sup>213</sup> This is consistent with his approval of Leitch’s claim that “the essence of stories are their ‘tellability,’ the degree to which they will make sense to a certain kind of audience.”<sup>214</sup> It is better in my view to say that the *reflective intelligibility* condition is too minimal to entail narrative unity; the coherence it defines may be less than narratival. My prior arguments against the logos-assumption also imply that the ability to give a reflective account also cannot replace more robust narratival conditions for the holistic interdependence in the felt meaning of items composing prerefl ective or *untold* human experience (call this the *holism condition*).<sup>215</sup> Nor is Christman’s notion of reflective intelligibility suffi cient for the teleological relations we found in extended plans for multiple lines of intentional action, which do provide a key part of the narratival structure of lives.

Moreover, there is a moderate conception of thematic unity between Christman’s strong and weak alternatives. In Northrop Frye’s famous analysis, drawing on Aristotle, “theme” is indeed closely associated with “*dianoia* or thought,” a leading idea that could be illustrated by separated characters pursuing different final ends related by a shared value, while mythos or plot is related to a single line of action.<sup>216</sup> But between these lies the unity of *ethos*,<sup>217</sup> a similarity in mode of action or a relation of ideas illustrated in different acts, a similarity in significance across multiple settings of action (partly due to the ethos of the other characters who operate in them). A theme as the unity of an ethos in this sense is a distinctive way of being (thinking, feeling, acting etc.) seen in motifs that do not reduce to a single idea, single didactic point, or single plot-line, but that is more than mere logical consistency or holistic coherence, because it is revealed in patterns repeated across different contexts in life and thus evokes a sense of common tone or manner of comportment. C.S. Lewis describes this wider sense of “theme” well in his account of how the description of agents and setting work together to evoke a whole way of being, which is often a spiritual insight for which poetry strives. In his view, the primary condition of good stories is not exciting adventures: “the plot, as we call it—is only the net whereby we catch something else,” namely the “theme.”<sup>218</sup> And the fact that a story’s plot never perfectly captures a theme in the sense of an ethos “constitutes .  .  . its chief resemblance to life.”<sup>219</sup> This idea may be easier to understand in terms of commonalities among characters; for example, consider the way that hobbits exhibit one *ethos* and high elves quite another in Tolkien’s *Lord of the Rings*. But an individual life also has a distinctive ethos, for better or worse (Kupperman’s notion of “character” as a “how” that endures across changes in higher-order volitions is closely related to this—see Chapter 1 §5). Elements in a life are thematically related when they express a continuing *ethos* for the agent experiencing them. Kierkegaard’s “existence-spheres” or existential stages are types of ethos in this sense: an individual’s ethos is a token of these types, depending on the basic stance of their will towards varied aspects of life and an associated “life 

*[p. 78]* ^pg97

view,” which controls the overall thematic shape of their life-experience at the level of unity-1.

This idea of an ethos as an embracing practical gestalt, or perhaps a ‘paradigm,’ will help us assemble the other conditions introduced above into a viable answer to the triviality problem. We saw with Bratman that plans of action require present psychic states to *refer* to earlier and later psychic states, not merely to continue them. As noted (Chapter 2 §2.2), such a chain of reference-relations over time is a good candidate for narrativity, though it may be more than is required. We also briefly considered Rudd’s response to Schechtman that a self-narrative is made up of a hierarchy of expression-relations in which particular experiences and actions express more general attitudes, emotions, or character-traits, which in turn all express a continuing self. I suggest that what the interrelated components of a life featuring at least unity-1 all directly or indirectly express is a shared *ethos*, way of relating to settings, overall orientation to life, or implicit point of view from which the presence or absence of meaning in anything else (even in accidents that interrupt our plans) is apprehended. Although expression is broader than explicit reference and similar communicative acts, because it includes *manifestations* of an X that do not refer to that X, it is also a stronger relation than mere similarity or even allusion, and this is why a sequence of experiences and events over time related by the *ethos-expression condition* looks distinctively narratival. When our life expresses an ethos, we experience ourselves at least implicitly as coauthors (in so far as we are agents) and as recipients of meanings that we do not control (passivity).

Christman would surely ask whether this ethos or middle type of thematic unity can change over time, as the idea of paradigm-shift from an “aesthetic” to an “ethical” and ultimately a “religious” frame of reference in Kierkegaard’s anthropology certainly implies. Alterations in ethos are possible, and this shows that the *ethos-expression condition* does not do all the work of unifying a narravive. We also need a notion of progression that is more than mere advancement along a “trajectory” in Jones’s sense. For advancement can be merely quantitative e.g. an airplane is further along its flight path after four than it was after two hours By contrast, narratives involve what I’ll call *cumulative development*, which requires that later elements in the sequence build logically on earlier elements, so that holistic relations of meaning accessible synchronically to the consciousness of an agent (a “web”) at most times are richer in depth, complexity, and number of connections than the web at an earlier time. For example, most curricula are cumulative in design; one can only approach the later topics if one understands (to some extent) the earlier ones. Similarly, building a business or political movement, building a movement of music towards its peak, and (notably) reading a story are cumulative processes, while the natural evolution of a language is not (gain and loss in words may be roughly equal). Development of a theory is cumulative; traditions of work and thought 

*[p. 79]* ^pg98

in different arts or sciences are cumulative: Einstein “builds” on Newton and Maxwell in a richer sense than a bricklayer “builds” a wall on a lower foundation. The mastering of diffi cult techniques is usually cumulative; one has to pass through key thresholds to move to the next stage (for example, when a child first succeeds in sounding out a short string of simple words, rapid progress in reading may follow). Cumulative processes are typically punctured not just by salient turning points, but specifically by thresholds in this sense, often experienced as moments of breakthrough, insight, enlargement of perspective, synthesis in understanding, or qualitative enhancement of a whole. By contrast, a merely *ac*cumulative process, like the inflation of a balloon, growth in the balance of a bank account through regular interest, or performing five unrelated tasks on a to-do list, is mere growth or movement along a quantitative index. But progressive cumulation should not be too rigidly conceived as development towards a peak. It results in part from the way that relations (R1a) among earlier elements and (R1b) between later elements generate higher-order relations (R2) between R1a and R1b that modify the first-order relations—a symptom of holism.

This is hardly a systematic analysis of cumulation, but such a definition by paradigm cases will be enough for our present purpose. For example, it fits George Connell’s point that in Kierkegaard’s pseudonymous work, “the ethical self’s repetition builds on the previous moment’s accomplishments. It assimilates rather than rejects the reality” brought about by past eff ort and thus “makes true history possible.”<sup>220</sup> We may be able to identify forms of cumulative development in this sense that do not seem narratival; the progress of an argument, whether informal or in symbolic notation, may be a case in point. But my proposal is only that *cumulative development* is a necessary condition of narrativity. And if we start combining the conditions, it gets harder to imagine a cumulative development of an *ethos* or paradigmatically related set of themes that is not an intuitively narratival trajectory.

It may be objected that literary narratives sometimes become more simple, or shed layers of complexity, after they pass their denouement—much as living agents usually rest after finishing complex tasks. Although this is true, cumulation does not have to apply between every two points in a narrative sequence for it to apply to the whole. And the ‘winding down’ of a story or unwinding after major eff orts in life still gains much of its significance from what has gone before; otherwise it would be mere disintegration or lassitude (respectively). Similarly, the unity-1 of a life can include much interruption, fragmentation, and unraveling of plans, as long as the cumulative development begins again. If it stops entirely or only devolves, we rightly say that a person’s psyche is disintegrating, and his or her competency for planning agency deteriorates to the same extent. It might also be objected that there are some alterations in character that are neither progressive nor regressive but just radical: consider a strong religious “conversion” or the altered values a person displays after a powerful near-death experience. Yet in fact, if 

*[p. 80]* ^pg99

these do not constitute the sheer replacement of one persona by another, as in dissociative identity disorder, the person must be able to experience (and to some extent explain) the change as one that maintains other lines of continuity, or that constitutes a development from her past.<sup>221</sup>

There is one more crucial kind of connection to add. Because narrative realism holds that the unity of a narravive that constitutes a practical identity must at least reach the level of unity-1, the unity-0 of a consciousness and teleological connections involved in planning agency form the framework or skeleton around which the other significance-relations in a narravive coalesce—Rudd and Carr were right to see this type of connection as basic for narravives. This point is embodied in the *effective-agency* and *complementarity* conditions. Yet Christman is also correct that teleological relations are not necessary for all types of narrative relation. There are other kinds of linkage that contribute to narrative development (even if they are not suffi cient for it by themselves) which operate with some independence from teleological relations. These include the broad range of meaning-relations that I simply label *associative connections*: similarity, contrast, shared connotations, parallelism, being a mark or trace of something else, metaphorical and symbolic connection, allusion and suggestion, and a host of others named by tropes and figures of speech.<sup>222</sup>

The analogy thesis, and thus the idea of narravive, is supported by the fact that these relations named by literary terms which operate in artifact-narratives also apply to groups of elements in lived experience in ways that may or may not advance our purposes or make our pursuits more complementary. They often disturb or complicate other relations, or add another less well-recognized layer of significance to pursuits and interpersonal relations that a focus on action theory alone will miss. Practical identity requires planning agency but is not exhausted by it: for agents with robust memories and imaginations reaching towards the farther future, the web of significance-relations present at a given time extends beyond means-toends relations, planning, goal-setting, and instrumental constraints arising from our present circumstances and our past, and the field of differentially accessible options for action in the near future. The demands of planning agency put all these relations into our horizon of concern, but along with them go much more elusive ties of resemblance and imitation between present and past—at the level of particular objects and signs, the level of activity patterns and situations, and arguably also within our mostly prethetic ongoing awareness of bodies. While awareness of our physical limitations may go into our planning, sometimes associative links are partly visceral, revealed in the way our sympathetic nervous system reacts to stimuli—as when bodily discomfort, or an unusual smell, or even a taste of a madeleine cake alters our mood and colors the significance of other more cognitive connections in the “vast structure of recollection.”<sup>223</sup>

These significance-modifications by associative connection are the hardest to describe of all the ties involved in narratival sequence, but their 

*[p. 81]* ^pg100

importance cannot be underestimated. They can dramatically affect our emotions and understanding of the values and options that are practically relevant to action.<sup>224</sup> Sometimes even a look, a gesture, a phrasing, or unexpected perspective on a familiar object can suddenly recall something or someone from the past, give us a premonition of the future, or reveal a previously unrecognized aspect of something or someone familiar to us.<sup>225</sup> For example, who knows how often, or to what extent, the significance I apprehend in words and actions of my younger daughter are affected by certain memories of my grandmother, who she seems to resemble in indefinite yet salient ways. The past comes alive in Proust’s spoonful of tea or Kane’s snowy orb; multiple images of possible futures flash through a mother’s mind when her child is born. And these connections alter other meaning-relations that were already operative, like ramifying adjustments in a network (compare this to the prior definition of ‘compounding’). Even on the prerefl ective order of narravive, such meaning-relations interact to some degree with many others in our experience, like overlapping gravitational fields.

Thus the aptness of the *weave* metaphor for the diachronic development of synchronic webs of significance in human lives. Trying to articulate all such modifications of connotation in a finite narrative would be like trying to give the formula for 100 billion massive objects in a galaxy all pulling on each other according to Newton’s law of gravitation. Even to calculate the interaction of ten stars exceeds present computing power, yet they do all move together in a unity we call the Milky Way. Similarly, fields or webs of significance move together, growing and accumulating complexity over time, in the fabric of a narravive. In these terms, we can define ‘rosebud’ components more carefully as nodes in this temporal weave that exercise a pervasive influence on many others, yet remain unarticulated; they are not reflectively addressed or worked on by the agent. The possibility of such hidden centers of semiotic gravity arises because narravive first forms at prerefl ective levels of consciousness. However, it should be noted that artifact-narratives make use of associate links in ways derived from their natural occurrence in life: as Diana Jones says in commenting on Tolkien’s art, “To shape a narrative, you have to phase the various incidents and so control their nature that you set up significances, correspondences, foretastes, and expectations. . . .”<sup>226</sup> This occurs more spontaneously and easily in real narravives.

Associative connections of these kinds are probably not suffi cient by themselves for narrative sequence; their narrativity-making function depends on the ways they *add* to forms of cumulative progression and teleological ordering that provide the central thread of narrative sequence. Taking all the conditions we have considered together, we can now state a definition of narrativity that I simply label the *robust conception* (RC):

A robust narrative sequence (or weave) is one in which a temporally ordered set of events, actions, interactions, emotions, attitudes, and 

*[p. 82]* ^pg101

related psychic states constitute a pattern of *effective agency* (coordinated lines of intentional action planned over long terms within comprehensible social and natural *settings*) for at least one character pursuing at least minimally complementary activities, and are experienced (at least from the perspective of that character) as forming a holistic web of meaning-relations that *develops cumulatively*—in part, through the progression of action and modification of plans in light of new contingencies, and in part through compounding *associative connections* between elements of the sequence.

This is a stronger conception than may be required for some artistic artifacts that we might want to say constitute narratives or tell a “story,”<sup>227</sup> especially if we do not insist on a coherent plot, minimally effective characters, or unity of theme and allow self-deconstructing stories to count. But that is consistent with the mimetic thesis (C): for artistic narratives may be in some ways *less* and in other ways more narratival than lives with personal identity. The *robust conception* is tentatively proposed as the kind of narrativity necessary for the unity-1 of a *narravive*. At least it approximates to the sort of narratival sequence that makes for practical identity, though it may be impossible to give precise necessary and suffi cient conditions for anything so subtle. It may be objected that this definition has left out the plot-connection condition, which is crucial: as Robert Caserio says, plot-lines in stories mirror to an extent the way “mind itself thinks chronologically and sequentially” because “the operations of nature are successive” and in causal order. The experience of plot also reflects the way the present of a self contains past and future: plot is a “muse of memory and prophecy, directing the mind always to look backward and forward.”<sup>228</sup> However, I mean the plot-condition to be implicit in the robust conception: the causal ordering necessary for different lines of intentional action will be internal to each as part of *effective agency*, though they may intersect only indirectly as structuring causes (the pursuit of one line of activity limits or facilitates the extent to which another line may be followed). Causal intersection is also obviously involved in interaction between persons, and thus with social dimension of planning agency. Thus all the conditions that have been considered crucial for narrative sequence are included in the robust conception.

It might also be objected that good literary narrative involves formal features that RC leaves out. Lamarque is right that unlike life, literary presentation often draws attention to its modes of presentation and rises to a greater seriousness of theme than we usually find in ordinary life.<sup>229</sup> But he overstates the banality of real-life narratives and underestimates their capacity to become more serious when agents are willing. Nor does it follow from his analysis that real lives cannot have narratival form unless they typically exhibit the complex literary devices we find in great novels.<sup>230</sup> There are many forms of narrative connection, such as foreshadowing, 

*[p. 83]* ^pg102

anagogic symbolism, and personification of a natural phenomenon, that have aesthetic value in art but that would normally lack aesthetic or other types of value in real lives; yet this is no evidence against the analogy thesis (A). That real life only has a large subset of the narrative features of literary works is no problem for a narrativist conception of personal identity.

My view, *contra* Strawson, is that reaching and maintaining the kind of narravive defined by RC that allows for practical identity is a natural aspect of the human telos. Given its connection with planning agency, a person who cannot achieve unity-1 is bound to feel frustrated by this. If a human being lacks diachronic apperception, or the substantive episode-memories that are normally operative in maintaining a sense of one’s life-story, she would normally feel moved to try to reconstruct the story of her history from a third-personal point of view, with the help of others and sources of information such as recordings, photos, etc. This seems to be what the evidence of amnesiacs in particular shows: as Christman notes, they may retain a sense of self, but cannot maintain a sense of themselves “as the subject of ongoing plans,” though they may “construct a narrative of their lives with others’ aid” and intact semantic memory abilities.<sup>231</sup> The need for unity-1 also explains why people with gaps or opacities in their history usually try to figure out what happened or make sense of their passage from earlier to current states.

#### 4.3 The Self-Deception Objection

In light of this defense of narrative realism, it becomes easier to answer objections to narrative identity theory that concern more reflective self-portrayal and self-interpretation. My fourth proposed addition to the Ricoeurian model concerns the problem of self-deceit in ‘editing’ one’s life-story. Consider Lippitt’s argument regarding Kierkegaard’s pseudonymous author “Johannes Climacus:” he suggests that various personal remarks in the *Postscript* along with a famous “graveyard scene,” in which Climcaus’s literary “whim” becomes a serious project to explain the diffi culty of Christianity (in response to overhearing a grieving father talk about his dead son’s loss of faith), put in doubt the narrative unity of Climacus’s life. For it seems dubious that a life-project could be based on witnessing a “single emotional scene” in this way.<sup>232</sup> More generally, these points suggest that

the potentially inaccessible ‘inwardness’ of others is a problem for the ‘narrative unity’ view \[of practical identity\], which we must add to the problem of self-deception that haunts any attempt to judge whether one’s own life possesses ‘narrative unity.’<sup>233</sup>

On the contrary, the example of Kane suggests that inwardness is no problem for narrative realists: the concept of narravive *explains* how Kane, Climacus, and real people can be practical selves that transcend what all 

*[p. 84]* ^pg103

narratives about them tell; yet at least for real people, this surplus of “rosebud” elements still has narravival structure. Notably, Kierkegaard was such a person: he knew that his story would never be told in full, that his readers would never understand all the reasons why he did not marry Regine Olsen; he may not have been certain of them himself, despite his many enigmatic remarks on the subject. Though this has not stopped biographers from trying, their accounts all feel like the stories that others told about Kane: they are palpably inadequate, and will probably remain so. We cannot know Kierkegaard’s “rosebud,” but it is evident from his pseudonymous remarks about “inwardness” in general, and the more dangerous form of “inclosing reserve” in particular (CA 123), that he understood the rosebud phenomenon and related problems of self-deception.

Still, Lippitt is right that self-deception is a serious and pervasive practical problem in our lives: our self-understanding may diverge significantly from our actual narravive. But these errors consist in failures of reflection and prethetic ‘self-images’ to match a real process that has something like a narratival form.<sup>234</sup> Thus the realist ontological reply to objection (2) also takes care of the *theoretical* problem in objection (3), though of course we all have the *practical* problem of self-deception. The only way to avoid self-deception is through *truer* self-narratives, where this means interpretations that are more honest and accurate in relation to one’s actual narravive. This holds even if there is no feasible way to decide between a few most plausible renditions; in that case, the agent may need to make new choices if she wishes to clarify her identity to reflective self-interpretation or the understanding of others.<sup>235</sup> As Rudd puts it,

. . . the only way in which we can see that people are self-deceived is to compare the stories they tell with more truthful ones. . . . There is no possibility of getting away from the narrative form altogether . . . \[because\] the facts of human life—such as my making coffee or deciding whether to stay late at the offi ce—are already in narrative form, as aspects of lived narratives, which is why they can be truthfully narrated. A particular narrative may be false; but narrative is not intrinsically falsifying.<sup>236</sup>

In other words, only narratives stand a chance of adequately representing our narravives (even if we only have partial direct or reflective access to the latter). As Polkinghorne says, “When we want someone to know who we are, we tell them our life story.”<sup>237</sup> There is no other alternative that is free from the dangers of omission, half-truths, slant, and distortion. Yet precisely because such narratives aim at something real (and communicate this aim), they make an interpersonal truth-claim of a certain kind. Thus, as Maria Schechtman has argued, a person’s “narrative self-conception” must satisfy a “reality constraint.”<sup>238</sup> MacIntyre agrees: although I am the “subject” of a unique personal history, this history is partly revealed by “what 

*[p. 85]* ^pg104

I may justifiably be taken by others to be in the course of living out a story that runs from my birth to my death.”<sup>239</sup> We can and do challenge the stories that people tell about their experiences and actions. As Cheryl Misak argues in a medical context, patient narratives “are reason-structured or open to rational criticism,” which allows them to play a beneficial role in ethical deliberation.<sup>240</sup> Through rich reports of experience, we may gain insights into values and norms that are not easily accessible in other ways; they may reveal “moral landscapes” in new clarity.<sup>241</sup> And though narratives are often “rife with exaggeration, omission, and self-deception,” we correct for this by not taking them to be incorrigible, by subjecting them to interpersonal discursive tests.<sup>242</sup> For example, we ask whether the narrator only seems focused on telling an entertaining tale, or whether “the events recounted fit with the known facts.”<sup>243</sup>

We should also recognize that self-deception often takes place in ways that tend to disintegrate the continuities of experience associated with robust narrativity, rather than by way of neater self-images or narrative self-interpretations tightened up by conveniently editing out the most troubling bits. Denial or repression of painful past experiences tends to make it harder, not easier, to face present choices as an effective planning agent (even if coming to terms with the past is very hard). If neurology makes possible selective memory erasures in the future, such a procedure will doubtless have many takers; but the gaps left and resulting interpolations would degrade the cohesion of a narravive, and be harder to correct than the results of telling gilded tales about oneself. The continuities involved in the unity-1 of a narravive are arguably necessary to avoid dissipation of one’s energies and loss of mental focus. Though unity-1 may not be sufficient by itself to ensure engagement in one’s life and continued interest in one’s potentials, it is a necessary condition of these.

### 5 Reflexive Additions: How 
Secondary Self-Narratives Enter into Our Narravive

The ontology of narravives sketched above off ers a clear alternative to constructivist theories involving the logos-assumption; but so far, it has focused only on the prerefl ective level of continuity in significance involved in planning agency and related forms of narratival linkage. Yet a personal narravive unavoidably involves many reflexive beliefs, construals, or impressions arising through reflection on self, which is what makes the logos fallacy so attractive. In my view, the logos-assumption (as posited by would-be defenders or critics of narrative theories) usually results from conflating the first-order of narravive with (i) the effects of reflective thoughts and narration-acts on the weave of significance-relations within unity-1, or with (ii) the types of reflexive attitudes and explicit self-conception that are involved in personal autonomy or higher levels of narravive connection 

*[p. 86]* ^pg105

beyond unity-1, or (most often) both. As these phenomena in (ii) grow out of (i), it is easy to default to a simple view that practical identity results from a self-logos—a set of stories about oneself, one’s central cares, relationships, or goals—which loses the nuances in the analogy thesis (A) and the level distinctions (thesis B).

On the other hand, narrative realists must explain how the first-order elements of narravive that originate prerefl ectively are enriched by manifold kinds of reflective activity, from explanations of what one is doing and logoi about one’s place in the world to descriptions of one’s personality, articulations of one’s long-term ends, impressions about one’s character-traits, and so on. There are different levels of reflection in this range, of course; depending on the context, an apology may include some explanation of one’s motives without more holistic reflection on one’s whole character or the overall direction of one’s life. Much of what we ultimately hope to learn from a narravive theory of practical identity requires adding back in the more reflective levels from which I have been abstracting while trying to overcome the logos fallacy. My sketch of narravive identity supports the analogy thesis that Williams dogmatically denied, but it does *not* require the maximal priority thesis: although the primary order of narravive identity arises prior to “telling,” and new experience is constantly added to it without reflection, obviously from a very early point in their lives, children’s sense of who they are *is* deeply affected by stories they hear about themselves, their family and communities, and how others characterize their abilities, interests, and potentials.<sup>244</sup> This is, of course, why negative construals of one’s character by significant others can become self-fulfilling prophecies, even when criticism is warranted. These initial self-understandings get woven into the significance of different aspects of their environment and their personal relations, and children learn to make explicit statements about themselves to others from such experiences (and from witnessing others self-interpret as well). Given their very limited range of interpretive schema at this initially stage, young children naturally typologize themselves in overly simplistic ways; yet these blunt or unnuanced self-images seem to be psychologically indispensable first steps.<sup>245</sup> As Flanagan says (in apparent agreement with Velleman),

Because self-representing is an activity internal to a complex but *single* system, it does not leave things unchanged. The activity of self-representation is partly constitutive of actual \[practical\] identity.  .  .  . Although self-represented identity is identity from the subjective point of view, it invariably draws on available theoretical models about the nature of the self in framing its reflexive self-portrait. We represent ourselves by way of various publicly available hermeneutic strategies.<sup>246</sup>

That is how we must start, though later we may innovate beyond familiar trait descriptions and stock personality types. However, even maturing 

*[p. 87]* ^pg106

young adults remain strongly influenced by the stories prevailing in their culture. Hilde Nelson puts it well: “As repositories of common norms, master narratives exercise a certain authority over our moral imaginations and play a role in informing our moral intuitions.” Although such narratives “need not be oppressive,” they may warp reflective self-conception when they are biased, even altering the agent’s preferences.<sup>247</sup> What Nelson calls “counterstories” are eff orts to repair these effects by relating a truer history that corrects errors in “morally degrading depictions of an oppressed group.”<sup>248</sup>

As noted in introducing thesis (D), narrative realism recognizes that the basic distinction between primary and secondary narratives allows for a complex causal relation between them: quite a bit of secondary narration and reflective interpretation gets woven into our ongoing prerefl ective understanding of things, even though the former also draws on the latter as input. As Schechtman says, though the “life-narrative” that conditions the meaning of experiences for an agent often operates as an “implicit and automatic” background, it also requires her “ability to articulate her narrative locally when appropriate” by giving at least simple explanations of what she is doing—though Schechtman agrees with Strawson and Christman that this does not require a grander “unifying theme” or “quest” that runs through one’s whole life.<sup>249</sup>

This point about articulation is fundamental because it implies that the practical identity of a person cannot be constituted *only* by meaning-relations formed prerefl ectively, or in which all the significance-links formed through reflective thought have receded into prethetic awareness and cannot be reflectively rethematized. The former would be the kind of narravive that a very young child (or perhaps certain higher mammals) have prior to any explicit thought about themselves as distinctive beings; the latter would be like the narravive of someone who had lost all powers of higher cognition. Personal identity requires second-order meanings because at least some *actual* reflection on ends and means, along with an *ability* to develop one’s conception of what one is doing by further reflections when needed, are minimal conditions of planning agency and social interaction. As Schechtman says, “In day-to-day intercourse we take it for granted that people are able to answer questions about themselves.” Even those who reflect little on their motives can explain to some extent if challenged; for example, if I ask someone about a diffi cult choice between jobs, “I expect an explanation in terms of her long-term goals, personality traits, talents, likes, dislikes.”<sup>250</sup> Obviously some people are better at giving this kind of account than others, and levels of insight and authenticity vary widely, but the articulative ability needed to count as a planning agent is fairly minimal (though it can be lost, as severe dissociative disorder illustrates). It certainly does not require that someone keep a diary or even a planner, or that they are constantly reflecting on their lives via narration, as Rudd noted in reply to Lamarque (see §4.1 above). In fact, too much or the wrong kind of 

*[p. 88]* ^pg107

self-narration could get in the way of living, as we see in Margaret Walker’s description of Robert Shields, who spent at least four hours each day obsessively typing out a record of everything (else) that he did or that happened to him in absurd minutiae. As Walker says, this kind of compulsive singleness of purpose does not make his life more narratively intelligible.<sup>251</sup> What is needed is precisely a narrative ability to pick out patterns, themes, and key details that are relevant for valuable reflective purposes. In this respect, Shields is no better off than someone who tries to explain a complex event simply by replaying a video of it without commentary or analysis.

Several other aspects of personality also require kinds of reflective selfthematization, especially if we are to reach levels of unity beyond unity- 1. But the interpersonal accounting requirements of planning agency are already suffi cient to show that what Schechtman calls an *articulation condition* must be added as a fifth defining thesis (E) of narrative realism about unity-1: various kinds of reflexive *logoi* are needed to develop the prerefl exive weave of significance acquired through unity of apperception, unreflective action and experience, and associative connections in memory and anticipation, into the narravive of a self. Both the capacity for different forms of explicit self-explanation and some actual reflection on one’s activities and characteristics is essential to unity-1. Moreover, even if it contains errors or self-deceptions, the self-understandings achieved by reflection (on some parts or aspects of one’s life and activities) must be largely *continuous* with prerefl ective experience, meaning that they are able to add without much friction to the implicit self-understandings already operating, though occasionally we may have to accept radical alterations in our self-perceptions. The reasons for this part of the articulation condition are twofold. First, if the implicit self-understanding with which we always operate too often undergoes erratic jumps and large shifts, unity-1 breaks down; we can no longer go on as planning agents. Second, if reflective findings about our experiences and activities seem constantly out of tune with our implicit self-understandings, or constantly alienating, as if we were observing the story of someone else—as Dostoevsky’s “Underground Man” finds—then first- and second-order nodes of meaning are too out of sync to attain narravive unity-1.<sup>252</sup>

Like Williams, narrative theorists sometimes mistakenly formulate the articulation thesis (E) as if it conflicted with thesis (A) or the mimetic thesis (C). For example, although Widdershoven agrees with Ricoeur that “Life already has meaning before it is a subject of stories,” he argues for what he sees as a middle way between constructivists like Mink and realists like Carr: “the hermeneutic position emphasizes that life and story are only meaningful in and through mutual interaction.”<sup>253</sup> Although this middle way follows Merleau-Ponty’s view that the “prerefl exive” meaning in life-experience provides a foundation for stories about it, the resulting narrative identity is not just discovered: “it is shaped and structured in the process of articulation.”<sup>254</sup> Thus MacIntyre is wrong to think that the “unity that is 

*[p. 89]* ^pg108

put forward in the story” we give to explain our actions “is already present in the action\[s\];” for “in telling these stories,” we also “change the meaning of our experiences and actions.”<sup>255</sup> Nor, Widdershoven implies, is all such alteration through narrative interpretation that is recited to ourselves or to others nefarious or falsifying; some “reconstruction” is necessary to focus on what matters most to us,<sup>256</sup> to give direction to our lives. Similarly, as Charles Guignon says, it is through the reflective order of narration that we determine the “overall shape” of our lives.<sup>257</sup>

But in my view, MacIntyre can consistently accept these points, because they simply deny that *all* the significance that a set of actions and events have for our practical identity is already there prior to reflection on them and to later decisions regarding them. This formulation of (E) is consistent with analogy thesis (A), which only rejects the logos-assumption that *all* the meaning that a set of actions and experiences have for us is a product of the reflective articulation of stories about them (by ourselves and others). A middle way that recognizes the complex interactions of prerefl ective and reflective nodes of meaning in narravive is available within realism. But narrative realists must then be careful to avoid common formulations that, while rhetorically simple and pleasing, obscure the crucial difference between the logos-assumption and thesis (E). The plausible claim that experiencing “your life as an ongoing story with identity-defining aims and an underlying direction” requires a reflexive logos concerning your central commitments should not be conflated with the much stronger and dubious claim that all the meaning that events and actions have for us results from “telling them to ourselves and to others.”<sup>258</sup>

Obviously these points are far from a full theory of the many ways in which secondary narratives about ourselves, our cares, and the values on which they are based enter into our narravives. They are only desiderata for such a theory, and there are many tricky issues to be resolved in spelling out a more detailed articulation condition for unity-1. For example, Schechtman argues that there is a “sense in which the elements of a person’s narrative that he cannot articulate are less fully his than those he can” explain, because they are not subject to the same “scrutiny” and cognitive control that reflectively articulable elements are; instead, “their influence is rigid and automatic.”<sup>259</sup> While I agree, I regard this as a point about the less *autonomous* status of these deeply unconscious or unarticulable aspects of experience or significance which bears on unity-2. My claims about ‘rosebud’ elements commit me to including them in the unity-1 of a narravive, though the diffi culty that such elements make for self-understanding is evident precisely by contrast with the at-least-partially articulable nature of most meaning-relations in a narravive. Of course it helps to have friends, who can sometimes understand aspects of us that are occluded from our own reflective inquiry.

For a complete articulation condition, more needs to be said about ways in which manifold types of reflexive states and attitudes get woven 

*[p. 90]* ^pg109

into our narravive. These include tacit self-imagery, self-regarding emotions (including second-order emotions), more explicit self-interpretation, and sometimes even the application of psychological theories to oneself. Even episodes of self-deception become part of ‘our story’—though a narravive itself cannot consist *entirely* of deception or intentionally misleading accounts, which do not include the full history of our deceptive moves. There comes a point where a person trying to maintain an almost completely fictional persona to others and to self becomes too dysfunctional to count as a planning agent. There are also forms of obsessive reflection on self and self-absorption that can become inimical to narrative identity, though usually these negative effects pertain more to blocking unity-2 than preventing unity-1. However, these desiderata considered in thesis (E) provide a suffi cient framework in which to consider the special reflexive attitudes involved in the thicker and more evaluatively significant forms narrative unity beyond unity-1, on which many of the narrato-skeptics’ questions focus. That is the task for the next chapter.
