> [!quote] Me
> You know how mes school students (and law too, probably) have memnonic devices and systems that they "drill" themselves with to remember things like the bones in the hand or whatever? I think I want to do something similar for theology and religion knowledge, especially the more obscure and forgettable bits.

Yes — and the med-student system is really two separable things, which is worth keeping straight before you build anything.

**The engine is spaced repetition, not the mnemonic.** Anki (or similar) doing daily retrieval is what actually moves facts into long-term storage. Mnemonics are just the encoding layer you bolt on for items that resist it. Med students' Anki decks do most of the work; Sketchy-style visual mnemonics get reserved for the genuinely arbitrary stuff — drug side-effect lists, bacterial taxonomy, things with no internal logic.

**That distinction matters more in theology than in medicine**, because a lot of what looks memorizable is actually structured. The christological heresies, for instance: Arius, Apollinaris, Nestorius, Eutyches sit on a two-axis grid (too little divinity / too little humanity / two persons / one nature). Learn the grid and the names fall out. An acronym would give you a brittle string that doesn't survive being asked *why*. So the first task is triage: what has structure gets understood, what's arbitrary gets drilled.

**The arbitrary residue is where you build the deck.** In your territory that's things like: council dates and what each one actually canonized, Hebrew and Aramaic vocabulary, sefirot names in order and their standard epithets, which rabbinic authority held which position, Akkadian tablet numbers and line references, transliteration conventions, Latin and Greek tags. Cloze deletion works better than Q&A for most of this — one fact per card, and never a card that tests two things at once.

Method of loci is worth the setup cost specifically for *ordered* sequences — the seven ecumenical councils, the ten sefirot, the Creed's own thirteen sections if you want them cold. Order is what loci does that nothing else does as well.

If you want, I can draft a starter set — say forty cloze cards on the councils and the christological controversies — so you can see whether the card design suits how you actually think, before you commit to building out a system.

Anki shows you the sentence with the bracket blanked and you supply "apauruṣeya." > [!quote] Me
> Also plot lines through Scripture (at like a 'chapter' level). I get mixed up in the Torah not to mention the Ramayana and Mahabharata.

Narrative sequence is a third problem, and cards are the wrong primary tool for it. Discrete facts want retrieval practice; a plot wants a **spine you can recite**, with detail hung off it afterward. Build the spine first — ten to twenty beats for a whole book — get it fluent, then drill the beats individually as cloze once the shape is already in your head. Cards built before the spine exists just give you disconnected fragments.

Most of these texts supply their own scaffolding, and it's better than anything you'd impose.

**Torah.** Genesis is structured by the ten *toledot* ("these are the generations of...") — that's the author's own outline, and it cleanly separates primeval history from the patriarchal cycles. The real confusion, though, is in the middle books, and it has a specific cause: the narrative stops. The camp arrives at Sinai in [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^19-1|Exodus 19]] and doesn't move again until Numbers 10 — something like fifty-eight chapters of legislation, tabernacle construction, and priestly material with almost no forward motion. If you hold onto "everything from [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^19-1|Exodus 19]] to Numbers 10 happens standing still at one mountain," most of the mixing-up resolves. Numbers then resumes travel, Deuteronomy is a single long speech on the plains of Moab.

**Ramayana.** The seven kandas are named for places and phases, and the plot is geographic — it moves steadily south from Ayodhya to the forest to Kishkindha to Lanka and back. This is the one text where method of loci is redundant, because the story *is* already a route. Learn the kanda names in order and you have the plot.

**Mahabharata.** Hardest of the three, because eighteen parvas of wildly unequal character sit under nested frame narratives, and two enormous blocks (Shanti, Anushasana) are didactic rather than narrative — Bhishma teaching from the bed of arrows, not events. One useful handle: the war parvas are named for the successive Kaurava generals in order — Bhishma, Drona, Karna, Shalya. Four generals, four books, and you know where you are in the eighteen days.

For drilling sequence once the spine is learned, the card type you want is "what comes next" — beat on the front, following beat on the back — plus position cards ("which kanda contains Hanuman's leap to Lanka"). Both are thin and fast, and they only work if the spine came first.

Close — "acrostic" classically means the initial letters of successive *lines* spelling something out (the alphabetic psalms, Lamentations). What you've built is a syllabic acronym: first syllables, not first letters, compressed into a pronounceable word. The distinction doesn't matter for use, but "acronym" is the term that'll get you the right search results.

And *Sulak* works for a specific reason worth naming, because it determines when the technique fails: it has the phonotactic shape of a real word. Nonsense strings don't survive. If a lineage's initials give you *Skvbhkṣ*, the device is dead on arrival and you shouldn't force it.

Two limits to design around:

- **It gives you the set and the order, not the pointers.** *Sulak* won't tell you Lakshmanagupta taught Abhinavagupta rather than the reverse. You still want individual cards for the succession links.
- **It's a retrieval index, not an encoder.** "La" only unpacks to Lakshmanagupta if that name is already solid. So the syllable→name expansion needs its own cards, drilled first.

Also worth noting your example is the Pratyabhijñā succession specifically — Vasugupta and Kallata sit upstream on the Spanda side, so you may want a second device, or an extended one, depending on how you're carving the school.

Rhythm and fixed syllable count carry long ordered lists far better, and they self-check — a dropped name breaks the meter audibly. That's the tool for a full parampara.

Both points are right, and the first one explains the second.

Sanskrit's syllabic structure isn't incidental — it's why *Sulak* comes out pronounceable instead of as a consonant pile. Every Devanagari akṣara is already CV, so slicing at syllable boundaries gives you units that concatenate into well-formed words automatically. Greek and Latin don't do this, which is why the classical mnemonic tradition in the West went to initials and full sentences instead. Your technique is language-specific in a way worth knowing, because it won't transfer to the patristic material.

The off-card point is the more important one though. What you're describing is **free recall** — producing the whole chain from nothing, unprompted, with no cue on a screen. Cards give you cued recall, which is strictly easier: the card supplies the context and asks for one missing piece. Free recall is what you actually want for a lineage, and it's a different exercise.

So the acronym is doing something the deck structurally can't. It's a portable handle that makes the list rehearsable in the shower, on a walk, without a device. That's not a lesser version of the card — it's the better exercise, and the cards exist to support it.

That reframes what the deck is for. Cards should build the pieces the free-recall exercise consumes: syllable→name expansion, the individual succession links, correct spelling. Then the recitation is the actual drill. Which also means the deck shrinks — you don't need cards for anything the chain reliably reproduces once it's fluent.

One thing about ToSuHaPa: Śaṅkara's four principal disciples are traditionally tied to the four maṭhas, so the acronym has a second structure running alongside it — Padmapāda to Dvārakā in the west, Sureśvara to Śṛṅgeri in the south, Hastāmalaka to Purī in the east, Toṭaka to Jyotirmaṭha in the north, with the standard attributions varying by source. If you can get the acronym to run in the traditional directional order rather than arbitrarily, you get both facts out of one handle. Worth trying a few orderings before you fix on one — a mnemonic is hard to re-learn once set.

> [!quote] Me
> Descent narratives can also be a tool for free recall

Yes, and it's the older technique — genealogy was transmitted as recitation long before anyone wrote it down. It has two advantages over the acronym that matter here.

**It handles branching.** *Sulak* only works because the Śaiva succession is linear. You can't make an acronym of the Mezritch fan, but you can narrate it easily: the Maggid gathers disciples, sends them out, and this one goes to Berditchev, this one to Liadi, this one to Lizhensk. The story accommodates a shape the list can't represent at all.

**It self-checks, like meter.** Causal connective tissue means a dropped step leaves a gap you can feel — someone has to have taught the next person, someone has to have gone somewhere. The chain resists silent corruption in a way a bare sequence doesn't.

The failure mode is the mirror of that second advantage, though, and it's sharp in this particular material. Causal glue also *manufactures* links. If you can't recall who taught whom, narrative pressure will supply a plausible connection and it'll feel like memory. Hasidic descent material is the worst case for this, because the tradition's own transmission already works this way — *Shivchei ha-Besht* is hagiography, and the received chains are partly constructed backward to establish legitimacy. So you'd be using a narrative mnemonic on a body of material that's already the product of narrative mnemonics.

Practical consequence: keep the story and the evidence in separate compartments. Recite the descent freely as a recall exercise, but let the cards carry the flat attributions — X was a disciple of Y, attested where — so you retain a version that isn't smoothed. Where the historical link is genuinely disputed, the card should say so, the same way the Nestorius card does. Otherwise the fluency of the recitation becomes your confidence in the history.

> [!quote] Me
> The tantric margas kind of do this already. Aren't their some kinds of Asian Buddhism where every monk can trace his lineage into antiquity?

Chan and Zen, above all. Every Chan monk's ordination places him in a chain running back through Bodhidharma to Mahākāśyapa receiving the Buddha's flower-sermon — and the lineage documents themselves, the *Transmission of the Lamp* literature, are structured exactly as recitable descent. Tibetan traditions do it too: each has its own transmission lines, and the Kagyü chain from Tilopa through Nāropa, Marpa, and Milarepa is the standard example.

The complication is that Chan is the best-documented case in world religion of descent narrative being constructed retroactively. The early Chan lineages were assembled during factional disputes over legitimacy, and the twenty-eight Indian patriarchs between the Buddha and Bodhidharma don't survive historical examination — the list was worked out in China, and competing versions circulated because competing factions needed them. The Northern/Southern school split produced rival genealogies for exactly this reason.

Which is the same warning as the Hasidic material, sharpened. These chains are superb mnemonic instruments — they were engineered to be memorized and recited — and they are not, in their early sections, historical records. The instrument's fluency has nothing to do with the claims' reliability.

That actually suggests a card design worth adopting across all of this. A lineage card can carry a status field: attested, traditional, or contested. Bodhidharma to Huike sits in a different epistemic category than Mahākāśyapa to Ānanda, and Marpa to Milarepa in a third. If you drill the chain without marking those boundaries, you'll retain a smooth sequence and lose the joints — which, given the work you do, is the failure that would actually cost you something.

> I would like you to create an anki deck for the Devanagari alphabet. I would like every consonant and vowel combination. It will be a lot of cards

450 notes: the full 33 × 13 matrix, plus the independent vowels, the diacritics, and the three conjuncts traditionally recited with the alphabet. Recognition direction — Devanagari on the front, IAST on the back.

Everything is tagged, and that matters more than usual here, because the deck has a redundancy problem you should decide about deliberately rather than discover at review 300.

Devanagari is an abugida, so the mātrās are almost perfectly regular. Once क and ि are solid, कि is derivable — you're not learning a fact, you're applying a rule you already have. Roughly 400 of these cards teach one rule 400 times. The counterargument is real, though: reading speed comes from whole-syllable sight recognition, not from derivation, and derivation is slow. Which case applies depends on whether you're decoding śāstra slowly with a lexicon or trying to read at pace.

So the tags let you pick:

- `irregular` — the genuinely non-derivable forms. Every `ि` card (written left, pronounced right), रु and रू. Small set, high value, never suspend these.
- `rare` — every ṝ and ḷ combination. ऌ occurs in essentially one word. Suspend on import unless you want grammatical completeness.
- `v-a` plus a varga tag — the 33 bare consonants. This is the tier to learn first and alone.
- `v-aa`, `v-i`, `v-u` and so on — lets you introduce one vowel across all consonants at a time, which is how the barakhadi is traditionally taught and a saner sequence than alphabetical.

My suggestion: import everything, then immediately suspend `rare`, and study `v-a` to fluency before unsuspending anything else. Thirty-three cards is a week. The rest goes much faster once the consonant inventory is automatic, and you may find you can suspend most of the regular matrix at that point.

Not included: conjuncts beyond the standard three. There are several hundred, they're where real reading difficulty lives, and they'd want their own deck built against actual text rather than generated exhaustively.
