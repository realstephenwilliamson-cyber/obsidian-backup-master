### Introduction

1\. My understanding of transcendental/metaphysical argument is indebted to that of David Tracy, and, somewhat less directly, to two of his sources, Emerich Coreth and Bernard Lonergan. This understanding unites the Kantian conception of the transcendental-as-the-necessary with older Greek and Christian understandings of metaphysics as inquiry into ultimate reality. See David Tracy, Blessed Rage for Order: The New Pluralism in Theology (Minneapolis: Winston-Seabury Press, 1975); Emerich Coreth, Metaphysics, trans. Joseph Donceel, with a critique by Bernard J. F. Lonergan (New York: Seabury, 1973); and Bernard J. F. Lonergan, Insight: A Study of Human Understanding, revised edition (San Francisco: Harper & Row, 1978).

2\. This causal view is a basic expression of modern “instrumental rationality.” See Martin Heidegger, “The Question Concerning Technology,” trans. William Lovitt, in Martin Heidegger: Basic Writings, ed. with an introduction by David Farrell Krell (San Francisco: Harper & Row, 1977), 283-317. For such thinking, the world is viewed as intellectually controllable and practically

See David Tracy, Plurality and Ambiguity: Hermeneutics, Religion, Hope E (San Francisco: r & Row, 1987).

4\. I note that relativism has been analyzed into a variety of types and degrees of strength. Among the ostensible types of relativism are those of perception, concepts, truth, reason, and ethics. I believe that the distinctions between these types are ultimately artificial and may lead to confusion. Thus conceptual relativism will refer to perception, will have implications about truth and falsity, will define reasons, and will be correlated with an axiology or ethics

*[p. 172]* ^pg192

about which concepts should and should not be acknowledged. A relativism of perception will likewise apply to concepts about perception, the truth of tion, what it is reasonable to regard as perception, and so on. My focus

on relativism/skepticism as an approach to interpretation is intended to include all of these types. I also believe that the distinction between strong and weak kinds of relativism is not useful. Sometimes gradations of strength are made only on the basis of the quantity of topics of reality, truth, reason, norms, and so forth, that are considered relative. Sometimes weak relativism amounts to nothing more than acknowledgment of the plurality of interpretations and of the difficulty of arbitrating between them. I do not believe that relativism with regard to a particular topic admits of degrees. Either a subject on which there is disagreement is relative or it is not. Examples of efforts to articulate relativist positions of different strengths are found in Michael Krausz, ed. with introduction, Relativism: Interpretation and Confrontation (Notre Dame, Ind.: University of Notre Dame Press, 1989).

Another problem in discussing relativism is that many thinkers who are frequently identified as relativists do not call themselves such, or are not considered so by some of their interpreters. This is more than a disagreement about names. It is often pointed out that one or another of such thinkers has in his/her works some clearly nonskeptical intellectual or ethical agendas. In my view such evidence does not necessarily affect the classification of such intellectuals as relativists. I shall argue that some realistic understanding of reference is transcendental to interpretation. Therefore the presence of nonrelativist/nonskeptical features in such philosophies is actually unavoidable. My classification of relativists/skeptics will therefore include all thinkers who elaborate arguments that fundamentally undermine the validity of the reference of interpretation, regardless of the inevitable presence of strong nonrelativist elements in their thought or even claims they may make that they are not relativists.

I am focusing on postmodern relativist skepticism in order to maximize the relevance of the exposition to contemporary cultural theory. For a broader historical perspective on Western skepticism, see Myles Burnyeat, ed., The Skeptical Tradition (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1983); and the writings of Richard H. Popkin, e.g., The Encyclopedia of Philosophy (1967), s.v. “Skepticism,” The History of Scepticism from Erasmus to Spinoza (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1979), and The High Road to Pyrrhonism, ed. Richard A. Watson and James E. Force (San Diego: Austin Hill, 1980).

58\. I do not believe that the understanding of dialogue that has been advocated here should be seen as much different from the Platonic-Socratic model. It has become increasingly apparent that the “arch-foundationalist” Plato was intimately aware of the issues complicating the progress of dialogue. Contra the usual textbook summaries, Plato does not give a neat account of everything on the basis of his ahistorical criteria, the Ideas. Rather, he uses irony—in the mouth of Socrates or through other literary devices—partially to negate almost everything he asserts. Readers are led into the dialogical process by means of the aporias in the texts. Plato was just as sensitive to the tragic power of “nonepistemic causes” of belief and action—which prevented Meno from being initiated into the mysteries of recollection, and led to the death of the one who descended back into the cave. A couple of useful works providing a different understanding of Plato are Paul Friedländer, Plato: 1 An Introduction, trans. 33\. I note that, though the Śaivas have an advantage in their sensitivity to narrative, Lonergan does engage in some considerations analogous to their theory of agential syntax. First of all, Lonergan approaches an explicit syntax of agency in an explanation of causality supporting his cognitive argument for the existence of God at Insight, 651-57. Lonergan attempts to demonstrate that causality is “intelligent emanation” rather than natural causality, David Tracy, The Achievement of Bernard Lonergan (New York: Herder & Herder, 1970), 69. This involves “the transition from familiar but anthropomorphic notions of external causality to their root in a universally applicable principle,” Lonergan, Insight, 652. This principle is an originating “unconditioned intelligent and rational consciousness.” Ibid., 657. Lonergan’s argument takes the form of demonstrating the necessity of God as the ultimate representative of three of Aristotle's causes, that is, the efficient, final, and formal (which Lonergan calls exemplary) causes. While we cannot go into this technical discussion here, it may be observed that the three notions of cause are roughly similar to some of the Indian conceptions of agency radicalized by the Śaivas on the basis of their idealism of recognition. The conception of God as final cause may be related to the idea of the transcendental self-recognitive agent as having the intention or desire (icchā) to make the effort (yatna) to act. The conception of God as efficient cause may be compared with the notion of the agent as independent or noncontingent (svatantra) instigator (prayojaka) of the subordinate actions in all of the verbal cases. And the conception of God as formal cause may be related to the idea of the agent as supporting/synthetic locus of the subordinate actions (vyāpārāśraya) in his self-recognition.

Here I am only talking of what Lonergan calls external causality. At another level, of internal causality, Lonergan attempts to demonstrate a complex isomorphism between the stages of human knowledge and the structure of reality. Ibid., 486; and Hugo A. Meynell, An Introduction to the Philosophy of Bernard Lonergan, 2nd ed. (Basingstoke, U.K.: Macmillan, 1991), 83-85.

“Proto-syntactic” considerations are also adduced by Lonergan in a section where he tries to show that his proof for the existence of God subsumes all other theistic proofs. (Lonergan makes this claim in the statement at Insight, 672, which I quoted at the beginning of the consideration of his proof.) He takes as examples that are encompassed by his proof the five theistic arguments of Thomas. Again, Thomas's three cosmological proofs may perhaps be related to the Saiva conception of God as instigator, as independent, and so on, and the teleological to those of God as having the originative intention and as the locus of the cre-ative process. Perhaps there is no Śaiva parallel to Thomas's inference of God from the levels of being. Anyway, for Lonergan all of these arguments are “so many particular cases of the general statement that the proportionate universe is incompletely intelligible and that complete intelligibility is demanded.” Insight, 678. In our terms, we may suggest that various parameters of the philosophical syntax of superagency—

42\. Panentheism means “all-in-God-ism.” According to this viewpoint the world is real as it exists in some sense in God. God accordingly is both transcendent of and immanent in the world.

As my comments on dialecticity just made indicate, I believe that traditional Christianity has always had ways of describing the immanence of God. This is often underestimated in the rhetorical formulations of contemporary panentheists. I believe that in virtually all theological doctrines, from the

*[p. 248]* ^pg268

Hebrew prophets, the Koran, and Calvin to Eckhart and Spinoza, there is some dialecticity. While the contemporary panentheists may thus to some extent use the terms “traditional theism” and “pantheism” as strawmen, I believe that they are placing a significant new emphasis on dialecticity to address the conflicts of tradition and modernity.

Of course, this metaphysical strategy has not been accepted by all contemporary theists attempting to grapple with the conflicts of tradition and modernity, and postmodernity. Many neo-orthodox thinkers as well as postmodernists such as Levinas continue to emphasize the transcendence of God in the face of secularity and pluralism.
