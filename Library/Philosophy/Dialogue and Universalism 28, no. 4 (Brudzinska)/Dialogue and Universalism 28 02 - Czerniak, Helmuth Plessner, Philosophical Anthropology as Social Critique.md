## Czerniak 2 Plessner PO KOREKCIE

*Stanisław Czerniak* 

HELMUTH PLESSNER: 

PHILOSOPHICAL ANTHROPOLOGY AS SOCIAL CRITIQUE 

*ABSTRACT* 

The author goes out from Helmuth Plessner’s book *Die Grenzen der Gemeinschaft* to show how the basic categories of Plessner’s philosophical anthropology, especially the eccentric position conception, apply to his critique of community-oriented societies like communism and fascism. Plessner saw the alternative to a community-based society in a model where social bonds took place by association, and in which the anthropological *a priori* enjoyed the optimum conditions for self-expression(in such dimensions of the public sphere as ceremony, prestige, diplomacy and tact).This social model also allows the full establishment of social roles in the anthropological sense, something that is annihilated by community-type societies. The author also addresses the different ways in which the “social role” category is interpreted by Plessner (the anthropological approach) and Ralf Dahrendorf (a functionalistic approach drawing on Marxism and the concept of alienation, which Plessner felt unfamiliar with), and concludes with a few concrete and methodologically grounded objections to Plessner’s theory. 

Keywords: philosophical anthropology, community, association, the eccentric position, social role, ceremony, prestige, diplomacy, tact, social critique 

1\. INTRODUCTORY REMARKS 

The “social critique” category is typically associated with social theory, political thought, or political economy, and that has been the case since Karl Marx. The philosophy of man understood as a part of broader doctrinal entities—such as the Aristotelian or Thomist tradition—possessed descriptive character. Only a demarcation line, which separated it from normative ethics, could suggest that there should be an “optimal” society guarding humanity’s “good deeds,” with its perfect template stemming from a critique of empirically existing social entities. A utopian Renaissance reflection attempted to follow that intellectual lead long ago. 

*[p. 10]* ^pg12

During the twentieth century anthropological reflection tended to be closer to the theory of cognition than to critical social philosophy as presented in Sartre’s *Being and Nothingness*. But anthropological themes intertwine with critical social philosophy most clearly in the works of neo-Marxian Critical Theory. Classics of the Frankfurt School, such as Max Horkheimer or Theodor W. Adorno, developed a kind of social critique by referring to anthropological categories as a particular criterion for assessment within the social sphere. According to Axel Honneth—the current director of the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt am Main—that connection constitutes the doctrinal originality of the critical theory[^1] and defines the philosophical positions of the representatives of the school’s second generation, and particularly that of Jürgen Habermas, whose categories of the “ideal speech situation” and “*a priori* conditions of possibility of interpersonal communication” he employed in his critique of the “colonization of the lifeworld by systemic factors—political power and the market,” which in his opinion typify contemporary capitalism.[^2] Below, however, I would like to take this historical-philosophical analysis in a different direction. 

When speaking of “philosophical anthropology” I mean solely the so-called classical philosophical anthropology of the twentieth century, with Max Scheler, Helmuth Plessner and Arnold Gehlen recognized as its creators. Here are some general remarks about this doctrinally rather cohesive philosophical current:[^3] 

— These particular thinkers looked for theoretical inspirations in biology-related sciences (especially ethology), as well as in different branches of psychology, such as Gestalt psychology.[^4] Here philosophical reflection is usually confronted with the legacy of the exact sciences. On the other hand, however, philosophical anthropology as such is said to possess a methodological, superior status against many other sub-disciplines of philosophy. Scheler’s philosophical anthropology provides basic terms for the philosophy of history, culture or sociology of knowledge.[^5] In the case of Plessner or Gehlen one clearly sees the theoretical anchoring of philosophical anthropology in the fundaments of social philosophy. These two thinkers would be the ones making the most use of anthropological argumentation in their critical works devoted to contemporary

2\. THE ECCENTRIC POSITION CATEGORY 

Plessner’s anthropological concept of the human being begins with the “lack of identity of man with himself,” also referred to as “eccentric position”—an ability to grasp oneself “from without,” a point (a distancing “I”) from which one can see different “incarnations” and “embodiments” of that, “which he is.” Plessner does not substantialize that “I,” neither is it a kind of “spirit” to him (understood in Scheler’s terms), that entails the subduing of bodily energies. It has the characteristics of an act, a human being both “is” and “observes” that he or she “is,” objectifies it, but the very observation point avoids objectification. In his publications, and in that context, Plessner often refers to differences between the ontic and cognitive status of man and animal. An animal is “centric”—its nervous center steers its physicality. The animal “has” body, has control over it in a variety of circumstances connected to fulfilling the most basic needs (hunting, as a prime example among predators), and it also “is” the body (a specimen of a particular zoological species), but it “does not know” that it is simultaneously both, hence is unable to “observe” itself in that simultaneity. The animal hunts, but it “does not know” that it constitutes a hunting predator, that it hunts in a particular way, for a particular prey, etc. Plessner will say that the animal never situates itself “outside” itself, and that only man is eccentric in his acts of self-knowledge, transgressing the mode of being and possessing. A man knows that he or she “has a living body,” and simultaneously “is a living body” (let us imagine a runner who starts the run by moving his body, but simultaneously knows that on that particular day the run feels harder, because, for example, he broke some dietary rule concerning his body, in this case understood as a bio-chemical system). As Plessner states: “Every requirement of physical existence demands a reconciliation between being and having, outside and inside.”[^13] 

From those general, philosophical assumptions, Plessner derives consequences that bring direct implications within the realm of social reflection. This doctrinal function is being performed by the explicated main rules of the *humanum,* especially the principle of “mediated immediacy,” and “natural artificiality

3\. THE ANTHROPOLOGICAL LIMITS OF THE COMMUNITY 

In his work Plessner employs his experiences as an active participant of the social and political transformations of the Weimar Republic after the Great War. From the perspective of the extremely vibrant student movements in which Plessner was directly involved,[^16] his confrontation with the rudiments of the fascist movement (Luddendorff’s and Hitler’s coup in Munich) as well as with the emerging Marxist proletarian revolutionary movement (the Red Army of Ruhr) lead him to reflection on social ethics (fascism and communism versus capitalism and bourgeois society with a developed ethic of the public sphere). The main idea behind that book was to show, by means of the language of anthropological philosophy, that fascism, as well as communism (originally, Plessner wanted to title his work *The Limits of Community—Critique of the Communist Ethos*[^17]) both encounter irremovable boundaries at the level of their implementation. And the source for those limits lies within the anthropological condition of man that finds its expression in defined psychological reactions. That particular diagnosis leads Plessner to juxtapose the community (*Gemeinschaft*) with society (*Gesellschaft*)—as a synonym of civic society in the Hegelian sense—and to present argumentation for the specific anthropological “superiority” of the latter. 

Within the tradition of European sociology, it was Ferdinand Tönnies, author of *Gemeinschaft und Gesellschaft. Abhandlung des Communismus und des So-*

4\. THE PUBLIC SPHERE AS A MODE OF MAN’S REALIZATION.[^36] 

GENERAL ASSUMPTIONS 

The concept of the limits of a community leads Plessner to praise society as a competitive alternative for community-type social bonds. The philosophical foundations of that praise are, *par excellence*, anthropological in character, and could be referenced directly to the previously analyzed category of eccentric position. The need to preserve distance is a key anthropological need. Man strives to express himself, but in that very act of expression there is a desire to remain hidden. Man distances himself from any form of exteriorization. He avoids clear auto-definitions. Plessner writes:

 Only in small communities, based on mutual love between their members (family, sexual/partner communities) is there a narrow window—and under very specific circumstances—where an individual, out of his or her own volition, could agree to surrender intimacy, agree to the suspension of that anthropological duality of expression and remaining hidden. Communities of the utopian ideal, emotional communities aspiring to encompass entire nations, or even entire humanity, do not create such opportunities for individuals, and expose them to ridicule wherever self-expression does not find counterweight in a tendency to preserve intimacy and defend one’s own “potentiality.” Only deep love, Plessner believes, can protect an individual from that kind of ridicule: 

“If psychological being expresses itself, if it steps under the law of appearance, then it one-sidedly limits itself and loses that depth and richness without which it cannot be taken for what it wants and deserves to become. In the manifestation, psychological being loses something necessary: but, because it intrinsically can do nothing to stop this from happening and, therefore, must look on helpless before this loss of significance, it pretends to be always more than what it factually is. This is what happens with regard to that intrinsically inscrutable ridiculousness of all uninhibited expression of emotion \[…\] This antagonism explains the phenomenon of kitsch, the strange fact that a work or word coming from an authentic intention and emotion can be false, shallow, a *cliché*.”[^38] 

 According to Plessner, man can be protected from the ridiculousness of lacking duality, or the effects of self-expression’s pathos, by “form.” His conclusion is: “Psychological being needs the compensation that balances such a qualitative loss of significance; it needs to be clothed with form so that it also remains on the surface what it is in its invisible depth.”[^39]

with the existence of social roles. They constitute a collection of “forms” of all individual acts of expression. Within communities, social roles “melt” in the overwhelming melting pot of individual emotional fidelity towards a leader or a utopia. If there are some social roles fulfilled within those communities, they lose their contours entirely through that very mechanism of emotional submission. The roles are not “individual forms” protecting individuals from ridicule, but become ridiculous themselves in their complete alignment with the workings of a community. Within society as association, however, the roles are an anthropological screen, protecting individuals from the risk of society’s overzealous intervention into the sphere of their undefined potentiality. An individual fulfilling role X hides behind a mask and remains within the distance between him and that role, while building up his own identity based on that act of distancing: “I am a bearer of my social role, but simultaneously I am not ‘merely’ a civil servant, teacher, businessman, or politician.” This role allows for a shaky balance between expression and hiding away, which characterizes—according to Plessner—human beings as such. Community demands individuals to surrender the intimacy that stems from social roles, which can be managed to a certain extent, upon entry into its structures. Individuals lose their right to co-define their roles, since all those roles become reduced to one – that of an emissary of a common ideal, or a bearer of collective emotions founding the community around its animator. Such roles do not perform an anthropological, defensive function, nor do they refer back to any hidden potential in individuals. Within the structures of a community, individuals constitute merely a kind of “blank” actualization of their fidelity towards the fundaments of that community. Their anthropological potential fades away, or becomes minimal. What takes place is the annihilation of “individual form” based on distance, and rooted by Plessner in the anthropological condition. 

After that anthropological introduction, Plessner moves on to more detailed analyses of the main types of social relationships in which society/association respects—in a practical dimension—the above-mentioned, *a priori* stipulations of the human condition. 

5\. THE STRUCTURE OF THE PUBLIC SPHERE: 

CEREMONY, PRESTIGE, DIPLOMACY AND TACT 

Plessner gives anthropological, psychological, and sociological sense to the categories listed in the title of this subsection, but a detailed description of each of them would go beyond the scope of our analysis. We will hope to combine them with the conclusions our reflections have led to up to this point. 

We have concluded that “searching for a form” is an inherent characteristic of the human condition, and that the main anthropological function of the form is to “provide individuals with unassailability.” Ceremony and prestige are, in 

*[p. 21]* ^pg23

Plessner’s view, a “path”[^40] leading to it. Ceremony is static in character. It creates “a rigid life order that protects the individual.”[^41] 

“Individuality is classified and subordinated to ceremony; what is general binds an indeterminate multiplicity of persons into a unified conduct governed by objectively regulated forms. As the personal surfaces of friction between persons are reduced to a minimum, so increases simultaneously the security and dignity of the conduct.”[^42] 

 In other words, ceremony makes an individual unassailable within the network of relations, called a game by Plessner; relations in which something “counts as valid” and another as “invalid.”[^43] Breaking rules is humiliating, and following them strengthens man’s dignity. Ceremony grants individuals “representational meaning”[^44] and is compensatory in character towards the individual, who does not reveal his or her undefined interior during the social game, but simultaneously is given an opportunity for self-expression on a public arena. 

Prestige, as opposed to ceremony, is much more individual and dynamic in character. It constitutes a “form of becoming detached” by making it easier to react to the changing circumstances of social life. Unassailability within the ceremony is “formal-abstract,” while prestige “enhances the individual\`s striving for self-assertion.”[^45] Plessner writes about “an individual unassailability—a power not clearly definable, stemming from his personal nature, a power that invests the individual with credit and makes the social environment believe to him. Such a power contrasts with the formal abstract objectively regulated type that ceremony produces. Prestige advances the plans and actions of the person within the cloud of the aura of force (*Kraftnimbus*) specific to him; it produces space and commands respect.”<sup>46</sup> Hence, both ceremony and prestige grant individuals a substitute form of unambiguity, causing their indefinable interior to be recognized socially on a permanent basis. Unlike the community, they allow individuals to be confirmed in a compensatory fashion without revealing their inner lives, and to retain their intimacy. They constitute a barrier for an attitude Gehlen will call pathological expulsion or psychological exhibitionism.[^47] Plessner clearly highlights: “We need inhibition for our own sake. We need restraint and suppression to have difference; and ceremony and prestige demonstrate this significant service through the consideration that they demand of us.”[^48] In

The public sphere, according to Plessner, includes the totality of social interactions, which are not regulated by emotions, sympathies or beliefs, as in communities of the ideal. This is precisely the sphere in which people “become acquainted with one another,” conduct political or commercial negotiations, or converse with each other on a purely casual basis “nurturing purposeless interaction.”[^50] For Plessner, diplomacy and tact constitute the two main modes of action in the public sphere. In his view, diplomacy is concerned with representatives of centers of power/political or economic activity, who are striving to reach a compromise on some issue, and consists in defending the dignity of one’s partner in negotiations, even when the conclusion is forced by concealed threats. It is the art of keeping up appearances, of refraining from humiliating one’s opponent/partner, who is presumed to be in a symmetrical relationship with us as far as the “right” to use the same “cover threats” is concerned. Their suspected presence does not disturb the prestige of all sides involved in the negotiations. Plessner writes: 

“The difference is decisive: whether (in the course of the diplomatic game in which all means are equally available to the other opponent) we attempt to force the other person to declare himself conquered by using strategy and tactics, or whether we use the means of force and violence—themselves alien to the game—or perhaps dissemble false facts to reach the same end.”[^51] 

The sides agree that it is a form of confrontation, they assume that also “struggle for life and death run its course within forms.”[^52] The author refers here to the example of Bismarck’s diplomacy in the period before the unification of Germany, thanks to which ”the expansion of Prussia through the establishment of empire appeared justified; without this background, the expansion would have appeared as the pure self-interest of the state.”[^53] We could add that the thinker is most likely referring to when Bismarck, upon winning the Austro-Prussian War in 1866 (the famous battle of Sadová), did not force any concessions on Austria that would compromise the unassailability of its territory. He also did not demand any painful contributions, which most likely helped in

Tact, on the other hand, is an attitude which, according to Plessner, characterizes “games of a lighter style,”[^54] typical for so-called sociable time and not relations between states or companies, and is understood as a rule of civility that “never permits too much closeness nor too much distance.”[^55] Tact allows for the individual treatment of every man, „it is the willing openness to see others an, in so doing, to remove oneself from the field of vision; it is the willingness to measure others according to their standard and not one\`s own;”[^56] it signifies “a sense for other persons and the ability to not let the other person notice one’s own muted expression.’’[^57] Going back to the title of his work, Plessner concludes that “social radicalism could be described as ethics of tactlessness.”<sup>58</sup> On its basis appears “a fanaticism of ethical laws and a valorization of clarity; a pathetic commitment to unconditional authenticity in expression.”<sup>59</sup> Tact, however, similarly to diplomacy, is guided by an opposite principle, which states that “untruth which succours is always better than truth which damages.”[^60] 

In conclusion, we could say that Plessner ascribes, in a sense, all four concretizations of ways of life in society/association (ceremony, diplomatic, tactful behavior, correlated with prestige) with the function of a necessary condition to uphold the internal balance of the public sphere. Without those, the spirit of the community could gradually absorb it with its cult of violence and ultimate rule of the stronger (in the sense of a privileged nation, civilization, or social class). It is worthwhile to emphasize, that while the community of the ideal is based, according to Plessner, on an emotionally anchored primacy of values, an association respects the primacy of what is situational in social life. Every circumstance is unique, and depending on that situational context, the prestige, tact or diplomatic attitude of an individual will become involved every time and in a particular way. Acting according to established values (considered as *a priori* important), or universal ethical norms that oblige individuals to deliver a “clear message,” does not leave a margin of freedom to act out, and does not agree with the chronic “ambiguity” which characterizes the existence of the individual in the public sphere as understood by Plessner.

6\. THE ANTHROPOLOGICAL AND SOCIOLOGICAL UNDERSTANDING 

OF THE SOCIAL ROLE. PLESSNER’S POLEMIC 

WITH RALF DAHRENDORF 

Plessner returns to that question in an article published in 1960, entitled *Die soziale Rolle und menschliche Natur*. For a historian of philosophy it is a truly interesting text, since it shows how drastically historical contexts of use can change. In *The Limits of Community—Critique of the Communist Ethos* Plessner employed the notion of a role in his critical contradiction between society as association, and certain specific types of community. In *Die soziale Rolle und menschliche Natur* contemporary capitalist society becomes an object of his critique, along with its characteristic, well-developed “functionalization” of social roles that leads to the omission of what Plessner calls the „anthropological understanding of the role.”[^61] 

His main (although mentioned only once, and only in passing) adversary in the context of those reflections is Ralf Dahrendorf, author of the *Homo sociologicus. Ein Versuch zur Geschichte*, *Bedeutung und Kritik der Kategorie der sozialen Rolle*, published in 1958. His work raised strong interest among commentators and critics (including Arnold Gehlen, Helmut Schelsky and Jürgen Habermas). Dahrendorf’s stand could be summarized as follows: 

— Contemporary societies are complex networks/systems of roles and social positions defined as certain functions. Social positions constitute “empty spaces,” which can be occupied by individuals (Talcott Parson’s notion of *achieved status*). For every social status understood and predefined that way there are certain social expectations (“roles”), which a given individual is able to fulfill, or which he or she cannot handle, thereby exposing him or herself to social disapproval. The individual itself, its identity built through a kind of anthropological distance towards the role as assumed by Plessner, remains here entirely in the background. 

Gernot Böhme describes this situation as follows: 

“On the one hand, we are dealing with a multitude of socially normalized and defined ways of behavior, with roles which can be performed by a particular individual, and on the other hand—there is the individual, as if naked, and defined only through the abstract freedom of living up to, or failing to meet the expectations assigned to a given role.”[^62] Dahrendorf describes such circumstance of the individual as “alienation.” It is always “others, who decide what an individual should be socially.”<sup>63</sup> He

Plessner does not define that role through any conception entailing a social sanction to “live up to” society’s demands arising from division of labor understood functionally within the entire society. That role does not allow itself to be reduced to a function ascribed to it within that entirety, but is defined solely in reference to revealing/hiding functions, which it performs for the benefit of the bottomless subjectivity and potentiality of humans. Hence, we are faced here with contradictory definitions of “functions” of social roles. 

 Plessner is aware of those differences, and attempts to identify them notionally in his article. He concludes that in contemporary American sociology—whose fundamental assumptions Dahrendorf seems to be inspired by—the anthropological and functionalistic concepts of “social role” merge in terms of meaning into one. What is more, similarly to Heidegger’s concept of *das Man,* the alienating aspect of all social being can shift to the foreground. It entails that which is social, the social-forced fact of “taking an action” in a particular way can potentially threaten “authentic” human existence (the Heideggerian category of *Eigentlichkeit*) lost in social roles, even when an individual is satisfied with the level of perfection of his or her practice of social roles.[^65] In a society as described by Dahrendorf, individuals identify with roles to such a degree that their identity becomes completely exhausted in a mindset of “achieving social success through fulfilling a given role.” At the moment of experiencing failure in that respect, an individual begins to oppose society, to alienate himself/herself. At the genesis of that attitude Plessner notices the Marxist concept of the self-alienation of man, who should “reconcile with himself”[^66] in a future “free community of producers” liberated from the machinery of capitalist objectification. In capitalism roles are degraded to their alienating aspect, they are forced upon individuals from the outside like the trade value of a commodity. Only a Marxist “jump” from the kingdom of necessity to the kingdom of liberty could return social identity back into the hands of individuals, since it would no longer be assigned by a network of objectified social functions. Writing about society as an *Ärgernis*, Dahrendorf (Plessner appears to assume) gives in to the utopian Hegelian-Marxian calling for anthropological self-reconcilement. 7\. SUMMARY 

Plessner’s above-reconstructed concept provokes many questions, but to answer all of them would require an entirely separate article. Below I would like to limit myself to a list of what in my view are the most important directions in this valuable discussion. 

A. Plessner’s work is essayistic in character, hence many of the questions it touches upon would require to be specified, and could at least use some terminological tuning. The first example that comes to mind is the category of ceremony. Plessner inscribes it into the context of his deliberations on societies as associations. But communities create, and become attached to, their ceremonies as well, and it would be worthwhile to define the structural and functional differences that come into play in this case. 

B. One of the conclusions that arise from Plessner’s critique of Dahrendorf could be brought down to the statement that not every form of civic society constitutes a social niche which respects Plessner’s anthropological principles. What is the status of the category of “society” in the philosophy of Plessner—descriptive, or normative? It is not clear, since in the *Limits of Community …* he seems to be referring to civic society (and community) as to certain historical realities. Meanwhile, Dahrendorf’s claim is that contemporary capitalism is not a form of association in a narrow sense, as understood by Plessner, because social roles lose the anthropological functions he assigned to them. What is more, one could forward the hypothesis that the “capitalism of accomplishments,” which feeds off the mentality of omnipresent competition, becomes more of a community than a civic society in terms of Plessner’s categories, since it “forces” individuals to do more in the form of strictly defined expectations, rather than “allow,” for example in the sphere of self-expression, or build independent identities. Such a society is no longer a collection of various types of creative “games,” as Plessner assumed in the *Limits of Community …*, but 

<sup>67</sup> Ibid., 35. 

*[p. 27]* ^pg29

a system rooted in strategic behaviors as understood by Habermas,[^68] with the internal functions of its particular elements strictly regulated. Since contemporary capitalism is indeed like that, where in the contemporary historical space is Plessner’s civic society, along with its “public sphere,” “hiding”? Maybe one should ask if Plessner’s categories had the character of Weber’s ideal types from the beginning (coming close to Tönnies’s mentioned categories in one of their interpretations), or if the historical transformations and particular “degenerations” of contemporary civic society have caused Plessner’s description to lose its empirical character, forcing it into becoming a kind of normative template for assessment purposes, similar to those employed by representatives of the Critical Theory? In that vein, it could be useful to compare the attitude of Plessner, author of *Limits…* and *Social Role …* with that of Habermas, author of *Die Zukunft der menschlichen Natur* \[*The Future of Human Nature*\]. Habermas poses the question openly, and writes about the “*a priori* conditioning” of morality and the “ethical self-understanding of the species,” employing in this context—in its normative meaning—the term “transcendental necessity.”[^69] In his central work, *Die Stufen des Organischen und der Mensch*, Plessner also addresses the *a priori* model character of his analyses.[^70] Both discussed Plessner works should undergo a more thorough methodological diagnosis in this respect. We should also establish demarcation lines between the descriptive-empirical and the model-normative interpretation. 

C. Plessner’s concept of “the limits of community” is ambiguous. Is it concerned only with the phenomenon of the quantitative “inflexibility” of communities we have pointed to, or with the more general conclusion that a community as such displays “anthropological limits”, which are absent when we deal with societies understood as associations At this point we are entering a field never fully explored by Plessner—the anthropological status of emotional relations within small communities, for example in dual relationships between mother and baby, in work groups, or in communities with a legal status(e.g. minorities). Is Plessner\`s principle of eccentric position and search for “form” also valid for such relationships?
