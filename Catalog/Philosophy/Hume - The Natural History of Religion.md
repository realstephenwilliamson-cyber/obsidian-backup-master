## About

David Hume's essay on the causes, effects and history of religious belief, first published in 1757 as the opening piece of his *Four Dissertations*. It traces religious belief to hope, fear and ignorance.

- **1757:** *Four Dissertations*, London, printed for A. Millar. The other three pieces were "Of the Passions", "Of Tragedy" and "Of the Standard of Taste".
- **How the volume was formed:** according to the editorial notes of Hume Texts Online, the planned fourth piece was an essay on the metaphysical principles of geometry, withdrawn after Lord Stanhope found a fault in it. Told the remaining three were too short, Hume sent "Of Suicide" and "Of the Immortality of the Soul", then regretted them; he and Millar suppressed them, and he wrote "Of the Standard of Taste" in their place.
- **Argument:** a crude polytheism was humanity's first religion (NHR 1.1), born of hope and fear about the future. Monotheism arose as believers magnified their own god above rivals, and the mind moves in a "flux and reflux" between idolatry and theism (NHR 8.1).
- **Polytheism and theism compared:** Wikipedia says Hume held an enlightened monotheism more defensible in reason, but in practice more intolerant than polytheism and more given to mortification and passive suffering. The SEP stresses his concern with theism's intolerance and hostility to liberty.
- **Ending and reception:** Hume ends in scepticism, finding doubt and suspension of judgment the only results of careful inquiry. In *My Own Life* (1776) he says the essay was received obscurely except for a hostile pamphlet by Dr Hurd, whom Wikipedia links to the Warburtonian school.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it a naturalist account of religion. The SEP reads its genealogy as the same conflict Hume stages in the *Dialogues* between Cleanthes's anthropomorphism and Demea's mysticism.
