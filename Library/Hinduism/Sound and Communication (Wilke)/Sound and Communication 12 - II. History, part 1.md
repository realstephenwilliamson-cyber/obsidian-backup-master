age of the text is of no importance for this book, because historical dates do not automatically communicate findings concerning the factual life at that time. In arts subjects and cultural history dates only become truly relevant when they are linked to known processes in mankind’ s mental history. After all, there is an essential difference in whether a particular sentence was uttered at the time of the Crusades or during the Enlightenment. But as long as prehistory remains pre-history and we know as little about a culture, which flourished around 4000 BC as we do about a culture, that did so around 2500 BC, numbers like these remain devoid of meaning because they do not relate to any identifiable stages in cultural processes. By contrast, much more important for our observations is the significance of the Veda for the history of spiritual development in India, and in this respect the great age of the text plays a not inconsiderable role. Practically all religious movements relevant in India today regard the Veda as something primeval and they have tried to find some sort of link with this ancient text.° In botha historical and a spiritual sense, the Veda is thus the root of the cultural memory and the cultural identity of Sanskrit Hinduism, in quite the sense in which Jan Assmann determined cultural memory: “societies imagine self-images and over succeeding generations they propagate an identity in which they form a culture of memory.”\* This culture of memory provides identity, because it is “memory of a commonly inhabited past.” 

This cultural memory is not about history as it happened, but history as it is remembered. And in the case of the Veda, what is remembered is not even merely legendary, but already highly mythical owing to the cryptic nature of the text. The text has been passed down to us, but its historical setting has been completely forgotten. So the Veda is even more heavily clothed in imaginings and has itself finally become a myth. For example, the Veda is often interpreted as the basis and origin of all culture, even as the origin of our world, in some cases as causa materialis, as Brahman.

All these factors ultimately led to the Veda being placed not only at the beginning of the world, but to it being regarded as quite outside our world and outside time. Many Indian philosophers have held the view that the text never even came into being, but has always existed. For this reason it has no author or creator in the form of a person (purusa), neither a god nor a human being; it is rather the case that the Veda is a “non-personal” revelation (apauruseya-śruti). This has also become a popular folk belief, and some people regard the view that the Veda has no author as the fundamental credo of Hinduism. The Vedic tradition itself supports this interpretation, as it emphasizes that the words of the Veda were “seen”, i.e. perceived intuitively. Unlike the Quran, which according to tradition was given to Muhammed by the angel Gabriel, and also in contrast to the Christian Bible, which is traditionally regarded as inspired by a personal god, the Veda text was “perceived,” in the same way that Plato perceived his eternal ideas. 

Despite the incomprehensibility of the language in large sections of the texts, in this way the Veda became a class suí generis and finally an end in itself. The preservation of the text in its original form was the most important desideratum and there were and still are some schools whose sole function is to teach Brahmin priests the Veda by heart. But note that in these Veda schools — similar to the Quran schools — it is not the ‘sense’ of the Veda that is taught, but only its recitation. The Indian tradition has rarely found it problematic that this results in many Brahmins being able to recite the Veda vocally while few know the meaning of its content. Even if they recite words that do not make much more sense to them than “abracadabra” does to us, this is in no way detrimental to the sacredness of the text. In contrast to Western concentration on scriptures and on Jogos, in India the spoken (performed) word and the word as sound material have their own validity. Even today in Brahmin circles it is common to recite Vedic texts, whether in daily prayer, at certain festivities or during rituals in the temple. And with today’s popular audio media, which play such an important role in the religious life of the NRI (“non-resident Indian”) — i.e. an Indian computer expert living in the USA — for example, while driving to work, in addition to the modern devotional songs it is often the old Veda recitations that are heard and that sweeten a manager’s hectic life by providing at least 10 minutes of religion.

This results, for instance, in a group of Brahmins being able recite a text in total synchronicity, without having rehearsed it beforehand in any way. All the pitches and the lengths of the notes are absolutely predetermined by the phonetic structure of the text, that is, by the accentuation of the words and the length of the syllables. However, there is one thing that is common to all Veda schools: the sound structure of the recitation is determined by the semi-musical enhancement of the characteristic intonation and the characteristic rhythm of the Vedic Sanskrit language. Although the melodies have not been composed, instead being solely dependent on the structure of the language — that is, are determined by something outside music, there is still no state of total chaos, as is sometimes found in modern experimental forms of music, for example when a computer generates completely random sounds. Certain musical motifs that are set by traditional rules (i.e. the rules governing the association of accents and the lengths of syllables with the pitches and durations of the notes) and by the language itself (relationships between lengths and shortnesses, average word lengths, etc.), are continually repeated and at the same time generate specific melodies, while other motifs are prevented from occurring by the rules.

Finally, there is a third mode of reception of Vedic Sūktas that can be called completely artistic. Veda recitation can in fact simply exist as a musical accompaniment to festivities of all types — religious and non-religious — or it can occupy the center of interest as an artistic performance in its own right. At such occasions, it is neither the content nor the specific ritual function that are especially important, but only the mood elicited. The recitation promotes emotional involvement, similar to the function of a school orchestra in Germany providing festive background music for the school-leavers’ celebration. Of course, such occasions as these require the reciters not only to know the Vedic mantras by heart, but also to perform them in a pleasing fashion. Traditional phonetics (९7८5) has in fact formulated numerous rules for a pleasing Veda recital. These rules teach musicalizing phonetic manipulations that turn the text into “language-music” that exists in its own right, valid without semantic sense or ritual function. Although probably only very few Brahmins have read the 51155 works, the rules have become flesh and blood to them via the process of imitation.

If one wants to read the Veda as a historical document, the textual and conceptual unity disintegrates into various texts and text types from different cultural epochs. 

The Rk-Samhitā, a collection (samhitā) of hymns (rc) to the gods, forms the oldest section of the Veda. This decidedly archaic text is characterized by mentioning a large number of deities, who were often interpreted in the research as personifications of natural forces and ethical principles. But in fact the Rk-Samhitā (known as Rgveda) is the product of a culture that comes exceptionally close to Kurt Hübner’ s ideal mythical world orientation. Unlike the later Hinduistic deities, the Vedic deities still coincide very precisely with Hübner’s characterization of the early Greek gods. For example, just as the Greek goddess Demeter is in every ear of corn, the Vedic goddess Uṣas (“dawn”) is in every dawn. But there are also more complex deities, e.g. Varuṇa, the god of the rta (“rightness”), who is present in every contract, that is, everywhere where rights are concerned. He also inhabits the starry sky, where every star unerringly follows its “right” path and he is furthermore related to water, since water never misses its “right” direction — a river for instance invariably meeting the ocean. The Vedic deities are not “persons” and are correspondingly not imagined in anthropomorphic forms. Instead they are consistently imagined mythogemes, almost “soul substances” which, unlike our idea of souls, can also inhabit physical events (such as “the dawn”) and even abstract terms (such as “rightness”). As all these things that we consider lifeless are actually animate beings to the authors of the Rk-Samhitā, they can also speak to them and ask them for help. 

Because what is “personal” in this early text is defined completely via mythical substance, a hermeneutics of the Rk-Samhitā is a difficult undertaking for us modern people, and this fact has led to manifold, in some cases diametrically opposed, interpretations. The language of the early hymns has neither pure abstract ideas nor clearly defined concrete ideas. Just as the Greek Gaia can signify either an Earth goddess or simply earth, in the Rk-Samhitā one can never distinguish clearly whether it is now speaking of a numen or an object that is inhabited by this numen. 2.3.1 

##### 2.3.1 Hymns, the Food of the Gods

The sole purpose of the hymns of the Rk-Samhitā was apparently to aid communication with the gods, even when they spoke about the gods. They were thought to be formulations of visionary insight, whose creators, the kavis, were also simultaneously poets, priests and ecstatics. The prevailing belief was that only particularly inspired words reached the gods at all. Therefore such words in the ancient Vedic ritual possessed a special meaning for those priests whose task it was to call upon the gods during the sacrifice. These priest families of otrs (“callers”) began to collect certain especially well-done hymns (rc) and to pass them on with care. At some stage far back in time these family collections were collated, and so gradually the work that we know today as Rk-Samhitā came into being. 

It is popular in the early Veda to think of the sacrifice as a banquet for the gods which included also the intoxicating soma drink. Although real dishes were offered, the hymn of praise was the true food for the gods. “Good thought” (mati) was offered to the god in the same way as food, so that he could partake of it, be strengthened and delight in it. This concept, too, of language as “food” can actually only be understood using the mythical notion of the arché. According to Kurt Hübner, a godďd’s Arché is that which marks the god out as something regular, for example, which allows sunrise and sunset to be ascribed to a god of the sunrise and a god of the sunset. The prime position at the beginning corresponds symbolically to Agni’s coming first in the holy year, which is expressed at various levels: astrologically, ritually and often also mythologically. At the start of all human culture there is the possession of the (hearth) fire.

According to Tatyana J. Elizarenkova,”# the anagrammatical form of this first hymn in the Rgveda sets the program for the whole Rgveda. The whole is peppered with onomatopoeia, alliteration, assonance, ellipses and parallelisms, which occur particularly often along with the names of deities. According to Elizarenkova this phonetic peculiarity is largely responsible for the “magic poetry” or “poetic magic” of the text. The principle of alliterative poetry is so intensively applied that one can actually speak of a semanticization of sound sequences.® Language is a symbolic reflection of content. The initial position of Agni in the verse examined is not merely a case of assonance, but also suggests his/its “always coming first.” Expressive messages are thus imparted not only semantically, but also at the sonic or phonetic level. 

The use of such “poetic magic” is especially obvious in the case of magic spells. The use of magic was not uncommon in Vedic times. Most of the corresponding text material is found in a separate Samhitā for private use, the Atharva-Veda. Such magic spells are often constructed so that the desired effect is evoked by the constant repetition of certain key words and is anticipated by suggestion. In the rite given below, a jealous wife tries to eliminate her rival, and to do this she buries a certain plant under her bed. The following words are spoken over the plant, and according to Āpastamba they must be repeated three times every evening before the woman sleeps with her husband:

2.3.4 

##### 2.3.4 The Keeper of the Brahman

Interestingly, the evocative power that is located in the magic poetry of the Rk-Samhitā, that is Brahman, is itself not a god — and this in a culture, where nearly everything seems to be deified — in total contrast to later periods when on the one hand the Brahman was personified as the creator god Brahmā, and on the other regarded as the impersonal Absolute. However, here it is subordinated to a “keeper” (pati) and thus almost allocated to a “divine speaker”, namely the Brahmaṇaspati, also known as Brhaspati. He is the “keeper of the Brahman,” who “creates all the words of blessing” (RV 2.23.2)“ and to whom they “cry out” and “flow” (RV 10.68.1, 1.190.2). He is the numinous being that has precisely that special affinity to Brahman, which enables him to invent the powerful formula — the Brahman (RV 10.67.5); due to this affinity the Brahman occurs to him spontaneously, and at the same time it radiates from him. The song of Brhaspati is powerful, “thundering like the heavens” (RV 10.67.5), and the explosive force of the tones is always emphasized in his singing. 

In his divine and numinous identity, Brhaspati is represented as a kind of fabulous being: he is reddish and golden and has a blue back. With a “clear voice” he speaks out of “seven mouths,” i.e. in the seven meters of hymn-writing. He is the “poet of poets.” He has “sharp horns,” which indicates his bull-like nature, that is his (pro-)creative power. He moves with a “hundred wings,” possibly a reference to the otherworldly origin of sacred language and its visionary power. He bears an axe made of gold and a metal cleaver, along with arrows and a bow. The sinew of the bow is righteousness (qta).

##### 2.3.5 Language as a Goddess

In the early Rk-Samhitā, language is only thematized when the topic is powerful poetic speech and thus evocative, magic language - that is, bráhman. But in the later hymns of the Rk-Samhitā not only the Brahman, but also the whole Vāc, that is every type of language and sound, is treated as something numinous. Interestingly, as soon as the numinous identity of Vāc becomes the topic — in contrast to Brahman — it appears from the very beginning as a goddess in a personal form.
