### 3.1–6: Healing on the Sabbath

*[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-1|Text]], p. 76* · ([[Matthew 12#^12-9|Mt 12.9–14]]; [[Luke 06#^6-6|Lk 6.6–11]]). Jesus responds with another Sabbath exception, also found in rabbinic tradition: “Any danger to life overrides the prohibitions of the Sabbath” (*m. Yoma* 8.6). The *Damascus Document*, associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls, is less flexible concerning saving human lives on the Sabbath (CD 11.16–17). ^3-1

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-4|4]]:** Although Jesus characterizes the healing terms of *good* or *harm*, *save life* or *kill*, the man’s condition is not life-threatening, and so it is perhaps metaphorical as well of a saved life. ^3-4

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-5|5]]:** *Hardness of heart*, here calls to mind the hardening of Pharaoh’s heart. At [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^4-21|Ex 4.21]] God hardens Pharaoh’s heart, while at [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^8-11|Ex 8.15]],[[Torah 14 - Vaera#^8-28|32]] Pharaoh hardens his own heart. At 4.12 Mark quotes [[Isaiah 06#^6-10|Isa 6.10–11]] in a way suggesting that God controls the hardening. *Was restored*, the passive may indicate that Jesus did nothing that could be considered “work.” ^3-5

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-6|6]]:** Only here does Mark say that the Pharisees tried to *destroy* Jesus. In the Gospels, it is generally the chief priests, scribes, and elders who seek his execution (14.1–2), and that only in regard to the Passion narrative (8.31). *Herodians*, supporters of Herod Antipas, Rome’s client-ruler of Galilee (6.17–29). ^3-6

### 3.7–12: Summary of Jesus’ successes

*[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-7|Text]], p. 76* · ([[Matthew 12#^12-15|Mt 12.15–21]]; [[Luke 04#^4-41|Lk 4.41]]; [[Luke 06#^6-17|6.17–19]]). [[Isaiah 02#^2-1|Isa 2.1–4]] predicts the ingathering of Israel and the streaming of Gentiles to Jerusalem; here that process is focused on Jesus and not Jerusalem. The reference seems primarily (if not exclusively) to be to Diaspora Jews. ^3-7

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-11|11–12]]:** *Unclean spirits*, see 1.22–24n. In ancient exorcisms, it was usually the exorcist who spoke, not the demons (cf. 1.24–25). Jesus must respond forcibly to the demons by silencing them, which is likely related to the theme of the messianic secret (see Introduction, p. 57, on the messianic secret). *Son of God*, see 1.9–11n. ^3-11

### 3.13–19a: Appointing the twelve

*[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-13|Text]], p. 77* · ([[Matthew 10#^10-1|Mt 10.1–4]]; [[Luke 06#^6-12|Lk 6.12–16]]). ^3-13

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-14|14]]:** The *twelve* suggests the twelve tribes of Israel, a symbolic number representing the people of God. The twelve are disciples (Gk “mathētai,” originally “learners, students” who gathered around a teacher); they are here named *apostles* (Gk “apostolos,” “one sent out” as a representative or to carry out a mission). In the NT, only one woman, Tabitha in [[Acts 09#^9-36|Acts 9.36]], is explicitly called a disciple, although Jesus had female followers (15.40; 16.1). Paul claimed the status of apostle (Gal 1–2; see also [[Isaiah 08#^8-16|Isa 8.16]]; [[Isaiah 50#^50-4|50.4]]; [[Isaiah 54#^54-13|54.13]]). The Hebrew term “lammud,” “one who is taught,” may refer to disciples. Elisha, one of the sons of the prophets (e.g., [[II Kings 02#^2-3|2 Kings 2.3]]) is depicted as a disciple of Elijah, called his “father” ([[II Kings 02#^2-12|2 Kings 2.12]]). ^3-14

### 3.19b–35: Beelzebul and Satan

*[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-19|Text]], p. 77* · ([[Matthew 12#^12-22|Mt 12.22–32]],[[Matthew 12#^12-46|46–50]]; [[Luke 08#^8-19|Lk 8.19–21]]; [[Luke 11#^11-14|11.14–23]]; [[Luke 12#^12-10|12.10]]). ^3-19

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-21|21]]:** *His family . . . restrain him*, the Gospels portray Jesus’ relatives as misunderstanding him or opposing his ministry ([[Matthew 12#^12-46|Mt 12.46–50]]; [[Luke 08#^8-19|Lk 8.19–21]]). ^3-21

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-22|22]]:** *Beelzebul*, a name for Satan derived from the Canaanite high god Baal, meaning perhaps “Baal, the prince” ([[II Kings 01#^1-2|2 Kings 1.2]],[[II Kings 01#^1-3|3]] spells the name “Baal-zebub,” “lord of flies,” an insulting parody). This passage shows how charges of magic develop: the perceived miracles of one group are ascribed to demonic forces by another. ^3-22

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-23|23–27]]:** The *parables* refute the accusation of demonic power by drawing an analogy with a human *kingdom* working at cross-purposes. Jesus then draws another analogy: one who has bound a *strong man* so that his property *can be plundered* is analogous to Jesus who plunders the demonic world by defeating Satan. ^3-23

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-28|28–30]]:** Holiness in Israel was associated with God and the Temple, with angels, and occasionally with the whole nation ([[Torah 17 - Yitro#^19-6|Ex 19.6]]) or prophets; cf. [[II Kings 04#^4-9|2 Kings 4.9]]; [[Mark 06#^6-20|Mk 6.20]]. Here the *Holy Spirit* appears to be associated with prophecy among the followers of Jesus, and it is not understood as part of the trinity; that doctrine was developed later (but see [[Matthew 28#^28-19|Mt 28.19]]). See note at [[Mark 01#^1-40|Mk 1.40–45]]. In some Jewish apocalypses (*1 En.* 38.2), the Holy Spirit was also the divine power that brought the new community into existence. In rabbinic texts, unforgivable sins were sins against God and the sanctity of the community (*m. Avot* 3.12, “R. Eleazar of Modiim said: If a man profanes the hallowed things and despises the set feasts and puts his fellow to shame publicly and makes void the covenant of Abraham our father, and discloses meanings in the Law which are not according to the *Halakah*, even though a knowledge of the Law and good works are his, he has no share in the world to come”). ^3-28

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-28|28]]:** *Truly*, Hebrew “amen,” which was retained in the Greek; see 1.15n. Jesus uses “amen” not in the typical biblical and Jewish way as an affirmation of *apostles* b Other ancient authorities lack *So he appointed the twelve* another’s prayer but as a solemn affirmation of what he is about to say (see [[I Kings 01#^1-36|1 Kings 1.36]] and [[Jeremiah 28#^28-6|Jer 28.6]], where the prophet uses “Amen” at the beginning of his speech to relate \[probably ironically\] to the previous speech of the prophet Hananiah). ^3-28-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 03#^3-31|31–35]]:** In new religious movements, members are often called “brothers and sisters” to establish themselves as a new family; see 10.29–30n. ^3-31
