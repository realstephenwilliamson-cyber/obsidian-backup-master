### 11.1–36: The preservation of Israel and the inclusion of the nations

*[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-1|Text]], p. 308* · Jews who do not believe the gospel are not rejected, but by God’s design they have not yet been persuaded. However inscrutable this design might be, God is preserving (keeping safe/saving) all Israel, that is, all Jews (and it seems throughout these chapters that Paul is indicating the same for all the rest of Israel, i.e., those from the northern tribes who were “lost” after the Assyrian conquest). Gentiles who respond faithfully fulfill their own responsibility to the success of this design. Paul now discusses divided Israel, continuing the allegory of heralds to the nations, some running successfully while others are stumbling. But the thrust of the argument is to show non-Jews how their inclusion in God’s plan for Israel’s restoration is proceeding, unexpectedly being included before all Israelites have joined Paul in the task of declaring this news. Paul hopes to provoke empathy for those Israelites suffering this stumbling now. He creates an allegory of grafting a wild shoot onto an olive tree, some of whose branches have been broken (bent), to explain the precarious place of these non-Jews in God’s design. The broken branches are being “protected” (“saved,” “kept safe”) by a callus until the wild shoot’s graft takes hold and ensures that it too can bear fruit for God. The present alienation of some Israelites serves to bring about the inclusion of these non-Jews. Based on the promises of God to the ancestors, even the stumbling Jews are beloved and their preservation is assured. ^11-1

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-1|1]]:** Diatribe continues with a question of whether Israel has been “pushed back,” which Paul denies ( [[Psalms 094#^94-14|Ps 94.14]]). Paul’s argument since 9.30 is that some Jews have stumbled and not reached the goal to which Torah pointed, that of being heralds of the news of Christ. Rather than *rejected* (Gk “apō-sato”; 11.1), “pushed away,” is preferable, keeping the idea that though the way that Israel will complete its role of bringing the message to the nations may be unfolding differently than most expected that God had planned, the Jewish people as a whole remain included in the kingdom (vv 11–12). Paul points to himself as proof that one cannot deny Israel’s inclusion, for he is an Israelite, a seed of Abraham (i.e., one who has been circumcised when eight days old), and even declares his tribe, Benjamin. ^11-1-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-2|2]]:** *Foreknew*, in the sense of knew from before, not knew in advance. Elijah does not plead against Israel, but for his own safety from unfaithful Israelites. ^11-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-3|3]]:** See [[I Kings 19#^19-10|1 Kings 19.10]]. ^11-3

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-4|4]]:** See [[I Kings 19#^19-18|1 Kings 19.18]]. ^11-4

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-5|5]]:** Cf. 9.29; 1 QS 8.6. *Grace*, see 1.5. ^11-5

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-6|6]]:** Israelites who are faithfully proclaiming the gospel, like Paul, were not chosen to do so because of their actions (possibly, completion of rites); this is the result of God’s choice for how things should unfold, God’s gifting; cf. 9.16. *Grace*, a gift of benefaction, see 1.5n. ^11-6

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-7|7]]:** See 9.6. *Failed*, not in Gk; *were hardened*, lit., “were being callused.” See 11.25n. ^11-7

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-8|8]]:** Loose citation of [[Torah 50 - Ki Tavo#^29-3|Deut 29.4]] conflated with [[Isaiah 29#^29-10|Isa 29.10]], *it is no longer on the basis of grace, otherwise work would no longer be work* Paul often cites parts of verses and sometimes juxtaposes two different verses, and, as here, from two different books. [[Torah 50 - Ki Tavo#^29-1|Deut 29.2–3]] tells of Israel not yet recognizing the mysterious way that God used Pharaoh. *This day*, signals the end of the lack of perception. Isa 29 expresses a similar theme about Judah’s prophets failing to perceive Judah’s initial deliverance from the nations (vv 9–14), while deaf and blind Israelites understand (vv 17–21), with a message of preservation of those who have erred or grumbled (vv 22–24). ^11-8

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-9|9–10]]:** Paul draws from [[Psalms 069#^69-23|Ps 69.22–23]], the Writings/Ketuvim, linking this with Deut 29 from the Torah, and Isa 29 from the Prophets/Neviʾim, by way of the link word “eyes” present in each. ^11-9

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-11|11–16]]:** Paul addresses the non-Jewish audience of the letter directly, not via diatribe. He explains that he declares the glory of his ministry to the nations in the hope that he will provoke his fellow, currently stumbling Israelites to jealousy of (the success of) his ministry, so that they will want to emulate him by joining him in declaring this message of Christ to the nations. It is neither the salvation of the non-Jews per se that he seeks to declare, nor is it jealousy of the non-Jews that Paul seeks to stir; rather, it is his own success among the non-Jews that he magnifies, and thus he seeks to provoke jealousy of his ministry among the nations. Paul thinks of the fellow Israelites who are stumbling as his own flesh; how he knows himself is inseparable from his embodiment with them as Israel. Whether Paul calculated this or even realized the implications, the non-Jews might feel slighted by this statement, both because his expression (“my flesh”) keeps alive the idea that the Gentile followers are not a part of Israel or fellow-members with Paul; even his ministry among them is in the service of a goal beyond themselves, although certainly it is for them too. The verbs are subjunctive in v 14, expressing the action or the event as something one wishes “would” happen, and follow a conditional conjunction: this is the outcome that Paul hopes will result from his strategy, but he is not certain of it. Gk “ethnoi,” i.e., “Gentiles” or “nations,” should be translated “nations” throughout both to reflect the dynamics in view for Paul and his target Roman audience of members from the nations other than Israel as well as to highlight the divided nation Israel at issue in this argument. ^11-11

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-11|11]]:** Quotations of judgment in vv 8–10 provoke introduction of the diatribe question of whether these Israelites (“the rest”) have stumbled (been unfaithful) and thus will not reach the goal of proclaiming God’s righteousness to the nations — which he emphatically denies. Paul draws heavily on Isa 8; 28; see [[Romans 09#^9-30|Rom 9.30–33]]. Second case of the translation *stumbling*, however here lit., “misstep, delay.” *To make Israel*, lit., “them”; “Israel” not in Gk. ^11-11-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-12|12]]:** *Stumbling*, lit., “misstep, delay”; *defeat*, better “lagging behind”; *full inclusion*, better “completion (of the course/task).” ^11-12

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-14|14]]:** Lit., “If somehow I might make my flesh (i.e., those sharing my ethnic descent) jealous.” Paul thinks of the fellow Israelites who are stumbling as his own flesh. *Save*, or protect, see 1.16. ^11-14

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-15|15]]:** *Rejection*, better “loosing pace.” *Reconciliation of the world, what will their acceptance be*, lit., “what (results from) gaining of them (back to participation).” ^11-15

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-16|16]]:** These two comparisons — of the meal offering and of the root and branches — to the whole of Israel serve as a transition to the olive tree allegory. Paul has been working with an image of heralds sent to the nations, some having tripped — so three comparisons are at work in Rom 11. The first allegory is not developed after it is introduced: [[Torah 31 - Emor#^23-14|Lev 23.14]] mandates that offering the first of the dough makes the rest of the dough holy; [[Torah 37 - Sh'lach#^15-17|Num 15.17–18]] explains that when a cake made from the first of the dough is offered to God, then the rest of the cakes can be eaten by others who are profane, i.e., not priests. Paul uses the allegory to say that holiness of the first part offered extends to the whole: the whole (Israel) is made holy, i.e., set apart to God, by the dedicated first part, although he does not say who that is (e.g., the fathers \[cf. v 28\], or the remnant \[cf. v 5\], or the presently stumbling \[cf. v 11\]). The second allegory is developed in the olive tree comparison. Paul does not define the root or the branches, but simply makes the point that if the root is set apart to God, then so too are the branches growing from it. ^11-16

### 11.17–24: The olive tree allegory

*[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-17|Text]], p. 310* · This confronts any temptation of arrogance among the Christ-following Gentiles audience toward the Israelites who are not Christ-proclaimers. ^11-17

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-17|17]]:** The wild shoot is singular. *Were broken off*, lit., “were broken/bent”; *were grafted in their place*, lit., “being grafted among them”; *in their place*, not in Gk; the wild shoot is described as grafted among some branches that have been injured, placed into the same bough of the tree, which is how olive tree grafting works. ^11-17-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-18|18]]:** *Remember that*, not in Gk. ^11-18

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-19|19]]:** Similar tree metaphors are used in [[Isaiah 10#^10-5|Isa 10.5–15]] and 37.24–25,30–32, whereby the king of Assyria expresses similar presumptuousness, which will be turned against him. *Broken off*, lit., “broken/bent.” ^11-19

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-20|20]]:** *That is true*, lit., “Well” (i.e., “OK \[but\]”). *Broken off*, lit., “They were *you* c Gk lacks *of Israel* being broken to \[represent\] the unfaithful, but you were established to \[represent\] the faithful.” ^11-20

> [!note] Grafting the Olive Branch (ROM 11.17–24) *(p. 310)*
> The olive tree allegory is designed not to describe Israel per se, but to present the precarious place of the wild shoot among Israelites. Since it suggests that some Israelites have been cut off in order to make the a fortiori case that this wild shoot is all the more vulnerable to being cut off, this comparison conflicts with that of some heralds stumbling, though they have not fallen, and the overall message that all Israel will be preserved (kept safe during this threatening process). In vv 17–21, Paul uses *ekkla*ō to refer to the “broken” branches; however, unless specifically referred to as “broken *off*,” *ekklaō* can refer to something broken, “dislocated,” or “bent, but not severed”; see [[Torah 24 - Vayikra#^1-17|Lev 1.17]]; Pausanias, *Descr.* 8.40.2. In v 22, when Paul warns the wild branch (which has indeed suffered being cut off and grafted in) that it could be severed if it looks arrogantly upon the broken branches, Paul switches to *ekkoptō*, “cut off,” and he again uses *ekkoptō* in v 24. That leads to the impression that Paul regards some natural branches as broken off, although that is an inference that does not seem to be intended when he begins the allegory; it is rather an inference developed from the effort to rebuke the wild shoot, which has been cut off from its tree to be grafted in, and thus is in a precarious place compared to the natural branches. See [[Isaiah 27#^27-2|Isa 27.2–11]]. The tree is not described as Israel, and Paul does not suggest that non-Israelites become members of Israel. The tree represents all who are in the family of God, Israelite branches as well as one from other nations.

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-21|21]]:** *Perhaps*, not in some manuscripts. ^11-21

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-22|22]]:** The natural branches described as “cut off” (Gk “ekklaō”), rather than merely “broken” (Gk “ekkoptō”). This change highlights the severity of the fate of the wild shoot if it should be presumptuous. ^11-22

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-23|23]]:** Isaiah, in the midst of plant and tree metaphors (see 11.19n.), portrays God miraculously preserving Israel. *Of Israel*, not in Gk; *and even those*, lit., “but also they.” *If they do not persist in unbelief*, lit., “if they would not continue to \[represent\] the unfaithfulness \[of not proclaiming the message to Gentiles\]. *Has the power*, lit., “is able.” ^11-23

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-24|24]]:** *Their own olive tree*, the tree from which the natural branches grew, unlike the wild shoot grafted among them. ^11-24

### 11.25–35: Further reflections on the cryptic process by which God is accomplishing the protection of Israel and salvation of the nations

*[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-25|Text]], p. 311* ^11-25

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-25|25–26]]:** Paul changes from developing a diatribe with the wild branch and entirely in allegorical terms to direct instruction to the Christ-following non-Jews although still drawing metaphorical elements from the allegory, urging them not to be arrogant but instead to recognize that the grace with which they have been blessed should be internalized and turned to regard the Israelite others suffering these various metaphorically described present weaknesses with a spirit of grace. ^11-25-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-25|25]]:** *A hardening has come upon*, lit., “a callus (“pōrōsis”; not the same as in Pharaoh’s heart being hardened \[“sklērynō”\] in 9.17–18, and in Ex) has formed for (the protection of).” *Part of*, lit., “for a while, temporarily” (“apo merous”); thus, “a callus has developed temporarily for Israel,” i.e., broken branches are protected as the sap creates a callus that keeps them alive (cf. vv 17 and 26). *Has come in,* or “commenced.” *Full number of the Gentiles has come in*, lit., “fullness of the nations commences/begins”; the time when the messengers of Israel (such as Paul) turn specifically to addressing fully the non-Jews, or the time when the grafted-in branch begins to bear fruit fully; traditionally interpreted to indicate the parousia or return of Christ following the evangelizing of all of the nations or complete number of converts from the nations, but that is not necessarily Paul’s view, since he appears to be envisioning a process that will be completed within his own lifetime and as a result of his own ministry. ^11-25-3

> [!note] Protection of Israel (ROM 11.26–27) *(p. 311)*
> Paul adapts and conflates elements from [[Isaiah 59#^59-20|Isa 59.20–21]] (closer to LXX than MT) with [[Isaiah 27#^27-9|Isa 27.9]] for the last clause. Isa 59–61 are full of plant metaphors, as are Isa 27–28. The context of Isa 59 is God coming to the rescue of Israel when no one else does. The context of Isa 27 is the gathering of Israelites from the dispersion (vv 12–13 involved the announcement of regathering). [[Isaiah 27#^27-6|Isa 27.6]] states: “In days to come Jacob shall take root, Israel shall blossom and put forth shoots, and fill the whole world with fruit.” The verb (*sōthēsetai*) “will be preserved/protected/healed,” or “will be kept safe/saved,” is also a common description of tree life, of being protected during stressful developments (such as the breaking of limbs) so as to produce fruit; Paul uses it to describe the threatening current situation wherein some Israelite messengers do not (yet) join with him to fulfill Israel’s purpose of restoring the nations to the Creator God. They might be so put off by the arrogance of the Gentiles who respond positively that they denounce the enterprise entirely. All Israel ([[Romans 11#^11-26|Rom 11.26]]), whose divided state has been the topic, refers to Israel according to the flesh, Jews (and perhaps all other Israelites from the northern tribes too), not “spiritual Israel” or the Jesus followers inclusive of Gentiles. In metaphorical terms, the wild shoot does not become a natural branch — the Gentile does not become an Israelite — even though it will draw from the same root (God).

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-26|26–27]]:** *And so*, lit., “And in this way” or “And then.” *Will be saved*, better, “will be protected/preserved (during stumbling/brokenness), healed, kept safe/saved (to be the messengers)”; see 1.16. Paul’s argument proceeds from the reasoning that those Israelites who have not undertaken (yet) the covenant obligation of bringing God’s words to the nations alongside himself are nevertheless being protected by God presently even if suffering some metaphorically described disabilities for a while as the process unfolds. He does not describe these fellow Israelites as if they needed to be re-admitted to covenant standing, although that is the traditional view, but as protected or preserved in their covenantal standing, being disciplined therein as a part of the process of bringing them faithfully to realize that they should now undertake their entrusted role as understood by Paul. Paul writes “out of/from Zion” instead of LXX “to Zion,” or MT “for the sake of Zion,” which may reflect Rome’s Diaspora situation. *He will banish*, lit., “he will turn away.” *Deliverer*, [[Isaiah 59#^59-20|Isa 59.20]] proclaims a “redeemer” (Heb “goʾel”); 59.19 speaks of the “LORD” (Heb “YHWH”; Gk “kyrios”). ^11-26

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-29|29]]:** *Irrevocable*, lit., “not regretted”; see e.g., 3.1–2. ^11-29

**[[NRSVue Romans 11#^11-32|32]]:** *Has imprisoned*, or “has joined together”; *all in disobedience*, lit., “everyone into a not-being-persuaded-state;” *so that he may be merciful to all*, or “in order that everyone might be (equally in need of being) shown mercy.” ^11-32
