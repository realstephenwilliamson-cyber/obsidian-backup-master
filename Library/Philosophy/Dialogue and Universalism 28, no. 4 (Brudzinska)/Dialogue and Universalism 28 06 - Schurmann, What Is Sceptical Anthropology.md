## Schurmann PO KOREKCIE

*Volker Schürmann* 

WHAT IS SCEPTICAL ANTHROPOLOGY?[^1] 

*ABSTRACT* 

The article compares the philosophies of Ernst Cassirer and Helmuth Plessner concerning the modes of modernity. Plessner is one of those thinkers, who are most consistent in accepting modernity, whereas Cassirer is not. The point that generates this different level is the explicit self-reflection of Plessner in contrast to a systematic silence of Cassirer. One can see this difference by analysing the role of scepticism within these two modes of anthropology. 

Keywords: anthropology, skepticism, modernity, Helmuth Plessner, Ernst Cassirer. 

1\. WHAT IS MY MAIN THESIS? 

The philosophical anthropologists are, of course, modern thinkers, i.e. thinkers of modernity. Perhaps this could be discussed further in Max Scheler’s case, but even his somewhat metaphysical standpoint is a modern “metaphysics.” In general, there should be no doubt and no invitation to misunderstandings: Philosophical anthropology is part of the modern world (cf. Krüger, 2006), it is situated in the era after Kant, it is—in whichever mode—critical philosophy. That is obvious namely in the cases of Ernst Cassirer and Helmuth Plessner. However—and this is my point—there are levels, and not only degrees, of resolute thinking. Plessner is one of those thinkers, who are most consistent in accepting modernity, whereas Cassirer is not. The point that generates this different level is the explicit self-reflection of Plessner in contrast to a systematic silence of Cassirer. *Eccentricity*, according to Plessner, is, among other determinations, to speak of the systematic position from where the philosopher is enabled to speak —————————

2\. WHAT IS MODERN PHILOSOPHY? 

The central and characteristic indicator of modernity is a radical change in the self-consciousness of mankind. The social order is no longer supposed to be given or guaranteed by god, nature or whatever, but is postulated to be formatted by people themselves. Human rights are *declared*; each man and each woman is to be treated as being born with equal rights on the basis that he or she is a member of mankind. However, and that is the point of modernity, you are not able to read that in nature or in any Holy Book. What a person is and who a person is, is not something which can just be stated. 

To *declare* human rights is to treat them as a Holy Right Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1762) stresses two opposing things at the same time. For to be a *holy* right it has not to discuss *whether* people have to be treated as equals: we just have to treat each other categorically as equals. However, this holiness is not anything that can be guaranteed by nature or a god, rather it is grounded in social arrangement. All those who live together as *citoyens* (citizens) have agreed not to discuss *whether* they have to be treated as equals. It seems paradoxical, but it is as transparent as it can be: The declaration of human rights is an arrangement that human rights are not arranged; you just have them—by our arrangement. This remarkable logical structure is grounded in a real antinomy, an antinomy which is in this case not an error, rather it indicates the main achievement of modernity: the republican instead of the monarchical structure of society. Rousseau’s main point is that every concrete arrangement is an expression of a basing categorical discrimination, namely to be a candidate to make such arrangements or not. A slave was not allowed to do so; and therefore social order is not constituted by contracts as such, rather it is constituted by contracts between those who are candidates. When one speak of *volonté de tous*, the criterion *volonté générale* is just in use, because “*de tout*” is not “all natural bodies.” It is the same logic that forbids to talk about red thoughts. Thoughts are no candidates for coloured things, and to talk about concrete red, blue or yellow things is an expression of the basing categorical discrimination between coloured and non-coloured things. Such discriminations are categorical ones in so far as they base usual practices or well-adjusted games: no one will give an empirical proof to show that a concrete thought is not coloured. Within the same logic: To talk about contracts means to talk about the question which is enabled or accepted to 

*[p. 143]* ^pg145

make arrangements. Therefore Rousseau’s principle and request is that before you talk about contracts between people you have to analyse the act how *people become people* (Rousseau, 1762, 71ff.). In other words, modern people rule and are ruled in the same aspect. 

This radical change in self-consciousness has, of course, consequences within science and philosophy. In our (scientific) knowledge there is no longer supposed to be any *given* ground. Whatever principle of argumentation is chosen, it has been a choice. Perhaps that choice is made very well and perhaps there is not any occasion to change the chosen principles—by principle it is possible to do so anyway. Modern thinkers can no longer argue that nature (or god) tells, forces and justifies such choices. That is the essence of the famous Copernican revolution in the mode of thinking, and that means: The order of our living-together is grounded in freedom. 

3\. WHAT IS MODERN ANTHROPOLOGY? 

In a sense modern philosophy *is* philosophical anthropology. Modern philosophical anthropology is modern philosophy in the sense that it stresses a radical change in the meaning of truth. Philosophical truth in modern times means “our” truth—truth of social, human beings, and no longer divine truth. Of course, even Socrates only knew about truth of men, however that was just another meaning of “our truth.” In premodern times philosophical truth was supposed to be “only” human truth. In its very meaning truth was supposed to be independent of place, time and person, and every human aspect within our knowledge was a sort of error or restricted truth. Human beings deviate from truth *because* they have a body. Bodies confine natural beings to a specific place and time in the world, which was supposed to be a restrict from what truth is. That is only another version of the supposition that the foundation of cognition had to be *given*. In Leibniz’s *Monadologie* (1714, § 72) it is said explicitly: It is a characteristic and a privilege of God, and only of God, to be freed from a body. Exactly that supposition changed with modernity; the words used are still the same—human knowledge—but their meaning is reverse. Even modernity has to speak of “divine” or “infinite” truth, because “truth” deals with truth of the world as a (whole) world and not only with correct cognition of things within the world; but the relation of human and divine truth is going to change. To speak of “our” truth is going to loose its melancholic undertone: it is not something which is unattainable for “only” human truth, rather truth *is* truth of the world (as a world) *from a point within it*. That *is* human truth, and Hegel will call it “*falsche (christliche) Demut*” (wrong Christian humility) to anchor truth in heaven. The main logical point is what Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel called the difference between true and wrong infinity—“*wahre und falsche Unendlichkeit*”—which is indeed a sort of game with both oppositions, namely true/false and right/wrong. 

*[p. 144]* ^pg146

Ludwig Feuerbach was the first, I believe, who articulated the explicit anthropological version of this radical change. In 1837 he published a book dealing with Leibniz (Feuerbach, 1837). Feuerbach’s resolute reading of Leibniz ends with the opposing result: truth as such has a body, which means that the idea of a body-freed-divine truth is a sort of a sad-ghost story: Undetermined or disembodied beings are non-beings. The main point is: “our” human truth is truth which is anchored (“*verankert*”) within the world: there is no truth, at least none that we know of, that is not a perspective one (cf. Plessner, 1961, 164). 

This is a pretty broad sense of modern anthropology. But I think it is the very idea: modern philosophical anthropology is *that kind of* modern philosophy which, firstly, stresses the finiteness of human truth as *definite* for truth, and which, secondly, does not sacrifice the infinite dimension of truth on the altar of naturalism or historicism. In this sense, even Heidegger’s philosophy is a case of philosophical anthropology. 

On the other hand, this broad sense gives no specific means to separate the Philosophical anthropology (with capital “P:” Helmuth Plessner, Max Scheler, Arnold Gehlen) within this philosophical anthropology. It is not necessary to do so here and now, nevertheless it is necessary for it to be done. 

If you are not convinced of the systematic determination of philosophical anthropology (with small ‘p’)—as being too broad for instance—just take it as a pragmatic bridge: a determination that enables the comparison of Cassirer, Plessner and Antonio Gramsci—what I want to do. 

4\. WHAT IS SCEPTICAL PHILOSOPHY? 

First of all, the word “*sceptical*” is a distress signal. Whatever you know or think scepticism is, please wait a moment. Two distinctions have to be drawn. Firstly, two historic forms of scepticism exist—the academic and the Pyrrhonian one, and secondly, scepticism is a premodern mode of philosophy, i.e. a philosophy before Immanuel Kant. The result is distress in naming the concept of Plessner’s anthropology. When I am speaking of modern sceptical anthropology, I would like to characterise a specific mode of Critical philosophy (with capital “C”)—a mode which draws different conclusions from Pyrrhonion scepticism than Kant did. 

To clarify the first discrimination: Odo Marquard, a German philosopher who likes to call himself a sceptic, mentioned casually that two parties exist within scepticism and it is possible to end up with the wrong one (cf. Marquard, 1982). The wrong one is the academic mode of scepticism which is not scepticism at all—it is negative dogmatism. Academics *assert* that truth is impossible; Pyrrhonians do not assert anything, rather they search for truth. That is the very clear discrimination given by Sextus Empiricus, nowadays recalled by Marquard

*[p. 145]* ^pg147

, but forgotten in the very strange story of “scepticism” today. Especially within the Analytical philosophy (with capital “A”) it seems to be self-evident that scepticism is negative dogmatism—most positions end up with the wrong party of scepticism. According to Analytic philosophers you have to believe that Hume is a sceptic, and you have to have a problem with the existence of the outer world to be a sceptic. This is, historically speaking, nothing but a fairytale. 

Pyrrhonian scepticism—i.e., sceptical philosophy—does not assert “All cognition is insecure,” which is a stupid form of self-contradiction. Pyrrhonists want to *show* something. They want to show that the *principles* of argumentation still haven’t fit what they promised to fit. To stress it: it is not a question of to be convinced of the concrete argumentation or not. According to Pyrrhonian scepticism, argumentations do not match by principle. Up till now every argumentation has failed by one of three possibilities: infinite regress, dogmatic breaking off or bad circularity in argumentation. The consequence is evident: it is not yet possible to decide. The existence of two or more different argumentations in the case of the same topic is the very normality—if no argumentation fits, these different argumentations are balanced. Pyrrhonists called such situation a situation of *isosthenia*. The remaining possibility, and the possibility that indicates wisdom, is not to assert now (i.e.: to wait a moment, what they called to exercise *epoche*)—and search. The Pyrrhonian experience in this case, if one believes in their story, is: *Ataraxia* (calmness), not *Apatheia*, will follow like a shadow follows the body. 

Hans Albert (1968) pointed out exactly the same structure and called it Münchhausen-Trilemma. However just repeating it in the 20th century, after Kant and Hegel, is a premodern ideology. Pyrrhonism presupposes that every argumentation follows a linear structure: A is the argument for B, C is the argument for B, D is the argument for C and so on—in which case the Münchhausen-Trilemma is obvious. But the modern distinction between *Verstand* and *Vernunft* differentiates two modes of argumentation. Argumentation within the *Verstand* follows that linear structure; in contrast to this, and in so far as *Vernunft* is no longer grounded in a transcendent principle, *Vernunft* is grounded in itself. On the basis of that *Vernunft* is nothing but the foundation of *Verstand* with the means of the *Verstand*, or self-reflection of reason (cf. Cassirer, 1914, 201). “*True* infinity” in the sense of Hegel is exactly that: that the structure of argumentation within the *Vernunft* is not linear, but circular. In *this* case the mistake is to suppose linearity. With regard to *Vernunft*, the circularity is not an error, rather it defines what *Vernunft* is. The corresponding logical structure is an antinomy.

5\. CASSIRER’S VERSION OF SCEPTICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

When Cassirer (2005, 364ff.) tells us “the sceptical answer” to the question what man is, he does not talk about Sextus Empiricus, rather about Montaigne. He refers especially to the relation of Pascal and Montaigne or, systematically speaking, to the relation of “mysticism and scepticism, absolute faith and radical doubt” (365). According to Cassirer, Montaigne created a new anthropology as a consequence of the new Copernican cosmology. Man is no longer situated in the centre of the universe and is no longer its aim; man is one single point or entity among others, and the surrounding universe “is silent to his religious feelings and to his deepest moral demands” (366). Due to this new self-consciousness of man, Cassirer has to talk about Montaigne, and not about the Hellenistic philosophy of Sextus Empiricus. In talking about anthropology Cassirer is not interested in sceptical philosophy, rather in the anthropological consequences of the new and “groundless” cosmology. Cassirer’s sceptical anthropology is not generated by scepticism; the occasion is a rather general insecurity of knowledge, and the result is an early sort of a non-tragic, happy science (“*Fröhliche Wissenschaft*,” Friedrich Nietzsche): “Montaigne was perhaps the first modern man who was not terrified by this silence \[of the universe\]” (366). 

Cassirer describes Montaigne as a thinker who accepts “the new cosmology in all its consequences” (366). I think that is an appropriate description of Montaigne, but nevertheless a specific one of scepticism. “Man is not only unable to understand the infinite; he cannot even comprehend and embrace his own little world” (367)—that is the new self-consciousness, and Montaigne represents a position of calmness. One should not feel sorry about those limitations of understanding, rather to see them as a new freedom. Cassirer is explicitly not able to see a difference between Montaigne on the one hand and modern thinkers like Rousseau or Diderot on the other: “they could only repeat his arguments” (367). 

According to Cassirer, sceptical anthropology interprets itself not to be able to offer “a universal truth or a universal morality” (368). Truth and morality is “nothing else but a system of customs and habits” (368)—“powers \[that\] have only a practical value and authority” (365). Cassirer accepts this scenario; he accepts that the situation of mankind is described very well and that the only question is how to handle the very fact of the amorality of the surrounding world. 

“In order to flee the aspect of human life Pascal had to take recourse to an absolute dogmatic faith. Montaigne found his repose in his calm and serene scepticism. It became the task of the modern mind to find its way between these two extremes, between the implicit faith of Pascal and the radical doubt of Montaigne” (371). 

*[p. 147]* ^pg149

However, the Pascal of Cassirer is only one of two “Pascals.” Indeed, Pascal (1670) argues that man is a Nothing in the face of the universe and an omnipotence in the face of the Nothing, and therefore man is a middle between nothing and the universe condemned to perpetual uncertainty.[^2] It is *this* Pascal who knows in a very clear manner about the political consequences of this “scepticism.” People will remain calm and motionless within the social position that “nature had put them.”[^3] 

In contrast to this, the problem is more difficult. Another Pascal is a thinker of the Christian God and a mathematician, which for him do not conflict. He argues as follows (§ 233): We know *that* there exists an Infinite, but we do not know anything about its essence. We know *that* there is an infinite of numbers, but we do not know anything about the essence of this infiniteness. “Because of that” it is possible to recognise *that* God exist and not to know anything about him. So, a Christian cannot provide any (rational) proof of God. Not being able to say anything about the essence of God/ the Infinity—*deus absconditus—*he has to *believe* in God without asserting something about him. Therefore, the causes of reason are not able to prove God nor to deny God. What to do? 

At this point of argumentation there is the switch between academic and pyrrhonian scepticism. Spontaneously you will surely argue like an academic sceptic: if causes of reason do not fit then it is obvious not to assert anything and to assert that assertion is not possible. Further on, critical philosophy is beginning at the same point of argumentation in contrast to a self-misunderstanding of Pyrrhonism. To assert the existence of an Infinity is as wrong as denying it, so it must be right not to assert any of these alternatives. However, not to decide (now) is even a decision, and therefore Pascal says: “Yes, but …” His decisive argument is not to be free to choose here. *Whatever* one does is already an answer to the question, and there is no freedom to prefer not to answer (because that’s an answer too).[^4] 

That generates a fundamental diverse anthropology: Whenever someone has finite “human” knowledge, there is an assertion about the infinite in use (cf. nowadays Tengelyi, 2014). According to the structure of Pascal’s answer, there is no possibility to have finite knowledge without having infinite knowledge. It does not mean knowing anything about the “essence” of the infinite: human knowledge is “finite” knowledge. It just means that finite knowledge is a *relation* of finiteness and infinity. The consequence is to give up

6\. PLESSNER’S SCEPTICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

According to Plessner there is no *given* ground either in human cognition or in human action. The short formula *homo absconditus* postulates a sort of secrecy in every human activity, especially in the activity of the philosophical determination of humanity. Self-consciousness is the consciousness of someone’s shadow, and it is not consciousness of a (given) self as a whole. Nevertheless, there is no remaining secret that human beings are not able to know. Plessner stresses the structure of “true infinity:” In every activity there is a principle (or ground) in use on the basis that activity is finite or determinate activity. Determinate activity is to take this, and not that, principle in use; nevertheless, it is impossible for logical reasons to base this principle just in the same action in which it is used. In this sense human activity is groundless: Every activity has its own ground in use, but it would be another activity to justify this ground. The structure is just the same as in the optics of the human eye: the existence of a “blind spot.” Plessner calls that structure “*Unergründlichkeit*” of man, and because he wants to deny the structure of the wrong infinity he recalls a principle formulated by Georg Misch, namely the obligation (*Verbindlichkeit*) of “*Unergründlichkeit”* of man. *Obligation* is indeed a principle, and not only a subjective emphasis; it separates two meanings of *groundless*. 

All of this is only a reformulation of eccentricity. Eccentricity means that every single human activity is an action which is accompanied by an actor-asspectator’s look. 

In Plessner’s concept, his anthropology is not a consequence of a special theory of knowledge. Plessner’s sceptical anthropology is not deduced from another, first-given insight. It sharply differs from that what Cassirer calls the “sceptic answer.” Plessner’s anthropology does not mainly deal with the problem of how to handle groundless knowledge. Man himself is groundless—which should be taken as an obligation. Insofar, philosophical anthropology in the sense of Plessner conquers the place of old metaphysics: sceptical anthropology as the *prima philosophia*. Philosophical anthropology is not an application of a theory of cognition. 

*[p. 149]* ^pg151

The same difference can be marked within the modern theory of knowledge. There is a difference between historical (or cultural) determination of knowledge, on the one hand, and its perspectivity, on the other. On the one hand, every human knowledge is situated in historical time and cultural “place,” and because of that it changes with the development of societies. Modern thinkers would not deny that, especially for Plessner as well as for Cassirer, this modernity is out of discussion. They no longer believe in the divine, unchangeable truth. However, the second decisive matter is more difficult. Let us assume stopping that historical and cultural development of knowledge, and let us assume to analyse knowledge of a single era and culture. What is the expectation? As presupposed and constructed, there will be no (radical) change in knowledge. Is it the right and only correct conclusion to suppose one truth then, valid for that era and culture? According to Ludwig Feuerbach and Plessner that conclusion would be wrong. If knowledge is anchored in the world it is knowledge of the world from within the world, and thus it depends on that anchor-point. Therefore the “same” truth of a determinate era and culture is given in different perspective versions. The supposition of anchored knowledge is the supposition of a plurality of truth, which is not a historical or cultural relativity but rather a multi-perspectivity of balanced validity. Pyrrhonists called that isosthenia, Plessner calls it “*Unentscheidbarkeit*,” and a pretty proper version was given by Chladenius (1742), who called that anchor-point “viewpoint” (*Sehe-Punkt*): given two right and correct stories it might be a difference between them (§§ 308 ff.). That is not a logical mystery or a stupid contradiction. A specific perspective of truth is a specific mode of the “same” truth among other and different modes. The mode of telling is *not* something just given as a nice addition. Telling a right story is to tell it in a specific way, and it is not possible only to tell “the story” without telling it in any mode. For that reason, Søren Kierkegaard (1844, 19) spoke of a possible error in modulation: it is possible to tell a right and correct story, but to be false in the mode of telling. 

7\. TWO LEVELS OF MODERNITY 

All of this forces to distinguish between two aspects within the finiteness and anti-divinity of human knowledge, namely the aspect of historical and cultural continuous change and the aspect of multi-perspectivity. It seems to be obvious that Cassirer knows all about that. It was Cassirer who became famous for the multitude of symbolic forms. Nevertheless, Cassirer is not resolute here—and he has very good reasons not to be. To stress the multitude is more than a narrow rationalism is able to think about, and we should not go back. However, the multitude is the problem and not the solution. A philosopher has to talk about the relations between the different symbolic forms, and especially about a sort of ranking. It would not be convincing to suppose validity in the 

*[p. 150]* ^pg152

same manner for all forms. In the face of German fascism, it is necessary for Cassirer to distinguish the scientific validity from that of the “Myth of the State.” 

One has to speak about (the) unity of the multiple types of cognition. However, the very good reasons to do so are not sufficient to conclude the singularity of that unity. According to my reading, Cassirer doesn’t have the theoretical means to offer a concept of perspectivity. According to Cassirer, human spirit, which is the name of that unity, has to be one because it is always the same human being who has (!) that different types of knowledge. Cassirer knows about man as something that is identical with himself, and he knows about man as a pre-supposition of knowledge. The switch can be seen very clearly now: *Because* Cassirer does not have a concept of perspectivity of that unity, he has to say that the unity of the symbolic forms or the unity of man is a never-ending task—a task, but a task which is necessary to try to fulfil. That is Kantianism, and Cassirer’s silence is a systematic one. He is not forced to speak about his own position because the *Philosophy of Symbolic Forms* is only a sort of record giving a humble never-ending story on what has been going on and what is going to happen. “True infinity” is another story than Kantianism. Feuerbach offered a very short story on that plurality of the unity of multiple ways of world views: “Christianity is the contradiction of polytheism and monotheism” (GW, 9, 244). 

The difference in the level of modernity is to accept or not to accept the structure of true infinity. To accept it (or to bet on it in the sense of Pascal) is to suppose that true finite knowledge is not a task—neither ending nor never ending—but a relation of finiteness and infinity anchored in the world. The presence of infinity within finite knowledge is mainly stressed by Hegel, the perspectivity of such finite-infinite human knowledge is mainly stressed by Feuerbach. The result is the well-known hermeneutic trick, namely to understand Montaigne in a better way than Montaigne understood himself: Life could always be quite another one. 

8\. THE CONSEQUENCE: POLITICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

Three main problems remain. Two of them will only be mentioned here while the third one, which is the sceptical problem in the narrow sense, will be discussed. 

The first problem is a crucial logical one. To bet on true infinity is to bet on a concept of contradiction, traditionally called dialectical contradiction or hermeneutic circularity. That is nothing but the promise not to be a logical contradiction and therefore not to be a stupid error. The pure form of that sort of contradiction seems to be an antinomy, and further on specific cases of antinomy that Wittgenstein (1956, 178) called “true contradictions.” The problem of antinomies

*[p. 151]* ^pg153

is well known: A German says “Every German is a liar!” Is that a lie or not? True antinomies are antinomies also, and therefore it is necessary to differentiate between the object-level and the meta-level (in order to prevent a stupid error). On the other hand, and that’s the characteristic of *true* antinomies, it is impossible to separate these two levels. These cases force a distinction between two levels within the same matter. Self-consciousness is a classical example, the spelling of *spelling* is a very simple example given by Wittgenstein (1976, 14) (“we do not call that *spelling of the second order*”), the proof of Kurt Gödel is a well known and accepted example, and the question which is the level of language from which we talk about *all* the levels of language is a crucial example. In addition to that pure formal logic of true infinity, a more semantically logic occurs. It is necessary to prevent errors as well as romanticism when talking about “dialectical contradictions.” To differentiate between the mode and the essence of something, and to think about criteria for cases when the mode is not an addition, rather it belongs to the “thing itself,” seems to prepare a way to a solution, and to study the prosaic work of Josef König seems to offer the best protection in that field (cf. Schürmann, 1999). 

The second problem occurs when asking for the principle to individuate that point where knowledge is anchored in the world. According to Feuerbach, natural individuals are anchored by their bodies, and human individuals are religious in so far as they celebrate that they are anchored. According to Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, knowledge is anchored in class-consciousness. However, that term is no longer usable because Marxists and their critics are harmoniously united in not understanding that class-consciousness as not an empirical concept, but rather as a category used when talking about empirical knowledge. According to Plessner in the year 1931, the principle of plurality of perspectives seems to be *people* (*Volk*), which is not really attractive today. It is hard work to separate Plessner’s democratic meaning of that concept from its fascistic use.[^5] The third problem is, in a broad sense, the ethical one. Critique is “groundless” now (Plessner, 1918), and no reasons in the narrow sense (of reason) force a decision within the plurality of truth. Traditional philosophy argues that there *must* be a rational choice—and Cassirer as well as Plessner follow that tradition. But in contrast to Cassirer, Plessner does not conclude that there is only one rational possibility to decide. Therefore, Plessner has to open the concept of rationality. World knowledge may be “true” in a rational manner, but truth cannot be reduced to true/false assertions. You have to talk about the mode of assertions. Betting on true infinity is to anchor world view in an *ethos* (cf. Misch, 1926, 115).

Some political philosophies deal with that groundlessness and they fight on the boundary of rationality and irrationality. Feministic theory, for example, tried out a concept of *standpoint epistemology* (Harding, 1993); *situated knowledge* (Haraway, 1995) and further on *situated ontology* (Jutta Weber, 2003) is thought to avoid the divine trick. Pierre Badiou, Jacques Rancière and Jean-Luc Nancy articulate the French traditions in different concepts of *groundless politics*, followed by Thomas Bedorf (2005; 2007) working out a phenomenological version in the tradition of Bernhard Waldenfels. Only some of those attempts are aware that they recall Plessner’s philosophy. Norbert Axel Richter (2005) tried to connect the contemporary political philosophy with Plessner’s concept. He stresses a common ground of Plessner and Michel Foucault, contrasting it with a common ground of Jürgen Habermas and Carl Schmitt. 

This ethical or political foundation of knowledge is Plessner’s variation of Max Weber. Weber insisted firmly upon it: 

“World views are not and will never be the result of stating facts, but they are produced on the basis of a sort of decision or ethos. In this sense they are ideals, which are holy for a community and situated in competition with different ideals accepted as holy by other communities” (Weber, 1904, 154). 

Plessner agrees without any reservations. Perhaps a difference is the relation: Stating facts and having a world view is not something given as a logical addition—that is the point which becomes clearer with Plessner. Of course, stating facts has to work in a climate of “cool necessity,” as Hegel postulates, and critique has to be “inconsiderate,” as Marx and Engels would say. However, that only means holding distance and making sure by institutional means not to be involved directly. Nevertheless, a critic cannot catapult himself out of the situation. He is involved and even the wish not to be is the divine trick. Weber does not seem to be clear, or more precisely: his postulation of “*Wertfreiheit*” is often understood as to postulate the possibility of stating facts without any world view. According to Plessner, not only the consequences of facts are to be judged, but even the facts themselves are anchored in an ethos. So, every anthropology is one anthropology among others and therefore it is situated within the competition. Not only the answer is a determinate answer, rather it is even the mode of the question itself that is a determined one (Plessner, 1931, 221). 

This consequence distinguishes Plessner’s concept from Pyrrhonism, on the one hand, and from Kantianism on the other. In contrast to Pyrrhonism, Plessner follows Pascal: an anthropologist is not able not to bet. He is situated within the game and there is no option in reverting back to the role of a pure spectator. In contrast to Kantianism, anthropology can’t situate itself above the competition, because *Vernunft* is not a neutral court of arbitration, rather it is also one of the playing parties. 

*[p. 153]* ^pg155

Plessner’s anthropology is very specific. His sceptical or political anthropology is quite a different story than Scheler’s, Gehlen’s and even Cassirer’s anthropologies. Of course, Plessner is not a lonesome hero. 

Gramsci, an Italian Marxist dealing with the everyday culture of people and with the problem of gaining hegemony in the political-cultural contest, seems to be one of the resolute thinkers of modernity. He marks the decisive problem resulting from groundless knowledge and this problem organises his philosophy. The problem is the self-application of groundlessness: Marxism postulates the groundlessness of truth theoretically, and therefore it is extremely difficult in political praxis to also postulate the groundlessness of Marxism without destroying self-confident and single-minded action (Gramsci, 1991–2002, b. 11, 1476). 

In my opinion, the best way to understand Gramsci is to read him with Plessner. Perhaps it will be possible to reverse the direction. Perhaps, one day, a good way to better understanding of Plessner will be to read him with Gramsci. In contemporary political philosophy Gramsci is already discussed. Explicitly in the texts of Chantal Mouffe and Ernesto Laclau for example, and implicitly in the French tradition via Louis Althusser (cf. Marchart, 2010). However, those connections are not yet given in a good and transparent text, rather they form a tangle of threats. The suggestion is to use Plessner’s anthropology as a tool to disentangle that “new confusion,” or in other words: to enlighten postmodernism about itself.
