## About

Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan's edition of the chief Upanishads: the Sanskrit in roman transliteration, an English translation with verse-by-verse notes, and a long introduction.

- **Editor:** Radhakrishnan (1888–1975) was professor of Eastern religions and ethics at Oxford from 1936 to 1952, vice president of India from 1952 and president from 1962 to 1967 (Britannica). Britannica says his books aimed to explain Indian thought to Western readers.
- **1953:** published in London by George Allen & Unwin in the Muirhead Library of Philosophy, 958 pages (Penn catalogue; Princeton's copy also names Allen & Unwin as first publisher). Wikipedia names Allen & Unwin in London and Harper in New York, and in one place says Harper published it first.
- **Contents:** a preface dated 1951; an introduction of 129 pages in 19 sections, on Brahman and Ātman, māyā, karma and rebirth, ethics and more; the texts with translation and notes (pp. 147–938); appendices on Tagore's and Edmond Holmes's readings; bibliography and index. Wikipedia lists the eighteen Upanishads in order: Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya, Aitareya, Taittirīya, Īṣa, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Śvetāśvatara, Kauśītāki, Maitrāyaṇi, Subāla, Jābāla, Paiṅgala, Kaivalya and Vajrasūcikā; a 1955 review it quotes says he chose "the eighteen most important".
- **Later editions:** all of 958 pages. Library catalogues record a centenary edition (Unwin Hyman, 1989) and a Humanities Press paperback (1992). Wikipedia's list differs: Allen & Unwin 1969 and 1978, Oxford University Press 1989, Prometheus Books 1992, HarperCollins India 1994, and a Hindi translation (1981).
- **Reception:** reviewed in Newsweek (July 1954), the Journal of Bible and Religion, Philosophy and the Journal of Religion (1955), and the Philosophical Review (1957). S. G. F. Brandon judged that, with many translations already available, its value lay mainly in the commentary and introduction.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia's reception section quotes reviewers who called Radhakrishnan "Asia's foremost contemporary philosopher" (Newsweek, 1954) and found the value of his version in "his commentary and the long introductory essay" (Philosophy, 1955).
