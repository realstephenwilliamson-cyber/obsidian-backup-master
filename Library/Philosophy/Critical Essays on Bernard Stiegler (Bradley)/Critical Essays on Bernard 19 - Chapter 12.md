## Chapter 12

ON THE STRUGGLE FOR *\[illegible\]* 

AND THE CRISIS OF THE *SAVOIRS*: 

STIEGLER CONTRA WILL SELF / 

WILL SELF CONTRA STIEGLER 

On How to Read the Essay 

Throughout this piece and during perambulations throughout the city of Tokyo, the voices of Will Self and Bernard Stiegler insinuate themselves into my unconscious. I take them with me wherever I go, whether it is Stiegler’s recorded audio lectures or Will Self reading his own books. The voices intercede my solitary reading and comprehension. What is revealed in this everyday experience is not only the difficulty and struggle to read per se but also the new incorporeal universes of reference (Guattari, 1995; Walkerdine, 2014), which open up when one moves, thinks, and connects in and with and through the city – in and with and through technology. The article is written as if one’s subjectivity were given completely over to bidirectional digital media (BDDM). As such the article toys with the idea that one is somehow transfixed, schizzed, and traumatized by the consistent “ticker-tape” of information, as Will Self puts it. It is out of this maelstrom of information, confusion, and noise that one (self- consciously) struggles to write and to critique the world *as it is*. In full knowledge and in disavowal one is constantly on the go, veering off course, ambulant, and hearing voices in one’s head. These voices or interjections are dispersed performatively in this article to show the difficulty in bracketing off the world. 

Introduction: Absolute Location but No Orientation I shall start with Will Self’s controversial comments about a sinister form of bidirectional digital media (BDDM), which he takes to be close to 

*[p. 241]* ^pg256

Marshall McLuhan’s concept of the unified electrical field as explored in *Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man*. For Will Self (2018; Self & Watkin, 2016), BDDM is what characterizes the suite of web and internet technologies that govern the stasis and disruption of serial time —that is, time sensed as a series of permanent presents. He makes the case that BDDM is somehow affecting not only the temporality of the now but memory as such, causing a proliferation of mental illnesses and obliterating human relations. It is indeed also true that Stiegler makes similar comments about modern technology in terms of how “industrial temporal objects” (Stiegler, 2010b) as toxic forms of tertiary retentions are short-circuiting the *savoirs* (the knowledge of how to do, to live, to philosophize, for example) and severing transindividuation circuits between the generations – for example, the relationship between parent and child, teacher and student) (Stiegler, 2010a). I endeavor to distinguish Stiegler’s nuanced and extended appreciation and pharmacology of technology from Will Self’s eotechnic antidote, which is to somehow free oneself from sedentary life and the permanent now of internet technology through a cloistered retreat into solitude with a previous codex – namely the printed word. 

In a catchy refrain, Will Self insists GPS technology offers *absolute location but no orientation* whatsoever. Echoing Paul Virilio in *Polar Inertia* (2000), he claims it leaves the user in a state of disorientation. It rips us away from our immediate milieu. It leads to the pollution of distances. It leaves us adrift and forlorn: 

I’m shamefully lost because I’m neglecting all of those abilities that I have to orient myself. I’m neglecting to look at road signs, I’m neglecting to feel the wind on my cheeks, I’m neglecting to notice which side of the tree the lichen grows, I’m neglecting to talk to people in the street and ask them directions. I’m neglecting to look at the street. I’m neglecting everything but the little blue dot. And I feel hopelessly lost. GPS technology offers the absolute location and no orientation whatsoever. . . . Think of that as an expansive metaphor for our current political, social, philosophical impasse. (Self, 2019) 

The comparison between the philosophy of Bernard Stiegler and the writings and social commentary of Will Self is thought-provoking because it shows how literature can learn from philosophy and vice versa. Why so? At first glance and for the most part, there seems to be a deficit of sustained 

*[p. 242]* ^pg257

philosophical reflection in the perspective of Will Self. His thoughts appear to be a motley collection of quotations from R. D. Laing, Baudrillard, Virilio, Debord, and McLuhan and references to 20th century modernist writers such as Kafka and Ballard. He admits as much, stating that he has never read the aforementioned in depth as a philosopher would and has just made use of a few ideas to supplement his own perspective. In summa, his observations are penetrating but lack a fully-fledged philosophy of technology and indeed a philosophy of time to sustain them. My position is that Bernard Stiegler is an excellent supplement to Will Self’s analysis of BDDM. Thinking Stiegler alongside Will Self and, *mutatis mutandis*, Self alongside Stiegler, allows for a robust critique of the present philosophical *impasse* mentioned above, that is the struggle for *epokhē.* It is this philosophical impasse that I am naming the “struggle for *\[illegible\]* (struggle for a path to sublimation), which is simultaneously a crisis of spirit and a crisis of the *savoirs –* the crisis of knowledge as such. 

I think that today education is destroyed not only in the university or in the school, but in the family. It is destroyed for example by the smartphone; parents who use a smartphone don’t look at the babies or the children. Today, society in general is destroyed by social networking, by digital technologies. I say that because if we need to be educated, it is because we are very strange animals. Our main organs are not in our body but outside of the body. . . . This is the reason for which the human being must be educated because he has always to learn how to use artificial organs, that is language, social rules, religion, rituals but also computers, podcasting, et cetera, et cetera. If we understand that education must be based on this process, we have to completely redefine what is education. (Stiegler & Gagliano, 2019) For his part, Stiegler (2018) discusses the possibility of a prosthetic or programmatic *epokhē. '* The *epokhē* is provoked by technological change but also enclosed by it. The *epokhē* interrupts the epoch, calling the past and the gathered knowledge to account. The *epokhē* suspends the epoch awaiting the reconstitution of knowledge and new forms of life, behavior, culture, that is, new circuits of transindividuation and a vision of a new form of society. However, the *epokhē* of the epoch is disrupted*:* 

The problem, today, in the period of what we call disruption, is that it seems impossible to reconstitute any knowledge, and that forms of behavior are 

*[p. 243]* ^pg258

now produced, not by social systems, cultures and knowledge, but by marketing that exploits ‘big data’ and digital tertiary retentions insofar as these are calculable, computable and as such constitute the worldwide data economy. (Stiegler, 2020a, p. 31)

Stiegler addresses what he terms a “doubly epokhal redoubling’”— which is first as pharmakon and second as the production of new knowledge. It is that which seeks a way beyond the epokhal technological shock, “the shocks of infidelity” (Stiegler, 2020a, p. 46), beyond disruption and stupefaction. This passage is from shock to surprise, sur-prehension, to com-prehension. Through new forms of mnemotechnics, or new forms of ‘codex’ in Will Self’s terminology — there remains a faint prospect of an improbable possibility of a new way of life:

The advent of a new form of mnemotechnics constitutes, like the advent of a new technical system, a technological epokhé, a suspension of the collective and behavioral programs currently in force, and their replacement by new ways of life. (Stiegler, 2020a, p. 98)

The technological epokhé is redoubled by a further epokhé. The first redoubling opens an epokhé that disrupts the entire social milieu. The second redoubling acts as a therapy, a therapeutics, a technique of self and spirit. This secondary suspension constitutes a new pathos that is a new kind of philia (Stiegler, 2020, p. 203), and Stiegler insists it is necessary to care for youth, to teach young people to take care of themselves, to teach techniques of the self (tékhné tou biou), to teach them to care for the self, that is, to teach them how to bracket off the world to create another. For Stiegler, this is to take youth on what he calls a “journey of knowledge” (Bradley, 2020, 2022a, 2022b), which he pursues through the use of different educational software and collaborative applications. As we shall see, my argument is that the use of technology to mediate the immediate milieu can be a “journey of knowledge.” The task is to understand the new codex or modality and to transform it therapeutically. In turn, this demands an understanding of tertiary retention and how they function in exosomatic, prosthetic, digital — often toxic — technologies that are transforming modes of inscription and attention as such.

On this point, and in the time of the coronavirus, I think Stiegler’s compatriot, the French philosopher Catherine Malabou, has spoken

*[p. 244]* ^pg259

eloquently about the thought experiment of withdrawing into the self, into the island of the self, and from there to begin to think of new forms of sociality, a new understanding of alterity as such (Bradley, 2022b). In Stiegler’s language a new understanding of alterity would mean the rebuilding of transindividuation circuits, the preservation of self-cultivation (*Bildung*). Malabou shares much with Stiegler on this point. In a recent lecture, Malabou says that it is only in withdrawing into the self in the time of isolation that one can understand the nature of alterity. For me, this is a rumination on the reconstruction of transindividuation circuits in the Stieglerian sense as it is only by withdrawing into the self that one can come to understand what the Other is as such. Transindividuation circuits would be those that are sustained across the generations, those between parent and child, teacher and student. Transindividuation circuits are essential for the cultivation of knowledge, for the transmission of knowledge, for the care of the *savoirs*, for self-cultivation (*Bildung*) as such. Cynics might call this a philosophical or phenomenological moment of grace, revelation or epiphany, but another way to put this is to say that the *epokhē* , the suspension and interruption that the virus has prompted has unfurled a “third world” (*le troisième monde*) of thought, in other words, the possibility of sublimation and the possibility of negentropic knowledge. In this crisis, in the time of breaking down there is also the possibility of reorientation, of exception, the possibility of a new “journey of knowledge.” This is about turning confinement into the freedom to create an experience, according to Stiegler. 

How can we enrich the relationship to the “third world” of which Popper speaks of, which in Stiegler’s argot one might call “negentropic bifurcation” or the “open system”? Stiegler speaks of his own prison life as being one of transformation, of a passage from crisis to creation (Stiegler, 2017). In his prison cell, in this enforced *epokhē* or bracketing of the “natural attitude” he finds a path and beginning (Stiegler, 2009). In *Le Monde*, Stiegler writes: “Confinement (for reasons of imprisonment, health or during war) is a kind of social pathology, and when it is necessary we should strive to turn it into the freedom to create an experience – which can bring extraordinary surprises bearing a salvatory potential for bifurcation, and engender what Canguilhem calls a normativity – that is, the invention of a new way of living” (Stiegler, 2020b). He goes on to state that in the time and crisis of 

*[p. 245]* ^pg260

confinement, there is an opportunity “for a revaluation of silence, for the rhythms we give ourselves rather than those to which we bend, for a very parsimonious and reasoned practice of the media and of all that, coming from the outside, distracts the human being from being human” (Stiegler, 2020b). In the time and experience of confinement, the question of how to live after the pandemic subsides has become a pressing issue. Stiegler asks what can emerge out of despair and “symbolic misery” (the dissociation between the producers and consumers of symbols) and suggests that there remains the possibility of new negentropic knowledge, a “sublime” form of energy: “Despair is also an experience, from which much can be learned . . . provided that we take care of it as what can, in certain circumstances, become a sublime form of energy.” 

In his critique of BDDM, which in his view is a dominant mode of technology that has toxic effects, Will Self questions the very possibility of *epokhē.* BDDM would be that mode of allopoetic technology, which is a system that produces something other than itself, something more toxic than itself. Discussing his book *Phone* (2017), its modernist techniques, streams of consciousness, and constant use of the continuous present, he tells an audience at Politics and Prose in 2018 of the difficulty of self-reflection when one is inundated with high-speed recursive feedback loops operating in real time. The result is not reflection or the practice of *epokhē* but forms of hysteria and trauma. For Will Self, technologies of acceleration and specularity have “massively” increased the production of trauma: 

My intention is to try and convey in prose the new world of bi-directional digital media that we’ve entered into. The continuous line of the text that does indeed run for six hundred twenty pages without a paragraph break or any other sort of break reflects the news threads and the stock market quotations that are continuously running like some sort of strange constant set of subtitles beneath our own consciousness. As if it were no longer possible to have the *epokhē,* the bracket, to take your consciousness and interrogate it with no preconceptions. But what I find nowadays when I interrogate my consciousness with no preconceptions is that there is a kind of ticker tape of text running beneath it already. (Self, 2018a) 

Will Self writes committedly on the traumatic interrelationship between speed and violence in modernity, when the fixed coordinates of space and time are disrupted by the one-time immediacy of global capital, a one-time 

*[p. 246]* ^pg261

of global terror. Will Self (2021) writes about the disastrous psychical impact upon our cognitive, mnemonic, and physical faculties by modern technologies such as the photograph, communication networks, the railway, the motorcar, the nuclear fission bomb, and the ubiquitous smartphone. The psychic life and the specular and accelerating technologies in modernity have entered a sort of “symbiotic relationship with one another, each proliferating by means of the other.” As such, a catalogue of new maladies mushrooms in the modern era as a consequence of the “ubiquity of traumatogenic technologies”: psychosis, schizophrenia, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and cases of autism and trauma explode, shocked by “specularity and acceleration,” by instantaneity and immediacy. He writes: 

I will argue that trauma is so widespread precisely because of the ubiquity of traumatogenic technologies in our societies: those of specularity and acceleration, which render us simultaneously unreflective and frenetic. On this analysis, the symptoms deemed evidence of PTSD are in fact only an extreme version of a distinctively modern consciousness. 

As such, we are caught petrified in an “amorphous swirl of semiconsciousness,” unable to interrogate our phenomenological experience of the world. *Subjectivity is the new wounded*, mental life is under assault. It is for these reasons that it is worth examining the contrast between, on the one hand, Stiegler’s demand to use technology pharmacologically, his search for digital tools and the contributory knowledge economy to counter ADHD, *bêtise* or stupidity, the loss of the *savoirs* or the proletarianization of knowledge and, on the other, Self’s rumination on BDDM and his critique of the mass media *in toto*. 

What they were now calling Autistic Spectrum Disorder is – he believes – a canary in the coalmine of the human condition, its cheeping heralding a decisive shift away from the human psyche’s attempt to understand itself . . . by itself to the conviction that enlightenment could be gained . . . from the screens. Really, autism was a misnomer – the so-called selfists were really . . . machinists, snipping, then sewing the long threads . . . one-zero-zero-zero-one-one-zero-one-one-one-zero-one . . . of their own didginess. (Self, 2017) 

*[p. 247]* ^pg262

But when Will Self (2016) says in disavowal: “This wholesale change in human beings that comes about with new knowledge technologies, you can’t say it’s good or it’s bad, it just is,” one must pause and insist this is uncritical and unconvincing. Here Stiegler’s understanding of the *pharmakon* of technology supplies a necessary supplement. It is for this reason that I aim to demonstrate that Stiegler’s philosophy is essential for understanding the crisis of the *savoirs*, the crisis of formation, and the very necessity of *\[illegible\]* In Will Self’s analysis we find a description of the woes of the day but no ethical, practical, or, importantly, political prescription. Yet from his reading of the Platonic dialogues, Stiegler’s response would be to cite the originary technicity of the human, the *pharmakon* of *tékhnē,* that is, both its sides – poison and cure. A constant refrain in Stiegler’s thought is that one has to use technologies in a curative or therapeutic mode. And one must invent new technologies to respond to the crisis of knowledge. One withdraws but always on the condition of a new beginning. 

Will Self’s antidote or response to the crisis of knowledge brought on by BDDM is to affirm the praxis of psychogeography, the way of the *flâneur*, of straying off-course, of drift, wandering and wayfaring, of travel without pretext or immediate goal. Psychogeography for Will Self is a practice to disentangle the meaning of psyche and place. He affirms the *dérive* made famous by Guy Debord (1958), surrealism, and the situationists and writes in *Psychogeography: Disentangling the Modern Conundrum of Psyche and Place, of the man/machine* matrix, which Guy Debord called the spectacle: “Psychogeography is a means of dissolving the mechanized matrix which compresses the space-time continuum and decouples the human from physical geography” (2007). 

Importantly, his escape from the bombardment of informational demands is through psychogeography, which for Will Self, is “a physical enactment of the phenomenological *epokheē,* its total experience and interrogating it on its own terms.” On this reading, it is by forging a relation to the outside, to the city, to the refrains of the immediate environs that one can undertake and participate in a phenomenological *epokhē.* What is the physical enactment of the phenomenological *epokhē* and how can it be understood as made possible through the mediation of technology? This *\[illegible\]* it is claimed, is immediate and in no need of technological supplement. No smartphone, no Walkman (Bogue et al., 2014), no 

*[p. 248]* ^pg263

PokémonGO (Bradley, 2019), no headphones, no Google Maps or GPS. No app can match the territory of the immediate milieu, its scale and dimension. There is something of value in psychogeographic practice and what he names the *eotechnical* worldview (a simpler or more primitive mode of technology, emanating more from the body and nonmotorized forms of transport), which has affirmative consequences for the comportment of the body with its immediate milieu. But critically we must insist the *eotechnical* worldview is still technique; it is a technique of the body and belongs to the flesh of the body and the brain. This is Self’s sleight of hand. However, for Self, simpler forms of technology grant a fuller experience of the environment, and here he echoes Merleau-Ponty (2004, p. 294) in *Eye and Mind* as the body is rendered tangible in the immediate milieu through the intertwining of vision and movement through an openness to the world. From this reading, we can understand psychopathologies better in the context of the living being immersed in the immediate milieu. But we need philosophy to account for the ecosophical point of view. 

In his ecosophy, Guattari also discusses the production of subjectivity under planetary computerization or reticular, semio-capitalism. In the cybernetic nature of contemporary culture, the production of subjectivity manifests through plural semiotic processes: this is the polyphonic dimension of subjectivity. Yet the other deleterious side of this is that the contactless BDDM addict suffers a form of machinic enslavement (Lazzarato, 2014). The pathic side to subjectivity here is important if we are to grasp the processes of subjectivation at work. This pathic side to subjectivity considers the role posture, movement, sensation, encounter, and contact play in the formation of subjectivity. Subjectivity and its production must be understood in terms of feeling and sensibility and its connection to the world and to lived time. The loss of vital contact with lived reality means a loss of sensing and sensibility. A loss of the movement of life. Connection to the outside world is a kind of openness and ambience, that is, the movement of a living being in its opening out toward the world, where movement is constitutive of the embodied milieu. Connection would be the capacity to contact with the self, with others, and the world – to be receptive to encounters. A vital union with the self and other would form an intersubjective relation, where connection to the outside is connection to 

*[p. 249]* ^pg264

desire as such. Contact arises out of an encounter, and from that there is the possibility of the emergence of a desire. 

There is something changing – not with the brain . . . but the mind, with the way human consciousness, taken collectively, is starting to experience its own reality. (Self, 2017) 

It is true that Will Self is a critic of toxic forms of technology, but he understands the role and limit of *eotechnical* forms and has no outright Luddite rejection of its use. As he insists: 

And I’m not a Luddite. I think it’s a waste of time trying to get rid of your phones or get rid of your technologies. But that doesn’t stop you making the radical and autonomous decision to shut it off, to abandon the metric and to move through space and through time under your own volition, or under random influences that will open you out to the world in another way, that will re-engage you with your poor body, which you’ve sadly neglected, except occasionally feeding it some antibiotics to try and keep it alive. And it will re-engage you with what we might think of fluvial time instead of that permanent now of the web and the internet. (Self, 2019) 

But in his idea of a phenomenological *epokhē* we find no critical commentary on the hyper-intoxication with portable devices and the mnemotechnic effects of industrial temporal objects like the smartphone. There is but the withdrawal into isolation and the cocooning of the self with literature to the distraction of all else. There is but the taking to the streets in some solipsistic, perambulant fashion. It seems we have little alternative but to become a medieval monk – itinerant or torpid. This is where Self’s conservatism and political liberalism is revealed as the question of dissensus is conspicuously absent. Why comport oneself to the world in a new way? What is changed and what modes of passivity and consensus are disrupted? Where is the journey of knowledge? Will Self has no clear understanding of how technology historically manifests and works or how it affects the minds of young people other than his appeal to the insights of Marshall McLuhan. One wonders if merely taking a walk and forming a relation with the outside is a sufficient recipe for transformation. For what of the extimacy of the world inside and outside, mental and social, which remains in a disaffected state (see Stiegler, 2014b)? Wandering in cities like Hotan in China or New Delhi in India and breathing in dangerous PM 2.5 levels 

*[p. 250]* ^pg265

reveals that a critique and understanding of the relationship of technology, capitalism, and mental and social ecologies is essential. Here Guattari and Stiegler whence combined offer an instructive, heuristic, and provocative perspective (Bradley, 2021b). Psychogeography is an excellent tool for dissensual resistance to a toxic form of sedentary and toxic mental ecology but only on the condition that the world and its nature turn away from a toxic social ecology hell-bent on consumption for the sake of consumption. Otherwise, the phenomenological *epokhē* too remains disaffected, narcissistic, self-regarding, and hermetic. What of the mass of rotting (*déroullier*) and the rotted in our urban technopoles? What of the self-marooned in disaffected states, such as the hikikomori in Japan? Will Self may wander aimlessly but not so far as to plunge into the hellholes of the *immonde*, to abide in nonplaces to transform them for the better. This is where Stiegler proves singularly insightful, for he has a general organology and industrial ecology of the spirit to account for not only the proliferation of disaffected spaces but also disaffected people. In the time of vast ecological damage, he writes politically and committedly on the enormous planetary challenge that presents itself, noting the cosmological and spiritual consequences that ensue: 

This recursivity is that of which cybernetic feedback loops are the computational grammatization, now effected through three billion smartphones spread across all continents of the biosphere, which has thus become a technosphere and an exosphere. (Stiegler, 2020a, p. 293) This loss of consciousness and affect, induced by cognitive and affective saturation, which constitutes the appalling reality of spiritual misery, at the very moment when the planet must confront and resolve so many difficulties, is what characterizes the lost spirit of capitalism. Today there are disaffected people just as there are disused factories \[*usines desaffectees*\]: there are human wastelands just as there are industrial wastelands. Such is the daunting question of the industrial ecology of the spirit. And such is the enormous challenge that befalls us. (Stiegler, 2012, p. 90) 

While Will Self appears to have no account of social ecology in his critique of BDDM, he does connect the illnesses of his characters in *Phone* to the historical, social, and technological material culture that envelops and 

*[p. 251]* ^pg266

nurtures them. We find the intermeshing of psychopathology and technological progress explored in the other books in the trilogy, *Umbrella* and *Shark*. In *Umbrella* it is the Parkinson’s-like brain inflammation, encephalitis lethargica, and the disruptive technology is mechanization ushered in from the industrial revolution. In *Shark* it is post-traumatic stress disorder against a backdrop of post-Hiroshima nuclear trauma and fear. While *Phone* questions how psychopathologies, technology, and the more encompassing military-industrial complex are somehow interconnected and how digital media, the Iraq War, and autism conspire together, Will Self offers no alternative or alternative future to this brute reality. On the connection between military communication and mobile phones, *spectrally*, and echoing Virilio and Baudrillard, Will Self (2019) writes of the dominance of the sign in fatalistic tones: 

When you think about it the year of the Iraq war was typified by the inception of bidirectional digital medias of all kinds, because for those of us who are old enough to remember Desert Shield in 91, it was notable for two things, the start of rolling, 24-hour TV news. And secondly, the so-called smart bomb, which had a camera in its nose cone. So it was the first war where you had an actual camera eye view of the bomb dropping on Baghdad. 

They’ve allowed the über-autists of Silicon Valley to feed them their thoughts, visions and reveries, and to calibrate their very movements, their brains have been rewired. (Self, 2017) 

Will Self intimates that addiction to portable devices may somehow be rearranging cognition, affecting memory, and fundamentally modifying human relations; he tries to account for the “wholly unprecedented, extraordinary popular delusions and madnesses” that have emerged in recent years, citing Alzheimer’s and autism as cases in point. Self goes on to suggest that the technological revolution is partly to blame for these maladies and that technology directly causes mental pathologies; however, again there is no antidote or countermeasure, or as he says, *it just is*: 

You have Alzheimer’s, the forgetting of everything, well, that’s not a problem because we got a device in our pocket that can remember everything effortlessly for us. And on the other hand, you’ve got the persistent urban myth that such a device is being created by highly functioning autistics in order to avoid the messy contingencies of direct 

*[p. 252]* ^pg267

emotional relationships. And we’re being steadily paralyzed by convolulus of cabling directed at us by human automata (Self, 2019). 

In the extraordinary time of the pandemic, we yearn for open spaces, for the “third world” of the *flâneur*, but we know all the while that our neighborhoods remain in distress and *disaffectation*. We live in uncontrollable societies of disaffected individuals (Stiegler, 2012). Inner-city spaces resist ambulation. Azouz Begag describes the struggle between what he terms *rouiller* (to rust or rot) and *s’arracher* (to tear oneself away from one’s setting). In this struggle, for Begag (2007) there is a struggle for the *flâneur* or urban self to create a “third space” or other space (see Conley, 2006, 2012). I read this as a struggle to create *epokhē,* as a struggle of *Zerrissenheit* or torn-to-pieces hood (Bradley, 2014). 

So what can we find in Will Self’s sense of psychogeography that can become a moment of *epokheē,* allowing us to think about the intimation of the third world in Popper’s sense and the third space of the *flâneur*? Can *dérive* be a therapeutic or palliative practice that tears us away from our sedentary, rotting addictions to form a new relation to the outside, giving us a little fresh air in our funk-filled, hypertrophied inner spaces? Can we find new addictions from this that allow us to escape our deadly refrains? Will Self self-critically, self-consciously, and indeed ironically mocks those who remonstrate with youth, who plead with them to stop tweeting, to get off Facebook, to stop social media, to refrain from watching back-to-back TV series streamed onto a tablet, phone, or computer. He ironically lambastes those belonging to a generation *like himself* who tell young people to get back to reading the classics lest “your brain is going to turn to gloop” (Self, 2016). 

An era when psychotics – modish, as they always are – were incorporating the new digital technologies into their delusions . . . faster than Silicon Valley can innovate o (Self, 2017) 

Clearly, in the time of “febrile waves of bidirectional, digitally mediated reality”, Will Self is right to note that we have in the making a generation *without memory and imagination*, that is immersed in the life of images, given over to the machinic production of affects. But again, what is his solution? Where can we find the punctum of the image? He questions the inflated epistemic value and importance we give to images disseminated 

*[p. 253]* ^pg268

without interpretation. His examples are the ISIS beheadings videos that contributed to or escalated the quagmire in Syria. Will Self (2021) insists that social media is inherently traumatogenic:

If we understand trauma to be a function of technologies that engender in us a sense of profound security underscored by high anxiety, then platforms such as Twitter, Instagram, and TikTok would seem purpose-built for its manufacture, offering as they do the coziness of Marshall McLuhan’s global village and its inevitable social problems: global gossip, global reviling, and global abuse.

Will Self states again and again that the novel is in crisis. The novelist is seldom heard over the cacophony of YouTube refrains, Facebook ads and Amazon jingles. How can the novelist compete in this marketplace of instantaneous release, euphoria, remuneration, and escape into the reticular ether? How can the novelist practice parrhésia (wapproia), to tell the truth about the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, if the time for epokhé, for solitude, silence, and the concentration necessary to read in a sustained way has been given over to the permanent present of BDDM time?

The smartphone is the cult object of digital domination. As a subjugation device, it acts like a rosary and its beads; this is how we keep a smartphone constantly at hand. The ‘like’ is a digital ‘amen.’ We keep going to confession. We undress by choice. But we don’t ask for forgiveness: instead, we call out for attention. (Han, 2021)

Echoing Byung-Chul Han who writes that the smartphone has become a tool of domination that acts as a rosary, Will Self describes the smartphone as a “worry bead”:

Hang on to the phone — that’s the thing to do. It’s all in the phone: my itinerary, my train times, my medical information — the whole lot. Hang on to the phone -— feel the smoothness of its bevelled screen........ Loe. eee. ! place your thumb in the soft depression of its bellybutton — turn it over and over . . . a five-hundred-quid worry bead —and all I worry about is losing the bloody thing. (Self, 2017)

*[p. 254]* ^pg269

Formation of Bildung 

Will Self, a critic of BDDM and the rhizomatic, open-ended learning format that the World Wide Web offers, tries to account for how we read in open-ended systems. He explains that we are not contained in this medium as we leap from resource to resource to find meaning: “If there’s an image you want, you can flip over to Google and find out what something looks like if it’s mentioned in the text.” It needs no further interpretation. Reading is interrupted by text message, call, or video meeting. In the BDDM multiliteral world, multiple competing narratives jostle for attention. He makes a strong argument on this point, and his reading is a challenge to the proponents of multiliteracy theory (Cazden et al., 1996; Cole & Pullen, 2009). Embracing multiliteracies is preferable if attention is recaptured from the marketing industries of Amazon, Google, Apple, Microsoft, and their ilk but only if the new literacies allow us to read the world in not only a productive and creative way but also a critical and skeptical one. But for Will Self, the self-contained inner literary world of a novel or treatise is invaded by unwanted purveyors of meaning. Simply, we need self-containment to read and think. Reading the constructed literary worlds of this or that author demands attention and the suspension of disbelief. With a plethora of multiple formats and multiple platforms, it is less and less meaningful to sit down and read for a sustained length of time. Why not hand over the production of meaning and affects to algorithms, reticular capitalism, image data banks, or sound archives? For Will Self, if we do this, writing becomes over-literalized, a mere description of the preformatted image. He says that the very technology with which his ideas and stories are conveyed to his readers makes it less attractive both to him and to them to present a book in the traditional fashion. As such, he decries the fact that many young people flip between phone and tablet and whatever else in a state of distraction, with mental health problems mushrooming as a consequence. Will Self complains that young people don’t read and that they have lost the art of wandering because memory and recall have been affected by BDDM. We no longer make an image in our own mind: the marketing industries make the images for us. But again this appears to be a description without ethical prescription. Yet Will Self at least tempers his pessimism, noting that one codex always naturally replaces another: “We’re 

*[p. 255]* ^pg270

changing. Our culture is changing. Our minds are changing. The ways we do things are changing. It may be that we’re not going to be quite as individualistic in the future as we thought, because we much more share a collective memory and a collective perception. Some people might argue that small print culture did: it created a shared reality, much more powerfully” (Self, 2018). 

Will Self’s point is that BDDM takes over this role with disastrous results leading to increasing solitude and further intoxication with entertainment, streaming films, et cetera. Here he is close to Stiegler who notes the devastating effects that the marketing-led World Wide Web is having upon the sensoria of young people. But again Will Self has no antidote and is complicit with it even as he reproduces this mass entertainment industry himself. His novels are converted into audio books, which he himself reads to those who turn away from the written word. What happens when we listen rather than read? That is the question that affects the codex. 

Perhaps what I have said is too harsh. Perhaps this is to use technology pharmacologically or therapeutically. Why not take another view and claim that the pharmacology of technology can be liberating in some way? Why not take Will Self for a walk to see what new incorporeal universes of reference are prised open? One can read his *word-stream-being-in-the-world* on the train using any number of electronic devices. Can this be a new form of psychogeography? Why not take the novel for a walk by way of wandering, drifting lines, with technology attached and voices in our heads? Can this be a new form of *dérive*? This is when and where we map the world around us, to make an image of the world in our minds, to remember parts of the city as voices and ideas stick to the buildings, pavements, traffic lights, station names as well as the undulations of the immediate natural landscape. 

It is true that with so many distractions in the world, being alone at times can become nigh impossible. As such Will Self makes the argument that young people should learn how to be in solitude in order to undergo the phenomenological *epokhē.* Given that the minds and imagination of young people are somehow given over to the technical imagination, to digital tertiary retentions, and the marketing industries (Stiegler & Roussow, 2020), and there is manifestly an inability to conjure an image of one’s own in one’s own mind, there are some very good reasons why we should be 

*[p. 256]* ^pg271

concerned about the loss of the imagination in the time of planetary technology. Will Self is of course right to note, and neuroscience confirms this, that when one is concentrating and focusing on what is presented to the mind in the pages of great literature, great novels, and great philosophy, something happens in the mind, which has great benefits for recollection and the imagination (Wolf & Stoodley, 2008; Bradley, 2021a). But Will Self warns that multitextual, hyperlinked text somehow does the job of forging preformatted images in the mind but *little else*. One can press the button for the definition of a word if one doesn’t know it, rather than trying to make sense of its meaning within the totality of the text, or one can see a photograph of an object instead of conjuring it up in the mind. One can hear music that one only has a vague recollection of or read the lyrics that one never really understood in the first place. 

I differ from Will Self because I see that the *dérive*, the wandering, mapmaking as such can be imaginative, experimental, and creative when it is mediated precisely *through technology*. How can the *dérive* or drifting be a “journey of knowledge”, a “journey of technique”, a technique of rapid passage through varied ambiances and refrains? One proposal is to think about nonhuman drone technology and how we can use something like Google Lens to see that the world is teeming with nonhuman entities and algorithmic data. This will be used to guide the user on a so-called journey of knowledge (Iwase & Bradley, 2021). What this means is that this will be part of an individuation process following Gilbert Simondon, where subjectivation takes place at the site where the individual *comes into being*. The phone screen can function as a mediated field through which we can comport ourselves in and to the world. With phone, with sound and music, just walking through the city might somehow connect schizo kids (Asada, 1984) with new cartographies of desire. Indeed, as we are slowly emerging out of the coronavirus pandemic, the task that confronts teachers and students alike is to find ways for young people to explore the world outside their homes and their bedrooms (Kim, 2021). One way to do this is to think about psychogeography through the activity of *dérive* (Debord, 1958; Plant, 2000). This would tie together postmedia, ecosophy, desire, and the unconscious with embodied phenomenological drifting. Through drifting, through spatial exploration and visualization, one can apply psychogeography via forms of mnemotechnics, through users recording 

*[p. 257]* ^pg272

video screens, taking photographs or recording sounds, or using GPS to record affective responses to the disruptive and disrupted environment in which they are immersed. I am interested in the passage through often unknown invisible urban spaces and what affects manifest in due course (Richardson, 2017). In this play of dislocation-relocation, technology can be used therapeutically, pharmacologically, and curatively. Here ecosophy would promote the logic of the flux and of wandering lines as a creative form of wayfaring. What is of interest is the disruption of perception through an errant urban trajectory and what is important is disorientation per se and the affective response to that unfamiliar feeling. At the heart of this schizoanalytic pedagogy is the question of affect and the critical exploration of not only the solitary unconscious but what we might call the collective algorithmic unconscious. This affective topography of the digital era — an algorithmic psychogeography — might allow users to explore emotions and mnemotechnics via smartphone use. This is where Stiegler is singularly important because he understands the struggle for self-cultivation (Bildung), for education, as precisely a struggle for freedom. He is preoccupied with the following questions: How does self-cultivation take place when one is enveloped in toxic, entropic forms of knowledge? How does one create, imagine, and shape one’s mind when one is surrounded by the total hell of the world as it is and as a result suffer its traumatogenic effects? How does one mature when transindividuation circuits between the generations are torn in shreds? These are questions concerning Bildung, that is, one’s spiritual and cultural sensibility, one’s growth into adulthood. This shows a concern with both the ecology and crisis of spirit. In the next section, I designate and interpret this as the theatrum belli of the imagination.

Theatrum Belli of the Imagination and Critical Postmedia As we have seen throughout his oeuvre Will Self consistently attempts to account for the intermeshing of disruptive technologies, ensuing psychopathologies, and the historical and politico-military culture that envelops them. For example, when Self turns to the Iraq war (2003-11), he is in effect discussing the theatrum belli of the imagination of the BDDM generation. This is the inescapable logic of trauma. The BDDM generation lives under the continual threat and trauma of drone strikes and the logic of

*[p. 258]* ^pg273

terrorism. The BDDM generation consumes the death drive of symbolic exchange and lives a counter-gift of death. They live a crisis of spirit or mind, one discerned by French poet and philosopher Paul Valéry (1919) in the years after the First World War (see Hui, 2020). It is a crisis of spirit that continues in the present and is exacerbated by the lost spirit of capitalism, cognitive capitalism, and BDDM technological systems as such (Stiegler, 2014a). The result is an “ecological crisis of spirit” (Stiegler, 2014b, p. 88). The BDDM generation is incapacitated by the traumatogenic effects of the military-industrial complex to think otherwise than the status quo. This is where Self’s thought connects with the (im)possibility of the *third world* of the imagination, the (im)possibility of *epokhe,* the irreality of the simulacrum, the collapse of the real, the circulation of symbolic exchange and death, and so on. And he is right to reflect that the collective amnesia regarding the bloody, cruel fiasco of the Iraq war as simulation has become a key prism to understanding the digital world. As he says: “I think it’s the biggest stain on our national character of the past 20 years. And I think that collective amnesia about it and refusal to engage with it is playing out in political decisions that are being made right now” (Self, 2019). Again, following in the line of critics such as Virilio and Baudrillard, he is critical of the flow of preformed images that replace the organic imagination of the individual; he questions how images conjured in the mind can be anything other than weapons of the war machine disseminated by BDDM technologies. Again, how can there be a punctum or bursting of the inflated epistemic value of the image? But Self misses a trick in his own dialogue between an Iraqi detainee and Captain Campbell in *Phone*, despite the influence from surrealism and situationism and despite mention of the atrocities of the Abu Ghraib torture and prisoner abuse in 2003. The Iraqi detainee under duress says: 

Now the Great Satan’s in on the act, too – those pictures of the abuse at Abu Ghraib, the prisoners all piled up on top of each other – Kurds on top of Sunnis, Sunnis on top of Shias – same martial-races policy, really – and that Munchkin standing alongside, looking like some public- school educated British subaltern of the imperial era, tormented by his homosexuality and his inability to grow a beard. (Self, 2018) 

*[p. 259]* ^pg274

While seemingly aware of the simulation logic of Baudrillard and the dromology of Virilio, Self fails to see that Abu Ghraib acts as an inverted second order simulacrum of the real. In the time of the pandemic this has become more apparent. Our sedentary life reveals the order of traumatic simulacra and fear of the outside. It hides the real through a pseudo-reproduction. Why? From my critical postmedia perspective, I read the events at Abu Ghraib as follows to show that Abu Ghraib does not lead to us a referent of the real but to the erasure of the real from a second and then third-order simulacra where a system of symbols and signs assumes reality by enfolding a new layer of the symbolic. 

My point here is that critical consciousness faces an ever bleaker prospect and as such we need much more robust critical models to account for the BDDM generation’s collusion in the hyperreal. Again it is Stiegler rather than Self who does more than most to account for this profound existential malaise and crisis, and it is he who offers a robust critique of the status quo. 

Outside all hell breaks loose. Deafening shell after deadening shell, relentless; the walls shake and the youth guards are petrified. They peer into their phones. The bloody horrors of the outside world are reflected in this small screen. They protect themselves there in that permanent now of the screen. They cocoon themselves deeper into the deadly ipseity of the self – repetitive compulsions, chaosmic spasms stimming away unconsciously – and disseminate more horror. From a third eye of self-surveillance, disavowing, *they are this horror*, acutely aware of it and complicit. They willingly join and condone this networked ecology and economy of images depicting bare life in the carceral zones of the US military empire. Yet the archiving of images shields them from what awaits outside the walls of the prison. This ecology of images on the phone is a simulacrum of the wider world. The dominance of this image economy over the organic imagination surrenders the reality of the world outside to a “catastrophic sense of incarceration” inside, as Virilio says: “Imagination today is in the image, and the image is in power. Right now there aren’t any ideas. Imagination today is in the image, and the image is in power. There’s no imagination for anything but the image.” Deprived of critical perspective, their horizons obliterated, the young soldiers find neither ethical relation nor proximity to the other. The pollution of time and distance goes hand in hand with the detonation of 

*[p. 260]* ^pg275

material substances outside. The body experiences the ruin of temporal rhythm as there is but one global time – the time of global terror – the permanent now of BDDM. This is what Virilio calls the contamination of space time by real time. Outside the prison, the bombs rain down. They kill and maim innocent people and children in the name of a brutal ecology of violence. The young, contracted guards inside the prison who hear the havoc and the horror outside carry out psychical and physical trauma on the inmates and on themselves. They create a group fantasy to protect themselves from the trauma of war and terror. This is a group fantasy not drawn from ideology but from a deadly cycle of desire, a deadly ipseity of the self, and a disindividuation process of profound consequence. 

The disseminated images at the Abu Ghraib prison express a shared fantasy. The violent images and affects – shared by young people who are not long out of college or high school – are taken, shared, stored, archived on their phones and indeed celebrated like the KKK postcards of lynching mobs in the 1880s and 1930s. With the collapse of existential time into permanent “now-time,” into the global present or global now, in true Orwellian fashion BDDM controls the present and thus controls the past. Abu Ghraib therefore is a simulacrum hiding a wider group or societal fantasy. The spectacle of Abu Ghraib is not a mere collection of images, but a socio-traumatogenic relation among young people, guards, family and friends, military personnel, and the media: all mediated by images. Here, the philosophies of Guattari and Stiegler are important ecosophical prisms to understand this socio-traumatogenic relation. The fact is that the rest of the world is like this now, constantly sharing war porn – ISIS videos, KKK postcards, hate speeches – take your pick; the constant sharing of images is our collective planetary group fantasy and symbolic planetary death drive. Abu Ghraib prison is a second-order autistic simulacra, reflecting the collapse of the global symbolic order – the psychopathological, traumatogenic effects of depression, despair, anxiety, the outbursts of sadistic violence, the acting out of the drives: these are the logical outcomes of this collapse. 

From a Guattarian, critical postmedia perspective, we must question the fluxes, existential territories, incorporeal universes, and the machinic phylum in this prison scenario to better understand the intimate and inextricable relationship between mental ecology, social ecology, and the 

*[p. 261]* ^pg276

wider environmental problem. We must understand the impossibility of *epokhē* here. And we can understand such phenomena using Guattari’s three ecologies, Gregory Bateson’s ecology of bad ideas, Virilio’s grey ecology, and Sontag’s ecology of images: but we can also turn to Bernard Stiegler and his industrial ecology of the spirit. This is not taken in any transcendent sense, as he is not trying to get out of the world and its horrors but to get deeper within it. This is also the concern of critical postmedia theory that is at root a metamodelization exercise to get deeper into the materiality of the world, into the *immonde* of the world, which is a world “*raw, stinking, and quotidian*” as Sartre says, to offer social and political and ecosophical solutions. It seems to me that it is simply not enough to describe the present situation but to offer the possibility of another world, to think about the critique of the present and to offer a new model of the future. This is why Guattari remains essential for it is he who clings to the idea of an unconscious desperately turned toward the future in the hope of extracting singular points of bifurcation: “The primary purpose of ecosophical cartography is . . . to produce assemblages of enunciation capable of capturing the points of singularity of a situation” (Guattari, 1995, p. 128). Here, schizoanalytic cartography is heuristic and essential in interpreting the relationship between mental health and space. 

Reflecting on the images from Abu Ghraib, Baudrillard (2006, p. 87) insists in his essay “War Porn” that “those who live by the spectacle will die by the spectacle.” To die by the spectacle? This is indeed the death-in-life of Stiegler’s miserable disindividual, a kind of destroyed self, suffering symbolic misery, who survives through the violent acting out of the drives as a form of negative sublimation. In what respect, as Virilio says, do technologically mediated images prevent us from thinking, or prevent the *epokhē* of the epoch? Virilio insists images have become an important tool in war and as weapons of war communication they take on a reality to effectively pacify and control the masses: that is, to stop thinking and criticism from taking place. Peter Sloterdijk says much the same. Abu Ghraib is a theme park; it replaces “bodily sensations” of physical activity. The guards are entranced by “pure optical illusions” and “imaginary mental sensations.” The *theatrum belli* is tied to a mental field so that the scene of the pictures, *the theatrum belli of the imagination*, is inextricably tied to the 

*[p. 262]* ^pg277

materialism of war. The war of images is part of the actual, perpetual war, part of a Realpolitik *constructed in fantasy* (see Arnaut, 2011). 

Outside, the masses are hypnotized by images, by the second-order simulacra of Abu Ghraib. The story and horror of Abu Ghraib is salutary as it demonstrates the normalization of violence in the time of BDDM reality, a time that suffers from the contraction of space and intensification of time. While Bernard Stiegler found his own moment of creation in the *epokhē* of his prison term, the guards of Abu Ghraib are given over entirely to the intoxication of mobile technologies. In *extrapolating* from the horrors of Abu Ghraib, we find the simulacrum is perversely inverted by a surveillant hyperreality, by recursive algorithmic feedback loops that circulate the violence of the image and inundate its users at speeds no one quite knows how to control. This is a fourth-order simulacra distorted by speed. This is explained by Pete Brook (2014) in *Wired.* He cites Virilio’s suggestion that speed is inherently political and hence never neutral: “The speed of changes to our communications landscape has overwhelmed our capacity to make real decisions, so they are increasingly being made for us. This is for some precisely the condition of the algorithmic reality which is upon us.” This sense of decisions being made *machinically* and *algorithmically* elsewhere seems consistent with Self’s critique of BDDM and the disturbance of fluvial time. In his reflections on speed and the loss of perspective vis-[illegible]-vis the image, Virilio says the following: “On ne rêve pas, on ne voyage pas, on est voyage, on est rêve” (We do not dream, we do not travel, we are travelled, we are dreamed) (Sina, 1996, p. 50). This I think is a view shared by both Stiegler and Self. 

The point is that we have become so accustomed to this ocular violence that we are immune and indifferent to it. Virilio reads this in terms of the loss of spatial sense and the triumph of instantaneous time or global-one time – that is one more visual and mental form of pollution streamed simultaneously *without a blink of the eye*. And of course without a blink there is no escape from the seduction of the image. We are left hypnotized and stupefied *en masse* in circuits of petrified desire and fantasy. As such, we, as prisoners of the image, as prisoners *without epokhē,* suffer “total enclosure.” Virilio’s explanation of the role of the phatic image helps us to understand the role of speed and kinetic imagery. Contra Deleuze, he insists that the kinetic nature offers no reprieve from the hallucination of 

*[p. 263]* ^pg278

manufactured images: “So let’s not entertain any further illusions about photography’s commercial prowess. The phatic image that grabs our attention and forces us to look is no longer a powerful image; it is a cliche attempting, in the manner of the cineframe, to inscribe itself in some unfolding of time in which the optic and the kinematic are indistinguishable” (Virilio, 1994, p. 62). 

In a broad sense I am in agreement with Self regarding the delirious and deleterious effects of BDDM but I read his caution through and alongside Guattarian and Stieglerian concepts: I see the time of BDDM as suffering from a chaosmic and chronic spasm (Berardi, 2017). In a form of negative sublimation, we are imaging ourselves to death to shield ourselves from the reality of the ecological anthropocenic nightmare that is outside our control. In response, we are tasked to metamodel and resingularize territories – to think beyond the chaosmic spasm of disseminated war porn and to step outside. As Guattari (1995, p. 135) says in *Chaosmosis*: “What are the ordeals of barbarism, the mental implosion and chaosmic spasm looming on the horizon?” This is what Guattari asks, but even before Abu Ghraib and the war on terror. Beyond the chaosmic spasm of group fantasies of torture and abuse, beyond the spasm of compulsive disorders, is the possibility of remodulating spasmodic vibrations that can pass through the *socius*: this is to “breathe regularly” to a different tune and new rhythm of life as Berardi says. The destructive refrain of torture image dissemination is an obsessive refrain of nihilism and trauma – a deadly repetition of the death-in-life of stupefied ipseity. Instead, schizoanalysis asks for a critique of info-neural acceleration, of the worst excesses of hyper-expressivity in semio-capitalism; it asks for the whereabouts of vibrant bodies and the resingularization of existence. Simply, we want an end to the refrain of suffering, addiction, burnout, panic, and depression. 

It is useful therefore to juxtapose Stiegler, Guattari, and Self, and this can offer a challenge to the hyper-spastic phase of Integrated World Capitalism, to its toxic refrains of acceleration, velocity, and speed, and to refrains that together produce painful vibrations in cognitive work and nervous exploitation as Berardi says. We must search for chaoides – “living decoders of chaos” (Berardi, 2015, p. 221) – to escape the refrains of symbolic misery, negative sublimation, and the unconscious gone berserk. We must search for singular rhythms of life. Beyond a world of chaotic 

*[p. 264]* ^pg279

hyperintensity, we ask for nothing less than a new ecosophy, for new chaoids of alterity, for a new sense of ipseity, and like Guattari for a new sensitivity. We ask for nothing less than a radical *punctum* of deadly repetitions and deadly images. 

Concluding Remarks 

What would be the points of convergence and divergence in Bernard Stiegler’s and Will Self’s work? It seems to me there are certain ways to address this question. One might focus on the question of *dérive*, situationism and surrealism, the role of the *flâneur* and the fact that one cannot move around the city without using mobile telephony. The idea would be a psychogeography, but the practice of *dérive* would be an excrescent or abnormal outgrowth of the simulacra, an extreme model, a fatal model, but at least one way out. But who are the *dérouilleurs* (“to derust or loosen up”) of the modern era? Who are the “de-rusters,” the movers in the time of stasis? The *dérouilleurs* might be those people who can break out of the repetitive refrains and funk and bodily comportments with screen culture. The *dérouilleur* would embrace a new praxis of the *flâneur*, the embrace of the leakages and seepages of errant lines of flight. Would the creation of a kind of Ballardian-Kafkaesque-Joycean-Debordian-Beckettian theoretical assemblage understand this better? More than this, we need an update to the McLuhan understanding of media, for it is in front of our screen that we freeze and rot. We need a new form of social and psychogeographic mobility – a new way to comport ourselves in sites of disaffection, contactlessness, and disconnection. We need to rip ourselves from the contactless screen, to embrace a new form of streaming desire: an *efflux* of subjectivity and desire, an effluvium of change, to hold out on the reemergence of youth from the coils of deadly ipseity. In other words, to affirm the outpouring of change and transformation with the unconscious rushing toward the future (Walkerdine, 2013). To bust out of the metric, to break with the man-machine matrix, why not trace a map of the local milieu, organized around everyday points of reference, and explore the rhythmic refrains and impermanence of minor movements performed in the territory during the day? Why not trace the drifting, non-titutularian lines, lines that 

*[p. 265]* ^pg280

are curved, repetitive, aimless — lignes d’erre (wander lines) — the contingent, immanent, and self-organizing lines?

The reply or answer to the above questions in a sense is both Stieglerian and Selfian. It is Stieglerian because in the passage of the “doubly epochal redoubling” one must use technology pharmacologically and in a dissensual, transformative, and therapeutic way. But it is also Selfian as one can find in the principles of psychogeography the offer of a phenological relation of the body to the immediate environment. Here, psychogeography connects with some of the recent thoughts of Berardi, when he calls for more concatenation and the commingling of bodies, and the ideas of a new relation to alterity one finds in Malabou. This also connects well with the ideas of Fernand Deligny (2016) at the La Borde clinic in France in the 1950s, where an experimental cartography of wandering, of drift, and racing of the movement of the body was practiced. I think a new practice could be undertaken as a kind of response to the excess of BDDM or the oxic iteration of the World Wide Web as it stands. This would be to search for a new social ecology, a relationship to the outside. At least this would be a better method than some of the more bizarre phenomena such as the perverse desire to feel what it is like to die by spending time in a coffin or by retreating from society for the weekend to be away from one’s mobile phone. I believe it is better to reject such narcissistic and autistic withdrawal from society. Instead, why not be part of its re-creation through wandering and drifting, embracing the mediated and phantasmagorical dérive of the mobile phone, a chaosmosis of itinerant therapeutic practice, even if it is without rhyme or reason?
