## The Contemporary Relevance of Sociology

*23 February 1951*

\[…\] I should like to thank you all for having turned up in such numbers here this evening. I think I am also justified in taking this as an expression of your interest in the matter in question rather than simply of an interest in the somewhat sparse and, I would immediately add, modest observations that I would like to make before you regarding the theme of our discussion today, namely the question of the contemporary relevance of sociology. For it is an essential feature of this discipline that it is actually very difficult to provide a general overview of the field which is covered by this subject, that it is easy to lose oneself in rather arbitrary reflections in this connection, and that any really substantial intellectual contribution we might have to offer can, to a very significant degree, only be produced in the context of quite specific concrete work. On the other hand, I feel compelled at the very least to introduce you to a range of relatively general problems, although I am also aware that I must content myself here with what are only ‘formal indications’<sup>1</sup> of these problems, to use an expression of Husserl, who has already been mentioned by Herr Solms.<sup>2</sup> And I would just ask you to bear this point constantly in mind from the very start. Now in order to bring out the relevance and significance of sociology today, and specifically in terms of the relationship between sociology and politics, it would probably be enough simply to remind you of the very recent past where this particular discipline is concerned. For sociology, as you know, was definitely *persona non grata* in the eyes of the National Socialists and was effectively, if not officially, repressed or discouraged by them. And it may not 

*[p. 18]* ^pg25

be the worst way of justifying sociology’s right to exist if we reflect for a moment upon what it was that actually motivated this extreme hostility to sociology on the part of the National Socialists. I would explain it in terms of the fact that the National Socialists – precisely because they were essentially concerned with imposing on people a form of politics which was nonetheless fundamentally opposed to the interests, to the real interests, of the people in question – took infinite pains to direct attention away from the actual life process of society, from the effective collective processes that determine our existence. And sociology was already suspect in their eyes simply because it is not concerned with offering a replica of the reality they wished to impose on people precisely in order to play fast and loose with reality itself. For sociology, by contrast, is concerned with what is actually happening within the social process as a whole, and indeed I would not hesitate to describe the task of sociology in something like these terms. However, I would ask you not to take this as a definition; I am well aware that one cannot really define such complex disciplines as our own, and perhaps I might add that, as a philosopher, I am enough of a Hegelian to harbour a profound scepticism about any investigation that commences with definitions.<sup>3</sup>

The National Socialists sought to distract people either by appealing to a fantasized notion of nature, which functioned as a substitute for social-historical reality and found its clearest expression in the racialist ideology of the movement, or by focusing attention on the sphere of organized political power, on the external relations with other states, on the concept of the Party and matters of this kind. All of this basically betrayed a profound fear before the prospect of what we might call self-reflection on the part of society – a fear of the moment in which human beings might in principle become aware of the prevailing relationship between them, the relationship that is perpetuated in the social production and reproduction of their life, a fear in short that the entire despotic system in that case would collapse in on itself like a house of cards. Whereas the powers that be felt far more secure by preventing any such thing, by being able to create a spurious conceptual superstructure instead. Now the relevance of sociology in this situation lies precisely in the way that it tends to promote the very self-reflection on the part of society which the National Socialist regime wished to prevent, and which I am convinced is also currently prevented in the other totalitarian system of today. Yet such self-reflection is the precondition if something resembling a society that is worthy of human beings is to emerge after all, instead of the possibility that everything might be engulfed in an unimaginable catastrophe. But this self-reflection, this truth that 

*[p. 19]* ^pg26

belongs to the self-knowledge of society and thus to the realization of its freedom, is all the more necessary today when the indescribable complexity of the social apparatus, of the prevailing social mechanisms, serves so much to obscure and distort the true connections in the context of our ordinary pre-theoretical consciousness.

This is why the ordinary exercise of human reason on its own no longer seems capable of penetrating the process in question, for what we call sound common sense, which we do not wish to consider directly here, actually requires the help of sustained systematic reflection if we are ever to understand what often appears to hang over every single individual as a kind of blind anonymous fate. But this task also involves a certain philosophical aspect, and here I would specifically like to emphasize, in line with the major philosophical tradition,<sup>4</sup> that I am convinced – and that the Institute for Social Research,<sup>5</sup> which I have the honour to represent in this regard, is also convinced – that philosophy and sociology cannot be separated from each other in a purely schematic fashion, and indeed that, on the contrary, these two particular fields, separated though they are through the academic division of labour, are nonetheless in some way essentially bound up with each other. Now it is unfortunately still the case that philosophy in Germany retains something of that authoritarian legacy which consists in emphasizing the concrete character of the fundamental questions, yet in the name of this concreteness appeals to the structures of human existence itself or those of being itself, structures which are supposedly free of entanglement in real social relations. Yet the task of sociology would precisely be to grasp truth itself in the context of this entanglement.

Here I should perhaps underline what Herr Graf Solms has already said, namely that we are not talking about sociologism in the sense of a relativism that simply asserts there is no truth because every claim to knowledge is socially conditioned. I believe that this extremely vulgar view can only appear plausible when we approach the problem of society in a detached and external manner. But as soon as we really look at social questions more closely, the so-called relativity of knowledge is dispelled by the binding character of social insight itself. On the other hand, I believe that the specifically Hegelian legacy, which lies in the attempt to think the question of truth and the life process of humanity together, has to a considerable extent been taken up into the task of providing a concrete analysis of society – something for which, in the German tradition at least, Hegel’s *Phenomenology of Spirit* has given all of us an initial paradigm. The assumption of some invariant truth that is indifferent to the social process in my view represents a regressive step in comparison to those 

*[p. 20]* ^pg27

elements of the German intellectual tradition which have proved so productive precisely because they neither betrayed the truth to the realm of the merely existent nor, on the other hand, stretched the concept of truth into something so abstract that it forfeits its binding obligation to reveal reality, and thereby delivers reality all the more emphatically to the blind play of forces.

Now I recall that, in the pre-Hitler period, Heidegger once declared that sociology<sup>6</sup> relates to the work of philosophy like someone who clambers up the outside of the house and steals off with what the others inside have produced.<sup>7</sup> I believe that this comparison is illegitimate and fails to reflect the truth, and I would actually prefer to say, if I may draw on my own work in philosophy here, that the problem of philosophy itself, at its very heart or core, is intrinsically confronted with social issues and relations, rather than suggesting that one should attempt to relate social problems externally to the problems of ethics or the problems of epistemology for example. And I will not deny that the so-called sociology of knowledge has created considerable confusion in this connection, where there have actually been attempts to explain Kantian philosophy in terms of the particular social situation of Prussian officialdom and administration in the second half of the eighteenth century.<sup>8</sup>

Now I believe that, if you consider any of the really responsible sociologists, namely those who seriously reflect on the entanglement of truth and society, you will be hard put to find a single one who believes that the problems we are talking about here can be resolved in such a simple fashion. I am certainly far from wanting to offer you some general prescription for the problem at issue. But I think that we cannot get anywhere either by adopting a relativist insouciance or by appealing to some dogmatic and absolutist position that shuns social self-reflection. For it is precisely here that we are confronted by one of the most important and most responsible tasks which sociology has to address if it is to fulfil its genuine intellectual vocation and its human calling. But I would also like to point out that the relevance of sociology is by no means confined to the questions specifically related to the human and social sciences, to the relationship between sociology and philosophy, or to the task of giving more concrete expression to philosophical issues. For it would certainly involve an all too idealist conception of the discipline of sociology if we simply attempted to ignore the fact that the contemporary German situation clearly demands, in the most drastic and immediate fashion, the insights that can be derived from continued social research. The things that have transpired in Germany actually need to be recognized, to a very large extent, as a genuinely collective 

*[p. 21]* ^pg28

phenomenon. This concerns the most obvious and visible things such as the destruction of our cities, and the degree to which society as a whole, and not only the particular individuals who make it up, has been fundamentally dislocated, so that merely planless solutions or ones conceived solely in terms of the principle of laissez-faire are largely out of the question. But sociology represents the only reliable means or resource that exists today with regard to the question of rational planning in the context of these German problems. And it is precisely here that we discover a direct coincidence between the philosophical demand for social self-reflection and what I would call the material demand that what actually happens today is measured in terms of the needs of society.

I would like to offer you a few examples, in very brief form, so that you can see we are really talking about very drastic and fundamental things here. Thus we are directly confronted by the refugee question that can only be resolved if we know, both objectively and subjectively speaking, everything that we need to know about these refugees. Now this is an eminently sociological problem precisely because, as far as the subjective consciousness of the refugees is concerned, we are immediately presented with an entire range of phenomena which, in a most acute or extreme form, involve things that are already bound up with the problem of the state of society in general. A whole series of individual investigations in relation to refugees, which I do not intend to discuss in detail here, have repeatedly shown that the immediate demands of individual interest take precedence over the general interest – in other words, over the political interest in the broadest sense.<sup>9</sup> Now this is usually explained in terms of the particular situation in which refugees find themselves. But I would say that, in relation to refugees, as is almost always the case with rather extreme phenomena, we can identify and study things which also concern the mainstream social reality, and indeed society as a whole. And I would say that this phenomenon of alienation from the political realm, that this problem of a reprivatized individualistic consciousness that seems to be the shadow image of modern mass society, that this key problem of our contemporary situation can be identified in the specific case of the refugees in a particularly drastic form. And all of the questions connected with the political future of the refugees, and with the political role they may play in shaping German affairs, are naturally also directly bound up with this circumstance.

A similar question arises in connection with the problem of reconstruction, rebuilding and housing. Here too, I would argue, we are talking about far more than simply identifying all those who require 

*[p. 22]* ^pg29

accommodation, or exploring the relevant housing possibilities, along with various other comparable more or less basically empirical considerations. You have only to reflect that to a considerable extent there is a certain contradiction between what on the one hand is objectively required in the way of housing for the preponderant majority of those who require accommodation – namely the kind of largely regularized and standardized dwellings that would meet the objective needs in question – and on the other hand the subjective taste of those seeking accommodation, which to a very large degree clings to individualistic conceptions of housing that still derive from the later nineteenth century.

Behind this kind of contradiction between objective needs and the subjective reflection of need there lie major and fundamental questions of our modern society in general – such as why it is, and what it signifies, that human consciousness changes so much more slowly than the real social relations under which human beings live. This fundamental social problem is also one that can be studied very fruitfully in this particular field. And finally let me mention a third contemporary problem in this connection, namely the whole question of the relationship between sociology and political science.<sup>10</sup> Today in particular this is a question that lies at the very centre of general academic debate. For I would say that the decision as to whether politics represents a special sphere that also deserves special scientific treatment and investigation as such is itself a question which in the broader sense falls within our own science, namely that of sociology. The political problems in the narrower sense represent only the institutional replication of the play of forces within society, while that play of forces itself, prior to its institutional reification, can only be grasped by the science of society. At the same time, however, the field of politics, of politics as a profession, has actually acquired a certain independence in relation to the reality of society, and there is something such as ‘politics’ as a special professional sphere. But this separation of politics from the immediate actual life of human beings themselves is, just as much as the social side of politics, to be grasped as a social phenomenon. Both questions would therefore fall within the range of problems addressed by a philosophically oriented sociology that is also subject to rigorous empirical discipline. Here you can immediately recognize something of the philosophical background of social science which I have already pointed out, for this problem regarding the relationship between society and politics – which of course I can only really touch upon in passing along with other things here today – provides a direct model for the philosophical relevance of sociology, the issue with which we began: 

*[p. 23]* ^pg30

namely the task of overcoming, through self-critical reflection, the reified<sup>11</sup> and ossified conceptual replication of life that increasingly characterizes the administered world. But since such an overcoming cannot possibly be achieved by simply opposing or supplementing this institutionally ossified and alienated life by recourse to romantic metaphysics, the exemplary task of sociology in this connection becomes clear: to awaken the consciousness of human beings that this world which confronts them as an alien and in decisive respects as an inhuman world is nonetheless a world of human beings – one that points back to human beings and the living relations between them, a world that can ultimately only be changed through a reshaping of these relations and of human beings themselves.

This is enough to suggest the general relevance of sociology. But what I have said to you so far is of course far too general. And you will all want to ask which sociology is particularly relevant here, for I do not deny that the concept of sociology is as manifold or as nebulous as that of its object, namely society, and this is precisely the problem that we have to address. Now I think that we can get closer to the heart of the problem if we make certain critical demarcations that will help us to identify some of the particular dangers that confront sociology today. I believe that once we have discussed a few key words it will also be much easier for us to clarify the idea of a sociology in the form that is required. First of all, I would like to say that, in the situation of today, the traditional German sociology, in the context of which I also grew up and to which we owe some very significant achievements, in the immediate form in which it has come down to us is no longer adequate to grasp the current reality. And this, unless I am much mistaken, is for two fundamental reasons. One of these reasons lies in the distinctly speculative character of such sociology, while the other lies in the way that to a large extent, partly with and partly against its own intention, it allowed itself to be enlisted in the cause of ideological obfuscation. The indifference of German thought towards the factual dimension of the human and social realm developed out of the dissolution of the great idealist systems that had once undertaken to make sense of concrete human reality by recourse to the constructive work of the concept; now all that remained was the activity of conceptual construction, which became thinner and thinner over time and increasingly forfeited genuine contact with the work of the concept. At the same time, the activity of concept formation itself was increasingly abandoned to a kind of empirical research or to mere empiricism, which in its separation from philosophical conceptuality is no longer adequate to meet the tasks in question. To illustrate this particular danger 

*[p. 24]* ^pg31

for you here I need only to mention the otherwise highly significant achievement of Dilthey, with its curious combination of Hegelian tendencies and positivist empiricism.<sup>12</sup> In this connection I would also recall a rather significant work by Tönnies that is probably known to all of you, namely his book *Community and Society*.<sup>13</sup> Here you can clearly see just how thin the concepts have become in the course of this development, and the thinner the concepts became the greater the violence they inevitably inflicted upon reality in order to force absolutely everything that is encountered within the confines of such a narrow and limited point of view. This results in a particular lack of concern for the way things are, an attitude which, in contrast to the particular outlook of scholars such as Tönnies,<sup>14</sup> eventually helped to foster a general mood which, with no regard for the ‘stubborn facts’, for the undeniable features of reality, felt itself justified, especially in relation to the field of social construction, in rigidly postulating certain value hierarchies without any further critical or objective assessment – value hierarchies that eventually led to the kind of distinctions which determined those who could be eradicated in the interests of the state and those who were spared such treatment.

I believe that it would be wrong, and indeed superficial, not to point out with some emphasis this dark and disturbing side of speculative social thought. Unreasonable though it would be to lay the blame for National Socialism upon any humane representative of German idealism, it is nonetheless the case that a certain contempt for the empirical domain, something to which we have all been exposed, helped to create the general conditions for paranoid systems of thought such as National Socialism. And I also believe that empirical social science – and I mean this in all seriousness – is one of the most powerful counter-forces which, if they are properly developed, could help to prevent human beings from being seized once again by such totalitarian madness, whatever form it might take.<sup>15</sup> Perhaps I might offer you an example specifically from our own concrete work in this regard. In the investigations into the ‘authoritarian personality’ which we undertook at Berkeley in California,<sup>16</sup> we discovered among other things in the case of antisemitism, which was the issue we studied in detail, that under specific social conditions the particular object of racial hatred and delusion can readily be replaced by other objects. In other words, we found individuals who, for more or less contingent social reasons, were prevented from becoming specifically antisemitic, although the social character or the entire socio-psychological structure of these individuals was constituted in such a way that such an attitude was absolutely to be expected, but they were perfectly capable of replacing potential antisemitism with 

*[p. 25]* ^pg32

a comparable attitude to other peoples.<sup>17</sup> When I tell you that they picked on the Armenian or the Greek minority in the United States instead, you will probably not be surprised. But when I tell you that the Swedes were also among those who were reproached for the kind of things that are commonly held against the Jews, you will appreciate the significant contribution that the findings of empirical sociology can make in countering deluded systems of thought.

I just want to point out that the great tradition of German sociology, in trying to isolate and identify a domain of the purely social, of the forms of socialization, to a very considerable degree also promoted the tendency to disregard the real force field, the actual forces that determine society.<sup>18</sup> And it then developed a tendency to put concepts such as that of purposive rationality<sup>19</sup> in place of the forces at work within society as a dynamic whole, which are precisely what a genuine analysis should reveal. It is no accident that the field of so-called ‘value-free’ sociology,<sup>20</sup> which was always concerned principally with its own pure concepts and issues of that kind, should produce a work such as Robert Michel’s *The Sociology of the Party System*.<sup>21</sup> The book actually contains a wealth of penetrating observations, but it suffers from the way that specific phenomena that express the ossified character of the modern parliamentary and party system – phenomena connected with quite specific social conditions during the last forty or fifty years – are ascribed to the form of the party system itself in a more or less *a priori* fashion. In terms of cultural and intellectual history we could say that, in the fate of Robert Michel’s ideas, we may read the drastic fate that befell this entire approach, for this particular construction was taken over in more or less identical terms by Spengler \[….\]<sup>22</sup> and to that extent we might say that the latter’s whole theory of how democracy leads to Caesarism<sup>23</sup> – a theory which had such an enormous influence on the ideology of emerging National Socialism – ultimately derives from the formal-sociological construction developed by Robert Michel. I do not wish to pursue this point any further here, but I believe that dangers of this sort reveal the need for a sociology that is much more strongly oriented to the facts in a very responsible way – in other words, one that recognizes the importance of precise observation and acknowledges what cannot be resolved into merely conceptual operations.

But, with that, I have only touched upon one side of what I wanted to say about the basic issues raised by our science, namely the side which explores how the tradition from which we come has itself become increasingly questionable. Yet there is another danger that just as clearly emerges from the opposite quarter today, although 

*[p. 26]* ^pg33

those of you who are not immediately familiar with the science of sociology are not perhaps as explicitly aware of this other danger in comparison to that presented by the sociology that derives principally from the tradition of the human and cultural sciences, as I have just tried to indicate. For I am now talking about the danger of an empiricism devoid of concepts, where the fear of metaphysics encourages an accumulation of facts that no longer possess any meaning. Whereas in Germany we still have to contend with the remnants of an idealist ideology of society, in the meantime the dissolution of idealism has already given rise to a new ideology, one that exercises a remarkable fascination where many members of the younger generation in particular are concerned. I am talking about a particular kind of positivism that models itself directly on the natural sciences, that does not address the problematic explored by thinkers such as Rickert and Dilthey<sup>24</sup> but simply forgets about them and thus threatens to sink into the barbarism of mere empirical registration, into the barbarism of the census. The reasons why this approach has proved so fascinating are by no means simply material in character, such as the prospect of obtaining the fairly lucrative positions that generally seem to be connected with such things. If that were so I would ultimately have to consider it a reproach as far as those seeking such employment are concerned. But what I actually think we see here is a remarkable need, very widespread among the younger generation, for insights that appear utterly secure and reliable, for insights where, as I would put it, we no longer need to take any risks, and this ideal of security is then frequently paid for by the meaninglessness of what we come to know. In addition, there is the further fascination exercised by every kind of new technique or apparatus in the broadest sense, including intellectual and cultural ones, something which today exerts an influence far beyond the admiration for the latest car models, and also seems to lend a distinctive charm or appeal to the very methods that, from the human point of view, should surely inspire the opposite reaction. Here I am thinking of two dangers in particular, and I believe I am justified in drawing these things specifically to the attention of those colleagues who deal with concrete sociological issues, since I cannot really be reproached with an idealist disdain or disregard for empirical tasks given the kind of research that our own institute has constantly been engaged in. But precisely for this reason, I think, we need to exercise considerable self-critical vigilance here.<sup>25</sup> One danger is that of undertaking investigations without conceptual criteria, where, without really knowing what it is that we are trying to know, we collect everything we can possibly learn about some specific sector or field of experience. 

*[p. 27]* ^pg34

Yet where there is no specific or carefully considered formulation of the problem underlying the investigation, nothing meaningful can emerge from it in principle. It is a fundamental principle of all empirical sociology that one cannot acquire any significant results from an investigation without reference to concepts we have already implicitly introduced. Now of course this does not exclude the possibility that, once we have developed certain concepts, we may well be able to acquire specific insights on the basis of the material itself, through correlations that suddenly come to light for example, insights that could not have been anticipated beforehand. I am not denying that, for otherwise there would be no need to carry out empirical investigations in the first place. But that empirical findings are essentially condemned to sterility if they are not already acquired and interpreted in the context of a carefully considered theoretical framework is something for which I believe there are some drastic examples today, insofar as empirical studies where that has not been the case seem on all sides – and not only in Germany – to have run into insuperable problems.

The second and perhaps far more serious danger that faces empirical sociology springs from the way it is directly involved in administrative and managerial questions, in questions connected with the provision of social services, and things of that sort, which means that the practical or pragmatic element in sociology is absolutized at the expense of any genuine connection with the totality and with truth. This immediate practical turn within a particular social sector encourages us to prioritize the inessential in place of the essential and to conceive of human beings as objects to be administered rather than as subjects. The need to grasp such a sector exhaustively in administrative terms, the so-called precision that is a desideratum here, is generally achieved at the expense of what it is genuinely important to know.

But I would also like to take this opportunity to point out – since the movement of thought I have been talking about generally tends to adopt American methods – that in America itself there has long been considerable scepticism about these procedures, and that it would really be rather dangerous if in Germany we just attempted to imitate American methods that have long been effectively superseded by the best American empirical sociologists. I need only to remind you that Professor Robert Lynd, the author of the famous studies *Middletown* and *Middletown in Transition* – works which exerted an enormous influence throughout the world – used a later work entitled *Knowledge for What?*<sup>26</sup> to express in the most emphatic way possible the need for a meaningful sociological approach to the issues 

*[p. 28]* ^pg35

rather than just blindly piling up the facts. But there is also something else that I would like to say here. For I certainly do not wish you to imagine that I am merely repeating the all too cheap and comforting demand for a so-called synthesis of speculative thought, of sociological theory, with empirical sociological research. Anyone who has conducted serious work as a philosophical theorist of society and as an empirical sociologist is well aware that the distance between the kinds of knowledge involved in these two fields is so great that the hope of one day confirming every theory in empirical terms is one that must be postponed indefinitely. We should mistrust all those scientific approaches that basically argue as follows: ‘We have not yet reached that point, we are not yet advanced enough, though this may come to pass sometime in the distant future.’ This kind of contemplative attitude, which reserves the solution of our most pressing cultural and intellectual tasks to the process of time *in abstracto* is quite impermissible for the philosopher or scholar. It is intellectually more honest to admit the rupture I have mentioned – or at least the tension, to put it less drastically – between empirical social research and the theory of society in a full and frank way, and to attempt to account for the deeper reasons behind it, than it is to trivialize the whole issue through such talk of synthesis. Now of course I cannot explore the fundamental reason for this in these few rather rhapsodic remarks I am offering here today, but I can at least suggest that the reason lies in society itself. For in our society, which is profoundly self-contradictory, the phenomena that it presents on the outside, namely that which is measurable and quantifiable, cannot simply be taken as an expression of the essence of this society precisely because at the same time these phenomena also conceal this essence in various ways. In this society the essence in question cannot be identified in a direct or immediate fashion but can only be disclosed through a rigorous exercise of thought which goes beyond the mere facts while keeping constantly in close touch with the facts. There is no truth, and no truth in social science either, which is not also always interpretation, and the notion that the highly reflective or theoretically oriented researcher is simply reading their own particular interpretation into the material is a form of argumentation that generally ends up by sabotaging truth itself.

Here I just want to talk about the problems that confront empirical social research in particular, and say a little to you about two specific ones, before outlining at least some of the demands which I believe empirical social science has to satisfy. The first problem is that regarding the social totality. On the one hand it is clear that all of the phenomena that we encounter today in our thoroughly 

*[p. 29]* ^pg36

socialized world are mediated through society as a whole. In other words, there is nothing which is purely and simply what it presents itself as being, for everything suggests that all of the immediate and objectified relationships between human beings are precisely an expression of society as an interconnected whole. Yet this kind of mediation cannot be grasped in a direct or immediate way. Here, to illustrate what I mean, I can perhaps give you a rather drastic example from an investigation that I have been quite closely involved with.<sup>27</sup> Imagine if you will an empirical study that was undertaken in order to investigate the relationship between individuals and institutions within a specifically designated sector of society; if you now restrict your attention to this particular sector, it will look as though the relationship of individuals to the institutions is essentially conditioned by the character of the institutions in the sector in which they find themselves. But whether for example people make much use or otherwise of some court or legal institution in a city to deal with their affairs does not in truth depend – or perhaps I should say, does not simply depend – on how this particular court functions, on what the personnel involved are like, or on the specific procedures it follows; it depends more on the generally prevailing cultural climate, on how the generally prevailing ideology has already affected people’s outlook in relation to the question: ‘Should I have recourse to law to resolve the issues that concern me, or would it be better not to?’ Thus an investigation that focuses its attention simply on particular sectors has already cut itself off from the possibility of fully including these essential elements within its purview, while the social totality itself, the mediating factor here, can only really be identified on the basis of a developed theoretical perspective, for it certainly cannot be determined within the perspective of the precisely defined and empirically surveyable sections of society that the investigation has in view.

The second problem that I also wanted to outline for you very briefly here is that of reification. Now a certain objectification is inevitably involved in the very methods of empirical sociology. In what we call empirical sociology you have to regard everything as measurable and quantifiable – and an empirical sociology that avoids quantification is a contradiction in terms – so in a sense you have to reify whatever it is you are trying to capture with empirically testable methods. You must make it into a ‘fact’, something that to some extent has been torn from the broader context that gives it meaning and from the overall dynamic context in which it unfolds. On the other hand, however, this reification, the fact of objectification and ossification, is itself one of the essential concerns of sociology. And here you come to a problem that may perhaps distantly remind the 

*[p. 30]* ^pg37

natural scientists among you of certain issues that have also arisen today in the field of quantum physics.<sup>28</sup> For you find that certain conceptual difficulties emerge here in the relationship between the instrument of investigation and the object to be investigated, insofar as the instrument itself is conditioned to a considerable extent by those phenomena in the object that can be explained only by reference to the instrument. Perhaps I can also clarify this for you with an example.

At the time when I directed the musical section of the Princeton Radio Research Project<sup>29</sup> I defended the theory that people who show an interest in serious music and are acquainted with it solely through the medium of radio have a relationship to such music which involves an atomistic and superficial sort of prestige – in other words, a reified relationship to it.<sup>30</sup> And we were faced with the task of somehow testing or substantiating this thesis. My assistant at the time<sup>31</sup> set about addressing this task with considerable energy, and indeed I would never have come up with the ingenious idea that occurred to him in this connection. For he compiled a list of composers and submitted them for evaluation by a range of experts in terms of general quality; he then undertook to determine how these serious composers, of various levels of significance, were regarded by listeners who knew them only through the radio and by listeners who also enjoyed some further musical background or experience. In fact the results that emerged from this investigation essentially served to confirm my original hypothesis. Yet I was hardly overjoyed by this triumphant discovery precisely because such a division of composers into this kind or that, such a reifying or objectifying approach to composers – even by appeal to the rather august individuals that experts tend to be – seemed itself to express the very prestige-oriented thinking that the investigation was supposed to expose and subject to critical examination in the first place. However, the difficulty that comes up here is not one peculiar to this particular study but is intrinsically bound up with the basic problems of empirical sociology in its relation to genuine knowledge regarding the broader significant context of enquiry. And I mention this example simply to show you that the thesis I expressed earlier, namely that concerning the fraught relationship between empirical sociology and the theory of society itself, involves a very difficult problem that has to be taken extremely seriously. Which is why we must be wary of indulging in edifying phrases about any imminent synthesis of both these disciplines.

Allow me to conclude by trying to outline a few of the demands that I believe we must address to sociology. The first of these, it seems to me, is the need to integrate all of the disciplines that are 

*[p. 31]* ^pg38

concerned with society. Now some of you will probably be horrified when you hear talk of ‘integration’, for you have surely heard this word repeated ad nauseam already. Here I would just say this: it is quite true that the demand for the integration of the various sciences has been raised again and again, although it has never really been taken completely seriously. It has generally not gone any further than a few colleagues getting together and holding some shared seminars, or sometimes just encouraging some general lecture courses for an audience drawn from every faculty – something which the students, often quite unjustifiably, then avoid like the plague. The problem clearly cannot be resolved in this rather ideological fashion; it can only be resolved if representatives of the various particular disciplines come together in a truly serious way in relation to concrete and extensive investigative tasks. If we consider the concept of society in the truly comprehensive sense that I have allowed myself to deploy here today, then it actually involves all of these possible disciplines already. These include sociology in the narrow sense, philosophy, history, psychology, ethnology, and the sciences currently pursued and developed in America under the name of ‘cultural anthropology’, as well of course as numerous other particular disciplines connected with the field of social science. And the task would be to cooperate and focus on specific areas, such as the investigation of the structure or typical structures of consciousness that prevail in Germany today, thereby enabling all of the disciplines to make their contribution to such an area of investigation in a very carefully planned manner that also serves to clarify the various interrelated aspects involved. Thus my view is that we actually do no service to sociology if we try and isolate or identify some sphere of pure or absolute sociology by separating it from other sciences or disciplines, which would probably yield nothing but a highly tenuous framework. The task, rather, is to bring a whole range of disciplines together, without anxiety about trespassing upon one another’s territory – to bring them together precisely from the perspective of an analysis of the life processes that underlie contemporary society.

In this context I should just like to remark very briefly on how all this also relates quite specifically to psychology. For a long time it was believed there really is such a distinct thing as ‘mass psychology’ or ‘social psychology’. In this connection I would simply remind you of the famous book by Gustave le Bon which is probably known to most of you at least by name.<sup>32</sup> But in relation to this very book we should note that Sigmund Freud, whose contribution to our science of sociology has to be taken extremely seriously, undertook to show in a very detailed and compelling way that the qualities 

*[p. 32]* ^pg39

which Le Bon ascribed to the masses qua masses definitely have their roots within the instinctual dynamics of particular individuals, and that the supposed ‘mass instincts’ of which McDougall spoke, for example, really involve those mechanisms of identification that can certainly be studied in terms of the individual.<sup>33</sup> Inasmuch as sociology is concerned with human and, indeed, specifically with irrational forms of behaviour, these need to be explored on the model of individual psychology, something which contributes decisively to our concrete understanding of social phenomena, such as totalitarian mass delusion, although – I would immediately have to add in qualification – individual psychology itself can only be thematized in terms of objective social relations. In other words, a meaningful social psychology is only valid within the context of objective social conditions. To the extent that sociology can be distinguished in any essential way from psychology, the point is not that the human being qua social being behaves fundamentally differently from the individual as such (although I am not denying that there are differences in forms of behaviour where individual or group situations are concerned). The point is that sociology is not simply, or certainly not primarily, to be understood as the science of human beings and their relations with one another but is also concerned with their isolation, with their alienation, with the dehumanization of their relations to one another – in short, with everything about their social fate that cannot be grasped simply on the basis of their so-called inner life.

Thus the object of sociology is, to a very considerable extent, nothing other than the objective sustaining foundation of our society, in particular the forms of organization through which society produces and reproduces itself today. Here I have only to mention the problem of bureaucracy that is at the centre of discussion in so many countries today, but also problems concerning the trade unions, and the relationship between trade union organizations and those of big business, the objective problems connected with strikes and all these related questions. These objective aspects and dimensions are just as important for our science of sociology as – and indeed I would say perhaps more important than – the sort of observation of particular forms of behaviour on the part of individuals or groups with which sociology is frequently confused or conflated today. Sociology, in the sense I have tried to adumbrate for you here, is principally concerned not only with human beings and their behaviour but just as much with the objective forces on which their behaviour depends. And here we are not talking only about the so-called influence of specific social institutions upon human beings – institutions such as the military, the political parties, the churches, and so on – but also and above all 

*[p. 33]* ^pg40

about the objective mechanisms which affect the social life of human beings behind their backs as it were, although they are mechanisms which can be changed by human beings precisely because, as we said at the beginning, they are in the last instance also produced by human beings. A simple example here is the superstitious belief that we can form a sufficient or complete picture of the major political tendencies of the time by confining oneself to polls, to the results of opinion research, and things of this sort. I believe that if in 1932, in the autumn of 1932, one had actually conducted such research in Germany the result would have flatly contradicted what in fact came to pass only a few months later. However important it is to know the direction in which the political sentiment of the people is moving, one could only offer a reliable prognosis if one knows the specific interests and political perspectives of the key groups which occupy the economically most important positions and to a large extent are in a position to affect or direct the behaviour of human beings themselves.

I would also like to add that the investigation of such interactions shows that we cannot just study forms of human behaviour as given facts but have to uphold the demand for ‘understanding’ that was emphatically raised by German sociology in its heyday. But what is at issue in this ‘understanding’ is no longer the comprehension of a so-called subjective meaning, as Simmel still put it,<sup>34</sup> namely as a specific mental act. I am talking about the comprehension of objective regularities which are expressed, if I may put it this way, through subjective forms of human behaviour. In terms of scientific practice this means that we must formulate ‘interpretive’ theories, theories that involve the ‘understanding’ of human action, but also translate them into investigative categories that allow us to decide on the truth or falsity of that understanding itself. Finally, I just want to say that what I have critically suggested regarding the contradiction between reified methodology and the problem of reification itself as it pertains to contemporary society is not quite as stark or absolute as I had initially presented it to you. Sociology must also strive to develop specifically new empirical methods through which this reifying tendency can be overcome – above all, methods through which the dynamic character of things can be brought out in the face of the rigidified phenomena. Then genuine understanding can take the place of merely registered and accumulated facts without forfeiting the rigour of disciplined investigation. It is generally the case in sociology that we pay for qualitative richness by accompanying statistical defects or by the insufficiently representative character of the data. Yet we can say, through active experience and engagement with 

*[p. 34]* ^pg41

questions of social research, that this contradiction does not prove to be as rigid as it initially appears to the neophyte. For with enough labour and perseverance the methods of empirical social research themselves can be developed in a genuinely flexible way that helps to overcome their own rigidity.

Perhaps by way of conclusion I can just say something about the ongoing investigations of our own institute, which is currently based here. We have undertaken a series of social experiments in which ten to fifteen individuals are brought together as groups and then exposed to a standardized ‘basic stimulus’; the groups in question are encouraged to engage in free discussion regarding issues directly related to this basic stimulus, in the course of which the person who is leading the experiment introduces a series of equally standardized arguments and counter-arguments.<sup>35</sup> Now the wealth of material that emerged here with regard to a whole series of extremely urgent issues in the context of current German ideology is astounding, and I believe that this material can in a sense be described as truly unique as far as understanding the contemporary state of consciousness in Germany is concerned. You might of course naturally retort by saying: ‘But all this material is quite \[unrepresentative\].<sup>36</sup> You have nothing to serve as a basis for comparison. How is it possible to draw any meaningful conclusions from this?’ Now to this I would reply as follows: first, it has conclusively been shown that, if we are ever in a position to replicate this study, which was initially conducted as a pilot study with strict statistically organized cross-sections, there is no reason to assume that the results would be any weaker or less comparable. But it has also turned out – by reference to a highly detailed ‘scoring manual’ or handbook to facilitate interpretation – that this material, in spite of its remarkably copious character, still exhibits a determinate structure that can be philosophically characterized as ‘objective spirit’.<sup>37</sup> This structure is so determinate that these 120 or 130 entirely independent protocols, most of which run to about eighty pages of typescript, can be reduced to an entire series of categories without detriment to the wealth of the material or the wealth of socio-psychological insights that it harbours. Precisely here, in other words, where the task is to connect the empirical labour of sociology with the meaningful theoretical contribution of the German tradition, we can see ‘Here is no call to be afraid’, as the saying goes. And I would like to conclude my observations here today on this rather optimistic note and thereby simply repeat what Herr Graf Solms said at the beginning, namely that it befits this kind of occasion if we speak more about problems than about solutions. But I believe that this is not a weakness of our science, for it only 

*[p. 35]* ^pg42

serves to express that this science is still essentially concerned with real and living questions, that we are not yet wholly confined to that state of reified consciousness the overcoming of which I would like to regard as the authentic task of social understanding.
