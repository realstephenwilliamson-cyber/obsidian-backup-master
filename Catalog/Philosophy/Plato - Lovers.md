## About

A very short dialogue in the traditional corpus of Plato, on what philosophy is. Socrates, narrating, enters a grammar school and questions two rivals for a boy's affection: an athlete who scorns philosophy and a cultivated young man who defines it as broad learning.

- **Title:** the Greek *Erastai* names the older partners in pederastic relationships, so "Lovers" can mislead. Ancient marginalia suggest *Anterastai* ("rival lovers"), the title Diogenes Laertius gives, with the subtitle *On Philosophy*; Latin *Amatores* or *Rivales*.
- **People:** Socrates; the athlete; the young man devoted to *mousike* (music, poetry and philosophy); and two quarreling boys. Socrates' audience is not named, and no dramatic date is given.
- **Argument:** Socrates rejects philosophy as many-sided learning, and as knowing every art well enough to rank second to its experts. He proposes instead the judge's knowledge of telling good people from bad, which he identifies with justice, self-control, self-knowledge and the arts of statesman, king and household head. The athlete agrees; the cultivated rival falls silent.
- **Authenticity and date:** generally dated to the second half of the fourth century BC, by an Academic writer if not Plato (Wikipedia). Stallbaum found the style excellent but the content lacking Plato's philosophical depth; Gerard Ledger's stylometry matched it more closely to Xenophon than to Plato; Julia Annas (1985) defended it as possibly early Plato, linked with *Alcibiades I* and *Charmides* on self-knowledge. The IEP lists it among works doubted in antiquity and would place it, if genuine, with the early or early transitional dialogues.
- **Place among the dialogues:** Diogenes Laertius records it in Thrasyllus's fourth tetralogy as *The Rivals, or On Philosophy*, classed "ethical", with the two *Alcibiades* dialogues and *Hipparchus*. At just over seven Stephanus pages (132a–139a) it is among the shortest; only *Clitophon* is shorter.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia notes that, if post-Platonic, it may challenge Aristotle's view that the authority of king, statesman and master are distinct, and that Aristotle may refer to it.
