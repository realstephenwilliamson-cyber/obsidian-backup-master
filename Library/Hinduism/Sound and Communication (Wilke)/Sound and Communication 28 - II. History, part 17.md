The cultural archive of signs, and particularly the metaphysical monism of the Brahman theology and of the non-dual Śaiva Tantra, allowed one to perceive the enjoyment of music as Brahman bliss. On the other hand, what the Platonists right up to Boethius do not list under the effects of music is a totally immediate religious effect of music extending beyond the earthly world and allowing one to taste the other-worldly in the earthly, as the Indians claim. Censorinus had indicated the existence of this idea by pointing out the religious use of music, and speaking of Pythagoras’ practice of “flooding his soul with its own divinity” every morning and evening. Augustine had introduced a Christian interpretation that, however, intellectualistically (and Neopla-tonically) supported the path of theoretical contemplation and explicitly rejected immediacy. What remains in Boethius is ultimately only theory. 

One can summarize typologically the obvious difference between Indian and European music discourses quite neatly using our basic framework — participation on the one hand, emancipation on the other. Reduced to this short (and somewhat abridged) typology, the Europeans developed the emancipatory orientation (distant attitude, analytical division, logical reasoning, etc.) to an extreme, while the Indians continued to develop participatory orientation to an extreme (fusion with the object, holistic thought patterns, unity of body and soul, experience-related metaphysical monism, etc.) — whereby (and this is central) emancipatory features, mathematics and theoretical speculations were by no means absent from the Indian musicologists’ considerations. 

What the European music of the spheres and the Indian Nāda-Brahman share, despite all their differences, are the proposition of a cosmic and metaphysical dimension to music and the embedding of life within a sacred cosmos. Yet even their starting points are fundamentally different. The basic paradigm for Augustine and Boethius was ultimately the instrument and not the human voice as in India, and out

The ancient concept of music of the spheres indeed had a sacralizing function, but unlike the Indian Nāda-Brahman it had little to do with audible earthly music. Its prevailing feature was access via numbers and proportion, which became so historically effective. Above all in the reception of the Neoplatonists, sensoriness and physicality are removed completely from the divine. Only earthly music is accessible to the senses, whereas the harmony of the universe can only be perceived by the ratio. The very different and diametrically opposed Indian field of discourse corresponds very well to Campbell’ s “Eastern frame of mind” and the binary oppositions he construed. 

The question thrown up in the last chapter, of why the European Joachim Berendt needed the Indian concept of “Nāda-Brahman,” is therefore easily answered. The Nāda-Brahman was so to speak an in-

What particularly fascinated Berendt and, he let slip, also his readers, was the “temple of the ear,” the ability of the ears to grasp with improbable accuracy numerical qualities and values of sequences of notes (e.g. the proportion 1:2 as an octave, 2:3 as a fifth, 3:4 as a fourth, etc.), and at the same time to hear them in a fully sensory way as C, G and F, that is, as tone values. This was precisely the object of fascination behind the ancient notion of the harmonies of the spheres, but presented there as removed from human hearing. Although Berendt did not find in Indian musicians a focus on measuring intervals in that manner, for him the Nāda-Brahman was still the key that could bring together both aspects ~ mathematics and sensation — at the level of human hearing in the here and now. By contrast, where hearing music was concerned, in his antique prototypes of music of the spheres and in modern natural science he found only the wing number. The natural holistic unification of both elements in hearing music “called” for the Indian solution model that in addition actually embedded the religiously loaded metaphor of the “temple of the ear” in a religious context. Berendt characteristically related his representation of the music-and-number complex to a modern Western alternative scientist, Hans Kayser.™ Kayser had immediately prefigured the finding that sound experience always encompasses and unifies two elements — the sensory (the tone) and the mental conceptual (the numerical value). It is telling that this predecessor with an alternative scientific style — to put it in anachronistic language, in contrast to Plato — had also read Indian philosophy and integrated it into his thinking. This is precisely what gave him an affinity for the Nāda-Brahman. Kayser did not only speak of a “fusion” of the two elements, but also ultimately gave the sensory part higher priority, and saw it as the more basic, when he stated: 

“Among all our human senses we only have one organ that is capable of this fusion: the ears. In this way sensation controls deliberation — or to put it differently: Our soul is thus capable of deciding on the correctness or incorrectness of an intellectual quantity. Conversely, the phenomenon of tone value also gives us the opportunity to develop proportions and numerical values in the realm of the psyche.””!

From the binary patterns outlined above it is relatively easy to deduce why the music of the spheres was insufficient for the holistic New Age program. In terms of typology there are not only two ways of listening to music, but also two different world-views and semantic fields that are juxtaposed. One can sketch this out somewhat bluntly as follows: on the one side is the world of separation, calculation, analytical division and comprehensive dominion of measuring reason, which Berendt criticized so strongly (this is where the idealistic Platonic and the empiristic Aristotelian tradition meet). On the other side is the undivided world of non-dual holism, which he was seeking. Division was already taking root in the ancient world-view of music of the spheres, because it was the world-maker, Plato’s world soul itself, that divided up the universe in accordance with intervals and consonances. Boethius also divided, by analytically dfferentiating the three types of music —that of the universe, that of men and that of instruments. The machine-like precision of spheric music and the main model, the instrument, were correlative. Boethius’ focus on instrumental music was a metonym for a certain understanding of the world. As the “machine of the heavens” is divided up by the world-maker in accordance with laws and joined together in reasoned order, so it is also with the rhythms of the year and the soul that guides the body to what is right, and the harmonic link between unequal parts of the soul and feelings that correspond to the various musical keys. Everything is put together in musical numerical ratios so that it is united in one single body or one single machine. Therefore science must strive to order that which is given to us by nature, and aspire to bring about the harmony if inequalities in a similar manner to the great world-maker, i.e. aspire to subordinate the

However, one can also express this positively. Berendt did not equate the antique “world music” and Nāda-Brahman for nothing. Both were universal mythical metaphors of totality. One can speak of two completely different holisms or meta-myths, that sketched a picture of the world-whole that was a sacred cosmos. Basically Boethius already had the same ideal as Berendt: the goal of a harmonic scholar should be to bring sense and reason into concord.’ On the “universal mythical” level this meant that all of Boethius’ three types of music are in perfect harmony, so that the musical sonic order and the whole of earthly life are “so to speak a reflection of the heavenly order and division. ”?® The unequal parts come together in beautiful universal concord to form a whole. Exactly analogous to the harmonies of the spheres are the four elements, the different seasons of the year, and the unequal parts of the soul, which are ordered and linked harmonically, and there is a direct homology between different keys and feelings such as anger, meekness and moderation, etc. The divine order in the spheres therefore corresponded to the earthly orders. This permitted micro-macro cosmic relations and made the heavens and the Earth comprehensible in a mutual exchange, and created cross-linkages among the individual parts. The basic model here is the harmony of unequal parts. The order of the cosmos itself was perceived as “beauty.” The harmonious world order, reflected in music, was a quasi scientific world explanation that simultaneously contained a system of ethics and in Augustine and Boethius provided the rationale for a spiritual interpretation of the whole of life. 

In Augustine the explanation of the cosmic whole and the embedding of life in a mythical metaphysical framework of meaning comes out especially clearly. The harmonic order of the cosmic whole is God’ s creation and is expressed on every level of being, beginning with the music of the spheres. Every part of creation is similar to the numerically ordered cosmic whole and man participates in the divine through his reason. This notion of ordo containing not only the beauty of inequalities, but also a clear hierarchy, was already shaken by nominalism (14th cent.), and from the Renaissance and modern age onwards it was no longer desirable. The universal myth of harmony of the universe was indeed warmed up again and again in cultural niches, to holistically bind the isolated individual anew into a cosmic whole, but it no longer left 

a mark on the culture. The timeless “God-given order” crumbled completely from the 18th century onwards and was replaced by the “orders” created by men. Order moved from the (collectively determined) transcendency into the subject. The new great meta-myths were democracy, individuality, a scientific world-view, technical progress and secularism. The separation of Church and State, and the successive dissolution of ecclesiastical directives and traditional religious ties, which many intellectuals felt were liberating, were however not necessarily the same thing as a disappearance of the religious religious needs. From the second half of the 19th century onwards the complaints multiplied of a “crisis of the time,” “spiritual impoverishment,” and “glaciation of the soul.” The search for a reactivation of the metaphysical substance, a reform of the whole of life, and resacralization becomes tangible in many discourses at the beginning of the 20th century, and at the end of the century it diffused a long way into popular culture. The “vacuum” that Campbell spoke of really is empirically tangible. Going back to the old ways was now unthinkable for many. A new departure was called for — and discovering India (among other things) was useful for this. 

Now, of course Sanskrit Hinduism was also a hierarchy of estates, metaphysically assured by the dharmic order. No other topic is as likely to be systematically left out of the reception of India in the modern spirituality discourse (from Vivekananda to Berendt) 

as the strict dharma rules. Yet the newly invented, subject-related, spiritual India was not a wholly new invention. Instead it already had a long history, because since the time of the Upaniṣṣṣads soteriology had been separated from the collective and anchored in the subject. For the discourse on music and spirituality it is sufficient to be reminded of Śārṁgadeva, who only mentions the collective of the order of castes and estates in passing in the first verse. His major argument is not one of ordo and the Nāda-Brahman is completely free of it. The key words “Nāda-Brahman” and “Yoga” fused cosmic unity and subjective soteriology. They promoted very personal access in the experience of holism within the improvisation and listening to music (which modern subjectivity could follow on from seamlessly). When Śārṁgadeva integrated music into the soteriology discourse and not into the dharma discourse of the order of the estates, he was giving music a totally different framework than that

The Nāda-Brahman was a mythical sensory substance with highly metaphysical content. The monistic explanation of the world corresponded with modal music, with listening to music understood to be Brahman-bliss and Yoga and with the unproblematic lysis (instead of disciplined morals and virtuous behavior). 

It was a model of music in which the typically Indian “third space habitus” of a fusion of sensuality and abstraction brought forth an especially interesting variation. Here one should not forget that in India, too, there was no lack of abstractions that abstracted from sensory substantiality (almost) completely, but neither was there any lack of repeated attempts to concretize such abstraction. One may remember that the starting point for the Nāda-Brahman was not least Bhartrhari’s global “language principle.” In Bhartrhari the relationship between real language and the “Word-Brahman” was still very similar to the musica mundana of late European Antiquity. The metaphysical foundation could be perceived intellectually and was convincing, because it explained phenomena, but it could not be experienced. Just as one cannot play or sing the musica mundana in Antiquity, neither can one speak Bhartrhari’s Śabda-Brahman. But precisely this was “corrected” with the Nāda-Brahman. \[1 music the participation in the universe was now immediately accessible to the senses and therefore could also be experienced somatically and affectively. At the same time, “spirituality” was preserved via the Brahman discourse and the theory of aesthetics. Moreover, the intellectual mathematical realm was also very much present in Śārṁgadeva, the “inventor” of the Nāda-Brahman. He investigated sound very similarly to the way in which the grammarians investigated the Śabda-Brahman. So Berendt was not totally on the wrong path when he projected mathematics and measurement into the Nāda-Brahman. The Nāda-Brahman itself was indeed only coded with expressive sound and not with mathematical speculation (whereby the stages of vibration put forward by scholars such as Bhāskararāya certainly went in this sort of direction), but in Indian music there was no shortage of mathematical calculations. However, not least the different metaphysical framework explains that in India the reception of the definition of sound via mathematics did not become powerful in the same way as in Europe and that the focuses were completely different. 

Depending on one’s perspective, one can maximize or minimize the differences between music of the spheres and the Nāda-Brahman. So although there is no immediate access to the higher cosmic reality in Augustine or Boethius, in their works theoretical speculation fulfills a function analogous to that of “hearing” in India. The theory was so important to the Platonic Neoplatonic thinkers because it represented a direct key to the “metaphysical substance.” It united that which was separated by the strict differentiation between audible earthly and inaudible cosmic music, because it provided the tertium comparationis that was common to both: reason and perfect order that had their place in the heavens. Participation in music was therefore only desired in the sense of theoretical contemplation and clear insight into order and proportion, for in the case of the music of the spheres no other type of participation was possible at all. Earthly music reflecting these proportions was an important indication of the perfection of the supernatural world. The thinkers of late Antiquity regarded contemplation of music as participation in the divine logos, in the highest deity, and in the perfect order and harmony of the universe. The numbers, that were founded on themselves, were truth of themselves, irrespective of teachings, and unchanging. Therefore they must exist beyond men, that is (in the Christian form of Neoplatonism) in God. In India sounds were similarly self-subsistent and eternal. 

A marked difference remains the superiority of reason and order, and the devaluation of the senses and of self-sufficing pleasure in Europe. The sensory accessibility of the cosmic whole and the non-dual perspective in India can be ultimately condensed into the main difference. In the Śaiva Tantric form of metaphysical monism that had a strong influence on the scholars of music, this meant that god and sound coincided: god is sound and has the form of sound. In both Europe and India, however, the spiritual was sought in music and music was seen as a means of harmonizing “the anxious, uncertain rhythms of human life with the more perfect rhythms of divine power and ultimate reality.” In both regions music had to do with interpretations of the cosmic whole and was placed in a comprehensive, universal mythical cosmological framework that sacralized the whole of life. 

So to summarize, one can speak of two quite contrary types of religious or metaphysical musical experience. On the one hand there is an intellectual path, the path of numbers and intellectual contemplation of music, and on the other there is the path of the Nāda, the concrete, expressive sound and affective sensory non-dual lysis. These two types can certainly be dovetailed, but they can also exist in hostile opposition and be played off against each other.

What European music of the spheres shares with the Indian Nāda-Brahman is the proposition of a cosmic and metaphysical dimension of music and the embedding of life in a sacred cosmos. The ideas about music, and the harmony of the spheres, that were attributed to Pythagoras are known to have been “demythologized” as early as in classical Antiquity by Aristotle’s pupil Aristoxenos, but still both the Pythagoreans and the Aristoxenans shared a preference for theory and logogenic access to music. Messiaen’s profound interest in religion was by no means shared by all avantgarde musicians but he was not the only modern classical musician to want to find his way into a “pure” music. For Messiaen, too, Indian music was not the only inspiration. But what he found in it was a different approach to the tonal material and the rhythms.

###### 6.3.11.1 "Glass Bead Game" in Europe and India

In our discussion of the Nāda-Brahman to date, the participatory aspect has clearly been the dominant one, while the mathematical aspect of music was indeed mentioned again and again but obviously remained in the background. This is understandable, since in India the practice of defining sound via mathematics did not develop the same force that it had in Europe. However, the impression should not arise that the numerical nature of music was not recognized by the Indians. This is true of the knowledge of the proportionality of musical intervals. But the Indians pursued quite different aspects of mathematical research in music. The “third-space habitus” (that is repeatedly emphasized in this book) linking the senses and abstraction, participation and emancipation, applies in particular in music also. Śārṁgadeva’s way was not only that of musical Yoga, lysis and the sensory Nāda-Brahman experience, but also an intellectual way not dissimilar to the ancient concept of the music of the spheres and music theory, a way via numbers, symbolic combinations and intellectual music contemplation. The situation is similar for Carnatic music, of which we so far have discussed in greater detail only one aspect, the devotional lysis. However, it has already been indicated that the mathematical calculations in India had a different focus than those in Europe: the exploring of the tonal and rhythmic spectrum by means of combinations. 

Śārṁgadeva’s treatise on music contains a comprehensive chapter on permutational combinations, according to which the diverse range of possible tone sequences (tāna) is determined, e.g. how many three-tone motifs can exist if the total number of tones available is seven. What Śārṁgadevas combinations also allowed was the indexing of the tone sequences. Some of the tone sequences calculated were even given a name, usually the name of a Vedic sacrifice from the Brāhmanṇas. 

In the case of Śārṁgadeva’s serial permutations one can speak of abstract glass bead games that had the power to fascinate in the music of Europe, too: from the complexities of counterpoint and fugue to the serial composition of the early 20th century. The relationships and structures emerge out of the material itself. Therefore in a way they are not “made by men.” The ordered system of tone sequences is already rooted in the numerical substance of music itself. The radicality of this approach of totally dissolving tonality was determined historically and was also historically overcome. Here the contact between Western musicians and Indian music was a factor. How can this development be explained? 

Modality was abandoned in Europe during the psychologization, representative charging and semanticization of music, starting in the Renaissance with the madrigalists and then becoming fully apparent in the music of early opera (such as that of Monteverdi). This process had culminated in the 19th century. The compositions of this period were often “tone poems” (in the truest sense of the word). Literature and music were fused closer and closer together, a development that reached its zenith in program music, musical stories without words. Here music became increasingly semantic. For example, Liszt set poems and epics to music, including classics such as the Divine Comedy, Faust, etc. In these works there was an increasingly large repertoire of musical sememes: tone painting, musical symbols and allegories, and quotations of style. Composers amused themselves pretty well with this new type of music for about a century. Towards the end of the 19th century Richard Strauss even attempted to set philosophy, that is, literature with an abstract content, to music — in his AZso sprach Zarathustra after Friedrich Nietzsche — although surely no one would guess that he was hearing the philosophy of Nietzsche if this was not indicated in the title. 

At the beginning of the 20th century this semantically overloaded musical language suddenly seemed “worn out.” The pure tone language (the qualities of the sounds as sounds) and the higher metaphysical reality shining through in the music (or that which one interpreted as such) had been lost. What remained was a repertoire for topoi and themes that were no longer of any interest to any one. The relations to literature and art history were stuck like tar to the musical forms, which made the music appear more and more out-dated. The expansion of tonal harmonics did offer new possibilities, but by the beginning of the 20th century they were exhausted. Remaining in the conventional tone language would have meant iterating the same antiquated topoi again and again. At this juncture three counter-models emerged. Igor Stravinsky’s radical denial of a natural emotional content, or any kind of content, in favor of reduction to forms, became the foundation of musical classicism. For the libretto of his Opera “Oedipus Rex,” for instance, he deliberately chose Latin as (a sacred) language in order to make the audience concentrate exclusively on music. A second approach was open to musical traditions with very different approaches to making

music than the psychologized classical music of the 19th century, such as non-European music, especially the Indonesian gamelan (Debussy), jazz (Krenek) that was just becoming known, and later also forms from folk music (Bartok). The third approach was that of Arnold Schönberg and his school: the dissolution of tonality and the radical reorganization of the musical material. However, it turned out to be difficult to compose in free atonality, i.e. to write music not related to any tonic and without any rules. It was very difficult to leave behind all the traditional music and not to copy or use old patterns. The solution intended to be the method of composing using twelve tones. The rules built into this method prevented anything from being (unconsciously) retained from the old period. The composer selects a certain permutation of these twelve tones, which are then allowed to appear only in this particular order and no other. This prevents a tonal center from even beginning to form, since the equality of the twelve tones is guaranteed by the sequence. 

The expressive character of the twelve-tone music is the exact opposite of modal music. In complete contrast to the Indian Rāga, in which the selection of certain tones evokes an ever present, closed atmosphere, in the twelve-tone music such musical atmospheres hardly ever come into existence because every tone has to appear again after a certain time. In classical Indian music, depending on the Rāga selected the mood is either gentle or tense, emotionally stirring or peaceful, depending on whether tones with these particular characteristics have been selected. In twelve-tone music on the other hand, all the tones are present simultaneously and appear again and again in close proximity. This results in a “non-human” or superhuman sound effect. 

Twelve-tone music is often thought of as pure formalism. Yet it was actually more than this. It apparently also intended to allow the tonal values, their self-communicating features and the “metaphysical substance” that had largely been lost in the 19th century under the burden of semiotic overloading to come to the fore again. Even if the hierarchies of the harmonic relations between the tones were negated, the substance of the tones was in fact shifted back into the foreground. This meant that the metaphysical musical glass bead game became audible once more — even if this came at the price of abstraction from all harmonic dimensions of sounds. It was perfectly natural that composers started to look for less extreme solutions than that of the twelve-tone style to make the “music of the spheres” audible again. Paul Hindemith should be mentioned, with his expanded model of tonality and his composition entitled “The Harmony of the World” on the life and work of Johannes Kepler (premiered in 1957). In this composition the planetary system as an instrument of the music of the spheres becomes a metaphor for Hindemith’s own arrangement of the twelve-tone scale that did not negate consonance and dissonance. 

But even more powerful effects were achieved by those composers who chose the path of expanding the indigenous musical spectrum by borrowing from other musical cultures. The French musical impressionism of Claude Debussy had already perfected the adoption of foreign forms of music: jazz, flamenco, and not least gamelan music from Indonesia were seamlessly worked into his new tone language. Religion, wholly absent in Debussy, was all the more present in the post and neo-impressionistic “Jeune France” that was founded in 1936, one of whose declared aims was to give music back its mystic religious character. The best known of them was the composer Olivier Messiaen, a deeply devout Catholic. In 1935 Messiaen began composing largely in the modal style and developed for this his own system of musical modes, called “modes à transposition limitée.” This was a scale-generating system functioning in a very similar manner to that of Veñkata Makhin, whereby the resulting seven scales are exceptionally symmetrical. The great inner symmetry of these scales does something similar to what the series of tones do in twelve tone music: the tonality is undermined because the inner harmonic relations between the tones of the scale are

not very complex. This brings about a kind of “light atonality.” On the other hand, these modes (unlike twelve-tone music) allow composition with harmonic colors because they function just like the Indian Rāgas. Another important element of Messiaen’s tone language is even more clearly derived from India: complex rhythms, called Deśī-Tālas, that he adopted from Śārṁgadeva’s well known treatise on music, and which in his music are usually also called “Hindu rhythms.” Messiaen treats these rhythms more or less as independent personalities and deals with them in his compositions like the baroque counterpoint composers did with their themes. They are augmented, inverted and turned into mirror images. Messiaen apparently found in Indian modal music a stimulus for his own “spiritual” tone language and metaphysical symbolism.” 

What distinguished Messiaen from other avantgarde musicians of his time was his deep religiosity, his search for something mystical, cosmic and divine. However, the de-humanization of a pure music of the glass bead game, which no longer involves a human audience, was not an option for him, unlike some twelve-tone composers. On the contrary, he strove for a music that was to be both strict and meditative, and at the same time full of color, sweeping and natural. For this reason he was also greatly fascinated by birdsong. He himself was a self-taught ornithologist and when travelling across France he recorded large numbers of birdsongs that he transcribed into musical notation and then used in his compositions. The music of the birds, which was neither thought up by human beings nor made for them, is still beautiful and thus points to God as the source of this beauty. This substantiality differentiated Messiaen very strongly from the concept of Augustine, who was unable to gain much from the song of the nightingale as simply nature “without-reason.” Yet despite this, Augustine thought something quite similar when he spoke of music (its numerical rhythm) as something indicating the perfection and absolute beauty of God. Messiaen found new traces in the rhythms of Indian classical music. Like the birds’ voices, Indian music supplied him with inexhaustible musical religious symbols and possibilities for expression that combined strictness and glorious color. Probably Messiaen’s most brilliant combination of all the mentioned aspects of a new spiritual tone language was his Turañgalīlā symphony, a huge hymn to joy composed shortly after the end of World War II, which does not elaborate any melodic themes but instead a single rhythm from Śārṁgadeva’s music book.

###### 6.3.11.2 Transnationalization of the Nāda-Brahman

6.3.11.2 Transnationalization of the Nāda-Brahman 

The fascination with sounds, whether they be linguistic or musical, has brought forth many and varied approaches and interpretations in India. That sounds make suitable abstract order patterns, was realized in the phonetic arrangement of the alphabet, for example. But the attraction of sounds lay not only in their ability to bring about recognizable numerical structures through compositions, rhythms, repetitions, etc., which Frits Staal also perceived in the numerical composition of the mantra sounds in the Vedic ritual. The Indian musicologists associated not only mathematics, but also emotional atmospheres with the musical structures. The emotional appeal of music was particularly important in the religious discourse. In the bhakti traditions, music became the central means for expressing the love of God. The more Yogic and Tantric thinkers, in their turn, were fascinated by a different aspect: the quality of sounds being present in the audible and non-audible range, and leaving behind vibrations both in the outer and inner space. They experimented with that, developed practices, and arrived at radical conclusions of self-interpretation and world-interpretation. Sound seemed to have something to do with the innermost nature of man and of the cosmos, and to point directly to a transcendental reality, even to lead into it, if one concentrated exclusively on the sounds. Sound was attributed the power of changing reality. Sound formulas were applied as means of self-transformation. In particular the Yogic interpretation of the Nāda has apparently more recently held an especially great fascination for Westerners, too. They found in it a way of dealing with sounds that they had not known before. India was attractive because it offered other possibilities for understanding oneself and the world. Precisely this difference set in motion new processes of providing meaning in one’s own, Western, culture. 

India was not usually received as it was by Messiaen. For those players who did not find spiritual satisfaction at home, India was a source of inspiration for relocating transcendency to the inner space, for seeing corporeality and sensuality in a new light and for re-drawing the boundaries between the sacred and the profane. Here one found a highly developed culture where the gap between the human and the divine had been dissolved, and the magical and mythical was not devalued despite the high degree of intellectuality that already characterized India in pre-Christian times. Messiaen found inspiration in the logogenic, “emancipatory” aspects of Indian musical tradition. Modern, alternative meaning-seekers look for a participatory approach to reality and a universal myth that provides a rational explanation for the participatory world-view and is accessible to experience. According to our thesis, they have

*[p. 992]* ^pg1016

interiorized (partly consciously and partly unconsciously) an Indian habitus whose original context is often no longer visible at all today. This is made very clear by the reception of Nāda and Nāda-Brahman. Today, the Nāda-Brahman has its own history in the West, without the Indian trigger having to be known. 

In the center of the following discussions is the noticeable fact of how strongly certain Indian thoughts on the nature of sound have spread transculturally and gain new contents through interaction with non-Hindu traditions. The talk of “energy/ies” and “vibrations” in modern spirituality is inflationary and here non-Indian traditions also played a role, such as Mesmerism (with which certain Indian notions were easily combined). Our aim is not to work through this very wide field of modern esotericism in all its facets. Instead, using selected Internet sources we wish to show how far ideas of the Nāda and Nāda-Brahman have diffused into contemporary religiosity. At the same time, the examples will illustrate how far modern reception of the Nāda-Brahman has become removed from Śārṁgadeva, and the broad spectrum of sound meditation and sound lysis that this now covers. Different authors from East and West focus on different aspects and show just how transcultural and transreligious the categories of Nāda and Nāda-Brahman have become, and how many different links in the discourse and sense-worlds (“Diskurszusammenhānge und Sinnwelten”) are associated with them. The Nāda-Brahman has “Yogaized” and “transnationalized” itself in the wake of a more general process of assimilation of “Indian mysticism,” whose main export goods have been the Vedānta (the Vedānta of Śaṁkara and forms of Neo-Vedānta), and since the 1930s they have also included Yoga.
