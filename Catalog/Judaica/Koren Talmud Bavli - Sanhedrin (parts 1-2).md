## About

A tractate of the Mishnah and Talmud, in the order Nezikin ("Damages"), on courts and on civil and criminal procedure. Its chapter known as Helek concerns the World to Come and who has no share in it.

- **Size:** 11 chapters and 71 mishnahs; 113 folios (dafim) in the Babylonian Talmud and 57 in the Jerusalem Talmud; 14 chapters in the Tosefta. It originally formed one tractate with Makkot, which also treats criminal law.
- **Contents:** the grades of courts and the cases each hears; the king and the high priest in court; witnesses, judges and procedure in civil suits; how criminal cases differ, including the examination of witnesses and the judges' vote; the four modes of execution (stoning, burning, beheading, strangulation) and the crimes for each; the rebellious son; and the commandments one must die rather than violate.
- **Chapter order:** the Mishnah and the Gemara order the last two chapters differently (Wikipedia lists the Gemara's order, and says the Mishnah has the last two reversed).
- **Dates:** Britannica says Judah ha-Nasi gave the Mishnah its final form early in the 3rd century. Sefaria dates the Babylonian Talmud tractate to c. 450–550 CE. The Wikipedia article gives neither date.
- **The Koren edition:** the Koren Talmud Bavli (Noé Edition), with Rabbi Adin Even-Israel Steinsaltz's commentary, prints the tractate in two volumes: vol. 29 ends at the end of chapter 6, folio 49a; vol. 30 begins at chapter 7, folio 49b (Koren's own pages say chapters 5 and 6, but Sefaria's chapter table puts folios 42b-49a in chapter 6 and 49b onward in chapter 7). It keeps the Vilna pagination, with vocalized and punctuated text and Rashi, and a bilingual section with English translation, maps and notes.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia presents its Gemara as anticipating common-law principles such as the presumption of innocence. Koren's description stresses its protections for the accused, rules of evidence and standards for examining witnesses.
