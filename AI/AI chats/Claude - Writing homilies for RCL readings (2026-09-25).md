> I think I have another idea that may divide my attention with this project for a while. Yes, "homily" fits. The word comes from the Greek *homilia* (conversation, discourse), and in liturgical churches it means preaching that expounds the day's appointed readings. "Sermon" is the broader term and can be topical. One distinction is worth settling early, though. A finished homily is written for the ear and usually follows one thread through the readings. "Building blocks" sounds more like homiletical notes or lectionary commentary: exegesis, links between the readings, several possible angles you'd never preach all at once. Either works, but deciding which you're writing will shape every entry.

A small correction on the structure: the first reading is usually from the Old Testament, but during the Easter season it comes from Acts. The psalm is meant as the congregation's response to the first reading, so it's rarely preached on directly, though it's fair game in written reflections. The bigger decision comes in the season after Pentecost, when the RCL offers two Old Testament tracks. In the complementary track, each reading is chosen to pair with the gospel. The semicontinuous track reads through books like Genesis, Samuel, and the prophets in order. The complementary track has been criticized for treating the Old Testament mainly as a foreshadowing of the gospel, while the semicontinuous track lets those books make their own argument. Congregations pick one, so you'll probably want to as well.

On timing, Year B (Mark) begins with Advent on November 29. John has no year of its own, but it supplies much of Lent and Easter every year, and Year B spends five Sundays in late summer on [[John 06#^6-1|John 6]]. That's where the new project could feed the John project instead of competing with it.

> [!quote] Me
> The churches I've attended have always used the complementary track. I will do the same and be careful to point out what each passage is saying in its own voice before using it in others

That discipline has a well-known precedent: the rabbinic principle that a verse never departs from its plain meaning (*peshat*), however much further reading (*derash*) is built on it. Reading each passage in its own voice first is peshat before derash.

It will matter all year, not only after Pentecost. The two-track split exists only in that season. In Advent, Christmas, Lent, and Easter, every Old Testament reading is already chosen for its link to the gospel. Watch the pericope boundaries too. Complementary readings are often cut to fit the gospel, so the passage's own voice sometimes lives in the verses just before or after the excerpt. Your first real test comes in Year B: on Lent 5, [[Jeremiah 31#^31-31|Jeremiah 31:31–34]], the new covenant "with the house of Israel and the house of Judah," is paired with Hebrews and [[John 12#^12-1|John 12]]. That text has done more supersessionist work than almost any other, and [[Hebrews 08#^8-1|Hebrews 8]] quotes it at length for exactly that purpose.

**Fifth Sunday in Lent, Year B** (March 14, 2027)

**[[Jeremiah 31#^31-31|Jeremiah 31:31–34]].** This passage comes from the "Book of Consolation" (chapters 30–33), which is addressed to exiles. God promises a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, unlike the one made at the Exodus, which they broke. This time the Torah will be written on their hearts. No one will need to teach a neighbor to "know the LORD," because all will know him, from least to greatest. God will forgive their iniquity and remember their sin no more. In its own voice, the promise concerns the same partners and the same Torah, now internalized rather than replaced. The next verses (35–37), which the excerpt stops short of, say Israel will remain a people before God as long as the sun, moon, and stars keep their order.

**[[Psalms 051#^51-3|Psalm 51:1–12]]**, or alternately **[[Psalms 119#^119-9|Psalm 119:9–16]].** [[Psalms 051#^51-1|Psalm 51]] is the great penitential psalm, which tradition ties to David after Nathan's rebuke. It is a plea for mercy and cleansing, with "create in me a clean heart" at its center. [[Psalms 119#^119-9|Psalm 119:9–16]] asks how a young person keeps their way pure and answers with treasuring God's word in the heart. It is a Torah-delight psalm, and it may be the more pointed echo of Jeremiah for your purposes.

**[[Hebrews 05#^5-5|Hebrews 5:5–10]].** Christ did not appoint himself high priest. God named him Son ([[Psalms 002#^2-7|Psalm 2:7]]) and priest forever after the order of Melchizedek ([[Psalms 110#^110-4|Psalm 110:4]]). In the days of his flesh he prayed with loud cries and tears and was heard because of his reverence. Though a Son, he learned obedience through suffering, and he became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him. [[Hebrews 08#^8-1|Hebrews 8]] isn't read here, so the supersessionist pressure comes from the pairing rather than from a quotation.

**[[John 12#^12-20|John 12:20–33]].** Some Greeks at Passover ask Philip, "we wish to see Jesus." The request passes through Andrew, and Jesus answers not with a meeting but with a declaration that the hour has come, after repeated "not yet" moments earlier in the Gospel. A grain of wheat must die to bear fruit, and whoever loves their life loses it. "Now my soul is troubled" is John's counterpart to Gethsemane. Jesus refuses to ask to be spared and prays, "Father, glorify your name." A voice from heaven answers, and the crowd hears thunder or an angel. The passage ends with the promise that, lifted up from the earth, he will draw all to himself. Most manuscripts read "all people" (*pantas*), while p66 and the original hand of Sinaiticus read "all things" (*panta*).

The pairing logic is fairly visible. Jeremiah and the psalms share the interior heart and forgiveness, and Hebrews and John share the troubled soul facing death.

The reading you prefer is where Hebrews itself starts. [[Hebrews 07#^7-2|Hebrews 7:2]] glosses the name as "king of righteousness" and Salem as "king of peace." Philo (*Legum Allegoriae* 3.79–82) and Josephus (*Jewish War* 6.438) gloss it the same way, so that etymology was common Second Temple exegesis, not an alternative to Hebrews. Your problem with Hebrews starts at 7:4–10. There Abraham pays a tithe to Melchizedek, and Levi, "still in Abraham's loins," is made to pay it too. That's the step that subordinates the Levitical priesthood.

The rabbinic counter-reading goes straight at that step. In b. Nedarim 32b, Rabbi Zechariah, in Rabbi Ishmael's name, identifies Melchizedek with Shem. God meant the priesthood to come through him, but Melchizedek blessed Abraham before blessing God ([[Torah 03 - Lech Lecha#^14-19|Genesis 14:19–20]]), so the priesthood passed to Abraham instead. [[Psalms 110#^110-4|Psalm 110:4]]'s *al divrati Malki-tzedek* is then read as "because of Melchizedek's words," addressed to Abraham. It won't convince most people partly because it postdates Hebrews and may be a response to Christian use. In Hebrews' own era Melchizedek could be exalted: at Qumran, 11QMelchizedek makes him a heavenly figure who executes judgment. So it works as a counter-reading, not a refutation.

Two things give your split more room than Hebrews does. First, the *-i* in *Malki* may mean "my" or may be an old connecting vowel. The parallel name Adoni-tzedek, another king of Jerusalem ([[Joshua 10#^10-1|Joshua 10:1]]), suggests a possible theophoric reading: "my king is Tzedek." On that reading, righteousness is the one reigning, not an attribute of the king. Second, Jeremiah joins king and righteousness on its own terms. In 23:5–6 and again in 33:15–16, just past this week's excerpt, a righteous branch of David is named *YHWH tzidkenu*, "the LORD is our righteousness." That name is widely read as a play on Zedekiah (*Tzidkiyahu*), the last king of Judah. That lets you build the king-of-righteousness reading from Jeremiah's own voice first, then bring it to Hebrews.

> [!quote] Me
> As a boy Melchizedek was (and still is) one of my Biblical heroes. I don't treat him as bad as the rabbis but I was disappointed to discover their take on him. I see the episode in the Torah as marking what my hindu side calls the yugic shift.

The rabbinic reading may be closer to yours than it first looks. Once Melchizedek is Shem, he is a man from before the flood. By the Masoretic chronology Shem even outlives Abraham, so here someone from the previous world meets the founder of the next and hands on the priesthood. That's the structure of an age turning. What the rabbis add is fault: the priesthood passes because he blessed in the wrong order. A yugic reading keeps the transfer and drops the fault, since in that scheme ages turn with time, not because anyone failed.

Where your reading needs care is ranking. The yugas run downhill: dharma stands on four legs in the Krita age and on one in the Kali. Put Melchizedek in the earlier age and he outranks Abraham, which is Hebrews' own argument in 7:7, that the lesser is blessed by the greater. What keeps your reading from becoming Hebrews is yuga-dharma. Manu 1.85–86 gives each age its own dharma: austerity in the Krita, knowledge in the Treta, sacrifice in the Dvapara, giving in the Kali. The later form isn't a degraded copy of the earlier one; it's what that age can carry. The tantras make the same claim for themselves, presenting themselves as the revelation fitted to the Kali Yuga. Hebrews moves the other way. It reaches back to the older order in order to set aside the newer one, and within a cycle the yugic scheme doesn't allow that kind of reversal.

For the homily, this lets you honor Melchizedek on [[Hebrews 05#^5-1|Hebrews 5]]'s terms, with Christ as priest in his line, without taking [[Hebrews 07#^7-1|Hebrews 7]]'s further step of subordinating Levi.

> [!quote] Me
> The Greeks role in this part is confusing. They ask to see Jesus and we get the formula about Philip's origin again, mention Andrew, but then Jesus seems to be informed by the arrival of theses Greeks that the time has come and he gives a speech.

The Greeks work as a signal, not as characters, and the verse just before the reading shows why. In 12:19 the Pharisees complain that "the world has gone after him." In the next verse the world shows up: Gentiles, probably God-fearers, since they've come to worship at the feast. John has been building to this. In 7:35 the crowd mocks the idea that Jesus might go teach the Greeks of the dispersion. In 10:16 Jesus has other sheep not of this fold. In 11:52 Caiaphas unwittingly prophesies that Jesus will die to gather the scattered children of God into one. So when Greeks come asking for him, the gathering has begun, and in John it can only happen through the death. That's why the answer to "we wish to see Jesus" is the grain of wheat, ending with "I will draw all to myself." They can't come to him on the terms they asked; they'll come through the lifting up.

Philip and Andrew aren't incidental either. Both have Greek names, and the repeated hometown formula places Philip at Bethsaida, on the edge of Gentile territory, which makes him a plausible first contact. In John the two are also a pair. Both appear among the first called in chapter 1, and they are the two consulted before the feeding in chapter 6. Andrew's role is always to bring someone: Peter in 1:41, the boy with the loaves in 6:8–9, and here the request. Philip's role is seeing. He tells Nathanael "come and see" in 1:46, and in 14:8–9 he asks to be shown the Father and is told that whoever has seen Jesus has seen the Father. The Greeks' request goes to the disciple whose whole arc concerns what it means to see Jesus.

One caution: the narrator never says the Greeks triggered the hour. The text says only that Jesus "answered them," meaning Philip and Andrew, and the Greeks then drop out of the story, much as Nicodemus fades out of chapter 3. The causal link is a strong inference from the juxtaposition and those earlier verses, not something John states.

Read that way, the Greeks are what Jesus was waiting for: the first fruits of the harvest arriving before the grain has fallen.

Your instinct is right. Of the three pilgrimage feasts, Passover is the one the Torah closes to them. [[Torah 15 - Bo#^12-43|Exodus 12:43–49]] bars any foreigner from eating the lamb unless he is circumcised, and at that point he is a proselyte, not a God-fearer. So these Greeks couldn't join the meal. John says they came up to worship *at* the feast, not to keep it. They could pray in the Court of the Gentiles, and Gentile offerings were accepted. Solomon's prayer asks God to hear the foreigner who comes for the sake of his name ([[I Kings 08#^8-41|1 Kings 8:41–43]]), and [[Isaiah 56#^56-7|Isaiah 56:7]] calls the temple a house of prayer for all peoples. Josephus, describing a Passover census, lists foreigners present for worship among those not permitted to eat the sacrifice (*War* 6.427), so their presence was normal. The limit was literally carved in stone: inscriptions on the barrier around the inner courts warned that any foreigner who passed it would be to blame for his own death.

The prophetic feast of the nations is a different one. [[Zechariah 14#^14-16|Zechariah 14:16]] has the nations going up every year to keep Sukkot, and John's Sukkot chapter is where the crowd mocks the idea of Jesus teaching the Greeks (7:35). So the Greeks don't arrive at the feast prophesied for them. They arrive at the one feast whose meal they can't eat, in the week John makes Jesus the Passover lamb: he dies on the day of Preparation, when the lambs were slaughtered, and 19:36 applies to him the rule that none of the lamb's bones be broken. John doesn't draw this line himself, but it's there to draw: they stand outside the meal until the lamb who will draw all is lifted up.

Your friend has the text right. In the Mishneh Torah (Hilkhot Melakhim 11:1), Rambam says that anyone who doesn't believe in the Messiah, or doesn't await his coming, denies not only the prophets but the Torah and Moses. It's also the twelfth of his thirteen principles. The famous line "I will wait for him every day" is from the later liturgical *Ani Ma'amin*, not Rambam's own wording. He also doesn't count it as a separate commandment among the 613, though he clearly treats it as binding.

I don't think it hurts your sufficiency claim, and it may help it. The same chapter says the Torah's statutes are eternal, that nothing is added to them or taken away, and that the Messiah won't change them (11:3). Rambam's Messiah supplies nothing missing from the revelation; he restores its full observance. So the waiting is itself commanded within the covenant, and a Jew who keeps Torah and waits is doing exactly what the revelation asks. The point would only cut against you if "awaiting" meant "incomplete until Jesus is recognized," and Rambam closes that door himself. A claimant who is killed, or fails to rebuild the Temple and gather the exiles, is not the Messiah (11:4).

- **The uncensored end of 11:4.** In this passage, cut from the printed editions by censors, Rambam names Jesus and Muhammad and says their movements served to prepare the way for the King Messiah by spreading Torah, commandments, and messianic hope to distant peoples. That's anti-supersessionism running the other way: Christianity given a providential role within Israel's hope, not the reverse.
- **The Pontifical Biblical Commission's 2001 document** *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*. > [!quote] Me
> What I think I was getting at is that even if Jesus were the Jewish messiah (which he clearly isn't) the posture of awaiting him would still be commanded if you hold that the law still applies and if you hold that the law doesn't still apply it doesn't matter.

The dilemma holds, and the first horn is stronger than it might look, because Rambam's own standard of proof carries it. In Hilkhot Melakhim 11:4 he distinguishes two stages:

- **A presumed messiah** (*bechezkat mashiach*): a Davidic king who studies Torah, leads Israel in keeping it, and fights the wars of God.
- **A certain messiah** (*mashiach vadai*): one who has succeeded, rebuilt the Temple, and gathered the exiles.

Certainty comes only when the work is finished. So even if Jesus were the Messiah, no Jew could be obligated under this law to recognize him before the work is complete, which in Christian terms means the second coming. Until then the law asks one thing: wait. The Jew who waits and the Christian who prays *maranatha* ([[1 Corinthians 16#^16-22|1 Corinthians 16:22]]) are pointed at the same event.

The second horn is valid but narrower. If the law no longer applies, Rambam's commandment has no force. But then a supersessionist wouldn't be arguing from Rambam anyway; they'd argue from Hebrews or Galatians. So the dilemma disposes of the Rambam objection either way, but it doesn't establish sufficiency on its own. That depends on the first horn being true, which is your actual position.

For the first horn, Paul is on your side. In [[Romans 11#^11-26|Romans 11:26]] he quotes Isaiah, that the deliverer *will come* from Zion, and places Israel's salvation at that future coming, after the full number of the Gentiles has come in. He doesn't make it depend on recognizing Jesus now.
