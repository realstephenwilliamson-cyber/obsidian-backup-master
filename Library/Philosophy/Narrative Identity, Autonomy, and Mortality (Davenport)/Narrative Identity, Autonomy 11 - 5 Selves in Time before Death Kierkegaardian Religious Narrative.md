## 5 Selves in Time before Death: Kierkegaardian Religious Narrative Unity

1\. 

### 1 The Problem of Mortality and the Ontology of Narrative Selves in Time

1.1. The Dilemma for Narrative Realists

In the prior chapters we have seen how a narrative realist account of practical identity can answer several objections to narrative accounts in general. We have also seen how rich forms of narrative unity can help explain personal autonomy, and how a distinctively Kierkegaardian narrative theory of selves including both of these developments can support ethical conditions for autonomy that contrast with all aesthetic versions of practical identity. That Kierkegaard’s work provides support for such a model has proven defensible against further objections to narrative interpretations of Kierkegaard’s stage-distinctions and portrayals of identity. I turn now to the two remaining central problems not yet addressed from our initial list (Chapter 2 §1): the objection (6) that living selves cannot achieve narrative unity in death, and the more recent objection (8) that the sort of narrative realist account outlined in the prior chapters will have to equate a person’s identity with a four-dimensional whole only a temporal part of which is present at any one time—thus ending up in one of the horns of Schechtman’s dilemma that a focus on practical identity was meant to escape. I will argue that these apparent problems are closely connected; they are practical and ontological sides of the same issue.

John Lippitt raised the problem of mortality in evaluating Kierkegaardian narrativism, but as Kathy Behrendt and other contributors to the wider philosophical literature have argued,<sup>1</sup> the phenomenon of death is a problem for narrative models in general. Patrick Stokes has shown that Kierkegaard recognizes such problems when he describes death as indefinable, inexplicable, and apparently contingent in relation to prior events.<sup>2</sup> Stokes agrees with George Pitcher that the meaning of our life-narrative can be altered profoundly by our death or by events following it.<sup>3</sup> But then, given Kierkegaard’s point about the radical uncertainty of the time and manner of our death, “it cannot be brought into any narrative that confers 

*[p. 151]* ^pg170

meaning on it while the subject lives.”<sup>4</sup> Thus, although Kierkegaard shares MacIntyre’s concern for the “unified coherence of the responsible self in time,” the life in which this coherence is found “can only be understood as a narrative in a necessarily incomplete sense . . .”<sup>5</sup> While this point applies to all human lives, it also raises a question about Kierkegaard’s religious stage of existence: is there a further level of narrative unity that depends on faith? In the next section, I will argue that Kierkegaard provides the resources to address the apparent incompleteability of our narrative identities before death.

While this issue about mortality is typically introduced as a *practical* problem concerning how our lives can have meaning for us “as a whole” when they are not yet finished, Stokes’s new objection concerns the ontology of selves according to realist versions of “narrative identity” (NI) theory. Stokes argues that such theories (including mine) share with all broadly neo-Lockean theories the “Continuity thesis” that “the continued identity of selves across time is constituted by some form(s) of continuity between subjective states,” although they develop the kinds of psychological connectedness (“PC”) involved in self-identities.<sup>6</sup> His point is that on NI realist accounts, practical identity is not entirely separable from the metaphysical identity that neo-Lockean theories try to explain, although NI realists enrich psychological connectedness conditions to reflect several features of agency and narrative consciousness.

I agree and would add that I regard practical identities as real phenomena, not merely something it is convenient or even necessary to believe in from the “practical point of view.” For the agentive point of view exists because a plurality of agents capable of normal and deep responsibility (autonomy) exist, which is to say that selves or practical identities exist as *personal* in one central sense of that polyvalent term, namely the existential sense defined by ADR. What then exists according to the Analogy thesis (A) is a *narravive*, which is a real structure of relations among meaningful elements that are significant to an agent who is constituted by this weave of significance (which has a narrative-like form in the sense defined by the Robust Conception given in Chapter 2§4.2) And as theses (B)—(G) indicate, such a weave of significance with unity-1 that is capable of modifying itself in the direction of higher levels of narrative unity requires several types of embodied cognitive and volitional powers, perhaps including libertarian freedom, which are thus implicitly included in the constitutive conditions of personal identity according to my narrative realist account. These powers really exist when persons exist; they are not mere fictions or posits imposed on us by contingent social practices. Instead, practices of holding ourselves and others responsible, both normally and deeply, arise along with the possibility of personhood. Thus the term “narrative realism” rightly emphasizes the ontological commitments involved in this view. It may even be helpful, as Anthony Rudd has argued, to add that the self with its bodily and psychic properties constituted by a narratival structure 

*[p. 152]* ^pg171

is a kind of “substance,” because “the notion of the Self is prior to that of its constituent states” which are “modalities or expressions of the Self’s way of being and are experienced as such by the Self.”<sup>7</sup> But it will be a substance only in a nonstandard sense—a hermeneutic substance that is what the temporal gestalt of its meaningful or expressive states make it to be.

The trouble with these formulations, as Stokes sees, is that they risk implying that a self-substance (in Rudd’s terms) or an entire narravive weave (in my terms) is a fixed whole, or four-dimensional structure, like a Leibnizian monad-substance that contains everything that the person will ever do, feel, or experience throughout her life. Yet it is central to any existential conception of created persons, including Kierkegaard’s, that we exist dynamically and develop selves in time. Stokes puts it this way: “insofar as narrativity is a trajectory-dependent property \[in Jones’s sense\], and insofar as .  .  . the person qua narrative entity covers an entire life, this entity cannot be wholly present at one time;” yet we often do use “I” and other types of self-reference in ways that apparently refer to ourselves as “wholly present at one time.”<sup>8</sup> In particular, Stokes ties this to the sense that the being I experience as me in present consciousness and in exercises of agency is my *self* in one important sense of that concept. Thus as noted earlier (see Chapter 1 §5) he refers to an “apparent bifurcation of the self as a narrative whole and the self as a present, conscious agent,” or a “split between the self-as-agent and the self as narratively unified, diachronically extended entity that we are normatively enjoined to become,” or (following Damasio and Zahavi) the “narrative” self versus a “minimal, core sense of self” as a perspective on a stream of consciousness.<sup>9</sup> Stokes adds that my analyses of selves in *Either/Or* have ignored key passages which suggest a “naked self” that transcends all its contingent features in the moment of choice—as if the narrative interpretation of *Either/Or* would, contrary to Kierkegaard’s view, leave free persons no way to distinguish themselves from the total sum of their “externalities and particulars.”<sup>10</sup>

Note how close this is to the “Narrative Essentialist thesis” that I distinguished from the Analogy thesis and rejected earlier (Chapter 2 §3.1). The irony, then, is that the *narravive* account of a self as practical identity might unwittingly commit me to a position that is inconsistent with an existential conception of leeway-libertarian freedom as really operative in time such that before choices are made, there is no determinate truth-values for future conditionals concerning these choices—a conception of free will that I have directly endorsed elsewhere, attributed to Kierkegaard, and held to be consistent with a narrative conception of personal identities.<sup>11</sup>

In this stronger form involving libertarian free will, it is clear that the bifurcation or 4D objection (8) is closely related to the mortality objection (6); they are two sides of the same knotty problem. It is useful to reconceive them as two horns of a dilemma: if the narrative self is equated with its whole life-story as it stands finished at death, then it is distinct from the self as free agent making conscious choices in time (objection 8); but if the 

*[p. 153]* ^pg172

narrative self is instead identified with the sense that a living subject making free choices has of its practical identity at any given time, then it is in principle incomplete, or in a crucial sense not “a whole” until death, when it is no longer a self in that living sense (objection 6). I will refer to this as the *narrative wholism versus freedom-in-time* dilemma. While in this book I have set aside the task of arguing for libertarian freedom in any incompatibilist form, I need at least to indicate how its operation can be consistent with the kind of narrative realism outlined in Chapter 2. The problem also has an ontological aspect concerning how narrative identity is related to time, and a practical aspect concerning whether we can subjectively experience our lives as having a “whole meaning” that embraces our entire history even while we are still living. The rest of this section focuses on the ontological aspect, and the next section will address the practical aspect.

#### 1.2 Existential Narrative Realism: Narravives as Freely Growing Blocks

On the ontological side, this dilemma involves a complex set of issues for narrative realists: it involves ambiguities in what we mean by a “whole life” and in how we conceive the flow of time (a notoriously hard question). Space permits me only a very partial and preliminary response here. I begin from Trenton Merricks’ widely cited argument that if persons “endure,” i.e. are wholly present at each time that they exist, rather than “perduring” as 4D objects made up of persons-slices as temporal parts, then personal identity cannot be analyzed solely in terms of psychological connectedness. The reason is that a relation R of “psychological continuity” between distinct mental states cannot possibly explain a relation of *numerical* identity between a person at one time and the very same person at another time, which is what personal identity must be on an endurantist ontology. And a relation R\* between the mental states of the same person wholly present at different times could not be a relation of numerical identity.<sup>12</sup> This argument can be interpreted to support Stokes’ because his emphasis on the agent consciously choosing in the present may be understood as an appeal to an endurantist intuition that this notion of self invoked in common self-references must be the whole person, in some relevant sense. For the force of his 4D argument is that, if the self you are right now as you read this is just a temporal stage of a four-dimensional entity, then your present self cannot be a person in the sense we ordinarily take it to be (an enduring whole). And in rejecting narrative essentialism, which is a four-dimensionalist version of narrative realism, I have agreed that existential narrativists must preserve a clear sense in which your present self is *more* than a mere temporal slice of “you” as a 4D-whole (transcendence). Notice, though, that this negative proposition does *not* necessarily entail the positive proposition that the present self must be the “whole you.” It is only transcendence that follows from your present 

*[p. 154]* ^pg173

identity involving trajectory-dependent properties, which I have affi rmed as essential to narrativity in the robust conception (RC) (Chapter 2 §4.2).

In my view, there is an unstated dichotomy underlying both Stokes’s and Merricks’s arguments: either time is really a spacelike dimension in which all times are equally real and the moving-now is an illusion (the tenseless view) or the present time is uniquely real (the presentist species of the tensed view). The former inspires 4D versions of psychological continuity theories and the latter seems to fit the endurantist intuition that all of me is present as I make a conscious choice right now.<sup>13</sup> But there is a third, less familiar alternative: the “growing block” species of the tensed view, according to which “the past is real, the future is not, and the present is . . . that hyperplane of space that is the leading edge of the growing four-dimensional block that is our universe.”<sup>14</sup> On the version of this view that I endorse, conscious activity and choice can only take place in the present; what is now in the past has a status distinct from what is now occurring, though both exist; and the past is not an undifferentiated mass but rather retains at each point time-relative facts that record its “growth” (e.g., at the time Augustus ordered that all the Roman empire be taxed, Julius Caesar’s crossing the Rubicon was then-past, which allows us to explain the truth of pluperfect facts that are true *now*). On this view, reality or the set of truth-makers for propositions “grows” as the “now” moves forward along the time-dimension. Moreover, while the future is not real, there are several kinds of modal facts now about what is possible in the future, and facts about the probabilities of some future events. While it is not my task to defend this view here, I believe that this is the conception of time that existential narrative realists need. A Kierkegaardian can still allow a *formal* distinction between the present operations of a person’s consciousness and agency and their full practical identity, while holding that this is not a *real* distinction between entities, because present thought and free choice are aspects of the self’s “leading edge.” (I follow Kierkegaard’s use of “self” here, though Stokes follows Schechtman and Strawson in using “self” for present conscious agency instead.)

On this account, at any present time, my practical identity is constituted uniquely by my narravive with unity-1 up to and including that time. It is from the perspective of this identity that I experience new events and make new choices, which refi gure the relations of significance that have grown up in my past: the weave-structure *grows* as time moves on via this constant *integration* at its leading edge of new nodes of meaning with their regestaltive effects. We can even say that, as an agent choosing with libertarian freedom, this present face of my identity partially transcends many aspects of my past in the sense of being only inclined rather than determined by them. Yet the choices I make will still be an *extension* of the same narravive that has endured until now. The present agent, we might say, is the dynamic living face of a narravive identity moving into the future. This analysis implies that Wollheim’s initial distinction between a person, his 

*[p. 155]* ^pg174

life, and the leading of that life is too simple, but it agrees with his contention that the psychological connectedness characteristic of the lives of persons derives from the activity of persons, or the “way in which persons lead their lives.”<sup>15</sup> Wollheim’s idea that the “posture of hanging between the past and the future is special to persons” because of the way our “underlying mental dispositions” affect our present experience and activity through our “occurrent transient mental states”<sup>16</sup> can also help explain the notion of a leading edge of a narravive that operates as the nexus at which new content is woven into it. What the narravive model adds to Wollheim’s account is that the kind of life-leading that is characteristic of persons is the kind (e.g. planning in light of past commitments and recognition of mortality) that builds connections of the sort that make up at least narravive unity-1.

Because a living person’s narravive as it *now stands* involves multiple trajectories from her past, there is no such thing as an instant temporal part of a narravive (and thus I will not refer to someone’s “present narravive”). We have to adjust our vocabulary a bit to avoid implying dichotomies that the growing-block model rejects. Your *full* practical identity, as I will refer to it, is always uniquely constituted by your narravive up until now. There is more content in this narravive now than there was ten years ago, but it is also the *same narravive*, in the sense that its present form has grown narratively from its form ten years ago. In that sense, it would also have been the same narravive then and now had you made some different choices that made a big impact in your life five years ago, though there is another sense in which the life-stories that branch from that point to your narravive as it now stands and your narravive as it would now stand had you then chosen otherwise are distinct in content. In this way, a person’s narravive identity is always more than the identity specified by a maximally concrete set of contents up until now; it is flexible enough to be the same identity with diff erent content in other possible worlds, within certain limits (as some alternatives involve a break in unity-1). Thus diachronic identity of a narravive weave is like the identity of other processes over time, but it is more modally flexible than some processes.

We can say that on this model, your full identity “endures” through time and is fully present at each of the times in which you exist as a person (in the existential sense). But we cannot mean by this that an unchanging substance is numerically identical with itself at each successive moment, as if it wholly existed *just* within the present instant, if this presentist interpretation is what Merricks means by “endurance.”<sup>17</sup> On the growing-block model, the same narravive is numerically identical with itself *over* time because it grows by way of narratival addition, or integration of new material in the flow of the present; it endures the way a process endures. This sounds perilously close to affi rming that the numerical identity of a self over time rests on a complex kind of psychological connectedness relation in the contents of a consciousness, which Merricks has sought to rule out. But I do not think an existential narrative realist is committed to this claim 

*[p. 156]* ^pg175

by the analysis I have sketched. For a narrative analysis of a person’s practical identity is not necessarily equivalent to an analysis of the identity of a person over time, because it is may not be the whole of such an analysis. Although the sort of narrative relations between elements of lived experiences defined by RC are *necessary* for personal practical identity on my account, and thus one is not (in the existential sense) a person until one is capable of planning agency, this does not mean that there is nothing more to the *identity over time* of this person’s practical identity than the trajectory-weave of significance-relations constituting her narravive. Whatever constitutes the numerical identity of her consciousness, her cognitive powers, and her volitional powers may also be involved in the latter.

Here we have come full circle to the division of labor emphasized in Chapter 1: a narrative account explains the kind of practical identity that characterizes moral personhood (agency capable of extending to deep responsibility); and although this explanation points to a diachronic process, its function is not to say that the identity of the same person over time *reduces* to the diachronic process of a narrative weave. A narrative account of practical identity need not by itself constitute a complete account of the identity of a person over time, even if we should aspire to link it to such an account. For the theoretical identity of the “same narravive” may depend on other continuities besides the narratival developments it necessarily involves. As a result, on Wollheim’s analysis, my existential narravive realism would not count as a purely “relational” theory of a person’s life (of which Lockean psychological connection theories are one species); nor is it a “constructionist” theory, since it does *not* claim that the life of a person can be reduced to certain relations among events that make no mention of any person or the powers of an individual person.<sup>18</sup> The goal of narravive realism is to explain practical identities and what it means for them to be autonomous, rather than to provide a reduction of the identity of a person over time.

A leeway-libertarian may wish to add a stronger claim that the numerical identity of a person’s individual power of free choice over time may partly explain (rather than be explained by) her narravive identity, because it must be the *same* individual power operating at each time for new projection of ends, decisions, intentions, and intentional acts to be integrated into the same narravive. In this way, I could endorse Stokes’s idea that something like a transcendent subject is involved in Kierkegaardian selfhood, as we arguably see in his account of “spirit” as an individual leeway-freedom of self-relation (SUD 28). That said, it is a virtue of existential narrative realism that it can open up the black box of “agent-causation” by explaining the “agent” who causes a free decision as a narravive identity rather than as a bare point of freedom. Moreover, within the religious stage (and specifically Christian faith), Kierkegaard holds that a “self” in the sense of a personal identity for which we are responsible remains even after physical death (or demise) and thus when leeway-liberty has ended and a despairing self-relation cannot be changed (SUD 7, 21, 27–28).

*[p. 157]* ^pg176

This is not a complete solution to the wholism versus freedom dilemma though, because the ontology of practical identities as growing weaves within the growing block of the existing universe implies that even while they endure through time and include the earlier developments in the same narravives, they are always also incomplete as long as the persons whose identities they are retain the agentive powers to extend them. Death, more radically than other interruptions in unity-1 or partial loss of agentive powers, ends their extendability. The practical identity of a dead person is no longer a narra*vive*, as that term always implies extendability into the future, and into alternative possible controllable ways on leeway-conceptions. Rather, the deceased’s identity is the temporally ‘finished’ whole of what was a narravive, which is always ‘more’ than the full narravive up until now of a living person who can still develop his or her life-story. The solution to the 4D objection outlined here thus confirms the other horn of the dilemma, which we can now restate this way: how can a person’s “whole life” really have a unified meaning for her at any time while she is still alive and free, given that at such a time her narravive is still extendable into the future in ways partially under her control, and thus not identical in content with the finished life-story that her narravive will become upon her death? Even if the person’s full narravive at present is numerically identical to this finished life-story that will be hers, that is not enough to resolve the practical aspect of the problem.

### 2 Narrative Unity-3, Death, and Kierkegaardian Eschatological Faith

#### 2.1 Three Versions of the Problem

The previous section indicated why several critics have thought that the way our lives continue in time and end in death makes it impossible for us to experience any narrative unity of our “whole” life. But we still need to pin down precisely how skeptics about narrative identity understand this practical problem. On some construals, it seems to be the impossibility of the actual end of my narravive being meaningful to me, because I have to be alive to experience meanings. In response to MacIntyre, for example, Lippitt says “If my death is necessarily not an event in my life, I cannot grasp it as an episode in the story of my life.”<sup>19</sup> Kierkegaard agrees: “in death’s decision, all is over and .  .  . the transformation cannot fall in line with other events as a new event” in the agent’s life (TDIO 99). Leaving aside religious faith in an afterlife, from an immanent perspective, my being-dead is not a *living event* in my narravive; once this event is actual, I am not there to experience it. But when put this way, the problem seems to have a good answer developed by Kierkegaard and subsequently by Heidegger: I certainly can grasp in anticipatory understanding the fact that I will die 

*[p. 158]* ^pg177

and thus experience the certainty of my temporal finitude and an end to my eff orts as part of the overall meaning of my life before my death has happened. I may not correctly predict the circumstances and time of my death; so it will probably leave some of my projects unfinished. Moreover the manner in which I face it in my last few moments or hours could also undermine the main themes and commitments of my life. But that is the risk that “anticipatory resoluteness” towards death is supposed to control: in infinite resignation, the meaning of my cares to me should not depend on their completeability by me, and I will try to die with courage, even sacrificing my life for my ground projects if necessary (though most people do not get such a chance). Earnestness chooses “work that does not depend on whether one is granted a lifetime to complete it well or only a brief time to have begun it well” (TDIO 96).

But this may be an uncharitable construal of the mortality objection. On another interpretation, it says that because we must assume that we cannot experience the state of *our being* dead, we cannot see what our finished life-story actually is (though we may fantasize about a kind eulogy). Until our death, it seems that things could always unfold in more than one way for us, so the future is, as Lippitt says, “always a threat to whatever ‘unity’ I may have achieved.”<sup>20</sup> On this view, even if my narravive is strengthened by anticipation of a death that is certain to come eventually and may come at any moment, I still experience my life as fragmentary and open to a final twist that reverses or negates much of what mattered to me. Thus Sartre argues that sudden death is often “absurd” while few persons’ endings are like the beautiful chord that culminates a long melody.<sup>21</sup> This indeterminacy, like freedom according to Sartre, is a source of angst. The risky openness stops only when I have permanently ceased to experience anything, i.e. when it is too late to experience satisfaction in overcoming this risk via the closure of death. In short, I lack the kind of control that would be necessary for my death to be integrated into an autonomous life-meaning.

To this version of the objection, there are at least two important responses. First, as often as existential writers have made this kind of claim, it is an exaggeration. People often have fairly well-justified beliefs that they will probably die soon (even within some specified time period). Although absolute certainty is impossible, as wild contingencies could always postpone or hasten their demise, they have some time to prepare and decide how to act—a few months, a day, minutes, or maybe just a few precious seconds in which to make final decisions. If such ‘final moments’ are often filled with tremendous significance for the agent living them out, as many literary and film depictions have suggested, this argues for the possibility of narrative unity. Even if we can do little but suffer with courage, or make a last response, it can bring closure. Consider in this light the great weight that people often place on “last words,” such as Jesus’ final words on the cross: “it is finished.” There seems to be a clear sense of completion, even peace, in this affi rmation. Contrast Kurtz’s haunting last utterance, “the 

*[p. 159]* ^pg178

horror, the horror.”<sup>22</sup> In this, we hear a sense of failure so absolute that it assumes eschatological proportions: it is a breath of damnation.

This is a subject deserving more phenomenological study. We have often heard that in the seconds before impending death, a person experiences something like a terrifying or awe-inspiring rerun of their life compressed into a few instants—like a finale.<sup>23</sup> Indeed, one famous study of near-death experiences (which inspired a large genre of books on this topic) reports that experiencing a kind of “review” of one’s whole life, or its main themes, is one of a few motifs that show up in many patients’ reports.<sup>24</sup>Another study cites the “holographic life-review” as a frequent element in NDE reports.<sup>25</sup>For a fictional portrayal of this, consider the rapid montage of images that flashes through the mind of Bruce Willis’s character at the climax of the film *Armageddon* just as he is dying. His death makes him whole, for in it, he reconciles all his purposes. Of course critics will rightly call this a melodramatic Hollywood ending. Yet the motif is too prevalent to dismiss altogether. Even if we can do nothing more than acknowledge loved ones gathered to wish us goodbye, or make a final confession, we can sometimes *make* an end.

As we know, real life is often messier. Perhaps the Holocaust survivor who became a professor at Virginia Tech was not thinking anything high or noble as he barred the door with his body against a shooter on April 16, 2007 to give his students time to escape out a window. But whatever panic he felt, he was evidently also full of determination, and it is impossible not to think that his last act expressed much of his practical identity: it surely speaks volumes about the man. Of course, many people are not able to experience ‘final moments’ in my sense. They may be killed by a sudden accident with no warning, or drift off to sleep and never wake up. Probably Abraham Lincoln experienced nothing at Ford’s Theater other than the players on stage and a loud noise followed by sudden loss of consciousness. And yet, in another sense, he was *not* entirely unprepared. He did not expect or imagine this specific death, but for years he had known and accepted that assassination was a real danger. On leaving Springfield in 1861, at the train station, he told his friends and neighbors that “I leave now, not knowing when, or whether ever, I may return.”<sup>26</sup> For him, more than for most of us, mortality was quite salient. So although he had no chance to finish the business of reconstruction, or to tie his cares together in a final dramatic act, he may have experienced his life as unified by the purposes for which he was willing to die. This experience, gained through much eff ort and reflection, could have been present to him at the prerefl ective level even as Booth raised his gun. Thus we do not need time to ‘put our affairs in order’ for our will to be ‘in order,’ ready for death. Nor do we need to make a “momentous ending” (TDIO 72), as Kierkegaard says—though we might well choose such an ending over living on bodily to the point where all memory of our cares is lost and our practical identity has faded out.

*[p. 160]* ^pg179

Thus if the objection is that the time or manner of my death might undo the meaning that my whole life has had for me up till then, the second response is that freedom only needs to make deep narrative unity possible, not to ensure it. The fact that a person can choose in ways that destroy the unity-3 of her life in her final moments is no argument against a narrative theory of identity either at the descriptive level or normative levels. It is up to us to choose so that either (i) our final moments faithfully express our identity rather than undoing us, or (ii) our sense of embracing meaning continues right up to our sudden end, if it happens that way. Thus Kierkegaard thinks that witnessing a “sudden death” can help us realize that “as soon as one is dead, it is too late to become earnest” (TDIO 74). According to (ii), the absurdity or loss of meaning that Sartre finds in sudden death is only on the surface, or from the external biographical point of view; inwardly, for the infinitely resigned person, it is false that “one minute more or less may perhaps change everything.”<sup>27</sup> And according to (i) we do not need to wait to see what we will do, as if we are doomed to be passive spectators of a final choice that affects the meaning of all our past choices.<sup>28</sup> We can become resolute now to prepare ourselves for those choices.

However, there is a third way to construe the objection: our narravive can never achieve *complete closure* because, as beings of finite powers and limited time but indefinitely extending interests, aspirations and loves, there is always more we would do or say if death could be postponed even for a day, an hour, a minute. In that sense, the loose ends of our story can never all be tied up in time; our narravive *must* be fragmentary at its end, leaving threads that we either experience as incomplete, or would so experience if we knew our death was imminent in a few minutes. To a biographer, these storylines will appear merely cut off in midstream. In this light, Sartre says, the product-value of our eff orts becomes indeterminate.<sup>29</sup> Even if we have been wholehearted and feel a blessed contentment on our deathbed, inevitably some things will still feel unfinished if our memory is still working at all. This follows directly from the definition of a narravive as still-growing storylike weave of meanings that remains extendable in multiple ways partly under the agent’s control through planning and intentional action.

#### 2.2 Kierkegaard’s Answer

Of the three construals, this last one is as irresistible as the fact that no one who enters the ethical can finish life without any regrets, with nothing he would change. But this does not mean that our death cannot be a meaningful part of our living narravive. It is precisely because they are two sides of the same ‘existential incompleteness’ that Kierkegaard pairs regret with guilt as “two guides” that “call” a person to change while she still can. Thus guilt can be sharp when we feel that death is near:

*[p. 161]* ^pg180

. . . whether you are guilty of much or have left much undone, the guilt makes this eleventh-hour call; the concern of inwardness, which regret sharpens, grasps this at the eleventh hour. In the temporal sense, old age is the eleventh hour, and the moment of death is the last moment of the eleventh hour . . . but repentance and regret belong to the eternal in a human being, and thus every time repentance comprehends the guilt it comprehends that it is the eleventh hour . . . O eleventh hour, how changed everything is when you are present; how still everything is, as if it were the midnight hour; how earnest everything is, as if it were the hour of death; how solitary, as if it were among the tombstones; how solemn, as if it were in eternity! (UDVS 14–15).

In this key passage, we see the core of Kierkegaard’s answer: the incompleteness recognized in the death-objection can *itself* be made the basis for earnest reflection on the whole direction of our life. Without contingent life-crises or looming threats (such as a terminal diagnosis), we can always see ourselves as running out of time, as in the “eleventh hour.” Notice Kierkegaard’s wording: for a brief span of time, it is *as if* the midnight hour has already struck, as if we are already dead and our story finished, absolutely unchangeable. Thus we can experience ‘being dead’ *metaphorically* as being out of time, much as we anticipate the heart-stopping “pencils down!” command at the end of a vital test. To feel this, we have to realize that we are taking a test and care about it—just as consciousness that our life will have an eternal meaning is the condition for asking earnestly the existential question about what we will, making it “the fundamental condition for willing one thing in truth” (UDVS 127).

Thus Stokes is right that such an edifying “copresence with death” in imagination requires an infinite interest in the moral quality of my life.<sup>30</sup> In imagining death as “your lot” or as an accomplished fact, “you are then doing what death is indeed unable to do—namely that you are and death also is” (TDIO 75). This phrasing that inverts Epicurus’s famous formula<sup>31</sup> means that, in the earnest thought of our death, we actually extend our narravive to *include* our death as a possibility imaginatively actualized: in the mirror of eternity, we see ourselves as though we have lost the freedom to change, yet we *continue* to experience meaning. In this thought, we briefly exist in a kind of living death; we *experience* what it would be like to be dead, or to be unable to alter our narravive, and what that would mean to us if we were still able to experience meanings in that state.<sup>32</sup> We think ourselves into the state in which “all is over” for us (TDIO 79). This paradox is the limit of the narrative unity that mortal persons can achieve by their own eff orts: we feel the *narravive* significance of “being out of time” not just for one important task, but for *all* our purposes, cares, and projects. Kierkegaard calls this the “decisiveness” of death: nothing more can be added, “the meaning” of our life “is at an end” (TDIO 78).<sup>33</sup>

*[p. 162]* ^pg181

The link between Kierkegaard’s discourses on “Purity of Heart” and “At the Graveside” is clear here: for “the certainty of death” is the *universal obstacle* to the complete realization of our intentions in time (TDIO, 95), even when our powers are otherwise suffi cient for our task (which is rare enough). A fully wholehearted or “pure” will must be able to persevere in light of this recognition. Clearly this conception of wholehearted willing depends on the premise that at death, our practical identity *is* eternally what it has become: because our freedom to change ends in death, our volitional character is *forever fixed*. There are weak and strong versions of this idea; while the former requires no specific faith in life after death, the latter is explicitly eschatological. For example, Kierkegaard says that among three “changes in life” that “allow a person to learn whether he has willed one thing,” the change of death is followed by the “change of eternity,” in which “only the good remains” as a unified identity, and we must forever will the unity or the multiplicity that we willed in life (UDVS 27). Taken either way, this implies that a narravive-that-was acquires a *complete and final* meaning at death.

So imaginatively experiencing this does two things for us. First, it makes us measure the wholeheartedness of our commitments according to whether they are still worthwhile to us when seen in this light, as stopped by the final bell. We see whether in living death, we could still affi rm our ends and the ways we plan to pursue them as long as time allows. If we cannot affi rm our ends and means, then we are not wholehearted in our cares to the point of infinite resignation: when our narravive is imaginatively stretched forward to overlap our death, some of our cares appear less worthwhile. We are “halted . . . in order to renounce vain pursuits” (TDIO 77). Second, because we can never affi rm everything about our practical identity, we feel a measureless longing for more time to correct it. That is, we feel the infinite loss of being out of time with something vital as yet undone. A child who thinks of the right answer the second after the test is whisked away can barely imagine the regret we feel in this paradoxical state.

But after earnestly imagining ourselves at the midnight hour, when our bell has tolled and we regret infinitely that we are out of time with our work badly done or at least unfinished, we realize that in fact we are “still living,” that we are *not* actually out of time yet, though we could be soon. So it is still only the eleventh hour after all, and we can still change our answer to the existential question and thus the final meaning of our life! This experience combines both ethical urgency and the joy of unexpected reprieve, as if the test-proctor had told the child “Actually, I see that you have a few more minutes . . .” Then

the thought of death gives the earnest person the right momentum and the right goal towards which he directs his momentum. . . . Then earnestness grasps the present this very day, disdains no task as too insignificant, rejects no time as too short, works with all its might, even if 

*[p. 163]* ^pg182

it is willing to smile at itself if this eff ort is said to be merit before God (TDIO 83).

In other words, authenticity in the face of death strengthens the will to sustain infinite resignation in willing the good in everything we pursue; it seals unity-3 in a “love as strong as death” (*Song of Songs* 8: 6). And with a smile it accepts the ethico-religious jest.

Because this limit of authentic willing is reached through imaginative inclusion of our own death in our narravive, it is a paradoxical act ripe for representation in literary form, as many stories of ghosts, or recently deceased spirits, or near-death experiences suggest. None of these narratives can quite capture the paradoxical synthesis required, because to some extent they must portray the agent either as a second person looking at her life from the outside, or as continuing to act and change in death.<sup>34</sup> But a few have captured the spiritual harrowing that we undergo in experiencing being-out-of-time, followed by the shift back to living freedom, in which our remaining time and open future appear in a radically new light. Perhaps none does better than “Staves IV and V” in Charles Dickens’ *A Christmas Carol*. In the company of the terrible “Ghost of Christmas Future,” Scrooge first hears people he knew mentioning an unnamed man’s death without sorrow, and then sees a charwoman, a laundress, and an undertaker pawning off some paltry possessions of this man.<sup>35</sup> He then sees the man lying dead with his face covered and hears a couple express strong relief that their creditor has died.<sup>36</sup> He enters the house of his own assistant Bob Cratchit and sees the family grieve over the recent death of their son, Tiny Tim. Finally, the Ghost brings Scrooge to an unkempt graveyard, where he sees his own name on his grave and realizes that it was *his* life that no one missed, that was only a source of wrongs done to others.<sup>37</sup> In this awful revelation, he fears infinitely that he is out of time, that his story cannot be changed. But he begs for consolation, for one more chance.

Note that there is no explicit fear of divine punishment here, nor any descent into hell (as in the famous 1964 technicolor musical film version). What appalls Scrooge is the final meaning that his narravive could have as the story of a man who did no good for anyone. What he cannot bear, what he rejects now with all his heart, is the identity of a man *who let Tiny Tim die*. This turning lies within the ethical; it does not require faith in an afterlife. As if echoing Dickens, Kierkegaard cites a literary example of an old man who in a dream “looked back over a wasted life until he woke in anxiety New Year’s morning not only to a new year but to a new life” (TDIO 76). A life whose final meaning is entirely negative is itself suffi - ciently damning to have an apotropaic influence on the will that takes it to heart, as Scrooge now does. What he fears most is that he is “past hope” for *this* life, for his time on Earth to mean something better.

So on awakening and finding himself still alive on Christmas morning, Scrooge has the kind of experience that Tolkien calls a “eucatastrophe,” 

*[p. 164]* ^pg183

namely the joy of a reprieve beyond all rational hope that is felt as grace.<sup>38</sup> There is a strong analogy to faith here, for in his last moments with the final Ghost, Scrooge clings to one sign that what he has seen are “shadows of things that may be only,” that his fate is not yet totally sealed.<sup>39</sup> Hence his infinite joy on Christmas morning that “The Time before him was his own, to make amends in” parallels the joy of faith justifi ed by miracle, eschatological trust vindicated by the “absurd.”<sup>40</sup> And indeed Scrooge’s delight in every small detail shows the dramatic paradigm-shift in the meaning of temporality for him:

He went to church, and walked about the streets, and watched the people hurrying to and fro, and patted children on the head, and questioned beggars, and looked down into the kitchens of houses and up to the windows, and found that everything could yield him pleasure. He had never dreamed that any walk—that anything—could give him so much happiness.<sup>41</sup>

How much this sounds like Silentio’s “knight of faith” who finds all temporality fresh and new, who sees the miracle in every moment. Dickens shows that something close to this can still be felt by the person who has only achieved infinite resignation: for, “supported by the earnest thought of death, the earnest person says ‘All is not over’” (TDIO 85). She recognizes then that “time also is a good” (TDIO 83). The indeterminate time remaining to her now appears as precious beyond measure, as a chance to make a narravive worth having lived out to the last. The open possibility of the future is now appreciated with new inwardness: Scrooge says, “I am here—the shadows of the things that would have been, may be dispelled.”<sup>42</sup> The turn that began when he repented the chief error of his youth, and continued when he asked what would become of Tiny Tim, is complete: the rest of his narravive becomes a wholehearted will to the good in truth. As Dickens explicitly recognizes, this is an achievement of *diachronic* unity: both as he is leaving the final Ghost, and again on awakening, Scrooge vows: “I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future. The Spirits of all three shall strive within me. I will not shut out the lessons they teach.”<sup>43</sup>

At this limit, unity-3 does become more complex. In answering the existential question about what his entire narravive will mean, the person attains what Kierkegaard calls a “wish that pertains essentially to the whole of life” (UDVS 100). As we see with Scrooge, this unifying wish may not be clearly formed until later in life, but it reaches backwards to transform the meaning of everything that has transpired up to that point in his life. Yet towards the future, it works to cultivate continuity of purpose that can last, and it reconciles with the possibility that the work may be ended by death at any moment: “Earnestness, therefore, becomes the living of each day as if it were the last and also the first in a long life” (TDIO 96). The second half of this formula expresses the agent’s ethical determination; the 

*[p. 165]* ^pg184

first expresses her infinite resignation. This is the ideal result of integrating the fact of mortality into wholehearted willing.

### 3 Conclusion I: from Unity-3 to Unity-4 (Existential Faith)

Admittedly, a paradox remains in this solution: authenticity in relation to our mortality completes our narrative unity precisely by making us recognize the incompleteness of our narravive and prompting us to fix whatever is out of tune, to achieve harmony while time remains, while recognizing that we will not completely succeed. Given that we can never attain that perfect harmony, it always impels us forward—until it really is too late. In that sense, I grant to the skeptics about narrative identity that there is a kind of narrative unity—call it unity-4—that we never do attain in this life. We are disunifi ed and fragmentary in relation to this ideal, which is why the thought of death can have power in moving us closer to unity-4.

This concession amounts to saying that there is one problem of death that narrative theories require religious faith to answer. But this is exactly what we should expect: there is a point beyond which an immanent conception of narrative unity cannot go. Consider what looks at first like quite a different problem: at death, a narravive apparently goes out of existence; only narratives about it remain.<sup>44</sup> In this, there seems to be an infinite loss: this whole web of experiences, acts, and feelings, with all their meaning to the agent, should not vanish “like tears in the rain,” as Roy says just before his death in the film *Bladerunner*.<sup>45</sup> When this is felt to be a problem, we have crossed from purely ethical into religious categories. The issue becomes the preservation of narravive meanings in some kind of hereafter, so that all one’s ethical strivings (and even one’s failings) are not “for nothing” in the ultimate scheme of things. This is more than ethical ideals in themselves demand: here the motive for narrative unity actually transcends the ethical in asking for an ultimate meaning to our strivings and sufferings.

If narravive meaning is to be preserved *eternally*, then our identity in some sense must continue eternally, even if not with the same freedom it involved in this life. But if we continue, cannot our earthly work be completed for us (and even with us)? Cannot our unfinished work be shown to us finally in its finished form? Can we not live the full meaning of this completion, experience the infinite plenitude of its significance? Consider Tolkien’s artist Niggle, who only managed to paint a few leaves of the mystical tree he imagined and planned to depict in a masterpiece before his untimely death. Imagine his surprise in the garden of the next life:

There before him stood the Tree, his Tree, finished. If you could say that of a Tree that was alive, its leaves opening, its branches growing and bending in the wind that Niggle had so often felt or guessed, and 

*[p. 166]* ^pg185

had so often failed to catch.  .  .  . All the leaves he had ever imagined were there, as he had imagined them rather than as he had made them; and there were others that had only budded in his mind, and many that might have budded if only he had had time.<sup>46</sup>

Again, eucatastrophe. Thus Kierkegaard’s earnest thought of death need not be grim and dark. While it halts impatience, in it we may also hope for a Life finished as we would will it completed in our best moments, when wholeheartedly willing to do the good. This eschatological narrative unity of perfect completion is precisely the unity-4 that we seek but can never reach as finite mortal persons. If that is right, then the narrative structure of practical identity is incomplete in the final analysis because it points towards an eschatological telos in which we can only have faith.

4\. 

### 4 Conclusion II: The Next Steps

This book has argued that there is a narrative dimension to personal autonomy that builds on the narravive structure of personal identity in general, and that the kinds of narrative unity involved in autonomy are especially strong on Kierkegaard’s conception of wholeheartedness. For purity of heart involves not just coherence among our cares, but also coherence through commitment to agapic ideals to govern our cares, willed to the point of infinite resignation. The case for this reading could be further strengthened by an analysis of Kierkegaard’s signed essay on “The Present Age” and an explanation of freedom and volition in the account of selfhood in the *Sickness Unto Death*, which I have treated elsewhere.

Several other questions regarding autonomy remain for further work. In particular, I have focused on the narrative structure of the volitional processes involved in *formal* autonomy, without considering the closely related issues of (a) how the capacities involved in autonomous agency are developed in child-raising and education, and (b) what the source-conditions of personal autonomy are. For a narrative approach, it is natural to analyze the source-conditions in historical terms to clarify what conceptual and psychological resources a young person must be given in order to be able to develop a deep self for which she is responsible. This involves not just negative conditions to rule out autonomy-undermining forms of manipulation, but also positive conditions concerning autonomy-enhancing sources on which human agents can draw in evaluating and willing without being mere mimics or conformists. Recent work on “autonomy competences” by Friedman, Meyers, Barclay, Atkins, Mele and others off ers crucial insights here. While the romantic tradition sought these sources within the individual, the analysis I have sketched suggests instead that those sources must include a range of values really worth caring about, which we learn primarily through exposure, direct contact, practice in tending and helping, and 

*[p. 167]* ^pg186

being the recipient of care ourselves. We must also be taught to evaluate in light of experience, and to be aware of our power to choose. And we must be given opportunities to care, to work at tasks we can rationally perceive as worthwhile, with at least some appreciation of our eff orts and interest by our educators and peers.

A detailed analysis of these source-conditions could build on the account of narrative identity given here. This would underscore the crucial point that, while human autonomy depends on individual devotion and engagement, autonomous choice and eff ort cannot occur in a vacuum. It requires attunement to objective values that has social preconditions. A Kierkegaardian extension of the conditions that we find in the nature of caring would add further sources, such as an ultimate religious sanction for ethical ideals, revealed promises as a basis for faith that ethical eff ort is not in vain, and even individual callings or revealed vocations. The paradox of autonomy, on a Kierkegaardian view, is that free will is created *ex nihilo*, and thus God has an infinite claim on us, yet one of God’s requirements of us is that we become ourselves by autonomously forming deep selves in response to reality and the existence of other created persons.

Thus, this book only establishes a groundwork for a complete account of autonomy and of Kierkegaardian selves. It shows how the topics and key phenomena are related within a narrative realist approach to the identity of persons, and lays out the main concepts and distinctions that such a framework requires. Within this framework, the next task is to fill in a full account of existential autonomy and authenticity that can be defended as the best explanation of deep responsibility. But I hope enough has been said to show that this approach, inspired by Kierkegaard, off ers a serious alternative worth pursuing among the other ways of explaining personal autonomy so far developed.
