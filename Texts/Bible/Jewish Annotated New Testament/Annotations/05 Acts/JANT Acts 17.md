### 17.1–9: Events in Thessalonica

*[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-1|Text]], p. 258* · Paul was instrumental in establishing the Christian community in Thessalonica, and wrote at least one letter to the church there. ^17-1

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-1|1]]:** *Amphipolis and Apollonia*, cities along the Via Egnatia, the main east-west road across Macedonia (present-day Greece). *Thessalonica*, major Macedonian city. *Synagogue*, see 13.5,14; 14.1; 16.13; 17.1,10,17; 18.4,19; 19.8. ^17-1-2

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-2|2]]:** *Sabbath*, see 13.14; 18.4. ^17-2

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-3|3]]:** *Necessary for the Messiah to suffer*, no indication what passages Paul read. The idea of a suffering and resurrected Messiah is important in Luke-Acts, (1.16; 3.18; 9.16; [[Luke 02#^2-49|Lk 2.49]]; [[Luke 24#^24-7|24.7]],[[Luke 24#^24-26|26]],[[Luke 24#^24-46|46]]), but absent in the Tanakh and contemporary Jewish tradition. ^17-3

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-4|4]]:** Both Jews and Gentiles become believers (13.43,50; 14.1–2). *Devout Greeks*, Gentiles who demonstrate interest in Judaism and involvement in the Jewish community (10.2; 13.16,26; 16.14; 17.12; 18.7; [[Luke 07#^7-1|Lk 7.1–10]]). ^17-4

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-5|5]]:** *Jealous*, 5.17; 13.45; 17.5; on negative responses to Christian preaching, see 9.22; 18.5–6,28. Although Paul is charged with disturbing the city (16.20; see also 19.26–39; 24.5), Jews are actually the guilty ones. ^17-5

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-7|7]]:** *Contrary to the decrees of the emperor*, see 16.21; cf. 24.5. *Another king*, Jews lodge a similar accusation against Jesus in [[Luke 23#^23-2|Lk 23.2]]. ^17-7

### 17.10–15: Events in Beroea

*[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-10|Text]], p. 259* · Paul and Silas turn off the Via Egnatia. ^17-10

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-10|10]]:** *Synagogue*, see v 1. ^17-10-2

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-11|11]]:** *Examined the scriptures*, see v 2. ^17-11

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-12|12]]:** *Greek women and men of high standing*, Gentiles, including women, who participate in Jewish communal life; see v 4. ^17-12

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-13|13]]:** *The Jews,* only Jews are identified as opposing Paul. ^17-13

### 17.16–34: Events in Athens

*[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-16|Text]], p. 259* · By this time Athens lacked political significance, but it was still remembered as an important center of philosophical training. While Paul initially follows his pattern of preaching in a synagogue (see v 1) to Jews and devout Greeks (see v 4), this episode focuses on his engagement with Gentiles, particularly representatives of major philosophical schools. ^17-16

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-16|16]]:** *Idols,* Paul’s negative reaction to the presence of idols reflects one Jewish attitude that idols are lifeless objects that lead people away from acknowledging God ([[Isaiah 40#^40-19|Isa 40.19–20]]; [[Isaiah 44#^44-9|44.9–20]]; Philo, *Spec. Laws* 2.163–67; *Ep. Arist.* 135; *m. Sanh.* 7.6) and promote immorality ([[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 14#^14-8|Wis 14.8]],[[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 14#^14-31|31]]; *Sib. Or.* 3.586–600). Other Jews, particularly many rabbis, accepted the presence of images of pagan deities, but cautioned against associating with those images used for the purposes of worship (*m. Avod. Zar.* 3.3–4; *t. Avod. Zar.* 6.6). ^17-16-2

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-17|17]]:** *Marketplace*, Gk “agora,” a city’s main social area. ^17-17

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-18|18]]:** *Epicurean*, philosophical school teaching that gods played no role in human affairs. *Stoic*, philosophical school teaching that humans should use reason to live a virtuous life and to develop a will in accordance with nature. *Babbler*, derogatory term casting Paul as one who picked up scraps of new ideas. *Foreign divinities*, similar to the charge against Socrates (Plato, *Apol.* 24b-c). *Resurrection*, Gk “anastasis,” which the bystanders take to be the name of a deity. ^17-18

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-19|19]]:** *Areopagus*, Mars Hill, near the Acropolis and the city’s chief administrative council. ^17-19

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-21|21]]:** Athenians were known for being intel-lectually curious (Strabo, *Geogr.* 9.1.16; Livy, *Hist.* 45.27.11; Josephus, *Ag. Ap.* 2.130; Pausanias, *Descr.* 1.17.1). ^17-21

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-22|22]]:** *Extremely religious*, ancient speeches often began by praising the audience, although the Greek phrase can connote superstitious belief, perhaps making Paul’s words sarcastic. ^17-22

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-24|24]]:** *God who made the world*, creation is a principle activity of God in Jewish tradition ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-1|Gen 1.1]]; [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^2-11|Ex 2.11]]; [[Isaiah 42#^42-5|Isa 42.5]]; [[Psalms 115#^115-4|Ps 115.4]]; [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 09#^9-1|Wis 9.1]]) and Greek thought (Plato, *Tim.* ). *Shrines made by human hands*, Paul’s speech shares with much of Jewish tradition a critique of idol worship (see v 16) and an acknowledgment that God was not restricted to a single shrine ([[Isaiah 66#^66-1|Isa 66.1]]; Josephus, *Ant.* 8.227). His blanket rejection of humanly crafted shrines echoes Stephen’s speech (7.48), and runs counter to the understanding of the Jerusalem Temple as God’s dwelling place on earth. The implicit rejection of the Temple is consistent with Acts’ rejection of the Jews as God’s people ([[I Kings 08#^8-13|1 Kings 8.13]]; [[Psalms 084#^84-2|Ps 84.1]]). ^17-24

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-28|28]]:** *In him . . . being*, emphasizing the imminence of God and resembling the writing of Posidonius of Rhodes, first-century BCE philosopher. *For we too are his offspring*, quotation from Aratus, third-century BCE poet (*Phaen.* 5); also quoted by second-century BCE Jewish writer Aristobulus (frag 4.6). ^17-28

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-29|29]]:** Jews ([[Isaiah 44#^44-9|Isa 44.9–20]]; [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 13#^13-10|Wis 13.10]]; Philo, *Decalogue* 66; *Spec. Laws* 1.21) and philosophers (Seneca, *Ep*. 31.11; Plutarch, *Superst.* 6) commonly condemned idolatry. ^17-29

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-30|30–31]]:** Paul moves to distinctly Christian arguments (although he does not name Jesus explicitly), some based on concepts from Tanakh. Just as God creates all (v 24) and humans descend from a single ancestor (v 26), so God’s judgment applies to everyone (v 30) everywhere (v 31), and proof comes from the resurrection. ^17-30

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-32|32–34]]:** The response to Paul is divided, but with less intensity than previous occasions (13.43,45; 14.4,11; 17.4–5). ^17-32

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-32|32]]:** *Scoffed*, the idea of bodily resurrection was contrary to most Greek notions of life after death. ^17-32-2

**[[NRSVue Acts 17#^17-34|34]]:** *Dionysius . . . Damaris*, both unknown. The pairing of male and female characters follows a pattern in the Gospel and Acts ([[Luke 02#^2-25|Lk 2.25–38]]; [[Luke 06#^6-6|6.6–11]]; [[Luke 13#^13-10|13.10–17]]; [[Luke 15#^15-3|15.3–10]]; [[Acts 05#^5-1|Acts 5.1–11]]). *Areopagite*, a member of the court that met at the Areopagus. ^17-34
