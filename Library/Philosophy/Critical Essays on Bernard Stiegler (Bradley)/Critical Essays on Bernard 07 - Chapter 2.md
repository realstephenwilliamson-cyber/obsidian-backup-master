## Chapter 2

A.I. AND THE DESTRUCTION OF THOUGHT: How MIGHT GEORGE ORWELL AND BERNARD

STIEGLER SPEAK TO EACH OTHER?

\[T\]he machine is here, and its corrupting effects are almost irresistible. —George Orwell

It is no exaggeration to say that the internet is owned and operated by private surveillance capital.

—Shoshana Zuboff

Today our everyday life is completely overdetermined by automatization, for example through the smartphone.

—Bemnard Stiegler

The disciplines of language and literature and therefore education as a whole — the pure loci of knowledge production and the preserve of originality in the humanities — are increasingly pitted against artificial intelligence in terms of a violent clash between the authentic or inauthentic, the natural and the artefactual. Now, the humanities and AI partake of the agon of intelligence. To understand this agon, I will focus on the power of corruption, that is, the corruption of knowledge, the corruption of the dogmatic code. I am interested in the connection between corruption and hope. Here George Orwell and French philosopher Bernard Stiegler speak to each other because while the former looks to desire as such as a form of desperate resistance and corruption, the latter discerns a pandemic and planetary loss of desire and with it therefore the loss of resistance. Faced with this epochal crisis, Stiegler highlights the possibility of bifurcation, error, and the creation of what algorithms cannot forecast — namely the incomprehensible as such. Stiegler follows Deleuze and Guattari who speak of creativity and demand that artists, philosophers, thinkers — “functionaries

*[p. 32]* ^pg47

of humanity”, as Husserl calls them — “bring something incomprehensible into the world!” (Deleuze & Guattari, 1987, p. 378). In the Deleuzian sense, both Orwell’s and Stiegler’s philosophical interest is in bringing something new into the world, as a form of resistance and a double act of corrupting the corruption itself. It is in this sense that Orwell is even more radical than he is sometimes depicted.

Immense Linguistic Entropy Contra Google, Stiegler, who argues that automation is a form of standardization and as such produces entropy, makes no bones about his stance and insists that the search engine’s algorithms cause “immense linguistic entropy.” Although Google’s algorithms can translate the ”

languages of the world through “the pivot language” of English, what we are witnessing in the first few decades of the 21\*' century is a regression of intelligence. In the interview titled Nous devons rendre aux gens le temps gagné par l’automatisation with Pierric Marissal, Stiegler says:

The impoverishment of vocabulary and dysorthography \[a specific disorder of spelling) regresses individual and collective intelligence through a submission to the law of averages. Conversely, life produces, through exceptions, mutations that are impossible to anticipate but which are the very conditions of evolution. Poets and writers have shaped languages through their exceptional use of language. Algorithms erase all exceptions: they only work by calculating probabilities based upon averages.

Against the entropy of language and thought by algorithmic governmentality (Rouvroy & Berns, 2013), Stiegler proposes the negentropy of knowledge (or negative entropy following Erwin Schrédinger in his 1944 book, What is Life?). We shall see what this entails later. In Orwell’s Nineteen Eighty-Four, this is encapsulated in Winston’s desire for corruption. Orwell writes of Winston, the central character in the book: “Anything that hinted at corruption always filled him with a wild hope.” Winston hopes for decadence and corruption to win out over corrupt, ominous and permanently monitoring forms of power. This is to corrupt the corruption, in the sense that Badiou describes in The True Life (2017). Winston speaks of decadence as a revolutionary tool: “I hate purity, I hate goodness! I don’t want any virtue to exist anywhere. I want everyone to be corrupt to the bones.”

*[p. 33]* ^pg48

Contrast this “wild hope” with what Winston stands against in *Nineteen Eighty-Four* – the corruption of language found in Newspeak, the official language of Oceania. In *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, Winston’s colleague Harold Syme spells out the linguistic prowess of Newspeak: “The secret’s to move from translation, to direct thought, to automatic response,” where automatic response simply means the end of critical thought. Automatic response? This is where *Nineteen Eighty-Four* presciently speaks to the present moment, in which more and more decision making is handed over to machines, where more and more critical thought is handed over to AI. From medical research, collaborative scientific studies, and the analysis of the weather to the scrutiny of chess configurations, the machine holds sway. What does it mean to destroy words? In the time of Facebook, Google, Amazon, Twitter, Snapchat, Instagram… surveillance capitalism, machine language and automatic translation… the list goes rattling on – are we not witnessing the destruction of words akin to the machinations of Big Brother? Is this not suggesting an epochal shift in intelligence? If so, like Orwell, Stiegler, influenced by Herbert Marcuse, is right to be concerned with the one dimensionality of language. It is a true horror that one day we will automatically respond to a given stimuli. The true horror will be the absence of new words and thoughts. Both thinkers would resist “cutting language down to the bone” as Harold Syme proudly says of the essence of Newspeak in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. This is the elimination of all unnecessary words to "narrow the range of thought" and therefore to eliminate the possibility of ever committing Thoughtcrime. As Syme says: "The Revolution will be complete when the language is perfect. Newspeak is Ingsoc and Ingsoc is Newspeak." But how might we think beyond the language of Newspeak, or language designed to inhibit critical thinking and Thoughtcrime? In a world of surveillance, monitoring, trend prediction, algorithmic governmentality, the “total administration of desire,” what does it mean to say that language is perfected? 

*Although China’s social credit vision is invariably described as “digital totalitarianism” and is often compared to the world of Orwell’s 1984, it is better understood as the apotheosis of instrumentarian power fed by public and private data sources and controlled by an authoritarian state.* (Zuboff, 

*[p. 34]* ^pg49

In his principles for Newspeak in the addendum to *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, Orwell writes of the fascistic desire – in both red and black hues – to extirpate all heretical thought: “The purpose of Newspeak is not only to provide a modicum of expression for the worldview and mental habits proper to the devotees of Ingsoc, but to make all the other modes of thought impossible. It was intended that when Newspeak had been adopted once and for all, and Oldspeak forgotten, any heretical thought – that is a thought deviating from the principles of Ingsoc – should be literally unthinkable, at least as far as thought is dependent on words. Newspeak is designed not to extend but to diminish the range of thought.” We find that Orwell is concerned with the erasure of those thoughts which deviate from the current orthodoxy and therefore he warns against the curtailing of rival visions of how to live, work and think. And in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, Harold Syme tells Winston about the Eleventh Edition of Newspeak and the philosophy behind it. 

*“The Eleventh Edition is the definitive edition. We’re getting the language into its final shape – the shape it’s going to have when nobody speaks anything else. When we’ve finished with it, people like you will have to learn it all over again. You think, I dare say, that our chief job is inventing new words. But not a bit of it! We’re destroying words – scores of them, hundreds of them, every day. We’re cutting the language down to the bone.”* 

*“It’s a beautiful thing, the destruction of words. Do you know that Newspeak is the only language in the world whose vocabulary gets smaller every year?”* 

*“Don’t you see that the whole aim of Newspeak is to narrow the range of thought? In the end we shall make thoughtcrime literally impossible,* *because there will be no words in which to express it. Every concept that can ever be needed, will be expressed by exactly one word, with its meaning rigidly defined and all its subsidiary meanings rubbed out and forgotten.”* 

*“Every year fewer and fewer words, and the range of consciousness always a little smaller.”* 

*The Revolution will be complete when the language is perfect.* *“Has it ever occurred to you, Winston, that by the year 2050, at the very latest, not a single human being will be alive who could understand such a conversation as we are having now?”* 

*Winston replies: “Except the proles.”* 

*[p. 35]* ^pg50

Except the Proles 

While Orwell finds a form of *exception* within the already normalized state of exception, as it were, in the lives of the proletariat, in the act of forbidden love and exploration of sexuality between Winston and Julia. Stiegler finds the exception in what he calls unpredictable bifurcation or what Ross calls “improbable courage” (2017, p. 23), that is the act of dreaming as conjuring the “absolutely singular, absolutely improbable” (Stiegler & Nony, 2015). Orwell speaks of history as resistance, as courage, of “knots of resistance” as something that must be remembered. Indeed, the prospects are not absolutely irreversible, as he says: “But one can imagine little knots of resistance springing up here and there – small groups of people banding themselves together, and gradually growing, and even leaving a few records behind, so that the next generations can carry on where we leave off.” Even at his bleakest, he sees the possibility, a trace and memory of resistance to the one dimensionality of thought. It is such courage which demands that “we refuse to cede the future to illegitimate power,” according to Zuboff. 

That the lives of the proles (or proletariat) are considered the only possible escape or exemption from the totalitarianism of Big Brother is a fitting starting point to compare the philosophies of Stiegler and Orwell. On the one hand, we have the Stieglerian idea that the proletarianization of knowledge, through digital tertiary retentions (the externalization or archiving of memory in vast databases for example), is leading to the short circuiting of knowledge, the loss of the *savoirs*, to endemic *betise* or stupidity, and on the other, we have the Orwellian notion that Big Brother and the principles of Newspeak aim for the *diminishment of thought* as such. While Orwell will say “if there was hope, it must lie in the proles,” one might think of Stiegler saying “hope lies with deproletarianization”. Stiegler calls for the creation of a politics of deproletarianization, a politics of struggle against the process of unlearning or disapprenticeship, and resistance to the processes that destroy cognitive functions and short-circuit reason, or in the language of Adorno, against that which further *unreasons irrational rationalization processes.* In other words, Stiegler is searching for the *non-inhuman human* in the time of the post-human. 

Whether it is Big Data or Big Brother, Stiegler and Orwell are both concerned with the *narrowing of the range of thought.* In some ways, 

*[p. 36]* ^pg51

Stiegler argues, Big Data and Google operations are leading to the entropy of language and thought. Following Frédéric Kaplan, a professor in digital humanities, he is direct in his view that Google is destroying language. “Google is destroying the idiomaticity, the singularity of language” (Stiegler & Nony, 2015). For Stiegler, Google’s linguistic robots lead to a loss of semantic diversity, and therefore to a reduction in the range of new possibilities. 

In his fiction, Orwell is equally forthright that Newspeak – the language of authoritarian manipulation – is “diminishing” thought. For both of them, one may surmise, with the “diminishing” of thought comes the diminishment of the possibilities of life. While in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, there is no way out of the entropic nightmare of totalitarianism, Stiegler is more affirmative, demanding not merely recognition of the poison but its remedy – despite the equally perilous state of the world. More than just fancifully dreaming of another way of organizing the world, or imagining a world transcendent to this one, Stiegler actively searches for and contributes to the formation of negentropic tools and weapons to fight against the destruction of language. For him, it is by using technology carefully, curatively, pharmacologically, and therapeutically that the exemption *against the exception* can still manifest. 

In *Automatic Society* (2016), we find Stiegler invoking a negentropic principle to counter the rise of stupidity and algorithmic or voluntary servitude. Importantly, what is therapeutic for Stiegler is the notion of quasi-causality – that is a question of virtual (incorporeal) or transhistorical effects – becoming as such – a concept which appears in Deleuze’s *La logique du sens* (1969). In asking what kind of quasi-causality of the event can be invented beyond the framework of Big Data, the network effect and social engineering, Stiegler is addressing forms of individuation – the way that the human being is co-constituted with and through technology, in arts, in different philosophical, science and political fields. In this series, art has a central role *vis-à-vis* the strategy of invention and counter-effectuation or the “extraction of the event” as such. This sense of the *pharmakon*, or that which is both poison and cure, and what is therapeutic as such, is therefore tied to the notion of quasi-causality. 

*[p. 37]* ^pg52

Big Other, Big Data, Big (Br)Other 

In Stiegler’s positive pharmacology, there is a concern with subjectivation processes, that is, the bringing forth of the subject, and with knowledge production in impossible-to-control digital communication networks. Against this, Stiegler is arguing for a new politics of digital production, a new architectonic to counter the proletarianization of knowledge, a struggle against “massification and cretinization” by producing that which exceeds calculation. His point is that unpredictable bifurcations can enrich knowledge, rendering it more complex. And, bifurcation is therefore a question of care. For Stiegler, this is essentially a challenge to impure, instrumentally-driven reason (non-knowledge or anti-knowledge). His negentropic principle is a strategy of differentiation and diversity as it affirms the reconstruction of the faculty of reason which he claims has been devastated by Big Data operations. Consequently, Stiegler is writing against processes of standardization and the destruction of knowledge as such. For him, and at its starkest, platform capitalism is destroying the process of reproduction of life. While the “incredible linguistic capacity” of Google is powerful, fascinating and indeed, seductive, it nonetheless is leading to entropy in language. Google becomes an instrument for the corruption of meaning and syntax and the weakening of memory. Big Brother has become Big Data or the Big Other – digital networks or “impersonal systems trained to monitor and shape our actions remotely, unimpeded by law”, according to Zuboff. As she says: “Big Other knows everything, while its operations remain hidden, eliminating our right to resist.” This perspective is clearly informed by Orwell’s concern with the diminishment in the range of thought, but Orwell is not without his critics as we shall see in the next section. 

J. Ayer and Orwell 

In a slightly idiosyncratic way, philosophy in the ordinary language mode has too been charged with delimiting thought. The goals of George Orwell’s Newspeak and A. J. Ayer’s *Language, Truth and Logic* can be compared to show how these viewpoints or dogmatic worldviews on language set the boundaries of what can legitimately be said or not said. Orwell was acquainted with Ayer, who speaks highly of their friendship. There is also a question whether Orwell read Ayer’s book, was influenced by it, and 

*[p. 38]* ^pg53

introduced — subconsciously or otherwise — Ayer’s verification principle into Nineteen Eighty-Four. We can use the “slightly fanciful literary analogy” of S. R. Sutherland (Ayer & Griffiths, 1992) in the following way: Newspeak is the only proper language as it uses only empirical propositions. It makes sense because it can be verified. But more than this it completely reinvents what logic demands. Logically necessary statements such as 2x2=5 are tautologous in the Newspeak system. They are nevertheless true despite mathematical contradiction according to the linguistic system in use.

Moreover, moral pronouncements are expressions of personal emotion and therefore metaphysical and necessarily external to the system. On this account, desire as such is alien to system dynamics. Such metaphysical statements are strictly speaking, nonsense. In this closed system of meaning, who verifies the statement decides the truth or falsehood of a statement. On this reading, language remains thoroughly and miserably infused with the corruption of power. “Who controls the past controls the future and who controls the present controls the past” is a familiar Orwellian mantra and it is insightful here to contrast Orwell and Stiegler as both thinkers would have much to say to each other in our so-called post-truth era, where literally anything goes, including the truth.

According to this logic, the “wild hope” of Winston for a different order of things in Nineteen Ninety Four is strictly speaking metaphysical nonsense, as it is merely an expression of an unruly emotion or desire, which in itself must be expunged. The argument of S. R. Sutherland is that the verification principle proffered by Ayer “inevitably exceeded its useful purgative role in such a way as to clearly constrict our understanding and to hereby ‘diminish thought’”. He goes on to say that “if the implicit suggestions are correct then there is a case that conceptual possibilities are diminished, and that 1936 \[Language, Truth and Logic was first published in that year\] might in this sense lead to 1/984” (Ayer & Griffiths, 1992, p. 87). S. R. Sutherland adds: “My charge against Language, Truth and Logic is that there are just enough points of similarity, just enough bells were rung by Orwell’s words, to justify a prima facie case against Language, Truth and Logic that, whatever the intent of the author, the book is conceptually restrictive and, indeed, intellectually imperialistic in its character’ (p. 78). In other words, Orwell’s philosophy of clarity itself functions as closed a

*[p. 39]* ^pg54

system and is thus imperialistic in character. We might say it is inadvertently intolerant of the exception and the incomprehensible. 

Black Sun 

How can the *exception* be introduced into language to derail somehow the automatization of thought? For Stiegler, human thought and natural language continue to offer some means of resistance. Stiegler finds this in poetry and more concretely for example, in the notion of the black sun. One imagines that the words black and sun are easily juxtaposed by AI programming in a simple operation at the speed of light but the idea of a black sun is something which comes from natural language, from the human mind – from its most sublime expression, in poetry. The black sun captures our attention. It electrifies the brain. It is a fillip snapping the brain to attention. There is no violation of meaning but syntax is disrupted. The cognitively dissonant image of the black sun in the 19th century *oeuvre* of romantic poet Gerard de Nerval is provocative for Stiegler because *le soleil noir* is a contradiction and impossible to behold, while in the realm of poetic language it can be envisioned and hence suggests a form of linguistic resistance to the one dimensionality of language. *It is an exception*. And for Stiegler language, like life, is based on exceptions, on default (“fault” or “lack”) – (Wambacq, Ross & Buseyne, 2016). And Stiegler argues that such exceptions cannot be computed. And more than this, what is negentropic is incalculable (Bradley, 2018; 2020). In the mistakes and exceptions, we find life or language evolving. And that is why this exceptional image of the black sun is so striking; it captures our attention. This is an example of Stiegler’s search for the expression of negentropic bifurcation. For Stiegler, bifurcation is infinite, while calculation is finite. Stiegler argues: 

\[O\]pen systems can become closed systems, if we impose to the systems, mechanical functioning, this is what is happening for example, with Google. Not only to the Chinese language, French language, or English language, but to 500 languages composing the main languages of the world on earth. And this is a process producing what is called in physics, entropy. That is a destruction of differences. 

Life and language are what are termed dynamic, open systems. That means that they are capable to transform themselves according to the 

*[p. 40]* ^pg55

transformation of the milieu, of the relationships between the system and other systems. Such open systems can become their opposite if we impose on those systems a mechanical functioning. 

In other words, through mechanical functionality, open systems can close and can become subject to the rule of the algorithms and the manipulations of the marketing and culture industries. This is the mechanical operation of Newspeak in essence, or what is happening, for example, with search engines in their entropic iteration. Stiegler understands Google as a very powerful means of calculation and control which is entropic and toxic in the sense that it is curtailing the linguistic capacity of natural languages. Again, this is the task of Newspeak in *Nineteen Eighty-Four* – carrying out a destructive, glottophagic logic, leading to the destruction of difference as such. 

Commonly understood, glottophagy (*glottophagie* as coined by Louis-Jean Calvet) is the absorption or replacement of minor languages or dialects by major ones. Glotto is a combining form meaning tongue, word, speech and phagy is a suffix denoting the practice of eating a specified food. In *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, it takes the form of a war of and against natural languages. The result is language death, the very destruction of language. Victory for Big Brother occurs when natural language loses its last native speaker, its last proletarian speaker. And with the destruction of language comes the extinction of meaning. We can say Big Brother systematically carries out (matriarchal) linguicide, the death of the (mother) language for political purposes. Language death stems from linguistic imperialism or cultural assimilation leading to a language shift and the abandonment of a native language in favour of a sometimes imposed alien *lingua franca* – from the language of the proles to the language of Newspeak. For his part, Stiegler is calling to resist the erosion of natural language differences and his antidote to the toxicity of the digital pharmakon is to reinvent tools or weapons based on the model of free software, on the model of free collaborative knowledge, on the model of local, open production through networks. 

For Stiegler, the question is how to engineer an art of control which can forecast or anticipate what Google cannot. This presumably would be to create events beyond algorithm prediction, to make a bifurcation which 

*[p. 41]* ^pg56

cannot be anticipated according to the logic of the dominant paradigm or algorithm. As we have seen for Stiegler, despite the “incredible linguistic” capacity of Google, it is entropic as it is essentially curtailing diversity or defining the nature of possibility itself. The result of which is that Google is effectively “destroying language” (Feenberg & Stiegler, 2017). In a way, Google embraces a futurist manifesto which affirms the destruction of the syntax of language and the liberation of words from syntactic chains according to Groys in his *Google: words Beyond Grammar* (2011)*.* The Russian philosopher questions Google’s utopian promise, comparing the reality of the search engine and its utopian, infinite project: “One can say that avant-garde poetry and the art of the twentieth century have created a vision of a utopian Google – of the free movement of liberated words in social space. The factual, really existing Google is obviously a technicopolitical realization but also a betrayal of this utopian dream of word liberation” (p. 12). Groys continues: “Google itself also escapes linguistic representation. It practices preselection and a prioritization that are also acts of word curatorship. The subject of the Internet search knows that his or her selection and evaluation of contexts depend on the processes of the preselection and pre-evaluation that were effected by the Google search engine. The user can see only what Google shows. Thus, Google is inevitably experienced by its users as a hidden (and potentially dangerous) Subjectivity – operating in a mode of world conspiracy” (p. 15). We can put the point thus: Without “word curatorship” as a process of human-all-too-human negentropic bifurcation, AI will continue to hold sway leading to the further proletarianization of knowledge. Curiously, one can take this a step further as Groys’ point in some ways echoes Syme’s forecast to Winston in *Nineteen Eighty-Four* regarding the dystopian destruction of language and the perfection of Newspeak. Not *needing to think* is becoming the desire of the modern way of life: 

By 2050 – earlier, probably – all real knowledge of Oldspeak will have disappeared. The whole literature of the past will have been destroyed. Chaucer, Shakespeare, Milton, Byron – they’ll exist only in Newspeak versions, not merely changed into something different, but actually changed into something contradictory of what they used to be. Even the literature of the Party will change. Even the slogans will change. How could you have a slogan like ‘freedom is slavery’ when the concept of freedom has been 

*[p. 42]* ^pg57

abolished? The whole climate of thought will be different. In fact there will be no thought, as we understand it now. Orthodoxy means not thinking – not needing to think. Orthodoxy is unconsciousness. 

As we have seen, Stiegler demands a therapeutics of the pharmakon which he designates, following Deleuze, quasi-causality. The art of control is thus a strategy of invention or imagination. Stiegler asks how new connections can be made between the body and the mind when Google and Big Data, which have become essentially marketing corporations, are effectively short-circuiting knowledge. In summa: he is critical of the way Big Data and social networks manipulate and destroy societal relations. So the question for him is how to prise open closed systems of thought to ensure that negentropic knowledge and thinking as such can flourish. This is to leave open the promise of the exception *to the exemption.* 

Against the closed systems of language and thought like Newspeak, Stiegler’s counsel in his *Nanjing Lectures* is a timely reminder that in a closed system which reaches its saturation point, transformation seems structurally impossible, because it is that which is *incalculable* as such. Yet in this crisis, this is where an opportunity or a decision beckons (p. 17). The essence of his remarks is captured in a TV interview entitled *Eviter l’apocalypse!* in which he argues: 

In a system, when it reaches its saturation point, its own transformation seems to it to be structurally impossible, because it is incalculable. If you look at the curves, and you follow them out, that's calculation, we cannot escape. If you take the curves of demography, of climate change, of speculation, we can't escape. But it is precisely by something that comes and breaks these curves, the logics of these curves, that it is possible to get out of it. It's always been like this. All dynamic open systems have always been produced by bifurcations of this type. 

Other fellow travellers have expressed a similar idea regarding this sense or form of openness. In Édouard Glissant’s work, we find a need for a new form of openness – open words (*parole ouverte*) – which can exceed the “Closed Place” (*lieu clos*). This is explored in the Martinican philosopher’s concept of the Open Boat (*la barque ouverte*). In *Poetics of Relation,* Glissant finds the new world and language in this open boat, in the thought of errantry and exile. It is the language of poetry. He writes: “We cry our 

*[p. 43]* ^pg58

cry of poetry. Our boats are open, and we sail them for everyone" (Glissant & Wing, p. 9). Elsewhere, Glissant speaks of the trembling of the earth (*le tremblement de terre*) and trembling thought (*la pensée du tremblement*) (see Bradley, 2019) and it is this emphasis which is shared by Greek philosopher Kostas Axelos who finds in poetry this expressive, open, quaking power. If poetry shakes, the whole world trembles and what is opened is a new path, a path from the aporia out of which we currently struggle to emerge: 

*La systématique ouverte de la poésie et de l'art, dans la systématique générale, constitue une systématique privilégiée. La poésie nourrit certes l'art, et poésie et art sont susceptibles d'accueillir les interprétations les plus diverses, dont certaines sont plus prégnantes que d'autres. Toutes les dimensions du Monde et de la pensée – et ce qui en dérive – ont quelque rapport avec la poésie et l'art, sans pour autant les expliquer. Poésie et art échappent aux déterminations primaires.* 

*The open system of poetry and art, in the general system, constitutes a privileged system. Poetry certainly nourishes art, and poetry and art welcome the most diverse interpretations, some more significant than others.* *All the dimensions of the world and of thought – and what derives from them* *– have some relation to poetry and art, without explaining them. Poetry and art escape primary determinations.* (Axelos, 1984, p. 103 \[my trans\]) 

*Ce qui caractérise décisivement les oeuvres poétiques et les oeuvres d'art,* *de quelque nature qu'elles soient, c'est que leur monde est délibérément – ce qui ne veut pas dire volontairement – ouvert au Monde. D'où leur puissance tremblante… S'ouvrant au monde et ouvrant le monde selon leurs médiations propres, elles ouvrent un chemin.* 

*What decisively characterizes poetic work and works of art, whatever their nature, is that their world is deliberately – not voluntarily – open to the World… Hence their trembling power. Opening up to the world and opening the world according to their own mediations, they open a path.* (Axelos, 1984, p. 103) 

*[p. 44]* ^pg59

Open boats, Maritime Thinking, Nomadic Thought The thinkers mentioned in the last few paragraphs are essential for my argument because they suggest openness and nomadism and the trembling of thought. Badiou too writes of the prospect of a wayfaring form or *bifurcatory* form of thinking. He writes: “The world is vast, and it is on its scale that thinking must perceive and act. A thinking of departure, a true thinking of the ever-changing ocean of the world. An exact and nomadic thinking, an exact thinking because it is a nomadic one, a maritime thinking” (Badiou, Engelmann & Spitzer, 2015, pp. 48–49). And in a discussion on the question of the rupture, Badiou connects the corruption to the exception in a striking way – suggesting that to corrupt the youth is to become “part of an immanent exception” (Völker, 2019, p. 16). Elsewhere, he explains this sense of the immanent exception with respect to creation and the play of inner and outer: 

In fact, a great work of art, an invention, a creation, is something that, within particular conditions, necessarily transcends those particular conditions. This is what I’ve called an immanent exception. It’s an exception that’s internal; it’s not an external exception. And so if particular works are capable of having a universal value it’s because they are not entirely reducible to the particular conditions of their creation but are also an immanent exception within these conditions. (Badiou, Engelmann & Spitzer, 2015, p. 63) 

*Immaner* Exception to the Exception 

Given the scope and prowess of politics as the language of corruption *par excellence*, for Orwell, especially at the bleak time of his writing – during his illness and existential solitude and crisis – the corruption of language by politics itself was ongoing but nevertheless there was some prospect of making the process of language corruption “reversible.” In his “Politics and the English Language” penned in 1946, Orwell is of the view that it is still possible to rise above the “debasement of political language.” It is possible to confront the “great enemy of clear language,” that is, the insincerity of the writer. As such, writers are able to overcome the “mental vices” of abstract-noun-infested prose, to stop the process where “the concrete melts into the abstract,” to mend the rupture between the word and its actual usage, 

*[p. 45]* ^pg60

to curb thought’s corruption of language and language’s corruption of thought. Orwell believes that good writers are still able to form in the mind a suitable mental image (following Stiegler – incalculable or *unreckonable* thought) which can still show that the writer *is thinking the unthought* as such. Orwell insists, the “decadence of our language is probably curable.” Stiegler would agree but insist that only through pharmacological curation, both understanding the poison and *practicing* the cure – will we find a way to curate knowledge afresh. Yet Orwell at times sounds like Syme in *Nineteen Eighty-Four,* as he discusses the beauty of Newspeak and says that one of its aims is to eliminate all that is *superfluous* in language – and Stiegler speaks, too, of the elimination of the *exception* as such. Yet it is here that Orwell might counter the verification principle of A. J. Ayer because he says that one of the goals of good writing is to put to scrap every word or idiom “which has outworn its usefulness.” With that caveat added, Orwell nevertheless holds that language ought to be an instrument for expressing and not concealing thought. Clear language liberates from the “worst follies of orthodoxy.” And he resists those forms of political language “designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable, and to give an appearance of solidity to pure wind.” 

Conclusion 

In this short thought-experiment, indeed, my own act of exception, I have juxtaposed the thoughts of George Orwell and Bernard Stiegler to think about the “global mnemotechnical system” or Big Data in its current form – that is Google, Amazon, Apple and Microsoft and their ilk – and what this means for the production of negentropic knowledge or language which might foster bifurcation, singularity, the unanticipated, something suggestive of another way of living, a creative and curative outcome beyond confinement, beyond artificial intelligence, beyond control and the corruption of language. Beyond the confinement of thought in entropic forms of artificial intelligence is thus a possibility of a new creative and curative mode of living. 

From my perspective, in the deliberate and performative paranoid vision I am presenting here is a whiff of both the Orwellian and Stieglerian, as I am urging “constant criticism” of the digital networks. I am warning against 

*[p. 46]* ^pg61

the totalitarianism of the unified code led by the marketing or culture industries or what Zuboff in *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* calls *instrumentarian power*. As she writes: “There is no historical precedent for instrumentarianism, but there is vivid precedent for this kind of encounter with an unprecedented new species of power.” As we know, “\[t\]otalitarianism, if not fought against, could triumph anywhere”, Orwell earnestly believes. Stiegler similarly demands that we create the incalculable against this tendency, that we ensure that the system of organizing our technical relations remains open and not closed. We would do well to listen to their counsel and to continuously and robustly question the degradation of thought, language, and politics in the contemporary era. There simply must be the corruption of corruption and the exception to the exemption.
