## About

A short dialogue of Plato in which Socrates questions the sophist Hippias of Elis, first on whether Achilles or Odysseus is the better man, then on lying and wrongdoing. It ends in a conclusion that Socrates himself cannot accept.

- **Characters and setting:** Socrates; Hippias, a sophist and polymath visiting Athens, who claims mastery of Homer; and Eudicus son of Apemantus, his Athenian host. The talk follows Hippias' public display on Achilles and Odysseus, once the crowd has gone. The sources consulted give no dramatic date.
- **Argument:** Hippias holds Achilles simple and true. Socrates argues that whoever knows a subject, such as arithmetic or astronomy, is best placed to lie about it; that Achilles lies too (*Iliad* IX); and that doing wrong willingly is better than doing it unwillingly. Hippias is left helpless, and Socrates says he does not agree with himself.
- **Authenticity:** its antiquity is secure, since Aristotle (*Metaphysics* V), Cicero (*De Oratore* III) and Alexander of Aphrodisias refer to it. Wikipedia notes that only Alexander names Plato as author, and that some contend Antisthenes wrote it. The IEP treats it as genuine.
- **Date of composition:** early. The IEP places it in the early group, written after Socrates' death and before Plato's first visit to Sicily in 387 BC.
- **Place among the dialogues:** one of the early "Socratic" dialogues (SEP, IEP). Thrasyllus put both dialogues named *Hippias* in his seventh tetralogy, with the *Ion* and *Menexenus*, subtitled "On Beauty" and "On Falsehood" (Diogenes Laertius 3.60).
- **Why it matters:** the SEP cites its question, whether voluntary wrongdoing is better than involuntary, as one of the puzzles the early dialogues set. Wikipedia notes that its conclusion conflicts with the *Crito*, where Socrates says one should never intentionally do wrong.
