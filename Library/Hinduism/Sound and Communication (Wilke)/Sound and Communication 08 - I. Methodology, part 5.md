In relation to both rationality and interiority, India calls some assumptions of the media theory, in particular the literacy thesis, into question. Just like abstraction, interiority appears not to be necessarily tied to writing. Proponents of literacy and media theories have associated interiority and rationality too exclusively with the modern subject of writing. Separation of the outer and inner worlds, which the medievalist Lentes traces back not only to the change in the media but also to an “inner dynamism” in the late medieval piety discourse, never existed in Hinduism. There were other inner dynamics. Indian history of religion has therefore developed along different paths. Despite strong tendencies towards internalization since pre-Christian times, a complete separation of the inner and the outer has never come about. This correlates with the sonic culture combining orality and literacy, or what was called the “third-space habitus” above. Media are justifiably termed “training grounds for the formation of the subject.” Therefore the power of the media cannot be denied. But still other variables should never be forgotten. For example, the history of interiority in India was decisively influenced by how the relationship between intellectuality and emotions is understood and historically modeled in the system of socio-cultural symbols. This is one of the many interfaces to a dense semantic field where the Indian culture diverges from Western culture. The extent to which changes in media cause shifts or transformations and generate overlappings and new forms of “habitus,” depends not on the media as such, but on the media practices and their functional connections.

#### 1.7 The Power of the Media and Their Cultural Contingency

“The human subject in its perceptive structure — and one could and must add: in its entire structure as an ensemble of perception patterns, cognitive and affective schemata, of physical style and dispositions to take action - is constituted not only naturally, but above all culturally, socially and historically; it is a product of its social practice (which its ‘natural’ plasticity utilizes). The form in which this perceptive structure of the subject transforms itself now depends — undoubtedly not completely, but at least to a considerable extent — on the historical structure of the ‘media’ in which this perception is organized by technical artefacts modeling perception. ”?” The types of media that are most common and dominate transmission play a not unimportant role and contribute to shaping mentalities. In India orality and literacy were continua, just like emotion and intellect. Maybe this explains at least in parts the fact that there never was such a severe division between the outer and inner world (and encompassing the whole religious culture) as there was in the European late Middle Ages and the Reformation. In India we find bouts of rationalization and interiorization in almost every century, and also a lasting dominance of the spoken and sounding word. This has remained the case above all in the field of religion. Texts remained tied to their vitalization in the voice and to performance practices, and the texts themselves bear traces of the culture of performance. There are numerous written images of orality.

Media open up possibilities for experience and cognition. However, they do not do this by their own nature, but only in interplay with culture-related patterns and practices. This point seems to us to be fundamental because too often in discussion of the media in social and cultural studies the argumentation was all too monocausal and rooted in a universal view of history, and cultural contingency received too little attention. The orality and literacy theories are especially productive, particularly in terms of cultural comparison, but the complexity of real media diversity, intercultural and intracultural plurality and intermediary zones was underestimated. The success of the strict literacy thesis lies in the strength of its systematic and structural analysis. Its disadvantage is that it undermines other socio-cultural variables, is susceptible to ideologies, and masks the fact that the medium of writing did not necessarily elicit exactly the same effects everywhere, and was not always exhausted and evaluated in the same way. Not only is culture dependent on the media, but the media are also dependent on culture. Like everything else (whether natural or artificial), voice and word, writing and books, are subject to cultural evaluations and codings. This means that the understanding, evaluation and use of words and writing are also informed, coded and structured by a complex of implicit forms of interpretation and ascriptions, know-how, social institutions, physically anchored forms of behavior, preconscious routine sequences and culturally formed emotional motivational states. In media theories the culture-specific codings and their historical and cultural contingency were not always sufficiently considered, and therefore there was always also a low level of awareness of the possible relativity of one’s own practice — for example, how strongly the culturally formed patterns flowed athematically into academic representation.

##### 1.7.1 Orality-Literacy Debate and Sanskrit Culture

According to Jack Goody who, together with Ian Watt, first put forward his seminal theses on the effects of writing in the 1960s and 1970s, no other cultural technology had such a strong influence on culture as a whole, society and thought, as the creation of a written tradition. He argued that no other technology had brought about such a radical change and bout of innovation in the realm of economy, religion, social organization and cognition as alphabetic writing and, later, the printing of books. He stated that wherever these cultural technologies emerged there was a steep educational pyramid. The creation of a written tradition, according to the central argument, revolutionized the literary production, transmission and storage systems and fostered new ways of handling information and new systems of communication. Writing and printing established external memory banks, which rendered intercourse between human beings no longer subject to the transience of oral speech and made it possible to communicate across space and time, and

Goody’s theory was immensely powerful. The strict argumentation and the numerous examples were very convincing. 1.7.3 

##### 1.7.3 Sonality as a Third Space: New Aspects for Debate

One can agree with Goody that the knowledge-technology, educational and social relevance of writing and abstract sign systems cannot be questioned. Doubtless reduction of complexity is needed in differentiated social structures, large-scale collectives (towns, states, translocal communities arising through migration, etc.), and the accompanying elaborated and pluralized knowledge cultures. This is achieved by means of standardization, generalization, systematization, formalization and abstraction. The late Vedic sacrificial ritual with its differentiated system of priests was one such complex social structure and knowledge culture, which apparently correlated with the spread and differentiation of community life (early state formation, urbanization, migration into the Ganges plain, and the beginning of caste society). We recognize the strength of Goody’s argument, while critizising him for not taking into account the socio-cultural situation - Hindu India’s very pronounced ritual and performance culture. The debate for and against literacy in ancient India distorts one’s view of the fact that in India writing has not generated any cultural value and oral-aural communication systems continued to exist even after the introduction of writing. In fact the phonocentric bias was even frequently reinforced through writing. It appears therefore necessary to take a view based on the aesthetics of reception. Goody established a dichotomy of the oral and the literal that did not exist in India, and systematically ignored the aesthetic dimension. This dimension introduces a change in perspective, brings aspects into the field of vision that previously had not been perceived or were rendered invisible because they were blanked out and not reflected upon. The one-sided focus of rationality excluded the role of the senses. Aesthetic aspects held no interest even though they are a

One of the many reasons is that in Sanskrit culture abstract sign systems were formed not only graphically, but also by using sounds. This also remained common in later times. Our argument of reception aesthetics which posits the coexistence of orality and literacy and proposes a sonic “habitus” (in which the oral-aural medium was more important) avoids the historical thickets and uncertainties of an early culture of writing in India and ultimately removes any import from the question of “oral or written?” as far as it rests on an incorrect dichotomy with regard to India. Instead the foreground is occupied by cultural practices, cultural meanings and cultural codings. 

One basic problem with Goody’s argumentation is the media determinism, which Staal and Falk justifiably objected to. One can summarize Goody’s argument in one short deductive sentence: since in the Indian literature analytical thought and rational systems are present on a grand scale (technical information, moral judgment, universal definition, Kantian imperative, mathematics, epistemology, logic, etc.), the culture cannot be anything other than a literary 016.47 For India, however, this deduction is obviously too simple and lacking in imagination. On the one hand, Goody is probably correct when he claims that without signs there can be no abstraction and without abstraction there can be no “superior” culture. Sign codes are important for systematization, etc. But the possibilities for forming signs are more numerous and varied. Signs do not have to be graphical and visual. Pāṇini used sound codes to regulate the fixed text grammatically. The Tantrists are familiar with both, that is, sound representations (mantras) and graphical representations (yantras). Yet the mantra form, i.e. the linguistic form, always remained the more important one. That is to say, sound symbols were preferred over graphical symbols. These two examples (Pāṇini’s grammar and Tantric practice) are far apart historically, cover vastly different knowledge systems and cultural subsystems, and are in general rarely used by the same social groups — and in the case of the Tantra they are not passed down solely by intellectual elites. Yet in both cases the audible symbols do exactly the same as graphical symbols: they allow abstraction.

One may be justified in doubting whether writing systems are an absolute requirement for systematic thought, abstractions and formal logical operations. The phonocentrically organized abstractions, i.e. audible symbolic representations used by early Indian scientists such as Pāṇini (grammarians), and also later ones such as Vararuci and Āryabhata (mathematicians and astronomers), show that there were also other possibilities of sign formation that were abstracting and symbolic. Yet we would agree with Goody that abstractions require sign codes and that it is not all the same what of type sign codes these are. The audible abstractions are an especially interesting phenomenon of the aesthetics of media practice: they combine abstraction and concretization/sensory participation — i.e. they unite two functions that Goody and Ong allocated to literacy in one place and to orality in another. In concrete practice both graphic and sounding abstractions socialize formalistic thought, but they each promote a different aesthetic sense. In general terms, (silent) reading texts and (spoken, sung or performed) audible texts provide different technologies and training grounds for forming subjectivity and culturality (“Trainingsorte für Subjekt- und Kulturformation”). The effects on the perceptive apparatus seem to be vastly different, depending on whether audible or graphical representations are used, as different sensory stimuli occupy the foreground and different senses are either especially strongly activated or not. This is consistent with Goody’s rationalism thesis, but takes it further and makes a decisive shift towards an an-
