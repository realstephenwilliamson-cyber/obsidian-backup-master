**What I Mean When I Say the Creed**[^1]

*A gloss*[^2] *on the Nicene Creed — 1979 Book of Common Prayer, Rite II*

Stephen Williamson

## Introduction

The Creed begins with an act of borrowing and that should be acknowledged before anything else. *We believe in one God* is the *Shema*[^3] in Greek dress. This borrowing goes much deeper than the opening line as Paul had already taken the verse apart in a letter to Corinth[^4] before any of the Gospels were written—giving *God* to the *Father* and *Lord* to *Jesus*.[^5] The nuances of the comparison go much deeper, however, and they are essential for understanding what follows.

One could say that the *Shema* is closer to what Judaism *is* than to what Judaism believes. That is so difficult to express in figures of speech that when the text says, *bind them on your hand, set them between your eyes, write them on the doorposts of your house*, the binding is literal[^6] and the rabbinic tradition takes it as such because nothing less was deemed sufficient. Children learn to say *Shema* first; it’s said upon waking, before sleeping—the dying say it last.

In fact, most religions, in antiquity or now, located their integrity in practice rather than assent. Given that he poured the libations and performed the sacrifices properly an ancient Roman or Greek could conceptualize Jupiter as an Olympian, a Stoic metaphor for the cosmic rational logos, an Epicurean being indifferent to humanity, or an allegory for what we now call Nature.[^7] Rites and even deities were largely determined by family and society. In much the same way Judaism’s communal bond was carried by descent, shared practices, cultural structures—what could be called identity.

The communities to which Paul was writing had few or none of those things in common—no longer Jew or Greek, slave and free[^8]—but something had to carry that bond. The shared confession of the Creed is, like saying the *Shema*, *kabbalat ol malchut shamayim*,[^9] accepting that yoke of the kingdom of heaven. So, for all their differences they are both in some sense constitutive of their traditions. Which is why the respectful borrowing at the start is not decoration. The *Shema* is the sentence Israel says to be Israel, and when the Church needed a sentence to say, it did not compose one. It reached for that one, in Greek, and went on from there because its own situation gave it little else to lean on — not because the sentence was insufficient for the people who had it. (The Church’s need for more words is a fact about the Church, not about the *Shema.)*

The comparison is also interesting regarding the meanings behind the words. The *Shema* has been turned over for centuries without anyone imagining that reciting it commits the reciter to a single account of what the words mean. In fact, two of history’s greatest Torah commentators, Rashi and Ibn Ezra,[^10] do not agree about what the word *echad*[^11] claims; nobody thinks this disqualifies either of them from saying it.[^12] While the Creed’s words were selected to exclude specific interpretations and mandate others, the ecumenical history shows that even for words chosen with such care, the saying is the act; the meaning is the work of a life, perhaps the work of centuries.

The last detail I want to highlight is not one of theology, but rather calligraphy. In a Kosher Torah scroll the letter *ayin* of the word *shema* and the letter *dalet* of the word *echad* are written larger than the surrounding letters. Together they spell *ed* — Hebrew for witness. The one speaking the words is giving a testimony from a perspective, and every perspective has its own.

In perspective, as testimony, every disclosure described below becomes legible only afterward—nothing can be run forward. The third day is read back into Hosea; the cross read back from the resurrection; the criterion I use to say what is unique about Christ rests on what happens after it is applied. But the directionality itself is perspectival; the actual motion is *spanda*[^13] — revealing and concealing in one simultaneous act. That we all must meet the pulse “from somewhere” is not a defect in the system but rather the nature of embodied existence. Nothing is added in the seeing. Recognition is of what was always the case.

Two key principles follow and everything here depends on them. First, the pattern is not a prediction. In other words, when I say the third day was already a threshold in Hosea, the earlier reading survives intact and a second instance is added to it; nothing is converted into a placeholder for a meaning its first readers could not have held. Sufficiency applies to reading as well as to salvation, and the texts are not IOUs. The second principle is the reiteration of perspective itself.

There are two ways to say that a thing looks different from different positions. Take the ascension. From where we stand, something left — a man went up and a place he had occupied is now empty. From the Word's side there is no interval and nothing was vacated, because nothing was ever set outside G-d to be returned to him. One way of handling that is to pick: the departure is how it seemed, the non-interval is how it was. That is the move I never make. The other way is to hold both as true and irreducible — the leaving is not an illusion corrected by the metaphysics, and the metaphysics is not a consolation offered to the leaving. I use the second throughout and the first nowhere.

# Part One · Of God

> *It is I who created the smith*
>
> *To fan the charcoal fire*
>
> *And produce tools for each purpose;*
>
> *So it is I who create*
>
> *The instruments of havoc.*

— [[Isaiah 54#^54-16|Isaiah 54:16]]

The Greek Fathers give us a vocabulary for what the Creed is doing in its own word order. *Theologia* is G-d as G-d is in himself. *Oikonomia* is G-d as G-d is toward us.[^14] It is a distinction of register rather than of subject matter. What it is not is a partition. There is no second thing.

The distinction governs inference, preventing one move in particular: reading what G-d *does* toward us back into what G-d *is*. The Son is sent, therefore the Son is less — that inference is the one the Fathers built the vocabulary to block, and the block still holds when the partition goes. Nothing true of the economy licenses a conclusion about rank in the theology. The bass cleft and the treble cleft are the same song.

So, the break in this document falls three words into the first sentence. Part One is *We believe in one God, the Father*. Part Two is everything from *the Almighty* to *the life of the world to come*. But *Almighty* is a threshold, and a threshold belongs to neither room. It is the first word in the Creed that presupposes a world without requiring one — you can hold your own hand, and the distinction is real. The imbalance between the parts is not an accident of proportion. The shorter part carries the premise; the longer one carries what follows from it.

And theology does not stop at the break, because it never left. The densest theology in the Creed is in its second sentence — *eternally begotten of the Father, God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, of one Being with the Father* — and that sentence is in Part Two. Everything in Part Two is both at once, and so is everything in Part One.[^15] The Son is not where theology returns. He is where the two stop being separable at all, and where it becomes impossible to go on reading them as two.

## I. Of God the Father

> *We believe in one God, the Father, the Almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all that is, seen and unseen.*

*We believe in one God,* (I.1)

"One"[^16] is not the exclusion of rival deities but of rival existents;[^17] as it is written, look unto me and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth, for I am God and there is none else.[^18] "None else" is ein od in the Hebrew, an ambiguity the text leaves open — no other god, or nothing further at all — and I read it the second way. There is no second thing, including the we or the I that is saying the Creed. Deuteronomy puts it more plainly still: *ein od milvado*, there is none besides him.[^19]

We should also say plainly the implication of what I have said already. If there is no second thing, then what I have described is a type of monism, and I do not dispute that. What it is not is pantheism, and the difference is not a technicality. Spinoza's G-d does not choose and could not have done otherwise.[^20] Nothing here works that way.

The Kabbalists give the reason, and they give it a name. G-d is Ein Sof, without end — not the largest, greatest, best thing; not a thing at all. A limit would require an outside for the limit to face, and there is no outside. Nothing else exists because there is nowhere else to be.

*the Father,* (I.2)

In G-d's self-reference, I AM,[^21] the One is the "I" and the Father is the "AM": that anything is at all. He is prakāśa in Sanskrit — radiant Being, traditionally figured as Divine Light. Light's visibility is not about brightness but contact, however there is nothing else, so the only thing this light can encounter is itself. Reflection is light meeting itself and returning at another angle. Refraction is light passing through itself and emerging prismatic. The Creed names both, in two clauses far apart: the Son is Light from Light, and the Spirit has spoken through the prophets.

Here we should slow down, because one light behaving in two ways is very close to a heresy called modalism: the belief that the Trinity is modes adopted and set aside by G-d for occasions. Two things keep my account from being that. Light does not reflect and then refract; it does both always and simultaneously (or at the speed of light). And the Son is not an adverb. He is the one who says I without borrowing it, as we will see below.

## I. Of God the Father (continued)

> *… the Almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all that is, seen and unseen.*

*the Almighty,* (I.3)

"Almighty," read as all-holding, already requires that the held be genuinely other than the holding, without requiring it to be a second existent — you can hold your own hand, and the distinction is real.

"Almighty" is the word most likely to be misheard. The Greek is *pantokratōr*, all-holding, as stated above. The Latin *omnipotens* behind "almighty" is where most modern arguments about omnipotence come from. Once that is seen, the Kabbalistic account of creation, *tzimtzum*, starts being a reading of almightiness and the withdrawal is not a reduction of it. Making room is what an embrace does in order to hold.[^24]

Which does not mean what they refer to is lesser, on the contrary, it makes them *svātantrya* — the Sanskrit for absolute freedom, the capacity to be otherwise without being diminished. It is the first thing the Creed says about G-d after saying that G-d is. This is also why I worship G-d, and why I think worship is not superfluous even for people who have the metaphysics and/or morals right without it.

*maker of heaven and earth,* (I.4)

> *\[Currently has no gloss on making at all — both its paragraphs were about Almighty and one has been moved. The Maker material is in footnote 15 and needs rewriting: bara used only of G-d survives; poieten as fashioner does not, since plasso is the fashioning verb and ou poiethenta at II.9 is this same word. Also the svatantrya paragraph, if its orphaned opening can be repaired.\]*

*of all that is,* (I.5)

"Of all that is, seen and unseen" is doing more than filling out the list. It says both halves are made, which is the specific denial the clause was written for: no one gets to assign the visible world to a lesser maker and keep the invisible for the real G-d.[^25]

Nothing else got in — no lesser craftsman, no flaw with an existence of its own, no obscuring substance requiring explanation. Whatever separates us from G-d is not a thing.[^26]

> *\[Both halves are made; no lesser craftsman, no flaw with an existence of its own; whatever separates us from G-d is not a thing. Keep the bare claim here and the Greek and the anti-Gnostic history in the note, not the reverse.\]*

*seen and unseen.* (I.6)

And read alongside what precedes it, the phrase says something further. The unseen is not more divine than the seen. There is no ladder of subtlety with matter at the bottom, no hierarchy in which the closer a thing comes to being invisible the closer it comes to G-d. Both are shining, and the shining is the same.

Finitude is not demotion. The crumbs beneath the table are the same bread — participation at a lower place is not participation in something lesser. What makes a bounded life a fallen one is not the boundedness but the forgetting.[^27]

> *\[No ladder of subtlety; both are shining and the shining is the same; finitude is not demotion; the crumbs are the same bread; the forgetting rather than the boundedness.\]*

## II. Of the Son

> *We believe in one Lord, Jesus Christ, the only Son of God, eternally begotten of the Father, God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, of one Being with the Father.*

*We believe in one Lord,* (II.1)

"Lord" is not a rank. It is the Name.[^28]

The Septuagint puts *Kyrios* wherever the Tetragrammaton stands, so this line is not promoting a man to an office. It is saying the Name over him.

> *\[“Lord” is not a rank, it is the Name; Kyrios for the Tetragrammaton.\]*

*Jesus Christ,* (II.2)

Which means the Creed has not moved on from the first clause. When Paul wrote to Corinth he gave *God* to the Father and *Lord* to Jesus, and I used to read that as dividing the Shema.[^29]

It is not a division. It is the same *I* said twice, in two registers. *One God* is that *I* said universally — of everything, from nowhere in particular. *One Lord* is the same *I* said particularly, of a man with a name and a genealogy and a date of death. Nothing has been split. Something has become sayable that was not.

> *\[Paul at Corinth; not a division of the Shema but the same I said twice, universally and particularly.\]*

*the only Son of God,* (II.3)

Because everyone is fully G-d, and that is not what distinguishes him. The prophet Hosea says *for I am God, and not man*, and it stands, because in the register he is speaking in it is as true of him as it is of anything.[^30]

But he cannot say it as Hosea. Hosea qua Hosea is not the speaker, and the text records this rather than hiding it: *thus says the LORD* is the attribution, and it is visible on the page. The prophetic formula marks which register is in use.

Balaam, the foreign diviner hired to curse Israel, says, *God is not a man, that he should lie*.[^31]

One speaker is inside the covenant and one is not, and it makes no difference to their register and between them there is no position left to occupy. Speaking the universal *I* requires stepping out of yourself, and it requires that of everyone, regardless of standing.

> *\[Everyone is fully God and that is not what distinguishes him; Hosea and Balaam; the register argument. “That is what monogenes names” ends here — it is currently stranded under Light from Light.\]*

*eternally begotten of the Father,* (II.4)

Prophecy is built that way. It requires the Spirit to rest on someone, and what rests can depart.[^32]

Two parties and a join between them, and the join is what the attribution names. The Son is not a place the Spirit arrives at, which is why nothing appears in front of his *I*.[^33]

*Ego eimi*, spoken to a woman at a well at noon and to a crowd in the temple, arrives with no attribution at all.[^34]

> *\[Prophecy requires the Spirit to rest and what rests can depart; the Son is not a place the Spirit arrives at; ego eimi with no attribution. The criterion is established here — including the [[Mark 13#^13-6|Mark 13:6]] objection and “what is unique is not access.” This is where the unification with the cruciform criterion has to be made, before V, VI and VII rely on it.\]*

*God from God,* (II.5)

> *\[NEW — this sub-clause has no heading and no commentary in the current draft, which is where the Section II offset came from. Something belongs here.\]*

*Light from Light,* (II.6)

The absence is the criterion, and it is a fact about the text rather than a claim about his inner life.

There is an objection built into the Gospels themselves, and it is better to raise it than to wait for it. He warns that many will come in my name saying *ego eimi*, and that they will deceive many. So the words can be said by anyone. They are two of the commonest in Greek, and a man can put them in a sentence without any of this being true of him.

<span class="mark">Which is why the criterion is not the phrase. It is the absence in front of it — and the impostors of that warning are precisely people who arrive with an attribution and conceal it. *In my name* is a borrowed authority hidden rather than declared: the prophetic formula run backwards, with the source suppressed instead of named. *Thus says the LORD* makes the join visible. Coming in someone's name and saying *I am* makes it invisible, and the deception is the concealment and not the words.</span>

So the warning confirms the test rather than defeating it. A borrowed I can be spoken. It cannot be spoken without there being something behind it, and the only question is ever whether the text shows it.

So what is unique is not access. Everyone has the *I*; the whole of the first clause says so. What is unique is that he says it without leaving off being himself — the universal spoken from inside a particular life, which is the one thing that had never happened. And this is why the *fully man* side is the side that matters. He bears agency and finite selfhood as we do, and we can recognize him because we are the same kind of thing. Not because he opened a door. Because he is standing where we are standing and saying what none of us can say from here.[^35]

That is what *monogenēs* names.[^36]

> *\[Vimarsa; reflection as the behavior of light rather than a mirror; apaugasma and Wisdom 7:26. Currently sitting under “true God from true God.”\]*

*true God from true God,* (II.7)

Not the clearest disclosure but the one where disclosure stopped being representation. He is not the only place the divine is. He is the place where it stopped needing anyone to carry it.

The Son is *vimarśa*: the self-knowing by which the light knows itself as light. Not a second light, and not a product of the first — the knowing that is inseparable from the shining and is not the shining.[^37]

Which is what "Light from Light" is saying, and the word for it is reflection, provided the picture is right. What comes to mind is a mirror, and a mirror gives a copy: something that resembles the original, arrives after it, and vanishes when the original leaves the room. That is not it, and *begotten, not made* exists to say so. The reflection meant here is the behavior of light itself — not an image of the source but the source becoming an image, no interval, the same light. Hebrews says *apaugasma*, radiance, and Wisdom before it calls her a reflection of eternal light. You cannot have the sun and wait for the brightness.[^38]

> *\[“Not the clearest disclosure but the one where disclosure stopped being representation” — true as against carried or represented.\]*

*begotten,* (II.8)

Begotten and not made is the difference between what shares the begetter's nature and what does not. The vocabulary is at work in Genesis before it is at work at Nicaea: Adam is made in the image of God, and then, as it is written, Adam fathered a son in his own likeness, after his image, and called his name Seth. Made and begotten, two verses apart, doing the work the Council would need them to do.[^39]

> *\[Begotten against made; Adam made in the image, Seth begotten in the likeness, two verses apart.\]*

*not made,* (II.9)

But image will not carry *homoousios*. An image is not what it images, and "like in substance" is the position Nicaea spent itself rejecting. Which is worth pausing over: this is the one clause in the Creed with no verse beneath it. The third day has Hosea; the right hand has the Psalm; the Spirit's speaking has the whole prophetic corpus. *Homoousios* has nothing. That is why it took a council rather than an exegesis.[^40]

> *\[Image will not carry homoousios; the one clause in the Creed with no verse beneath it. Note that ou poiethenta is the same verb as poieten at I.4 — this is where the verb architecture has to be faced rather than at I.\]*

*of one Being with the Father.* (II.10)

So I take the clause to be true of everyone. *Of one Being with the Father* applies to me. It applies to me as a verb and to Christ as a noun — not because we are made of different stuff, but because I am the same identity in the mode of coming-to-recognize, and he is the same identity that was never in that mode. Which is the last clause said again in another vocabulary: I can say the *I*, but only by stepping out of being myself to say it, and he does not have to.[^41]

That formula has to be read carefully, because the natural misreading would destroy everything else here. It does not mean that knowing changes what I am. Nothing in me changes when I know it. Knowing what you already are is not a change in what you are; it is a change in your relation to it. The rope was never a snake — I was never threatened, just afraid of my ignorance. There is no becoming in this clause. The ontology is flat. What is not flat is knowing.[^42]

And that is what makes his case different in kind rather than in degree. Recognition is by definition a recovery; the word carries the *re-* and cannot be said without it. Everyone who arrives, arrives back. He alone has nothing to recover, and that is not a higher grade of the same event. It is the absence of the event.[^43]

> *\[Applies to me as a verb and to Christ as a noun; the rope was never a snake; recognition as recovery and his as the absence of the event. The verb/noun distinction needs a paragraph, not a clause.\]*

## III. Of Creation

> *Through him all things were made.*

*Through him all things were made.* (III.1)

The first clause said the world is fashioned rather than produced. This one says how: G-d speaks, and what is spoken is.[^44]

*Let there be* is not a command issued to material standing ready. There is no material and no one to command. The utterance and the thing uttered are one act, which is why the Word is the agency of manifestation and not an instrument G-d picked up to accomplish something with.[^45]

Maximus gives this its most exact form.[^46]

Each thing has its *logos* — the word by which it is said, which is at once what it is and what it is for — and all of the *logoi* are held in the one Logos without being dissolved into him. So the world is not a copy of a plan, and it is not a crowd of separate items. It is one utterance, articulated. Which is the same claim the last clause made about reflection: the light knowing itself does not produce a second light, and the Word saying the world does not produce a second thing.

> *\[G-d speaks and what is spoken is; Let there be as one act; the logoi held in the Logos. The opening back-reference to “fashioned rather than produced” needs rebuilding — see II.9.\]*

## IV. Of Incarnation

> *For us and for our salvation he came down from heaven: by the power of the Holy Spirit he became incarnate from the Virgin Mary, and was made man.*

<span class="mark">And the two halves of this clause belong to one sentence.</span> <span class="mark">*Through him all things were made* — and then, without a break, *for us and for our salvation*. The tradition usually reads a full stop in there: creation first, and then a rescue mounted after something went wrong. But nothing went wrong in the fabric, as the first clause has already said, so there is nothing for a rescue to repair. Read continuously, the second phrase gives the purpose of the first. This is what everything was made for. Not made, and then salvaged. Made *for* the salvage, which turns out not to be salvage at all but a showing.</span>

*For us and for our salvation* (IV.1)

Salvation is knowledge. I mean that as flatly as it sounds, and I mean it in only one sense — that what saves is a seeing.[^47]

Nothing was opened at the Incarnation that had been closed. Nothing became true that had been false. What happened is that it became legible: available to be recognized by people standing where we stand, in a life shaped like ours.

So the phrase is not made available. It is shown to be available, and the difference is the whole account. If something had been made available, there would have been a transaction, and a price, and a moment before which it was not. There was no such moment. The clause says *for us*, and what it means is that the showing was for us — not that G-d required something and got it.[^48]

> *\[The two halves as one sentence — made for the showing, not made and then salvaged. Salvation is knowledge, and the Gnostic disclaimer.\]*

*he came down from heaven:* (IV.2)

Coming down is not the acquisition of distance. Nothing is set outside G-d, so there is nowhere to descend to in the spatial sense, and the descent is not a journey.[^49]

It is the *I* becoming locatable — arriving at a standpoint, which is the one thing the universal has no way of being from where it already is.

John says it with a verb worth noticing. *The Word became flesh and dwelt among us* is what the English gives, and the Greek is *eskēnōsen* — he tented, he pitched a *skēnē*.[^50]

Behind the Greek is the Hebrew *sukkah*, the booth, and behind that is the tabernacle in the wilderness, which was a tent because the people were moving and G-d went where they went. So the Incarnation is not described as an arrival at a residence, but rather a shelter, for a sojourning people.

Which means the machinery of this clause is the machinery of the second, stated the other way round. There I said that speaking the universal *I* requires stepping out of yourself. Here is what it takes for that not to be necessary: someone has to be there, at a point, with a life, and be the one saying it.

The Sanskrit gives me three names for what a standpoint costs, and I need all three because the Creed's own word — *made man* — is stronger than we usually hear it.[^51]

The tradition calls them impurities, which is misleading if it suggests damage. They are conditions. *Kārma* is agency: being an actor whose deeds have consequences, who can be tried under a named official. *Āṇava* is finite selfhood: being a bounded someone, at a location, who can be addressed and can be left. And *māyīya* is the perception of real separateness — a world of genuinely other things, and oneself as genuinely one of them.

He takes the first two entire. He is an agent in history and he is a bounded self, and neither of those is a costume. What he does not take is the third, and everything in this account turns on that.[^52]

Because the third is where the trouble is, and not because it is a substance. I said at the first clause that the forgetting is not a thing — not an ingredient added to a life but the shape of standing at a point. Here is what that means precisely: a standpoint plus the perception of real separateness is the deception. Nothing arrives from outside to produce it. That is why the same conditions can be innocent in him and fatal in us. He has a location and no illusion about what a location is.

So he is bounded without being fooled by the bound, and it is explicitly different from what Sanskrit calls an *avatāra*, pagan or otherwise. And this makes him more human rather than less. He has the finite standpoint genuinely — which is why Gethsemane is a real recoil, and why the cry from the cross is not theater.[^53]

A recoil that costs nothing is not a recoil. One can will that a people be free, not will the war that freedom requires, and pursue it anyway; three acts, not one equivocation, and the pursuit costs something only because the second is real.[^54]

> *\[Coming down is not distance; eskenosen, the tent, the mishkan. The I becoming locatable.\]*

*by the power of the Holy Spirit he became incarnate from the Virgin Mary,* (IV.3)

From the Virgin Mary I skipped for years.[^55]

The virginity was easy to skip because it was never important to me: if G-d wanted to dwell in the world as a human, a miracle to get there seemed inconsistent. What lasted was the particularity — that a universal has to come out of one woman in one village. I now think the particularity is the whole point. A standpoint is not a general property. It is somewhere, and something has to put him at Nazareth rather than everywhere, and that is what *from* does. Her tissue, her genealogy, her mortality. The flesh is entirely hers.

The clause I skipped turns out to be the one holding the rest in place.

On the virginity itself I remain agnostic.[^56]

The weight sits in *from*, not in *virgin*, which is roughly where Paul left it: born of a woman, born under the law. What the doctrine does secure, whatever its historical status, is that no second person came into existence for the Word to join himself to. There is one subject here, and the humanity is his rather than someone else's.[^57]

> *\[The particularity and the weight on “from”; agnosticism on the virginity; [[Isaiah 07#^7-14|Isaiah 7:14]] and why the pattern is not the prediction — though that rule is now stated in the Introduction and this can point back to it.\]*

*and was made man.* (IV.4)

> *\[Currently empty. Footnote 38 — enanthropesanta against Apollinaris, a complete human being, mind and will included — is this sub-clause’s note and is presently attached at IV.2. The three malas are defined here, once, and VIII refers back.\]*

## V. Of the Crucifixion

> *For our sake he was crucified under Pontius Pilate; he suffered death and was buried.*

*For our sake* (V.1)

*For our sake* is the fourth clause again, and the Creed is repeating itself on purpose. It has already said that the making was for us and for our salvation. Now it says that this was too — and if the two phrases mean the same thing, then what happens here is not a repair introduced after a failure. It is what the making was for.[^58]

> *\[The fourth clause repeated on purpose; no debt, because nothing was owed.\]*

*he was crucified* (V.2)

Historically, the only account that makes sense to me is that Rome executed him for sedition. Crucifixion was a Roman penalty for revolt and not a punishment Judean authorities could impose; the charge on the placard was kingship; *my kingdom is not from this world* is not a denial of kingship but a denial of jurisdiction, offered to the man who had it.[^59]

I am not going to be delicate about the rest of it. The crowd scenes did not happen as the Gospels describe them, and the reasons are not subtle. Matthew's *all the people* is not a mob in the street: the word is *laos*, which in the Greek Bible is the covenant word for Israel, not *ochlos*, the word for a crowd — and the shift is deliberate, made by an author writing after the destruction of the Temple.[^60]

The custom of releasing a prisoner at the feast is attested nowhere outside the Gospels, and no Roman governor is known to have held such a practice.[^61]

And the prisoner has a name that gives the scene away. A body of manuscripts reads *Jesus Barabbas*, and Origen knew copies that had it and was uneasy about them. *Barabbas* is *bar abba*, son of the father. So the choice put to the crowd is between Jesus son of the father and Jesus called Messiah — the same name twice, one released and one condemned, which is the doubling the Day of Atonement already had with its two goats.

<span class="mark">I note it because the pattern is elegant, and elegance is the tell. What is doing the work in that courtyard is theology. The crowd is furniture.</span>

> *\[Rome executed him for sedition; the placard as charge sheet; stoning as the Sanhedrin’s penalty. The Barabbas material — rebuilt on the manuscript evidence rather than on elegance.\]*

*under Pontius Pilate;* (V.3)

*Under Pontius Pilate* does for the death what the genealogy does for the birth. It puts him somewhere. A standpoint is a where and also a when, and this clause supplies the second in the Roman manner rather than the Hebrew one — not a line of fathers but a magistrate's term of office, the way an empire records that a thing occurred.[^62]

And the Pilate of Philo and Josephus is a man notorious for executions without trial, eventually recalled to Rome for brutality, and he does not vacillate in a courtyard while being talked into something by the people he was appointed to hold down.[^63]

And the blood taken upon themselves and their children is a legal formula about responsibility, put into the mouths of an entire people by a writer who already knew what happened in the year seventy.[^64]

> *\[A when in the Roman manner; Philo and Josephus on Pilate; the privilegium paschale; the blood formula and [[Matthew 23#^23-1|Matthew 23]] as intra-Jewish argument.\]*

*he suffered death* (V.4)

It is not testimony. It is a sentence that has cost more Jewish lives than any other in the New Testament, and everyone who says this Creed should be aware of that.

And I should say the other half of it, because leaving it out turns Matthew into an antisemite and he is not one. Chapter twenty-three is the hardest chapter in the Gospel — seven woes against the scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites, blind guides, whited sepulchres — and it is not an outsider's polemic. It is a Jewish argument conducted in Jewish terms by a writer inside the community he is flaying, in the register the prophets used on Israel and which Israel preserved in its own scripture. Amos says worse. Jeremiah says worse. The Qumran sectarians say worse about the Jerusalem priesthood, and nobody reads the Damascus Document as gentile hatred.

What changed is the readership. A family quarrel became a foreign indictment the moment the church stopped being Jewish, and the sentences did not have to be altered to do it — they only had to be read by people standing outside the argument. Chapter twenty-three is what tells you what the crowd scene is: the same author, the same fury, the same claim on a people he takes to be his own.

That does not make the passage safe. It makes it a text whose danger is a function of who is holding it, which is a different problem from a text that is wicked, and a harder one, because it cannot be fixed by deleting a verse.

His finiteness is not complete until he dies. Everything else could be a costume; mortality is not. Which is why the Creed uses three verbs where one would do — crucified, suffered, buried — and the middle one is the one that matters.[^65]

And at the cross the third condition arrives. He had agency and he had a bounded self, and what he had never had was the perception of real separateness. There it comes, and the compound forms: a standpoint, plus the seeming of genuine separation, which is what the deception is and requires nothing to be added from outside. This is what the tradition means when it says he takes on the sin of the world, and what Paul means when he says God made him to be sin who knew no sin.[^66]

> *\[The middle of three verbs; his finiteness not complete until he dies; the third condition arriving and the compound forming.\]*

*and was buried.* (V.5)

Not a penalty transferred. The compound itself, formed in the one place it had never formed.

Why hast thou forsaken me is that, spoken.[^67]

Which is the same sentence I have said elsewhere in this document about myself, and I want to put the two together rather than leave them in separate clauses. Where the Creed says *begotten, not made*, I said the rope was never a snake — that I was never threatened, only afraid of my ignorance. This is that again, applied to another condition. He was never separated. Nothing was withdrawn, because there is nowhere for the light to go. What arrived was the standpoint from which separation is what the situation looks like, and the fear of a nothing is entire and costs everything. It is not a lapse in the knowing and it is not a false belief about a fact.[^68]

It is the place from which he and I both cry out.

The difference is direction. Every recognition that has ever happened runs upward: the compound felt from inside, and then seen through. His runs downward — the uncompounded takes the compound on, knowing exactly what it is, from the position of never having needed to. Everyone else meets the fear on the way up. He met it on the way in.[^69]

Nothing is paid here. What the death does is show, and the showing begins in the dark and is not finished in it. The centurion sees something. The curtain of the Holy of Holies — the architecture of separation — tears at the moment separation is undergone. The followers see nothing at all. Recognition starts in the lowest available place, in the mouth of the man with least standing to speak, and completes elsewhere.[^70]

Which raises the question of whose Gospel this clause belongs to, and it is not the one people assume.

John is the book of open declaration. *Ego eimi* is said in the temple, in daylight, to crowds, and the discourses explain themselves at length; I have leaned on John throughout this document for exactly that reason. But John is the exoteric gospel. Everything in it is said out loud.

Mark is the other thing. In Mark the demons know and are silenced, the healed are told to say nothing, the three are forbidden to report the transfiguration, and the disciples understand nothing from the first chapter to the last — recorded so relentlessly that it has to be the design. He explains privately to his own and speaks in parables to everyone else, so that seeing they may see and not perceive. And at the end, in the oldest text we have, there is no appearance at all: an empty tomb, a young man, and women who said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid.

That is an esoteric gospel in the exact sense — a book organised around withholding a disclosure until the conditions for receiving it exist. And it is the book in which this clause is at home, because the condition turns out to be the death. No one is permitted to say who he is until he is dead, and then the first person who says it is a Roman soldier with no training, no covenant, and no prior information, who has watched a man die badly.

<span class="mark">The secret is not kept because the information is dangerous. It is kept because it is not information. Nothing said earlier could have been received, and the same sentence is false in the mouth of a demon on the road and true in the mouth of the centurion at the foot of the cross. Which is what I have been arguing about disclosure the whole way through, worked out first by a man who put it as narrative structure and did not explain it.</span>

> *\[Why hast thou forsaken me; the direction of recognition, his running downward; the curtain; the centurion. Mark as the esoteric gospel — and the second criterion, which II.4 now has to have accounted for.\]*

## VI. Of the Resurrection and Ascension

> *On the third day he rose again in accordance with the Scriptures; he ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father.*

*On the third day he rose again* (VI.1)

*On the third day* belongs with *in accordance with the Scriptures*. It is one clause, not a date followed by a citation, and Paul cites the formula writing to Corinth as something he received, which makes it among the earliest Christian sentences we have.[^71]

The third day in the rabbinic pattern is a threshold. Three days before Sinai, three days to the mountain of the Akedah, three days in the fish, three days before Esther enters the throne room; proximity to death in all of these is incidental.[^72]

The pattern is not about revival. It is about an interval after which something becomes possible that was not. A readiness to receive is what the third day names.

> *\[The third day as threshold rather than revival; Sinai, the Akedah, Jonah, Esther; [[Hosea 06#^6-1|Hosea 6:1–2]] as the load-bearing text.\]*

*in accordance with the Scriptures;* (VI.2)

What he shed at the cross was the compound — a standpoint plus the seeming of real separation, which is what the deception consists of and is the only thing he ever took on that we would call sin. He did not keep it. What rises is the same man with the conditions and without the deception: a bounded self, an agent in history, and nothing in him that mistakes a location for a separation.

Which is what Paul is describing when he sets down his caveats about the resurrected body — sown perishable and raised imperishable, sown a natural body and raised a spirit-animated one, and flesh and blood not inheriting the kingdom.[^73]

He says all of that about our rising, and he says it because of this one. But the change he is describing is not a change of material; the scars are still in the flesh. There has been no substitution.

> *\[What he shed was the compound; Paul’s caveats on the risen body; no change of material, the scars still in the flesh.\]*

*he ascended into heaven* (VI.3)

And that is why the appearances go the way they do. He is locatable, touchable — and not recognized, mistaken for a gardener or stranger on the road. Mary turns only when he says her name. The pair on the road walk with him for miles, their eyes held, and know him in the breaking of bread, at which point he vanishes. The recognition was the entire purpose of the appearing. Even Thomas is not satisfied by his eyes and must put his hand in the wound.[^74]

The body is continuous, which is what the scars are for, and it is not the same, which is why nobody knows him.

So there are two things here and not one. Something happened to him, and something happened in them, and the second does not follow automatically from the first and yet I assert both.

Enoch walked with God and was not, for God took him; Elijah went up in the whirlwind; and the throne visions of Ezekiel became a whole literature of ascent in which the body is the difficulty to be survived rather than something left behind.[^75]

> *\[The appearances and the non-recognitions; Mary at the name, the road, the breaking of bread, Thomas at the wound; the Jewish ascent literature.\]*

*and is seated at the right hand of the Father.* (VI.4)

A human being taken up is a Jewish expectation before it is a Christian claim. The novelty is the most innocent word in the clause: seated. Four rabbis went up into the orchard, and the one who came out with his faith destroyed came out because he saw someone sitting down.[^76]

And he is seated. The humanity is not shed, the scars are not a stage, and Chalcedon's *without division, without separation* does not expire. Which is where the first clause was always going: finitude is not demotion, and the crumbs beneath the table are the same bread. A standpoint is not a costume worn for thirty years and returned. It is retained, and it is retained by G-d.[^77]

Which raises a question I have been putting off, and the ascension is where it has to be answered, because a retained humanity seated in G-d is either for everyone or it is a Christian possession.

The Jewish revelation is sufficient for Jews. I want that sentence to stand without softening, and nothing in this document requires me to withdraw it. Sinai is not a preliminary and the covenant is not a rehearsal. What gentiles lacked was not a covenant but a legible one — no Name said over them, no scroll, no seventy elders on the mountain, no formula on the page marking which register was in use.

<span class="mark">the *mitzvot* persisting in *olam ha-ba* is the only thing that defends 22 against being read as supersessionism with a delay, and it isn't anywhere in the document. That's an addition at 24 or at XIII, not an edit here.</span>

One mechanism, two routes of access. The mechanism is the one I have been describing since the second clause: the universal I, said. The routes differ only in what makes it readable. To Israel it is legible by attribution — *thus says the LORD*, and a people trained across centuries to hear where the join is. To everyone else it became legible in a man, at a date, under a magistrate, with nothing standing in front of him at all.

So I do not think the second route supersedes the first. A second door into a house is not a judgment on the first door. And it is also why the mission goes where it goes: to the nations, because the nations are the ones who could not read.

> *\[Seated is the novelty; the pardes and Acher; the humanity retained and retained by G-d. Then sufficiency — one mechanism, two routes — which is where the attribution problem has to be resolved rather than assumed. The mitzvot argument is not here; it is at XIII.\]*

## VII. Of the Coming

> *He will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead, and his kingdom will have no end.*

*He will come again in glory* (VII.1)

The coming is not travel, we have seen that it cannot be. I said at the first clause that nothing is set outside G-d, so there is no distance for anyone to cross and nowhere to cross it from. The Greek Old Testament turns out to agree: G-d's advent there is a matter of shining, of becoming visible, of a presence already present being seen.[^78]

*In glory* is not decoration. Glory renders *kavod*, the visible weight of a presence, and it is the operative word. What is promised is a visibility increased and not a distance closed.

> *\[The coming is not travel; parousia against epiphaneia; kavod as visible weight. A visibility increased, not a distance closed.\]*

*to judge the living and the dead,* (VII.2)

The coming is *to judge*, and the second word has taken as much damage in English as the first. What the Greek says is *krisis*, which is not the passing of a sentence but a separation, a sifting, a discernment — the point at which a thing becomes clear rather than the point at which it is punished. Our own word still has some of it: a crisis is when you find out.

So judgment on this reading is scribal: the recording and manifesting of what is already the case. Nothing is decided. Something becomes visible — which makes judgment and advent the same event described twice, once by what it does and once by how it happens.

And the dead are judged, which is to say the dead are shown. I have no separate account of the descent into hell and do not need one. There is nowhere he is absent from, including there, and if judgment is unavoidable seeing then it is not a rescue mounted into a sealed place. It is the same light arriving where everyone assumed it could not.[^79]

Which is where I owe Pharaoh an answer. I said at *he has spoken through the prophets* that he was held upright in order to be addressed, and that I could not yet say what it means that the address can fail.

Here is what I can say. If judgment is *krisis* and not sentence, then failing to see is not a verdict handed down but a condition that persists — and a condition that persists is not a condition that has ended. Pharaoh in the sea is a man at a moment. I said at the first clause that nothing is set outside G-d, which forecloses any account in which he is finally elsewhere. The hardening bought him a position from which seeing was possible; he did not see; and the light did not stop.

That is less than an answer and it is what the reading permits. What I refuse is the version where the address was never real — where he was made a puppet so that the plagues could proceed. Ramban's whole point is that it was real. A real address that goes unanswered is a worse problem than a rigged one, and I would rather have the worse problem.

There is a figure at the right hand whose entire function is accusation, and I have to say something about him, because I have just described the judgment as a clerical act and he is the other clerk.

*Ha-satan* in the Hebrew Bible is not a name. It has the definite article. It is a job — the accuser, the adversary — and in Job he is a member of the council doing that job, and in Zechariah he stands at the right hand of the high priest to accuse him, and is rebuked.[^80]

Standing, on the right, presenting a case.

Yes, I understand that I have just put Christ and the accuser in the same posture, side by side, approaching the same bench. Hear me out, because the resemblance is the point and not an accident. Neither of them decides anything. One records the merits and one presents the charges, and both are disclosure — which is what judgment is. Nothing is settled at that throne. Things are made visible there, and the accuser is not wrong about the facts. He is rebuked for the register, not for the evidence.

Which is the same thing I said about forgiveness. What is let go is the compound and not the terms — nothing is cancelled and no account is settled, because there was never a ledger. So the charges can be entirely accurate and it makes no difference. There is nothing to acquit. The case is real and the verdict was never his to want.

The wilderness belongs here, and not where it is usually put. We read the temptation as seduction — an offer made to an appetite, the interest being whether he wants it. That is not the form of the scene. Twice the sentence begins *if thou be the Son of God*, which is not an enticement but a question put to a witness; and the third exchange is not a question at all but an offer of jurisdiction, made by the one figure in the story whose office is to test a claim in the presence of the court.

Read that way the forty days are a cross-examination, and the examiner is doing his job. He is testing what this document has been testing: whether the I is his or borrowed. *Command that these stones be made bread* asks him to demonstrate the claim on demand, which is what a borrowed authority would do, having something to prove. *Cast thyself down* asks him to make the Father act, which would establish the relation as external. *All these things will I give thee* offers him the Name conferred — the world held by grant, from someone, which is precisely Metatron's position and precisely what Arius held.

He declines all three, and the declining is not resistance to pleasure. It is a refusal to produce a credential. Every answer is a verse and none is a demonstration, and a man who had to prove it would have proved it.

So the accuser and the accused meet twice in the same posture, at the beginning and at the end, and the examination goes the same way both times. Which is why I do not need the wilderness to be a struggle in order to keep it serious. A cross-examination conducted honestly against a true witness is a real event and produces nothing, and producing nothing is the finding.

And there is only one figure at that throne who is seated, which brings back the seated figure from the ascension. The parallel is exact and so is the difference. Metatron holds the Name because it was placed in him — Exodus says *my name is in him*, and a name placed in someone is conferred, held by grant, and therefore capable of being withdrawn.[^81]

That is a borrowed *I*, as discussed at "he has spoken through the prophets." Christ does not hold G-d's Name; he is G-d's Word.[^82]

Same throne, different grammar, and the Jewish option that was ruled out and the Christian claim that was made are the same shape seen from two sides.

I should be plain that this is where the most serious danger in this document sits.[^83]

An exalted creature granted the divine Name is precisely what Arius held, in another vocabulary, and nothing in my account rules it out except the criterion. The Name in Metatron is *in* him. Nothing stands in front of the *I* at the well.

> *\[Krisis as sifting and not sentence; judgment as scribal; the dead shown; Pharaoh answered. Ha-satan as office; the wilderness as cross-examination; Metatron, and the Arian door closed — with enhypostasia, not only with the grammatical criterion.\]*

*and his kingdom will have no end.* (VII.3)

*His kingdom will have no end* was added against a man who thought otherwise.[^84]

Marcellus held that the Logos goes out for the economy and contracts back into the Father when everything is finished — an eschatology with a terminus, in which the arrangement completes and the distinctions fold up. The clause exists to say no. Which is convenient for me, since I do not have a terminus either, though for a different reason.

Because no one is permanently damned. Universal restoration is the conclusion and we will never reach it — not because it fails but because it is infinite, and arrival would be a smaller thing than approach.[^85]

Which is why the coming and the never-arriving were never in tension. The light is always more, and that is the same sentence as the one about the advent: a visibility increased, with no terminus, because there is no distance left to close and nothing to arrive at that is not already here.

As to whether anyone is damned at a given moment: I do not think I am, most of the time, and I cannot answer for anyone else.

> *\[Marcellus; universal restoration as asymptotic; epektasis applied to the end, claimed as an extension of Gregory rather than a quotation of him.\]*

## VIII. Of the Spirit

> *We believe in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father and the Son.*

*We believe in the Holy Spirit,* (VIII.1)

The two Lords bothered me from childhood and the difficulty is real rather than childish. I have already said what I now think it is. Paul, writing to Corinth, gave *God* to the Father and *Lord* to Jesus, and that is not a division of the Shema but the same *I* said twice, universally and then particularly.[^86]

Which accounts for the Son. It leaves the Spirit with neither word, and the Creed never calls him God.

What he gets instead is a stack of things only G-d does, and a title that is not quite the Son's.[^87]

> *\[The two Lords; the Creed never calls him God; a stack of things only G-d does.\]*

*the Lord,* (VIII.2)

The strategy was to establish equality without using the contested word, and it worked, and it left the Spirit characterized entirely by function, which seems to be the point. Every predicate the Creed gives him is intransitive or passive: proceeds, is worshipped, is glorified, has spoken. This is the Person who does not act. Poured out rather than obligated; present rather than agentive; and the one active thing said of him — that he gives life — is a participle.

And that is what I find in every place he appears. The breath in which an utterance happens. The presence distributed as tongues of fire, one per head.[^88]

The presence that goes into exile with the people.[^89]

Not a lesser Spirit.

> *\[To kyrion, the Lordly one; every predicate intransitive or passive; the Person who does not act; Shekhinah into exile.\]*

*the giver of life,* (VIII.3)

*The giver of life* is both manifestation as such and the animation of creatures, and they are one act, because a word cannot be spoken without breath. As it is written: by the word of the LORD the heavens were made, and by the breath of his mouth all their host.[^90]

Through him all things were made said that G-d speaks and what is spoken is. This clause says what the speaking is made of.

> *\[Manifestation and animation as one act; no word without breath; [[Psalms 033#^33-6|Psalm 33:6]]; zoopoion.\]*

*who proceeds from the Father and the Son.* (VIII.4)

Which is also why I hold the *filioque*, and not as a Western political addition.[^91]

There is no word without breath and no breath shaped into speech without a word. The two are not sequential and neither is a product of the other. The Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son because the utterance is one.

And *proceeds* is the third of the Creed's three verbs.[^92]

The world is fashioned, the Son is begotten, the Spirit proceeds — and proceeding is the one with no product at the end of it. Nothing results from a breath going out. It is the going out.

One more thing belongs here, at the end of three clauses about who G-d is, and it is a piece of architecture rather than an argument.

Kashmir Shaivism names three conditions of embodied existence — the *malas*, usually translated impurities, which misleads if it suggests damage. *Kārma* is agency: being an actor whose deeds have consequences. *Māyīya* is the perception of real separateness: a world of genuinely other things, and oneself as one of them. *Āṇava* is finite selfhood: being a bounded someone, at a location, who can be addressed and can be left.

Each of the three clauses I have just written answers two of them, and each condition is answered twice.

The Father answers *kārma* and *māyīya*. *Maker of heaven and earth* is agency without consequence — a fashioner rather than a producer, whose act does not stand apart from him and therefore incurs nothing. And *one God* is *māyīya* refused at the root: not the exclusion of rival deities but of rival existents, so that separateness is exactly what is not the case. What he does not answer is *āṇava*, because there is no boundary in him to answer it with.

The Spirit answers *māyīya* and *āṇava*. He is the medium in which what is distinguished is not divided — the flame that rests on each of them separately without being portioned — which is separateness undone in the one place it looks undeniable, and boundedness inhabited without being confined. What he does not answer is *kārma*, and the Creed is careful about it: the Spirit is nowhere said to make anything. He is *the giver of life*, which is a different verb.

The Son answers *kārma* and *āṇava*, and answering them is the same as taking them. An agent in history, tried under a named official; a bounded self at a location, who can be addressed and can be left. *Māyīya* he does not take, which is the whole of the clause on the Incarnation — until the cross, where it arrives, and that is the sixth clause and not this one.

The pattern is not decoration. Set out that way, every condition is met twice and by different means, which is why I keep saying these are conditions and not defects. A defect is something to be removed. A condition met from three directions is a place to stand.

> *\[The filioque held on the grammar of utterance rather than as a Western addition; proceeds as the verb with no product. The mala architecture — referring back to IV.4, not redefining.\]*

## IX. Of Worship

> *With the Father and the Son he is worshiped and glorified.*

*With the Father and the Son he is worshiped and glorified.* (IX.1)

The Greek is co-worshipped, and it was fought for.[^93]

Worship is owed to G-d alone, and the word was chosen against exactly the reading that would make the Spirit's worship secondary — not veneration alongside adoration, but the same thing given to all three.

Which used to trouble me, because it looked like division. I took the *with* as modal: that the Spirit is worshipped *by means of* the Father and the Son. I no longer see that thanks to Basil's formula — in the Spirit, through the Son, to the Father.[^94]

Not a lesser object of devotion but the medium of it. And there is nothing here to divide in any case: worship of three Persons is not divided worship if there is one being, and there is.

> *\[Synproskynoumenon and why the modal reading of the English “with” is unavailable in the Greek; Basil’s doxological grammar. No comma to divide on — either leave undivided or state the principle that short sentences do not break.\]*

## X. Of Prophecy

> *He has spoken through the Prophets.*

*He has spoken through the Prophets.* (X.1)

I said there that the light has nothing to meet but itself, and that it does two things when it does — reflects, and refracts. The Son is *Light from Light*, which is reflection: the source becoming an image with no interval. This is the other one. Refraction is what makes one beam into a spectrum, and it is the only way light becomes many without becoming less.

*He has spoken through the prophets.* Many mouths, one word, and the plural is not incidental.[^95]

Every prophet is a color, and a color is genuinely the light and genuinely not the whole of it, which is why prophetic speech is true *and* borrowed at once. The Spirit rests on someone, and what rests can depart, and the resting is what the attribution names.[^96]

*Thus says the LORD* is the facets of the prism, made visible on the page.

So this clause and the second explain each other, and I did not arrange that. The Son has no attribution because he is not a place the Spirit arrives at. The prophets have one because they are.

And the recipients of prophecy are inside this too, not outside it. Balaam is a hired diviner and the spirit of God comes upon him. Saul falls in among the prophets and it becomes a proverb. Pharaoh receives signs whose stated purpose is that he shall know.[^97]

Pharaoh needs more than a half-sentence, and I will give him what I can here and return to it at the judgment.

The difficulty is famous. G-d says he will harden Pharaoh's heart, and does, and then punishes what follows, which looks like a rigged trial. Ramban's answer is the one I take. For the first five plagues the text says Pharaoh hardened his own heart; the hardening G-d supplies comes later, and what it supplies is not compulsion but capacity. A man broken by successive catastrophes will release slaves to make the catastrophes stop, and that release is worth nothing and means nothing. The hardening restores him to a position from which a decision is still his. It holds him upright long enough to be addressed rather than merely worn down.

Which is this clause's picture of prophecy, applied to a man outside the covenant and unwilling. The stated purpose of the signs is not compliance. It is repeated until it cannot be missed: that the Egyptians shall know. Pharaoh is not the obstacle to the disclosure. He is an audience of it, and hardening him is what keeps him a possible recipient instead of a broken instrument.

So the refusals are inside the spectrum and not outside it. Balaam is hired to curse and prophesies; Pharaoh is hardened and is addressed; and neither has to consent in order to be a place where the light lands. What I cannot yet say is what it means that the address can fail — that a man can be held upright for the express purpose of seeing, and not see. That belongs at the judgment, and I will take it up there.

A medium does not stop at the speaker's mouth, and if refraction is the right picture then there is no position outside the spectrum to stand in — which is a claim I will need at the end of this document and cannot yet fully defend.

One caution about what all this makes possible. Balaam had a particular gift: the midrash says he could tell when G-d was angry. Not a message received but a disposition read, and the joke of the story is that the technique was perfect and the week was wrong.[^98]

The man who could detect divine anger at a distance could not see the angel standing in the road. The donkey could. Legibility is not information, and a spectrum can be read by someone who does not know he is looking at light.

> *\[Refraction; many mouths and one word; every prophet a color; thus says the LORD as the facets made visible. Balaam, Saul, Pharaoh and Ramban on the hardening; the donkey and the seer. The opening back-reference now points to I.2 and should say so.\]*

## XI. Of the Church

> *We believe in one holy catholic and apostolic Church.*

*We believe in one holy catholic and apostolic Church.* (XI.1)

The four words have to be read together, and the congregation they describe is humanity.

It is one, and its holiness is a claim about that unity resembling G-d — set apart not from anything, since there is nothing to be set apart from, but as what difference itself constitutes. Holy therefore universal: *kath' holou*, according to the whole.[^99]

Sentness in the widest sense is individuality itself. You are sent to G-d from G-d, and the message you carry is who you are. Each thing's principle in G-d is at once what it is and what it is for, so that being *this* and being *for* are not two facts about a person but one.[^100]

Which is what the second clause was about, arriving here in a different form. The universal *I* can be said from a standpoint or it can be said from nowhere in particular, and everyone but one person has to leave off being themselves to say it. What a standpoint is *for* is that there be somewhere to say it from. This is why the ninth clause could call the Spirit distributed without calling him diminished, and it is why the first clause could say finitude is not demotion. A particular is not a portion of the universal. It is where the universal has a mouth.

I should put down here what I am actually thinking when I say this clause, since the whole document is an attempt to say that and this is the place it becomes sayable.

The Trika gives three: *Śiva*, *Śakti*, *Nara*. Awareness; the power by which it appears as anything; and the bound human being. The temptation is to line them up against the Persons, and the temptation should be refused — it does not work, and where it half-works it does damage. *Śakti* is not the Spirit, and *Nara* has no Person to correspond to at all, which is the useful fact.

It is not a rival Trinity. It is a second axis, crossing the first. The Creed's three are G-d's own life said from inside — *theologia*, the relations that are what G-d is apart from anything else being the case. These three are the encounter: awareness, the medium in which it appears, and the one to whom it appears. The first axis has no term for me. The second is nothing but the terms of my being here.

And they meet in this clause. *One holy catholic and apostolic Church*, read as I have read it, is the doctrine of *Nara* — the human being as such, sent, individual, and not a portion of anything. The congregation is humanity because *Nara* is where the second axis touches down, and a mission is what a standpoint is for.

Which is why I do not experience the two as competing when I say the Creed. I am saying the first axis and standing in the second, and the standing is not a private supplement to the words. It is the position from which the words are the case at all.

In the narrower sense, apostolic means carriage rather than attainment: those who were there and were sent.[^101]

Both senses are true and neither reduces to the other — every human being is a mission, and some people walked to Antioch.

That the two do not reduce is the whole of it. The universal sense is not the truth of which the particular one is an appearance. If it were, the twelve would be a costume the universal put on, and the walking to Antioch would not have happened to anyone.[^102]

And this is where the two kinds of witness belong. The church needs Thomas, who required the wound, and Paul, who never met the man and saw him anyway. They are in the same list in the same letter, and neither is the concession.[^103]

There is one more thing in this clause and I did not put it there. The Shema is written in the scroll with two letters enlarged — the *ayin* of *shema* and the *dalet* of *echad* — and together they spell *ed*, witness. To say it is to be made one. Which is the whole of what I mean by apostolic in the wide sense, said by a tradition that is not mine, in a text I recite in translation every week without knowing I am doing it.

> *\[The four words read together and the congregation as humanity; kath’ holou; sentness as individuality; Siva, Sakti, Nara as a second axis crossing the first; Thomas and Paul as two kinds of witness. The ed material lands here — stated as a return, since the Introduction has already given it.\]*

## XII. Of Baptism

> *We acknowledge one baptism for the forgiveness of sins.*

*We acknowledge* (XII.1)

The verb changes here and nowhere else.

Everything above this line is *we believe*. This line is *we acknowledge* — *homologoumen*, which is literally same-saying: to say the same thing as. In the Greek Bible it is the word for confessing sins, for confessing Christ before others, and for public assent to something already the case. You acknowledge a fact you are aligning yourself with. You believe a proposition you are holding.[^104]

I have always felt the shift and never had a name for it. The tone changes in the room too, or I have always thought so — the congregation is doing something slightly different for one sentence, and then goes back.

> *\[The verb changes here and nowhere else; homologoumen as same-saying; the tone in the room. This is where ed and witness do their work.\]*

*one baptism* (XII.2)

What the sentence asks is narrower than it looks. Not what baptism effects, not who should receive it, not how it works. That there is one, and that it is the same one everywhere.[^105]

Which I do hold, and it is the one thing in this clause I hold without qualification. The baptism is his — the Jordan, and not the practice. He went in not to be cleansed but to sanctify the water, and every washing since is participation in that one event rather than a new one.[^106]

Paul says we are baptized into his death, and not into something like it: one death, one rising, one baptism.[^107]

It follows that the celebrant's belief and character are not load-bearing, which is a mercy for everyone concerned, and which is structurally the same point I made about prophets. The words are said by someone and are not his.

There is one thing the clause does not ask that I want to say anyway, because it is the objection people actually have, and because it happened to me.

What is said over an infant is not a claim about guilt. Nothing in this account has an infant owing anything — sin is the compound, and a child has no more of it than the rest of us and no less. The service does not allege otherwise. What it does is put questions to adults. Will you be responsible for seeing that this child is brought up in the faith and life of the Christian family. It is a set of promises, and the promises are answered by everyone in the room. *We will, with God's help* is said out loud by the congregation, which is the tell: the sentence is not about the infant's condition but about a body of people binding themselves in front of each other.

Someone did that for me at seven, and one of them was a force in the trajectory that got me here. I am aware that is not an argument. It is the reason I do not treat the practice as a doctrinal embarrassment to be explained around, and it is consistent with everything above: the baptism is his and not ours, the celebrant is not load-bearing, and neither is the recipient's ability to give an account of what is happening to him. A washing that required understanding would be a different sacrament, and it would be one I could not have received.

> *\[What the clause asks is narrower than it looks; the Jordan and not the practice; Donatism and Augustine; the celebrant not load-bearing; infant baptism and the promises answered by the room.\]*

*for the forgiveness of sins.* (XII.3)

*For the forgiveness of sins* I have already answered, at the crucifixion and before it. Sin is the compound and forgiveness is its shedding, and nothing is transacted in either direction. I will not repeat the argument here. The Creed does not ask me to make one. It asks me to say the same thing.

> *\[Sin is the compound and forgiveness is its shedding; nothing transacted. Needs a pointer back to V.4–5 for a reader thirty pages downstream.\]*

## XIII. Of Looking For

> *We look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the world to come. \[Amen.\]*

*We look for the resurrection of the dead,* (XIII.1)

*We believe* runs the length of the Creed. *We acknowledge* appeared once. Both are about assent but this is a different kind of doing. *Prosdokōmen* — we look for, the same word for watching a road for travelers.[^108]

No belief is being held here, rather, something is being awaited, and as the last thing the Creed asks of me, I take it to mean it is the position I am meant to be left in.

It also puts me there at a tomb, early, while it is still dark, with people who came expecting to find what they think they have lost.[^109]

That is what I am doing when I say this line. Not affirming that the dead will rise nor claiming to understand what it means that they will, but watching for a body: particular, still marked, waiting to exclaim, "my Lord and my God!"[^110]

> *\[Prosdokomen — watching a road for travelers; the position the Creed leaves me in; the tomb, early, while it is still dark; my Lord and my God.\]*

*and the life of the world to come.* (XIII.2)

*Olam ha-ba* is one of those Jewish phrases we co-opted without understanding very well, and like the best rabbinic phrases it means something very specific and very vague at the same time. Some of what Jesus says about the kingdom of heaven sounds a great deal like what the rabbis say about the world to come, and both are given in parable, analogy, and metaphor, presumably the only way either *can* be given.

The analogy I think of when I say this part of the Creed is from the Talmud. *Olam ha-ba* is a party. The righteous will sit in a booth made from the skin of Leviathan and eat his flesh at a great banquet.[^111]

But the banquet does not begin, for the reason I gave where the Creed speaks of judgment: G-d's play with Leviathan does not stop, so the hide is not taken and the booth is not finished. And the prophet says the nations will come.[^112]

So the roof is open, and a booth is required to be built that way, so the sky shows through. A shelter still going up, and a watch being kept.

> *\[Olam ha-ba; Leviathan, the banquet that does not begin, the sukkah with the roof open, the nations coming. The mitzvot persisting — a practice that is not for anything cannot be completed — and krida, which the ending currently presupposes and the document never supplies. These are probably one addition.\]*
