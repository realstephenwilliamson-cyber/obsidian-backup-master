As we can see at a glance from the table of contents, this chapter is dedicated to seven depositions of reality principles, worlds and Mantras onto the body. The focus is on the space the units of the depositions occupy along the length of the body of a standing man. Although all of them are in the form of Mantras, that is not the concern here so none of them are given here. Abhinavagupta explains that they can be very easily worked out as they all share a simple basic structure which is as follows. First comes OṀ which is followed by the name of the world or the principle (*tattva*) that is being projected onto the body in the dative and ends in the standard salutation *namaḥ.* So, for example, the Mantra for the Earth principle is simply OṀ PṚTHVĪTATTVĀYA NAMAḤ. Similarly, the Mantra of the first world from the bottom – Kālāgnirudra – is OṀ KĀLĀGNIRUDRĀYA NAMAḤ. These Mantras are the purifiers of the world, principles or beings to which they refer and denote. By this is meant that their object (*artha*) is transformed into Mantra. According to Abhinava, the power (*śakti*), strength (*bala*) and fundamental nature (*svabhāva*) of Mantra is the reflective awareness (*vimarśa*) of the Light of consciousness. One with the Light, when directed inwardly towards the Light of its subjectivity, it unifies the entity which is its outer object (*artha*) when directed outwards. In this way, Earth and all the other principles and worlds etc are merged into consciousness.

In terms of their measurements in finger breadth along the length of the erect body, the depositions are classified into three categories: 1) Lower (Aparā), 2) Middling (Parāparā) and Supreme (Para). The lower depositions extend for eight-four finger breadths from the soles of the feet to the middle of the eyebrows. The middling depositions extend for ninty-six finger breadths along the body from the soles of the feet to the Cavity of Brahmā on the crown of the head. The supreme one extends for one hundred and eight fingers from the feet to the End of the Twelve, which is twelve finger breadths above the head.

*[p. 14]*

The *Mālinīvijayottara* simply grades depositions in this nondescript way with no reference beyond that whereas Abhinava equates them with the three Trika Goddesses Parā, Parāparā and Aparā. We know that he studied this Tantra in great detail with his Trika Guru Śambhunātha, so it may be that this interpretation is a traditional one, not just his own. Thus, just after he has expounded the deposition of the worlds onto the body Abhinava declares: 'That has been taught by Śambhu, the Supreme Lord, in the *Mālinīvijayottara*.'[^1]

Conscious of his higher identity, Abhinava regularly refers to his teacher Śambhunātha as Parameśa, Parameśvara, Maheśvara, Mahādeva and the like. Here it appears that Abhinava is referring to both him and Śiva, who utters the scripture. The teacher who imparts the teaching of the scripture is Śiva Himself. In other words, Śiva reveals the scriptures in his disembodied form, and in his embodied form he explains them. This means, as Abhinava sees it, that it is not always necessary for Śiva's embodied form to explain. He may do so just in his disembodied form through the inspired intuition that understands them, or indeed through both together.

The depositions taught here are presented by Abhinavagupta with comments and added details in chapter sixteen of the *Tantrāloka.* There they are presented as part of the initiation of an apprentice (*putra*) or 'spiritual son'. This initiation authorizes the initiate to assist the *ācārya* in Trika rituals and sets him on the path to himself receiving the consecration (*abhiṣeka*) as an *ācārya* in his turn if that is deemed appropriate. There is not even a hint of this application of these depositions of the *tattvas* and worlds on the body here in the Tantra or, indeed of the *putrakadīkṣā*.

The rite Abhinavagupta presents begins with a vision of the Triple Trident (*tritriśūla*) Maṇḍala. This is surprising as the Maṇḍala is normally revealed to the disciple at the climax of his initiation. This Maṇḍala is a higher, complex variant of the basic Trident Maṇḍala that introduces the worship of the Trika's triadic goddess revealed and worshipped in the initial regular initiation (*samayadīkṣā*). As such, the rite related to it is explained in a few words as being essentially the same as the single Trident repeated three times. It is presented at the beginning so that the outer objective order of Nature consisting of the three Qualities (*guṇa*) represented by the prongs of the Trident may be graced by the presence of the Three Goddesses who are the embodiment of the three energies of will, knowledge and action through which consciousness is projected externally. The triadic invocation and worship, three times reinforced, intensely energizes the purifying, transformative power of the rite. This unfolds through the deposition of the fundamental realities of the Six-fold Path, alphabets, Mantras and parts of Mantra, onto the disciple's body thereby filling it and transforming it into the Trika cosmos and its Mantras. The central importance of these depositions unfolds in the concluding section of chapter sixteen of the *Tantrāloka* where Abhinava enumerates the thousands of theoretically possible initiations based on the mathematical permutations of the varieties of depositions he presents. Clearly, the depositions are the ultimate purifiers and media of transformation of the cosmic order and its presiding pantheons. The teacher's apprentice – his spiritual son (*putraka*) - must be as intensely empowered and purified as possible by means of Trika Mantras and by his participation in Trika's ontology and cosmos sustained by the presence of the Trika goddesses.

*[p. 15]*

There are various ways of classifying types of initiation. A basic one is into two types. One is initiation by means of ritual action (*kriyādīkṣā*) and the other by means of knowledge (*vidyādīkṣā*). The 'knowledge' meant here is the liberating knowledge of the Mantras and the manner in which they are deposited on the body by virtue of which the disciple is empowered and liberated. The first set of initiations are procedures that develop by progressing through the stations of ascent through the worlds, orders of realities and forces. As the soul rises it is progressively purified by being conjoined to the stations of its ascent and so is accordingly called 'initiation by conjunction' or 'one that conjoins' (*yojanikadīkṣā*). This conjunction is marked by a birth into a higher world order order. This activity is essentially a progressive purification through, and as, successive elevations to higher levels into which the soul is reborn. Trika measures these ascents along the axis of the Trika Trident.

In this chapter, the *Mālinīvijayottara* teaches initiations based on knowledge (*jñānadīkṣā*). Abhinava explains how this type of initiation differs from the other type in chapter sixteen of the *Tantrāloka*. Like the one that takes place by conjunctions, this one also propels the neophant through a series of progressively higher, purer rebirths but it does so solely through the use of Mantra. Thus, Abhinava writes:

'When the purifying mantra is deposited in this way, the net of (the disciple's) fetters dissolves away by union (yoga) with its (purifying) rays (brought about) by the might of the teacher's visualization of it.[^2] (Thus the disciple) is made to take birth simultaneously in all the wombs within the reality to be purified. By thus experiencing (them all), when his Karma is exhausted, (he attains) liberation.[^3]

*[p. 16]*

Jayaratha explains that 'the net of fetters' is the Six Paths, and the reality (*tattva*) is a synecdoche that stands for all the Path, not just one of the thirty-six reality principles. As each unit is all the others purifying one purifies all the others.[^4] For this reason, everything is purified at once, not successively as in the other type of initiation.

Both types of initiation begin in the category of the Individual Means (*āṇavopāya*). The categories of means to liberation (*upāya*) not only comprise the means the disciple applies for his own liberation, they are also the means that the teacher imparts for the liberation of his disciples. Accordingly, we can talk about Individual (*āṇava*), Empowered (*śākta*) and Divine (*śāmbhava*) initiations as we do means to liberation (*upāya*). Indeed, we do find this classification of initiations in the scriptural sources. Although Abhinava must have been aware of this, he does not refer to it. The reason may be that this classification in the original sources mirrors types of Mantras rather than levels of absorption (*samāveśa*) or means to realization (*upāya*). Also, it must be admitted that they are not common in the earlier sources rather they assume more prominence in the later part of the early period, specifically in the Kubjikā Tantras. Anyway, Abhinava takes the opportunity here, as he does at practically every possible turn, to exalt the incomparable excellence of realization transmitted directly from the teacher to the disciple. This he calls *vijñānadīkṣā.* We can understand the prefix vi- here to mean '*viśeṣa*' in the sense of 'special' and, implicitly, 'extraordinary'. Generally, '*vijñāna*' means consciousness as in the name Vijñānabhairava, that Kṣemarāja implicitly glosses by referring to the deity as Cidbhairava – the Bhairava Who is Consciousness.

We also observe in the following passage that the context, as usual, frames Abhinava's extraordinary exegesis. The disciple is immersed in Mantra practice. Rising to its acme he discovers, as with all ritual and yogic action, its fundamental grounding in Śiva consciousness and how the discovery of that brings about the transformative recognition of his own liberated Śiva nature. Thus, he writes:

'If (the teacher) is to perform an initiation based on consciousness (*vijñānadīkṣā*), (he) should gaze at the disciple intensely, until (the disciple attains) a state free of thought (*avikalpaka*), by virtue of the might (*bala*) of the (inner) discourse (*saṁjalpaḥ*) of that (purifying) Mantra. Indeed, thought (*vikalpa*) is made of (inner) discourse (*saṁjalpamaya*) which reflects (on the nature of its object) (*vimarśaka*). And that is the essential nature of Mantra and reflective awareness (*vimarśa*), which is pure, and not the fettered state. Eternal, it is conceived (*upakalpita*) to be one with Śiva Who, without beginning, is the dispenser of (every) boon; by union (*yoga*) with that, the teacher's thought also attains Śiva's state.[^5]

*[p. 17]*

Thus, from the Individual level in which the Mantra is recited, to the Empowered level where it is the reflective awareness of consciousness that consumes thought constructs it reaches its ultimate level as the supreme, full and perfect subjectivity (*pūrṇāhantā*) of Śiva consciousness. We turn now to the text. In order to understand and practice it easily the depositions taught in this chapter have been presented in the appendix both as charts and graphically in the figure of a standing man.
