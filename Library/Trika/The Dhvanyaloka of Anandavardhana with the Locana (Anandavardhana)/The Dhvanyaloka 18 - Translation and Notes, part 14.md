A Furthermore, the property of words and meanings called suggestiveness is in conformity with a generally recognized relationship ang cannot be called contrary to the doctrine of any school.[^A592-1] Between words and meanings it is generally accepted that there exists a denoter-denoted relation. Quite in conformity with tbis but connected with a different set of conditions is an operation acccidentally attaching (cupādhika) to word and meaning called suggestiveness; and on this account it is different from the denotative power. A denotative power is the very self of each individual word, bound to it from the time of our first understanding the word, for the word is never known to be without it. But suggestiveness is not bound to the word, but accidental, for our apprehension of it is conditioned by context, etc. and it is not apprehended in the absence of those conditions. If it be asked why we bother to study its nature if it is not essential, we answer that we are not at fault, because while it is accidental to the word \[which carries it) it is not accidental to its object, which is

*[p. 578]* ^pg593

its suggested meaning. A suggestor in this respect is seen to act like the liga (middle term) of an inference. Just as the property of being a liga appears irregularly in the entity which possesses it, as it depends on one’s desire \[to infer\], and yet is invariable in its relation to what it works toward, |viz., the sādhya, the major term or probandum\], so is the suggestive power, as we have described it, \[accidental to the word but invariable in relation to what it suggests\][^A592-2]. From the very fact that it is not bound to the word, it follows that it cannot be taken as a mode of the denotative power. If it were a mode of the denotative power, it would be bound to its word, just as the denotative power is.

And this sort of accidental property of words must necessarily be assumed by him \[viz., the Mīmāmsaka\] who holds that there is an inbora (autpattika) relation between words and meanings.[^A592-3] He is an expert in the meanings of sentences and claims that there is a diference between human and non-human sentences. For if he did not assume such a property, he could make no distinction between what is stated by a non-human and by a human sentence, since the connection between the component words and meanings is the same in both cases.[^A592-4] If he does assume such a power, however, he can explaia how human sentences can be false by possessing an accidental operation, other than the denotative, superimposed upon the words in accordance with human desire, despite the fact that the words never abandon their coonection with their denoted objects.

We see that there are entities in the world which, without abandoning their essential nature, may act in contrary ways by some accidental operation imparted to them by various combinations of conditions. For example, such an entity as the moon possesses a coolness that refreshes all living beings, and yet it is well known that it can cause pain when viewed by men whose hearts are burning with the paio of separation from their beloveds. Accordingly, one who would explain the falsity of human sentences despite the inborn connection \[of their words) with the objects \[of those words) will clearly have to posit an accidental property of some sort in addition to the power of denotation. And this property is nothing other than suggestiveness (vyañjakatva), for suggestiveness is simply the revealing of a suggested sense (vyargya) and human sentences regularly reveal a buman intention (puruṣãbhiprâya). The sense is suggested, not denoted, because the words are not connected with this \[intended or suggested sense\] by a relation of denoter to denoted.
