##### 3.3.2 Phoneticians

Phonetics (siksa, 111. “lessons”) is probably the oldest auxiliary Vedic academic discipline, and in the traditional scheme also the first. The

Except for some precursors of Vedic linguistics, the oldest textual representatives of 5115 are the Prātiśākhyas (“textbooks for each branch” (22, of the Veda) which are concerned with the intricacies of Vedic recitation, e.g. very precise prononciation, and the determination with word stems. Later Śikṣā works are more specialised and eventually more systematic. phoneticians differ from the grammarians and etymologists in one aspect that is important for us: their motivation is not only to preserve the Veda as a body of linguistic symbols, but also as a body of sound, or a sound event. Phonetics is therefore not concerned with the deconstruction of language in the manner of grammarians, but with its reconstruction. The phoneticians’ primary aim is abstracting the pronounciation of the linguistic sound material in the most precise and perfect way, and some phonetic works, such as the PZri-Śiksā, will principally consist of describing in minute detail the individual sounds of the phonetically ordered alphabet (varna-krama). This includes the tonal impacts of their mutual combination, the Vedic accents and the durations. This methodic concentration on sound to the utmost precision is quite a different procedure than the dissecting occupation of grammar. However, this does not mean, that phoneticians are not concerned with grammar at all. A good phonetician will likely also be a good grammarian and although siksā does not occupy a prominent place in grammar, the study of grammar in fact starts with phonetics, i.e. with fixing the sounds of the alphabet (the order of vowels and consonants, and groups of letters according to the places of articulation and types of utterances like open vowel, closed vowel, liquid, sibilant etc.) which also orders the mind. Such systematic approach to the sound material of language is the foundation of phonetics and traditional Veda learning. Learning the Veda by heart (or the Veda branch of one’s family), is a very exacting procedure and an “activity governed by rules” as Frits Staal puts it.“ But it is also an aesthetic experience, as we would like to add. The phoneticians’ minute description of pronounciation is not only highly technical, but often inserts poetic and metaphoric imagery. In the following we start with the traditional training and the “cultural capital” acquired along with the technical skills of the mnemotic techniques. Thereafter we show how phonetic works describe the aesthetics of realization and invoke an emotional fusion with the subject through images of great metaphorical density.

In the Samhitā-pātha the student learns the text by listening and repeating its recitation. His teacher (often his father) would teach two or three words step by step, and then a line, thereafter a verse or mantra, and the student (the son) or a group of students would repeat twice. Along with learning new passages in the same manner, the passages already learned are regularly repeated as well (now only once by teacher and student, or by the group of students). This way of committing text material to memory involves the greatest attention to careful pronunciation (each student being individually checked by the teacher). The same teaching procedure (the student attentively listens and repeats twice, etc.) applies also in the following more complex readings and modifications. The next step will be to supplement the Samhitā-pātha or continuous recitation by the Pada-pātha, i.e. the word-for-word recitation in which the sandhis (euphonic combinations) of the text are dissolved and the words are “brought back,” so to speak, to their word stems. But the Śikṣā works also contain the rules of reconstructing the Samhitā-pātha from the Pada-pātha, i.e. the text with all the sandhi combinations re-introduced. This is achieved in the Krama-pātha, in which each word is repeated twice: first by linking it through sandhi with the word that precedes, and then with the word that follows. ###### 3.3.2.2 Phonetics and Aesthetics

We started this chapter by mentioning that phonetics differs from grammar by its function of reconstruction. The technical part of this reconstruction has been described, but the reconstruction also involves an aesthetic element: the reciter turns the Vedic text, which is always composed as language-music, back into a musically consistent performance. The living sound form is realized (anew) out of the skeleton of written signs. The following examples are taken from the Nāradiīya- Śiksā, a phonetic treatise of uncertain date on the Sāma-Veda,® but similar examples and classifications can be found in Śikṣā texts belonging to other Veda branches as well.“ 

As we pointed out in the description of the Veda recitation, today’s (serious) reciters still pay just as much attention to the modifications and lengths of sounds required by the phoneticians as they do to the integrity of the text itself. Articulation must be precise, even for the micro-sounds, since the individual sounds are regarded almost as independent personalities, who should develop their own character and their own timbre. The Nāradīya-Śiksā expresses this personality of phonemes in the following simile: 

“Just as a tigress takes her cubs tightly in her teeth without hurting them, whilst fearing that she might drop them and injure \[both of\] them, so one should approach the individual syllables.” ® It is precisely the hyper-precision of the phoneticians that reveals the joy at dealing with language and the love and care that are involved. Even in the highly academic texts one has a feeling that the Brahmins are definitely motivated by a type of eros where their beloved language

The complexity of the sound of language as an aesthetic phenomenon cannot be laid down in full even with such a detailed system as the siksā. It is true that theater studies applies very similar methods to those used in phonetics, which is not surprising, since ultimately both disciplines are dedicated to the sensory aspect of signs and sound. Indian theater comprises speech, play, dance, song and instrumental music. Strictly speaking, theater is dance drama or music drama. 

Just like the Vedic recitation manuals, theater studies also provide detailed technical instructions about how one should recite texts. ##### 3.4.2 Sensitization of Gestures

 Semanticization of Gestures 

Bharata describes the audiovisual communication of the rasas as having many stages. He begins with the most precise phenomenological description of 33 transitory, subjective emotional states (vyabhicāri- 

bhāva), added to the eight dominant emotional states (sthāyi-bhāva). This also includes states such as depression, doubt, tiredness, worry, jealousy, certainty, joyful arousal and madness. These supplementary emotional states encompass psycho-physical ones such as dreaming, agony, illness and amnesia. Just like the rasas, these real-world feelings are defined according to the conditions under which they arise and the ways they can be communicated audiovisually. For example, according to Bharata, madness can be triggered by separation from loved ones, by the loss of one’s good reputation, or by illness. It is expressed by laughing without any reason, weeping or screaming, speaking a continuous stream of nonsense, 

singing, dancing, reciting, smearing oneself with ashes or dirt, and carrying a skull. Interestingly, the latter indicators are found amongst the Saiva ascetics, who in fact in early Sivaism pretended to be crazy and were regarded by the general public as such. Bharata puts the emotional expressions of madness in the rasa categories of tragedy (karunā) and love(-sickness). Consequently, one and the same subjective state can figure in several rasas. 

The sāttvíka gestures — the embodiments of feelings — are systematically to be distinguished from the emotional states, but in practice they are often confused with them. This group includes such things as weeping, shaking, quivering, blanching and sweating. These phenomena can only be represented correctly if the actor is able to enter into the emotional states which trigger these symptoms. Hence the name sāttvika, derived from sattva, something “that really exists.” It seems unlikely that this idealistic requirement could be fulfilled. Therefore Bharata also provides instructions about how the (psycho-physical) sāttvika gestures can be simulated by use of the body and the voice. 

Because of the difficulty in representing the sāttvika gestures, the major media of communicating a rasa utilize sense perception, i.e. audiovisual signs: well-defined stylized gestures performed by the limbs (aṅgā-‘bhinaya), and “vocal gestures” or expressive speech (vāg-abhinaya). The concept of the theatrical rasa can in fact only be realized in a multi-medial fashion. There are two prerequisites for the theater’ s claim that it portrays all aspects of our world: (a) that all objects and events must be surrounded by rasa, i.e. must have a dramatic power and inexorably bring about emotional participation; and (b) that all objects and events in the world can also be represented in this atmospheric form of aesthetic moods. But at the same time, the theater should also fulfill the demand for artistic form. So in this respect the theater imitates events “in real time,” as if they were really happening; but it does so in a stylized form, which in turn is not pure mimicry, but instead an artistic representation. 

Language can only speak about a thing — but it cannot also be that thing. It can fulfill the demand for artistic representation, but it does not open itself up to immediate sensory perception. This is where the “language of gestures” becomes important — the bodily gestures and the sound gestures in the theater. An actor who comes on stage with his hands casually stuffed into his trouser pockets and announces in the voice of a newscaster that a terrible demon is pursuing him and wants to eat him up, is not especially credible and will not create a rasa. If, however, he follows Bharata’s instructions as he comes on stage, i.e. speaking the same sentence with his eyes wide open, an unsteady gaze and shaking limbs, intoning the same sentence with a dark and quivering voice, he 75 the fear that is to be represented.

##### 3.4.3 Expressive Vocalization

The mimetic gestures communicate via similarity and recognition, while the symbolic gestures communicate via signs and signals that can and must be decoded. In contrast, the sonic communication is purely expressive, does not call things by their names, and always works on a sympathetic level, never a conventional one. The purely acoustic aspect is neither mimetic, for it does not imitate anything, nor is it symbolic. It does not have to be decoded, since its value does not lie in its meaning

In Bharata’s theater this purely sonic dimension occurs primarily in the harp music played as background music to create an atmosphere. What the musicians play is much less connected with the staged drama than the multiple suggestive gestures such as the eye movements. With the exception of a rather loose allocation of certain styles to certain rasa groups, the music remains strangely vague — quite atypical of the Nātyaśāstra itself, which is otherwise overflowing with detailed instructions. And the texts of the songs performed by the singers in the orchestra do not necessarily have anything to do with what is happening on the stage, either. They are rather nonspecific and have more to do with the mood, and mainly with moods aroused by sceneries of nature such the monsoon. 

For the music, tellingly, there exist special gods. For the main singer there is the Gandharva Nārada, a go-between linking the world of the gods and that of men; for the harps there is Tumburu, a flat-nosed form of Śiva; Svāti, a centaur, takes care of the drums; and the Apasaras Urvaśī is the prima ballerina. What is striking is that heavenly beings are responsible for the music, while the theatrical language, gestures and feelings are the province of a human being (bharata), rather than these somewhat curious demi-gods. 

The instruments play an important role not only for the refrain songs, which are inserted into the plot, but also for the ‘overture’ (pūrva-raṅga). The function of the overture is not so much to put the audience in the right mood for the play, but instead is primarily a religious one. All the gods, spirits and demons have to be honored and “pacified,” if only to prevent the performance from being disrupted. For this reason the songs of praise to the gods are preceded by a musical passage in which the orchestra dominates over the singers. The singers are actually singing at the same time, but the ‘words’? they use do not belong to any comprehensible language. Only after the wicked spirits have been “pacified” can the gods be praised and the stage sanctified by a circambulation. As indicated in connection with the Sāman, “pure” music always has a tendency towards the world beyond, to what is numinous and heavenly, and therefore also always remains a little dangerous. The “theater expressing the whole world” necessarily also encompasses the ‘other world,’ but it does not incorporate it into the actual drama.

The tempo, too, has its own expressive function: slow speech signals a passive, receptive attitude, whereas a more expansive, proactive one is expressed in faster speech. 

Moreover, there is representation through phrasing (64) a sentence, i.e. the mood is also indicated by factors such as whether the sentences are pronounced fluently or falteringly, whether the speaker makes pauses or continues without any breaks, and whether a sentence is spoken with a rising or falling intonation. Eroticism and comedy are expressed through a well-formed manner of speaking and a sing-song intonation or a playful one. A voice that falters or gives up is characteristic of horror and disgust. The typical feature of the moods of the heroic, the terrifying and the fascinating is that a whole sentence is uttered without the speaker taking a single breath anywhere in the middle. And finally, monotony and absence of pauses identify the tragic mood. 

It is precisely in this finely nuanced voice model that we find what great attention Indian aesthetics has paid to exact phenomenology, unlike Western aesthetics. In the phenomenology of speech and voice we find an attempt to define the rasas in a very precise way at a purely psycho-physical level and to develop an audible representation of them. 

##### 3.4.4 Onomatopoeic Codes: the Language of Drums

Nothing at all can happen in a decent Indian theater if there are no drums. Every appearance of a character, every important change and every climax of dramatic development is introduced with special drum signals. In the theater drums link the otherwise rather disconnected areas of speech/drama and music, and turn the performance into a music

*[p. 517]* ^pg541

drama. For every class of characters there is a rhythm on the drums which does not only imitate the rhythm of the character’s gait, but also ‘describes?’ his characteristics in sound. And for this reason the drum instruments not only have their own special deity, but also a special mythology. It is a special mythology also in the sense of a rather unusual mythologeme — unusual because it is a very naturalistic one. The following fetching story occurs at the beginning of chapter 34 of the Nātyaśāstra, which is entirely devoted to drums: 

One day the centaur Svāti went to draw water from a lotus pond, and was overtaken by a severe storm. The raindrops were so large and heavy that different tones rang out from the various lotus leaves in the pond. What a miracle, he thought - how deep and sweet these sounds are, what a delight to the heart! He returned to his Ashram to meditate on how to build an instrument that could create sounds like those he had heard. Finally he invented the puskaras, the “drum instruments,” which bear this name because they resemble lotus leaves (also called puskara). 

Here the sound of drums is derived from natural sounds, rather like a ready-made sound. In complete contrast to their ad-hoc character in the myth, drums in the theater are played with the most complicated techniques and in the most varied situations. So there was a great need for notation for the drummers. The notation developed was not a graphical one, as would usually be the case in Europe, but — as with Pāṇini — a notation based on alphabetization and vocalization. The resulting language of the drums could be called an extremely refined link between pure sonality and “theater semantics,” i.e. those specific forms of communication that have developed in the theater. 

The drum notation is based on what is called the “vocalization” (vāk-karana) of drumbeats. In the first instance this means that one uses one’s voice to imitate a drummed rhythm. This imitation can then be written down. It helps that in Sanskrit there is strict differentiation between long and short syllables. Pronouncing the long syllables exactly twice as long as the short ones (as is usual in recitation), automatically results in a musical rhythm. So in this way the Sanskrit script itself can function as musical notation. The following example may well look like an alliterative poem, but in reality it is a drum solo:

Some form of participation is almost always there — at least in hidden form in the initial maṅgala verses of academic works. Our claim is only that mythos and Jogos have been less divided in India than in Europe. We find de-mythification and re-mythification, and secular and religious orientations. It is typical, however, that even within this very worldly oriented milieu a participatory orientation towards poetic diction arose, a sense of the suggestiveness of language and the rasa it confers. Although the mythical world-view of the early Veda has long been outdated in the post-Vedic period, new forms of mythical involvement kept re-appearing. We now find thinkers who give participation a metaphysical framework and develop what we call reflected universal myths. Their intellectual rigor invariably involved, as we are going to discuss, an intensive investigation of language, irrespective of whether they were poets, ritualists, philosophers or grammarians. Their exploration of language produced some outstanding results prefiguring modern linguistics — partly as mere by-products, as in the case of the Pūrva-Mīmāmsā school whose sole interest was to preserve Vedic ritual in a time of social and religious change.
