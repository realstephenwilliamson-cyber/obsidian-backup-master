## 5.	Conclusion

This research is an exploration of the constitution of the subject by technics. It is the image as technics, or ›image-technics‹, that provides the possibility of perceiving the world and constituting the being as such. The world appears to us not as we see it; in this sense seeing is illusion. The world appears to us through the technics of seeing, which provides the possibility of looking. In primary societies the freeing of hands helped to form bipedality as a technics. In turn, standing up freed the face and eyes to see the surrounding world. With the increasing adoption of technologies, technics is transferred from the eyes to its copy or to machines; therefore seeing is left to machines. Machines do the work for us, and any seeing is an imitation and the return to a primary impulse.

This question is discussed above through the reading of the work of Bernard Stiegler in its close connection with the film by Chris Marker. The main factor connecting them is the question of memory. The notion that memory is the function of remembering and oblivion underpins this question. It is the recorded memory, which is a non-organic, non-living matter, that opens up the perspective for the present to happen. In this perspective thought itself become possible or delivered by technics of the recording of the past. Both Stiegler and Marker are thinkers of this possibility and this connects philosophy and art by establishing the thinking as the basis of both disciplines. It is memory that provides the further dimensions of thinking, since memory marks the incompleteness of the existence which is in default and in need of the improvement. The second important feature is the thinking that makes their work powerful. Their work is an angle from where to look at the image as technics and the role it plays in constituting the subject.

*[p. 194]* ^pg195

›Images as technics‹ provide the constitution of the subject, and they bring fullness to the incomplete memory. The image-technics precedes thought, and the grasping of this technics enables thought to come into existence. The crucial aspect of my research also insists on the image as it makes thought possible. The image as technics helps to discover this missing dimension of the visual by creating the difference in the world of commerce, tourism, advertising and marketing where, images are used for the sale promotion. These activities, as they are used for the instant consumption, are constantly erasing memory, whereas the activity of thinking is recording memory and grammatising the visible. This is also the difference between the use of images in art and their use in culture industry.

The project is based on my research in philosophy of time and image as well as on a long experience of working as an artist. The question of how memory is reprogrammed in the condition of the proliferation of the image is one of the key aspects of my work. The notion is that memory is reprogrammed by the ideology through the intensive use of images and that it is the way subjects are shown not what they were but what they must remember having been. Therefore, the main question in this current research is based on the related question of the image and its role in the constitution of the subject. Not the question of the subject but the process which constitutes the subject is central to my work.

Time and image are both difficult categories to define, and there are many different definitions throughout history of the thought. My focus is on the use of the image in contemporary art and media; the question of the time is understood here from this standpoint. In fact, to a certain extend I argue that the image has replaced in a certain way the ›being‹, and very often images are the substitution of being. It is because in the world of the proliferation of images associations are assisting our perception of the world more powerfully than any other knowledge, and it is possible to say that images have opened up the perspective of a particular way of knowing the world, differently from the previous ones, like logic or rationality, and often as a mixture of associations with rational thought. Besides, the being is known to us only as the traces that objects in the ›time-space‹ leaves behind, and it helps to transform these traces into images as the recorded memory.

Bernard Stiegler’s work as a main source of my research has enabled me to criticise and to think of the perspectives which I hope will be helpful to work with images in their relation to a new phase of the development. This 

*[p. 195]* ^pg196

new phase is associated with the increasing shift towards the use of images in the virtual sense. One of the aims is to show that the shift towards virtual doesn’t cut off the relationship with already existing images; on the contrary this shift extends the ›reality effect‹ to the virtual. Both Bernard Stiegler and Chris Marker point to this paradigmatic shift, which to a certain extend underlines our confusions and beliefs in the fatal triangle created by the on-going speculations about the real, reality and realism. The growing speculations about this triangle create confusions on the one hand and many possibilities on the other as far as speculations about culture go.

The notion that there is non-living, inorganic matter expressed in differences is the starting point of my understanding of culture. Culture is motivated by the pure difference, which challenges the notions of ›purity‹ and ›dualism‹ and instead opens up the space for multiplicities. Nearly in all spheres of the recent human activity there is a consistent attempt to find a place in order to create a discursive space to rethink forms and attitudes in relation to our world. It is taking place in a very interesting conjecture of the situation given by the proliferation and enormous amount of differences available. It seems that the notion of the ›difference‹, which has helped to shift from the one-dimensional thinking of the 20th century, is not an option for our times any longer; on the contrary there is a strong desire to find the point which would help to articulate differences as they are derived from the singularity of thought. Difference became the fact of the reality; it became factical in a sense that there is no such a thing as the singular, but there is a singularity which is affected by the difference. This creates a very interesting possibility for thinking as an activity in its own rights. It also makes possible to allow thinking to move not only forward, as it was the rule of modernism, but also backwards in order to learn from the past. While creating the space for the singularity of thought in its trajectory of enhancing differences, thinking can go back as well as forward and provide the possibility for ›re-making ›from the different angle. I call this activity the ›return inquiry‹.

The return inquiry is partly derived from the more ubiquitous notion of re-making that has emerged as a style of the ›post-modern‹. This style poses new questions of the independence of thinking and making and clarifies that there is no reason to stay away from the past. Instead it has become clear that the past offers tremendous possibilities, because the past is reserved within the subjects that are excluded. These subjects were excluded from modernism’s way of moving forward only, but after the 

*[p. 196]* ^pg197

failure of modernism the return of the excluded subjects has become an option to renew the potential.

Paradoxically this potential is the result of modernism’s separation of thinking from making. As I mentioned above, before modernism there was no division of the roles in the sense that someone who makes the ›work‹ leaves the responsibility of thinking to critics. This idea has emerged with modernism intensifying the role of the division of labour. It was made in order to show the prominence of the visual, the idea which was marginal to thinking of that time. History is, in general, developed in such a way by alternating one idea after another and constantly returning to its excluded subjects. It develops in cycles. As a result, there is always something missing. Given the rapid growth of production and the speed of developments now and then, humanity finds itself left behind its own achievements. The reason is in the separation and in its role that created the rational division of labour. Initially it did not intend to separate thinking from making by dividing the labour. However, it became too convenient to ›assign‹ jobs to the divided labour: one thinks, the other makes. The most opaque argument in this sense is the theory/practice division. Besides, in the world of the proliferation there are enough means to do things, but there is not enough foundational knowledge to give shape and form to these things. This paradox can be explained by the fact that the speed of the proliferation exceeds the capacity of thought. On the one hand it suspends the possibility of thinking, while on the other hand it provides the possibility to develop the technics of thinking. Technics preserved human memory, and now every act of remembering is an encounter with the technics. Technics precedes thought, but the technics becomes a tool after being informed by thought. The paradox is that thought is always behind the technics and always late to its realisation. I argue that, because of a constant registering of the shift in the foundations of knowledge, there is the chance to fulfil the missing dimensions created by the proliferation of means. I insist that, while there is the proliferation of images on the one hand and the ›crisis of foundations‹ on the other, there is also an urgent demand to grasp knowledge produced in relation to these foundations. While inventing complicated machines and maintaining complex ideas, the study of the foundational knowledge basics has been slow. As a result, there is a paradigmatic paradox of a saturation on the one hand and a non-sufficiency of grasping this saturation on the other. This creates a difficulty to find the balance between the proliferation of images and their ›original‹ meaning. 

*[p. 197]* ^pg198

However, with the proliferation we realise that what is new today is a very well forgotten past that renews itself with returns and retrievals.

In the light of ›returns‹ and ›retrievals‹ thought is not given: it is discovered and it is a process and an engagement. In renewals and returns the thought is separated from the image, from its technics. This separation demands a new method in philosophising, which is an absolute necessity. Questions that arise in each decade are by their nature different from the questions of previous decades, and they need to be addressed differently as they emerge in a new context. The nature of events may be different, but the nature of thought remains true to the exploration when it comes to method. I look at the notion of the ›grammatisation‹ through its capacity of spatialising and inserting memory into images. Exactly in the same way as the grammar allows creating technics of speaking and writing, to grammatise technics is also possible. For instance, it is possible to make a car, but because of the goal of the car, which is to move, it will have a different grammatical function than speaking or writing. The same applies to images, and grammatisation of the image brings the dimension of thought into associations. The grammatisation of the image is also developed from the question of the derushage and discussed as the montage-association and the Kuleshov effect.

Early theories of montage pointed to the fact that the image has its own epistemological vocation and is knowledge among other forms of knowledge. The most interesting thing about images is that they help to exteriorise a true interior aesthetic potential of subjects. It also means that they help to produce the political recognition of exclusions. In this sense the old projects are unfinished projects. Their failures are closely connected to the dynamic of exclusions, which is specifically true of the project of modernism. Because of this I would like to insist on the idea that what is excluded in the past may be included in the present. The engagement with technics helps to find ways of making visible this problematic past by carefully and meaningfully discovering subtracted subjects. The subject of the critique is mostly in the realm of the visual culture, which is becoming even more problematic with its growth and spread globally. Two related points should be taken into account here. The first is that there is an incredible rupture between the origins of the intention of images and their current development. This is normal, since the projects are usually initiated in one way and developed differently. This difference opens up the space for the enduring attitude of ›thaumazein‹ and the doubt at the 

*[p. 198]* ^pg199

same time.[^237] It is the wonder which takes place independently, not thought: thought plays the role of the medium. Thought is independent because as medium it does not relate or correlate; it mediates what is discovered by the wonder and doubting. With the development of different forms of ›thaumazein‹, like empirical or analytical, the notion of time with the help of technologies became the medium to explore space and time; an example is Hubble’s telescope constantly filming the universe or the geodesic map, thereby exploring the movement of the Earth. The enduring attitude extends associations.

I argued that in the condition of the proliferation of images a contemporary subject more often perceives the world by associations, and time exists as far as associations take place. Time consists of associations. Time starts when associations take place, and it is interrupted with the end of associations. Because of this, memory acts as the oblivion and remembering at the same time. It is about remembering when associations are strong, and about the oblivion when they are interrupted. I refer to the classic notion that writing imitates speech by materialising it. In the reverse direction the speech becomes the confused imitation of the written word. The visual culture is structured by these paradigmatic functions of memory. The encounter with the image in the screen is not only the reminder of what happened or the well forgotten past, it is also the conjecture and the possibility of reproducing these images. In the paradigmatic attitude created by these imitations, subjects may also find a possibility to transpose images and narratives to reproduce their own imitation of the world.[^238] The imitation is an activity which performs the return inquiry that enables to correct and to bring the missing dimension.

Transposition takes place by assembling images and narration. This is the only possibility for meaning to be comprehensible. The image comes into existence with the discovery of meaning. Any meaning assumes its contra-meaning. In the same way any image assumes its ›non-existent other‹, like in an example of ›life‹: its other is ›non-life‹. Once reproduced, the

image is translated into its other and transposited. Since the existence is motivated by the ›pure difference‹, it demonstrates itself in the repetition, which is the utterance of being. The utterance keeps apart functions of opposition to make them work together without the primacy of the one over the other. They all partake of the same transposed formalism. In this transposed formalism associations play the role of technics. Since association with one image assumes its opposite non-image, this opposite de-substantialise a primary image. Associations assume disassociations immediately and this assuming gives rise to reproductions. Therefore, difference and repetition equals imitating and imitation. As a result, there is an automatic productivity of subjects which is increasingly becoming part and parcel of everyday experience. The question that remains here is the question of the imitation. The notion is that not only the future can be realised by the existential experience or creativity, which is the residual of the human subjectivity, but the matter of the technical recording of these residues is an important departure point for the ›grammatisation of the visible‹.

The question is to find the possibility of the ›grammatisation of the visible‹ within the paradigm of transposed formalism that provides the possibility to escape, which is to say to assist to overcome the default and resist the ›proletarianisation‹. I believe this can retrieve itself in the banal imitation. Since technics provides the possibility of trans-individuation, it also helps to ›grammatise‹. As I have shown elsewhere, the use of knowledge has ›grammatised‹ and shifted from the oral to the written to the programmed media. In the age of icons, logos, SMS and alike, the exteriorisation remains as an important form of ›grammatisation‹. The notion of memory or ›tertiary‹ is predicated on the dependence on the technics of recording that translates the subject. The translation will, in this sense, not depend upon the life experience but on the experiencing of the inorganic, non-living matter and of the assembling of forms following this matter from writing to numbers to sounds to image. As a result of the proliferation of images, the link between words, signs and thought is periodically broken. The image, developed from the state of derushage to its completion, plays the role of the restoration of this broken link.

This effect is masterfully demonstrated in the work of the French film-maker Chris Marker. How does the technicality of the image play the role of grammatisation of the visible? How does this constitute the subject? In the works of Marker and Stiegler, memory is an important aspect of time, image and thought. In fact, this brings into a closer relationship both an 

*[p. 200]* ^pg201

artist (Chris Marker) and a philosopher (Bernard Stiegler), as they are both engaged with the question of metaphysics through their work on the role of technics and time. It is the technics that makes the protagonist of *La Jetée*, Marker’s film, to travel in time and very convincingly connects the distorted image of the time, the broken link between the past and the present. It is also in this sense that I claim that, if the image became to play the role in the domain of thinking and not only seeing, it is because images grammatise and create new languages to bring together, perhaps not in the orderly manner, broken fragments of time. It challenges Wittgenstein’s call for looking in his famous thesis: Don’t think, look! (Wittgenstein 1922). It undermines the separation made between thought and image. Their existing apart from each other and their separation from each other is an impossible task. ›Look *and* think‹! instead of ›don’t think, look!‹. Looking means thinking and thinking means looking; thought is articulated in time, therefore recorded in memory and as such inscribed in images. Any activity of time and memory takes its beginning from the technics, from images as the inscription of the recorded memory.

›Montage-consciousness‹ is a term coined by Bernard Stiegler to describe contemporary subject’s perception of the surrounding world by associations. A parallel can be found in Chris Marker’s work. This is true of his work when his camera moves over the fragments of the city of Tokyo seen from the window of the moving train or when the character of his film finds himself in the museum of the future while thinking of the past. This affinity between the work of the filmmaker and the philosopher is the point and the crossing line of the thinking and the technics. Art is turned into technics to assist the subject to understand the role of memory, and in this process only images can create the cohesive picture of the world. In fact, the image here is everything: the sound, the gesture, the picture, the photography, the written word etc. These all together help to comprehend the infinity of the world, which consists of lists of the things in time and space. Because things of the world have been replaced or memorised by their traces, that is the image, thinking of the world is impossible without images. The strength of the work by Marker is resided in this technical feature of the image.

Here it is to note that the image as technics, which contains the traces of memory as recorded is an open question. This openness provides the further exploration of the image to constitute the subject. It is like the protagonist of Chris Marker’s film *La Jetée*, who exists in images which are 

*[p. 201]* ^pg202

rescued from the tragedies of the war and restored in order to create a certain image of the time. He is constantly moving to the place where he last met the woman, and this return to a past takes place in a future. All events taking place in this film occurs in the time of the image. This image is the restoration of the past, the present and the future, and therefore it is the time of the image. In the space created by the time of the image the past is stored for the present. What is stored is the recorded memory that enables to constitute the subject’s thought. It is because the image as the recorded memory provides an evidence to support the thought.

Technics is unthought, and once the thought is inscribed in it, technics acquires a new status and new constitution. It should be understood in a very new manner: technics provides the space for thinking. Before thinking one needs to acquire technics of thinking. This role can be played by images alongside with concepts. The image is the condensed fragmented form of the ›space-time‹. What is condensed is memory as it is recorded in the image. This is how the visible is grammatised and transformed into a language. Moreover, it can be re-grammtised again as the domain of the language. Thinking is often shaped in this manner. For instance, when we encounter the images of the people’s movement, what is called the ›migration crisis‹, we encounter parameters of thinking which influences the attitude of people in different corners of the planet.

Through this research I have repeatedly attempted to show the prominence of the image in the age of proliferation. With ›image‹ I mean anything from the alphabetic writing to the latest digital imagery. Yet my focus was on the cinematic images. It is not only the image that constitutes the subject, but it is the technics which assists the powerful translation of the recorded memory into the meaningful strata. In doing this, the mimetic functioning of the subject plays its role, since this function is the primary basis of any human activity. Like technics of bipedality was imitating the first gesture of freeing hands, in the highly developed technological societies the act of imitation (from the word ›imitari‹: making images) remains a primary gesture that enables to retain the tertiary memory that is disseminated in the complex proliferated world of the audio-visual culture. Imitation is a tool of imaging and to constitute the activity of the image as technics.

Imitation is also interesting by its ambivalence. Imitation defies two dominating tendencies of the profound thinking: the relative and the absolute. The relative is usually about having a viewpoint, to look at the world, the absolute is being immersed in the world (Bergson 1913: 1). In

*[p. 202]* ^pg203

stead of looking from a viewpoint the imitator inserts the viewpoint into the event. The absolutist point of view insists in the absolute involvement in the events, rather than standing outside in relationship to these events. But in the event of imitation the subject inserts the viewpoint from the outside to the inside and the image retains its power. It brings together the external and the internal to synchronise the power of grammatisation of the visible. In this sense the image created by imitation is neither relational nor absolutist: it is transpositional and moves towards the alterity while adopting what took place already. It is because to imitate means being inside the image, where the image itself is the remnant of the time. To imitate is also to grasp thought which is inscribed in the image. To imitate is transposing its other while being resided in the time of the image. In the world of the cultural clash between the relational and the absolute, where often populism is articulated in both, the possible is the third, the other, the transpositional: imitation. Making images is an activity of reconstructing time. Imitation is taking the image from one time to another. Wrongly understood as a simple copying of the past, it is usually forgotten that the event of imitation has a lot to offer, since it takes on the images with their history of contestation and may be a good answer to the industrial populism.
