Introduction 

Capitalism has lost its spirit: spiritual misery reigns.' Control societies have become uncontrollable, profoundly irrational, without reason, unable to inspire hope. Those who no longer believe they have anything whatsoever to expect from the development of hyperindustrial capitalism are increasingly numerous. 

Here I shall further examine the causes and consequences of capitalism’s loss of spirit through a critical reading of The New Spirit of Capitalism by Luc Boltanski and Eve Chiapello.2 They elaborate the idea that 1968 paved the way for a new age of capitalism. This idea is interesting, suggestive and frequently persuasive. What The New Spirit of Capitalism calls ‘artistic critique’ is specifically the discourse of May 1968 as a critique of the ‘bourgeois’ super-ego. According to Boltanski and Chiapello, it is this ‘artistic critique’ that would lie at the origin of a ‘new spirit” of capitalism. 

The interest in distinguishing ‘artistic critique’, as the authors of The New Spirit of Capitalism propose to call it, from what they refer to as ‘social critique’, is certainly understandable. Even so, I myself do not believe that ‘artistic critique’ has in any way been the cause of that mutation of capitalism that has led control societies to themselves become uncontrollable. My own belief is, rather, that 1968 was a sudden translation, a rising to the surface, of much older, subterranean processes that had been operating since at least the capitalism of the late nineteenth century, insofar as the latter constituted a new form of libidinal economy. 

*[p. 2]* ^pg8

The convulsions of 1968 were, on the contrary, the first political, economic and social symptoms of capitalism’s loss of spirit, which consists in a process of desublimation whose effects are being felt to their full extent as the destruction of spirit by capitalism. 

The way this process has developed since 1968 seems to be heading towards the destruction of capitalism; that is, to the total liquidation of the spirit of capitalism by this very capitalism itself. Libido, which is the energy of capitalism, is tending towards exhaustion, tending to fade, and is taking sublimation, the power of socialization, with it, giving way to the rule of the drives. The New Spirit of Capitalism fails to see the advent of this state of affairs, believing, on the contrary, that a ‘new spirit’ reigns. 

This analytical error arises from a perfunctory understanding of the philosophical references that, according to the authors, were mobilized by ‘artistic critique’. In particular, the significance of the thought of Herbert Marcuse is passed over in complete silence, despite the fact that it played a well-known role in the political currents at the origin of the events of 1968 in the United States, Germany, and especially France, and even though it consisted precisely in challenging the assumption that ‘artistic critique’ could ever be separated from ‘social critique’. 

For what Marcuse suggests is that psychopathological questions are, much more fundamentally, sociopathological questions. And this leads him, correctly, to criticize the Freudian tendency to ontologize historical situations. My own view, however, is that Marcuse’s critique of Freud is indeed legitimate, but is itself based on a misreading of Freud that is actually regressive in relation to the advances of Freud, advances that lead to thinking in terms of the composition of tendencies rather than the opposition of principles. 

. 

Here, the classically Marxist position held by Marcuse, which thinks in terms of class struggle, is an obstacle to understanding that which is completely new in Freud. At the same time, Marcuse repeats and accentuates the, weaknesses of Freud’s own discourse in relation to technics and industry, leading him to cling to the discourse of the emancipation of pleasure and the liberation of the instincts that is so typical of 1968, even though he had shown in 1955 that capitalism is the first and the true beneficiary of the 

*[p. 3]* ^pg9

liquidation of all super-egoistic barriers, and the true operator of the desublimation process. 

A critique of Marcuse, here, would involve showing that, in order to think ethics, manners and morality — that is, the super-ego - in their relation to justice and law — that is, to politics - at a time when capitalism is substituting the authority of the super-ego with what Marcuse already called, with good reason, the automatization of the super-ego, we must think the originary technicity of desire, and we must think it as a process of adoption, that is, as an originary potential for the libido to be diverted towards libidinal objects: libido is the originary diversion or redirection of libido, as the default of origin of the libido, which is also to say, as perversion. Marcuse, on the other hand, believed in the possibility of unearthing a golden age of the libido, which it would be a matter of recovering through revolutionary struggle, a golden age in which the pleasure principle would dominate the reality principle, where the ‘instincts’ would be ‘liberated’. Such reasoning fails to grasp that pleasure, which is formed through its confrontation with reality, derives from a composition of tendencies rather than from their opposition — the entire question being the binding of these tendencies through desire, and of their decomposition, the latter leading to the reign of the drives, that is, to spiritual misery. 

This is what results — as I argued at length in Uncontrollable Societies of Disaffected Individuals\* — from a systematic exploitation by marketing of addictive situations. Here, this analysis will be further refined, by positing that the object of desire is always, in its very structure, an object of addiction. The desired object is the object on which the subject of desire depends. Now, this dependence is also what Plato described in relation to hypomnesis, that is, in relation to that prosthesis of memory that is writing, which he referred to as a pharmakon. 

As technologies of the mind or spirit, that is, as supports of otium, of care, cura, ot, in Greek, therapeuma,' hypomnemata - the question of which was raised in The Decadence of Industrial Democracies’ — are pharmaka, that is, as much poisons as remedies. From this it follows that politics - understood as the care that a society takes of itself, a care that is inherently perverse, given that its spiritual and social energy, libido, by its very nature 

*[p. 4]* ^pg10

attaches itself to that which destroys it — faced as it is with this careless negligence \[incurie\] that hyperindustrial capitalism has become, must be thought as a libidinal ecology. 

This prospect is opened up in the conclusion of this work, with a view to drafting positive proposals concerning the new form of public power and of the industrial politics of technologies of the |
spirit that will be the subject of the fourth volume of Disbelief and Discredit.
