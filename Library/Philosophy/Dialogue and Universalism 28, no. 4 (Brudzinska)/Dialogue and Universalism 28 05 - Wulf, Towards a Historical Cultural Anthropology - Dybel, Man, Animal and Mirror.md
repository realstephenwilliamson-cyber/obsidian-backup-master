TOWARDS A HISTORICAL CULTURAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

*ABSTRACT* 

In today’s globalized world anthropology is a transdisciplinary and trans-cultural field of research. In the here-proposed concept it encompasses five paradigms: 1) hominization/evolution, 2) philosophical anthropology, 3) historical anthropology/mentality research, 4) cultural anthropology, 5) historical cultural anthropology. Anthropology contributes to the understanding of the human being at the beginning of the 21st century. Anthropology is characterized by a double historicity and culturality; it encompasses a great variety of research questions, methods and approaches and includes philosophical thinking and self-criticism. 

Keywords: hominization, evolution, philosophical anthropology, historical anthropology, mentality, cultural anthropology, historical cultural anthropology, mimesis, ritual. 

Over the 20th century the influence of anthropology was steadily growing, particularly in philosophy. Max Scheler explains that our era is the first in about ten thousand years of history in which man has become “problematic” through and through, but also the first in which he does not know who he actually is, and at the same time knows that he does not know.[^1] This situation also constitutes the starting point of pedagogical anthropology, which in the second half of the twentieth century developed into a very important field of educational knowledge. 

According to Immanuel Kant, the human situation is as follows: What human beings are depends on what they must be and on what they may be. Human beings are nothing in themselves and must make themselves into who they are and turn themselves into who they must become, whilst in so doing they frequently hurl themselves against their very own limits. ANTHROPOLOGICAL PARADIGMS 

If we wish to put the epistemology of anthropology on a more profoundly reflected footing, then a confrontation, at once critical and constructive, with the anthropological paradigms that are internationally significant is indispensable. I am referring to: 

— evolution and hominization, — the philosophical anthropology developed in Germany, — the historical anthropology and the history of mentalities initiated by historians in France and taking its cue from the *Annales-School*, 

— the North-American tradition of cultural anthropology, and — historical cultural anthropology. 

To provide a framework for anthropology, I suggest we use the paradigm of historical cultural anthropology as a basis for further research.[^4] This paradigm integrates the perspectives of the other four major anthropological paradigms, and provides a basis for an adequate understanding of phenomena, processes and institutions in a globalized world. Together with historical and philosophical methods, the ethnographic approach is one of the main methods of anthropology understood as historical and cultural anthropology. 

EVOLUTION AND HOMINIZATION 

The branch of anthropology that studies hominization stems from an attempt to fit the natural history of human beings into the horizon of anthropology, to thus gain more understanding of the human being. Yet the natural history of human evolution can be understood only when viewed as a part of social and cultural history. Its irreversibility, like that of the history of life itself, is today perceived as a consequence of material self-organization.

Hominization, the long process of evolution from the Australopithecus to primitive human beings, can be understood as a multi-dimensional morphogenesis arising from the interplay between ecological, genetic, cerebral, social and cultural factors. This process necessitated three types of change. The first was the ecological change which led to the expansion of the savannah and thus to an “open” biotope. Second, a genetic change took place in the highly developed primates, which were already walking upright. Third was the change in social self-reproduction due to the splitting off of young groups and the use of new territories. It was a new ecosystem—the savannah—which triggered the dialectic between the feet, hands and brain and which became the source of technology and all other human developments. The process of hominization was intensified by a prolonged infancy or neoteny, incomplete development of the brain at birth, and prolonged childhood with longer affective ties between the generations, with the associated potentials enhancing comprehensive cultural learning. Cerebralization, prolonged youth and increased social and cultural complexity were mutually dependent. The complexity of the brain requires a corresponding socio-cultural complexity. The creative potential of the brain can be expressed and develop only in a socio-cultural environment that grows in parallel. This dialectic relationship means that humans have been cultural beings from the very beginning, i.e., their “natural” development is cultural. 

The final stage of this process of hominization is, in fact, also a beginning. The human species, which has reached its completion in *Homo sapiens*, is a youthful and childlike species: our brilliant brains would be feeble organs without the apparatus of culture; all our capabilities need to be bottle-fed. Hominization was completed with the irreversible and fundamental creative incompleteness of human beings. The course of hominization also clearly illustrates that *Homo sapiens* and *Homo demens* are inseparably linked and the great achievements of humankind have their downside: the horrors and atrocities perpetrated by humans.<sup>5</sup> 

PHILOSOPHICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

While taking evolution into account in anthropology serves to highlight the shared lineage and mutual parentage of all forms of life and the long time-span of hominization as well as the general laws of evolution, philosophical anthropology turns its attention to the particularity of “man’s” character. 

The centerpieces of philosophical anthropology are the anthropological works of Max Scheler, Helmuth Plessner and Arnold Gehlen. In 1927, Max Scheler gave a lecture in Darmstadt entitled “Die Sonderstellung des Menschen,” which was published in 1928 under the title *Die Stellung des Menschen im*

*Kosmos* \[The Human Place in the Cosmos\] and is regarded as the beginning of philosophical anthropology. When Scheler died in the same year, he left no concrete preparatory material for the anthropological work he had intended to publish in 1929. The philosopher and biologist Helmuth Plessner, however, published his main anthropological work *Die Stufen des Organischen und der Mensch* \[Levels of Organic Being and Man\] in 1929. Despite their differences, Scheler’s seminal article[^6] and Plessner’s book[^7] share the assumption that organic life is structured into levels. Arnold Gehlen’s work[^8] took a different approach and focuses on humans as acting beings. 

The preoccupation of this strand of anthropological thought was to understand the essence, the nature of human beings in general. Within this framework, anthropology concentrated upon a comparison between *“*man” and animal,[^9] with a view to distinguishing shared features and differences. To grasp the *conditio humana*, philosophical reflections were brought to bear upon biological insights. It was thought that the conditions for the formation of the human species could be glimpsed in biological and above all morphological characteristics. This perspective has had two consequences: a focus of anthropological reflection and research on the human body, and a generalizing discourse relating to *one* unique and unitary model of man. 

Due to its focus on *the* human being as such, philosophical anthropology fails to address the historical and cultural diversity of human beings in the plural. To investigate the diversity of human life is the aim of the branch of historical science that is oriented towards anthropological issues.

HISTORICAL CULTURAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

 The specific situation of human beings in the world, the comparison with animals or machines, is no longer anthropology’s center of interest. Instead, historical and cultural inquiries focus on understanding the cultural diversity of social life. Noticeable is a particular and very pronounced interest in the study of current phenomena. Expanding on historical anthropology, historical cultural anthropology touches upon the historical and cultural determination of culture and its manifestations, and demands that their study and reflection take into account ethnological and philosophical perspectives and questions. Committed to this task, historical cultural anthropology makes an important contribution to the contemporary self-comprehension and self-interpretation of cultures and societies. In this process of cultural understanding, research efforts rapidly run the risk of being unable to move beyond the level of their own initial insights. To safeguard against this risk, historical cultural anthropology needs to reflect upon its relation to power and knowledge, as well as to make efforts specifically aimed at bringing to light the involuntary and often unacknowledged normative implications of its own research. 

Within this frame of reference, “historical cultural anthropology” designates multiform transdisciplinary and transnational efforts to pursue the universal idea of abstract anthropological norm and to continue analyzing diverse human phenomena. Historical cultural anthropology is the common denominator of history and the humanities. Nevertheless, it is not limited to the history of anthropology as a discipline, nor to contributing to history from the perspective of an anthropological sub-discipline. It rather attempts to bring into an accord the historical and cultural determination of its perspectives and methods with the historical and cultural determination of its object of study. It can harness insights gleaned in the humanities to those yielded by a critique of anthropology based on the history of philosophy, and bring both to fruition in order to create new perspectives and lines of inquiry out of a new consciousness of methodological problems. Historical anthropology is limited neither to certain spatial frames nor to particular epochs. Reflecting on its own historicity and its own cultural condition, it succeeds both in leaving behind the Eurocentrism of the humanities, and moving from an interest in history to concern about current and future problems.[^16]

MIMETIC PROCESSES 

Mimesis is not only an aesthetic but also an anthropological concept. First of all, mimetic processes play an important role in social and cultural life: we acquire most of our cultural and social competencies through mimetic processes. Secondly, mimetic processes are not processes of copying or mere imitation, but rather active and creative processes in which one draws, from something or someone, an impression, on the basis of which one perpetrates one's own acts and creates one’s own works. In human action, there is no such thing as simple repetition. Each repetition is a transformation, and at times even a whole new creation. This is particularly true of mimetic processes in which the reference to a model, or another world in the broader sense, is exactly that which allows us to create something which is in fact entirely novel. The model or the reference world is necessary for producing something novel in the realm of individual action, in social interaction. Frequently, the condition motivating an act of reference is the hope of becoming like the point of reference, nevertheless, becoming similar to something is not an indispensable precursor for the birth of mimetic processes. What is much more decisive is that a relationship be established, a relationship which in principle may just as well translate itself into a demarcation, into the hope not to be like the other.[^17] 

I would like to insist upon the great importance and frequency of the mimetic dimension in the cultural world. At the same time, mimesis must not be misunderstood, must not be grasped in that false sense which would make of it a universal phenomenon that is not culturally diverse. Acts are mimetic only if: 

— they are movements referring to other movements; — they can be considered as physical scenes which in addition possess a representative as well as a demonstrative aspect; 

— and if they are not just autonomous and admit of being understood in themselves, but also make reference to other acts or other worlds.

Mimetic processes play an important role in the perception of music and sounds. They allow sounds to be perceived in an unreduced form, which is vital for their understanding as sounds. This is an important issue in the study of sounds, where it is essential to avoid reducing sounds to talking about sounds. As the human body is also the site of sounds, people live in a mental world not only of images but also of heterogeneous sounds. However, none of the possible structuring activities result in the listener’s full mastery of the world of sounds. This makes us aware of how little control we have over sounds and opens us up to the aesthetic experience, whereby the identity of the sounds cannot itself be represented by anything else. Music and sounds have an intrinsic quality that constantly refers the listener back to the nature of sound as sound. What René Char said of poetry also applies to sounds: sounds know something about us that we would not know otherwise. They contain an element of surprise that we cannot foresee. 

The mimetic treatment of sounds leads to sound experiences. In mimetic processes people “recreate” sounds with the aid of listening, and store them in their mental worlds of sounds. The recreation of sounds is a process of mimetic acquisition in which the sounds, in their essence as sounds, are stored in the worlds of sounds and memory. The mimetic processing of sounds has the purpose of acquiring their essence as sounds, which persists before, during, after, and outside of any interpretation. Once sounds are stored in the mental world of sounds, they form the points of reference for interpretations. Regardless of each interpretation, the repeated mimetic treatment of sounds is an act of acquisition, or even cognition. It involves concentration and effort in the recreation of the mental sounds and continually demands that sounds be refreshed in an acoustic encounter with the real sounds or their reproductions. The acoustic re-creation of sounds requires the suppression of sounds and thoughts emerging from the mental world of the listener; the mimetic acquisition of sounds requires us to retain the sequence of sounds while we are listening, to open ourselves up to its sonic character, and to surrender ourselves to it. The mimetic process involves us as listeners in allowing ourselves to become similar to sounds through acoustically recreating them, absorbing them, and augmenting our mental world of sounds with them.

PERSPECTIVES 

As the influence of normative anthropologies has waned, anthropological research has begun to pay more attention to the body with its natality and mortality, which is both the product and the agent of its own socialization and enculturation. The lived human body is the result of multifarious mimetic processes that include not merely imitation, but active acquisition of cultural knowledge. In these mimetic processes culture is produced, handed down, and transformed. Here the performativity of the body, how it is staged and enacted, plays an important role. Performativity is of particular significance for language processes, cultural performances, and aesthetics. If rituals, for example, are perceived and interpreted only as “texts,” they lack a dimension that is associated with their material and bodily aspects. This led me to focus on the performative side of social practices and ritual acts in a large research project entitled “Berlin Study on Rituals and Gestures,” in which I examined these aspects in detail, taking a close look at how social practices, rituals and ritualization contribute to the performative formation of communities, and how they shape educational processes and promote learning.[^19] In these investigations, the material nature and

The fragmentation and “deterritorialization” of contemporary anthropology hold considerable potential for the development of new modes of anthropological reflection and research. They also provide us with an opportunity to free ourselves from the outdated traditions of our discipline, and to redefine the horizons of anthropology. Increasingly important in this redefinition are the perspectives that are emerging from globalization. Among other things, they have engendered criticism of the neoliberal economic trends that are marginalizing the social market economy and of the associated tendency for many societies to become more and more alike. Today, giving anthropology a global orientation means to open it up for research in all the societies and cultures of the world and to address the issue of what will be the most important conditions of human life in the future. 

Anthropology is a decentralized, polycentric science in which problems of representation, interpretation, the construction of deconstruction, and thus also methodological diversity are of central importance. This shows that in non-European cultures historicity and culturality are also significant dimensions of anthropological knowledge.<sup>20</sup> Through focusing on these dimensions, this research is making an important contribution to human self-comprehension in the 21st century. 

In what follows I present an outline of selected aspects of anthropology that cannot claim to be complete and will require further explanation and specification.<sup>21</sup> Now that the abstract anthropological norm that centered mainly on the ideas, images, values, and norms of Western culture has ceased to be binding, anthropology constitutes an attempt to conduct research on human phenomena in the conditions of a globalized world. As a result of this development, anthropological research is no longer fundamentally restricted to certain defined cultural areas or individual epochs. The aim of anthropological research is to contribute to a better understanding and better explanations of human phenomena and problems in our globalized world and thus also to a better understanding between people. The lively debates on the historical involvement of sections of anthropology in colonialism and racism, the problem of representation, and the extent to which the other can “speak back” are evidence of efforts to broaden the horizons of anthropology and open it up for new tasks. 

There is a *dual historicity and culturality* in anthropology that arises from the historicity and culturality of the different perspectives of anthropological researchers, and from the historical and cultural character of the contents and subjects of the research. The historicity and culturality of the anthropologists themselves form the background against which the phenomena and

New research questions and methodologies develop in a reciprocal relationship as researchers reflect upon this dual historicity and culturality. The approach to anthropology that I present here employs both *diachronic* and *synchronic* methods to investigate human societies and cultures. In addition to anthropological issues and the hermeneutic and text-critical methods from the historical sciences that are applied diachronically, field research with its numerous qualitative and quantitative methods still plays an important role as a method of synchronous anthropological research. The interpretative and reflexive methods, in particular, offer the possibility of lending expression to the individual and subjective perspectives. 

Many anthropological research projects are *inter- or transdisciplinary* and *multi*- or *transcultural*. Because of their *transdisciplinary* nature, many studies transcend the limits of traditional disciplines and yield new insight by examining new research questions and objects of research, by using new procedures and looking at things from new perspectives. The attempt to include multi- or transcultural aspects in anthropological research is also leading to the development of new research questions and perspectives that play important roles, particularly within the context of international anthropological research networks. 

One new challenge, and one that anthropologists have long failed to address, is how to define the *relationship between general insights and specific insights* relating to human beings as individuals and human beings in general. While in archaeology, biological anthropology, and linguistic anthropology it is permissible to make universal statements about human beings and humans, in historical and cultural anthropological approaches the emphasis is more on being able to use hermeneutic methods to make complex statements about particular historical-cultural phenomena. These approaches are oriented toward the exploration and assurance of cultural diversity. However, even when we are concerned with cultural diversity, the question still arises as to what is common to all human beings. In these times of globalization it is becoming increasingly important for anthropology to investigate the relationship between similarities and differences among human beings, cultures, and historical epochs.<sup>22</sup> In this context the question about the role of comparison in both diachronic and synchronic research in anthropology has taken on a significance that we urgently need to clarify. In my view, the aim of anthropological research is not to reduce but to increase the complexity of our knowledge about human beings. This requires interpretation, reflection and self-criticism, and calls for an ongoing, philosophically inspired critique of anthropology that must include an examination of the fundamental limits of human self-interpretation. MAN, ANIMAL, AND MIRROR. ORIGINS OF THE HUMAN “I” 

ACCORDING TO HELMUTH PLESSNER 

AND JACQUES LACAN 

*ABSTRACT* 

In the article I compare the concept of the human “I” of Helmuth Plessner that underlies his philosophical anthropology with the theory of “mirror stage” by Jacques Lacan. Both they have been inspired by the experiment of Wolfgang Köhler in which a child and chimpanzee reacted differently to their image in a mirror. Plessner and Lacan drew different conclusions from this experiment. Plessner maintained that the child who recognizes its image in the mirror as its own takes into account the possibility of its replacement by other “I” on the level of its social roles and functions. Yet, at the same time it knows very well that nobody will be able to replace its own individual “I.” While Lacan says that the ideal image of “I” has the status of the defence-symptom representing an alternative to the feeling of dismembered body that the child experiences in the early phase of its life. This image of “I” is not given forever but is always endangered by the possibility of destruction and regress on its early stage of the dismembered body. The telling testimony of this is the *passage au acte* by psychotics in which the ouburst of aggression is accompanied by the destruction of “I.” Or the cases in which the I assumes the pathologically exaggerated shape. This possibility of destruction of I that is inherently rooted in its structure has been misrecognized by Plessner. 

Keywords: I, ideal I, dismembered body, mirror image, psychosis, narcissism, personality. 

In his essay *Zur Frage der Vergleichbarkeit tierischen und menschlichen Verhaltens* \[How to Compare Human and Animal Behaviour\] Helmuth Plessner cites the well-known experiment by Wolfgang Köhler, who observed the behaviour of a young chimpanzee and a small child in the front of a mirror. Although the chimpanzee reacted to its mirror image it only recognised a specimen from its own species rather than itself, while the child was able to identify the reflection as its own image. When describing this experiment, Jacques Lacan comes 

*[p. 126]* ^pg128

to the conclusion that unlike animals, man possesses a notion of himself or herself as an individual “I.” This notion is grounded in the way the self relates to others; this way defines the “interchangeability of points of view determining the perspective”[^1-p126] forming the foundation of all social relations. 

This interchangeability means that man can imagine himself as one of many possible “I’s,” potentially replaceable by another. But at the same time he knows that it is virtually impossible to realize this possibility. Although the other can can take the place I am now in, he can take over my various roles and functions, professional, social, etc., he cannot become myself; he cannot identify with my “I.” Because when a human individual enters social roles which are “interchangeable” with other people, he has the possibility of withdrawing into himself. And thanks to this possibility he “occupies a specific place, an absolute ‘here,’ and despite changing circumstances he can invariably identify the other.”[^2-p126] Thus, he experiences himself as “uninterchangeable” (*unverwechselbar*), that this not replaceable by the other in what constitutes his individual “I.” 

 In this approach, the possibility to withdraw into oneself allows man to distance himself from any social roles, opening him to the individual dimension of his own existence, which Lacan calls personality. The reverse side of man’s social relations with others is his sense of individual identity, grounded in his ability to withdraw into himself. As a result, he is capable of an objectifying distance to his own body and to others, unlike animals. 

 This situation is paradoxical. Precisely because man can imagine someone else occupying his place, he discovers that as “one of many” he is irreplaceable in his individuality. The sense of one’s own individuality becomes possible only when opposed the notion of oneself as interchangeable with other “I’s.” The paradox is based on the fact that imagining himself as one of many man can achieve a distance to himself and realise that he is irreplaceable in his individuality. 

 Therefore, looking at his image in the mirror man sees himself as irreplaceable in his “I” and he is capable of distinguishing himself from other “I’s.” But an animal, incapable of imagining itself as “one of many” with an individual personality, cannot achieve such distance to itself, allowing it to identify its own image as differing from the images of others. When it looks into the mirror, it does not see “itself,” but the “other” as a representative of its own species. 

 Interestingly, Köhler’s experiment was invoked at an early stage by the French psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan, who based on it his theory of the “mirror stage.” But he points to other aspects of this experiment and he draws different conclusions regarding the foundations of human identity.[^3-p126]

 Lacan perceives the difference between the way man and animal relate to the mirror image of their own body in the fascination of the child with its mirror image. The child treats this image as if it were the image of its own body. This is the other of the child, the unachievable prototype it will try to match up to from now on. The different approaches of Plessner and Lacan to the role played by the notion of yourself as the “I” in human development raises the question of defining the ontological status of the “I” in both the conceptions. According to Plessner, man differs from other creatures through being a person, which, as Kant says, may assume the shape of the “I,” among other notions. And the “I” “means a special space which I occupy with my body, from which I see and act.”[^10-p131] 

 And slightly further he adds: 

 “The meaning of this word is revealed to the child only a couple years later thanks to the formulations of language. Only later it starts to understand that the ‘I’ means the source of its own actions, the possibility of withdrawing ‘inside oneself’—and hence the possibility to burden it with responsibility.”[^11-p131] 

In this approach, the notion of himself as the “I,” proper to man, has a special status among other notions. It results from the fact that man relates the notion of himself as the “I” to his being in some “here,” where another “I” could as well find itself. And this assumes in turn that imagining himself as the “I” man is capable of distancing himself from himself. Only then he can imagine some different “I” in his own place. In other words, only because in his notion of himself as the “I” man relates to himself as to his own “other,” interchangeable with other “I’s,” he can imagine that the place of his own “other” is occupied by some other “I.” 

 The self-reflexive structure embedded in the notion of yourself as the “I” is connected with the “mirror relation” between man/“I” and others in the social space. Plessner calls this relation the “structure of mutuality,” claiming that it is rooted in personality: “For now, the most crucial is only the structure of mutuality grounded in personality as such, a structure which precisely assigns one person to another, regardless of what it is and what it has.”[^12-p131] 

 Thanks to this structure, imagining himself as the „I” one person is assigned to another person/persons (another “I”/“I’s”) in the social space. The human notion of himself as the “I” already contains relating to other “I’s,” which can take his place. Due to the “structure of mutuality” defining it, this notion provides the foundation for human social being. And although the “I” may as well take the place of another “I” and play his social role, in fact only one of the “I” can do it. In other words, because someone else can take my place, he is capable of dominating me, precisely because he does not take this place in a literal, but

[^11-p131]: Ibid., 236.
[^12-p131]: Ibid., 238.

*[p. 132]* ^pg134

in a metaphoric sense (in my notion of the “I”). The former situation occurs only when the other kills me. But this is tantamount to destroying the “structure of mutuality” defining our relation to each other as “I’s.” Accepting the other’s dominantion by the “I” occurs only through the possibility, grounded in the experience of mutuality of perspectives, of the “I” withdrawing into itself. Only by withdrawing into itself the “I” can accept the domination of the other in the social space. This kind of distancing yourself from yourself is required by the act of accepting someone’s domination, unknown in the animal world. The domination of the other may be accepted by me only as the “I,” which in the act of acceptance—that is withdrawing into itself—defined its relation to the other. This means that when accepting the domination of the other, it never reduces itself to it. The possibility of choosing another relation to the other and to itself always remains within it. 

Therefore the assigning of the “I” to the other, of which Plessner speaks as an inevitable result of “the mutuality of perspectives,” needs not necessarily mean making the “I” dependent on the other. For the same reason, man as the “I” may resist the domination of the other. As „withdrawn in himself” he is never reduced—as the “I”—to accepting this domination. Likewise, I as an individual “I” can never be certain of the permanent nature of my domination over others. Recognising my domination, others may always revoke their act of recognition. But the existence of such a possibility—and it inherently follows from the “structure of mutuality”—lends a special quality to all acts of accepting someone else’s domination in the social space, incomparable to what we are used to call domination in the animal world. 

3\. 

Lacan definitely agrees with Plessner in one point. The “I” is a notion of yourself whose the primal “prototype” is the mirror image of your body with which you identify. They also shared the belief that this notion had a special status because of its structure. But they attributed a different ontological status to the notion of the “I.” 

 For Plessner imagining yourself as the “I” appears naturally at an early stage of human development and from then on it defines man’s relation to himself, to others and to the world. It is similar in Kant and the whole philosophical tradition inspired by him. The notion of the “I” is treated here as closely connected to the self-reflexive structure of human consciousness, so in a sense it is self-explanatory. 

 The contours of Kant’s philosophy also embrace Plessner’s claim about the “mutuality of perspectives” implied by the manner in which the human “I” relates to others. The source of inspiration for Plessner is the formula of “asocial sociability,” which Kant uses to demonstrate the ambivalence of man’s relating to others. New here is Plessner’s pointing at the connection between the emergence of the notion of “I” and the development of linguistic 

*[p. 133]* ^pg135

skills in the child. But Plessner’s comments on this matter are incidental and often vague. 

 Ultimately, in Plessner’s approach the human “I” is an autonomous instance within consciousness possessing a self-reflexive structure. This means that the attitude of man to others is defined by mutuality of perspectives, which lies at the foundation of his social being. 

 Lacan, in his turn, connects the process of emergence of the human “I” with the situation when the child, confronted with sensing its own body as fragmented, succumbs to a fascination with its fictitious mirror reflection, recognising in it the longed-for ideal unity of itself. The child is fascinated with this image, because it finds in it a counterweight to the unbearable schizophrenic feeling of its own body as being dismembered. This approach assumes that the equally painful sense of “chaos” of the child’s body is the condition for the fascination with its ideal prototype of the “I” and identifying with it. 

 Unlike Plessner (or Kant), Lacan believes that the notion of himself as the (ideal) “I,” proper to man, does not appear spontaneously. The “I” is not a self-explanatory ideal form of human self-consciousness, called by Kant “transcendental unity of apperception” accompanying all kinds of representation. This form may constitute itself in this shape only on the basis of the individual experiencing his body as fragmented. In Lacan’s approach, the notion of ideal “I” appears as a result of a defensive act where the individual wants to resist the destructive experience of chaos within himself. Therefore 

 “the formation of the ‘I’ is onirically symbolised by the image of a fortress or sportsground, its terrain fenced off from the outside area, separated from the surrounding muds and garbage dumps, forming two diverse battlegrounds on which the self feverishly searches for a distant and lofty inner castle—its form \[…\] very expressively symbolising the Id. Such fortifying structures can also be found in thinking. This metaphor appears spontaneously, as if deriving from the symptoms themselves, to describe the mechanisms of obsessive neurosis: reversion, isolation, reduplication, invalidation, displacement.”<sup>13</sup> The ideal “I” is imagined by the individual as a kind of “fortress,” intended to protect him from the “muds and garbage damps” stretching around him. Therefore, as Lacan later said, in the economy of human mental life the “I” is a defensive symptom, which does not have a “positive,” but only a negative justification: as a fortress/defence against what poses a danger to it. 

 The ideal “I” only emerges as a result of the individual’s attempted escape from the experience of “chaos” of himself, which destroys him and undermines him from inside. Only thanks to imagining himself as an ideal “defensive for-

<sup>13</sup> Lacan, J. 2006, op. cit., 96. 

*[p. 134]* ^pg136

tress” rising above the marshy muds and garbage dumps, the individual may achieve control over his own body. He then recognises it as “potential” unity, with processes and impulses that he will be able to control in the future. This will allow him to enter the cultural-social space as an “I” with a permanent sense of self-identity. 

 It does not mean that this process must always take place or that it is not subject to disruptions or deformations. “I”-fortress can be destroyed or not emerge at all. In such a case the relation of the individual to himself is dominated by a psychotic sense of his own body being fragmented, which leads to the formation of an unstable identity, where the ideal “I” is replaced by a bloated omnipotent “I” (“I”-Christ, “I”-Buddha, “I”-Napoleon). 

4\. 

Lacan’s ideal “I” imagined as a defensive fortress is an illusion, a fictional image of oneself produced by the mirror image with which an individual identifies, narcissistically casting himself in its role. The reverse side of this identification is the inherent possibility of disintegration, regression to the early stage of experiencing your own body as fragmented. The attitude of fascination with this “I” is transformed into the attitude of aggression towards itself or others, revealed in the situation when the individual notices some scar on the ideal image of himself or the other he identifies with. Therefore, the “I”—as Lacan’s says—is a symptom which can disintegrate under specific circumstances. This triggers an explosion of uncontrolled aggression against oneself or others, which may lead to suicide or acts of murder. The individual falls into a psychotic frenzy, completely losing self-control, as in the case of the Papin sisters. Under the impact of criticism coming from the houseowners, a regression took place in their minds to a phase preceding the “mirror stage,” namely of experiencing your own body as fragmented, the source of which was the traumatic experience of one of the sisters, who had been raped as a child by her father, an experience “absorbed” by the other sister by means of projection. 

Calling the human “I” a symptom in the mirror stage, Lacan radicalises Freud’s critique of the autonomy of the human “I.” The latter, speaking about the “I” as a servant who does not quite know how to reconcile the contradictory demands addressed to him by the Super-I and It, nevertheless attibuted some autonomy to the “I.” For him the “I” was a partly separate instance, because unlike the Superego and Id it wanted to respect the “reality principle” in its actions. Similar themes regarding the “I,” present in Freud’s writings, has been later developed by Anna Freud and various strains of American psychoanalysis called self-psychology. 

 But for Lacan the “I” as a symptom does not possess this kind of autonomy. Its emergence in the shape of the “ideal I” expresses a desperate attempt at finding some counterweight for the child’s experience of its own body as a chaos of separate parts. But this “counterweight” is weak and fragile, for there is no 

*[p. 135]* ^pg137

guarantee that once it emerges in the child’s fantasies, this “I” will never disintegrate. This idea has been later modified, when Lacan recognised the “stabilising” role of the symbolic order. But this is a subject for another article. 

5\. 

Let us dwell a little longer on the situation when the child is confronted with its mirror image. As I already mentioned, Lacan does not claim that the child immediately recognises its own image in it. At first the child recognises it as some “ideal” other “I” which it would want to be because—as it imagines—it would allow it to achieve control over its own “fragmented” body. The role of such another ideal “I” may as well be played by the image of the mother or another person from the child’s surroundings. In order to go through the emirror stage”, man does not need a mirror at all. The mirror is a metaphor, it describes this period in human life when the role of the ideal image of yourself, a prototype for the development of your personality and imagining yourself as the “I,” can be played by the image of another body you perceive. 

 A small child does not yet clearly distinguish the image of another self seen in the mirror from the image of another other in the surrounding world. At first, this difference is not significant for it, what counts is the ideal nature of this image, which makes the child perceive it as an image of itself regardless of whom in represents in the physical sense. This other whom I see is me, because this is what I would like to be. Fascinated with this image, I appropriate it for myself and become it. 

 An eloquent manifestation of this is infantile transitivism.[^14-p135] A child beats another child and cries, as if it has been hit itself. It happens because at this stage of development the child does not yet distinguish its “ideal” image from the image of others (i.e. from the image of its mother or its playmates). At first, it takes all these images for images of itself. This behaviour results from the fact that taking the image of another child for its own, it cannot bear the thought that it does not control the behaviour of “the other one.” We see a trace of this infantile frustration in the irritation of adult people who look at themselves in a mirror and try to comb their hair, apply lipstick or shave, and “the other one” makes identical movements in the opposite direction. So if the image of another’s body fascinates the child, it is because it looks to it like the desired ideal unity rather than because it is the image of “its” body.[^15-p135] 

 It is only with time that the child begins to distinguish the mirror image of itself, of its own body, from images of others, achieving this sense of irreplaceable individuality of its own “I” about which Plessner writes. So both the

The other is “I” in the literal sense, uninterchangeable. So when the child sees the image of its mother’s body, the child “is” this body; when it sees the image of its playmate, it “is” this playmate. Equally literal is the attitude of the child to the image of itself seen in a mirror. At first, there is nothing on the other side of the mirror. 

 But over time everyday experiences teach the child that it is not quite so. It reaches towards the mirror and the “other” does the same, but seems to do it with the wrong hand; it touches its cheek and the other one does the same, but on the wrong side of the face, etc. Not to speak about these “behaviours” of others over which the child has no control at all. This is the source of the child’s aggression—frustrated by the disharmony between its own gestures and how the mirror image of itself as the “other” behaves, it unleashes all its anger on it. 

 Ultimately, the attitude of the child to the ideal image of itself as the “I” is full of ambivalence. On the one hand, this image is for it a source of narcissistic self-fascination, on the other hand, it contains an alien element and produces frustration. This ambivalence of the child’s attitude to itself shows some affinities with Kant’s claim about the “asocial sociability” of man, invoked by Plessner. Kant said that man directed by ambition, lust for power or wealth, tends to attain “distinction for himself among his beared fellows without whom he, howver, cannot live.[^16-p136] 

 Working on the anthropological implications of this idea, Plessner observes that the striving to achieve distinction among others results from the fact that 

“ ‘wherever’ a person is, that is whatever place he or she occupies in the social structure, another person could also find himself or herself there, for they remain in a mutual mirror-like relation \[…\] The most crucial is only the structure of mutuality grounded in personality as such, a structure which precisely assigns one person to another, regardless of what it is and what it has.”[^17-p136] Lying at the heart of the Kantian idea of “asocial sociability” is the possibility, determining man’s social being, of taking the place of every other in the social structure by the “I.” In his pursuit of a career or wealth man may “not bear” others, take their social positions, etc., but nevertheless he remains with them in a predetermined mirror-like relation of mutuality. In other words, the fact that potentially anyone can take the place of the other in the social structure is the precondition of the social game where everyone has equal opportunities.

Thus conceived “asociability” is grounded in the “sociability” of man, who remains in a foreordained relation of mutuality with others. 

 Let us note the assumptions behind this argument. Although the other could take my place, because in a sense he is of the same kind of “I” as me, this possibility of substitution regards only the place which “I” occupy in the social structure rather than the “place” of my “I” taken in the individual specificity of my being. There is absolutely no reason for me to fear that the other will replace me as the “I.” He can replace me in my job, take over my professional, familial, or social functions, but he cannot simply “enter” inside me and become my “I.” Therefore, even if he takes some of my functions, he never robs me of myself, because it is ontologically impossible. And I can always withdraw in myself, change my job, etc. So I preserve my “I” in all its individual aspects. Although the “structural homogeneity” lying at the basis of social being makes it possible for “everyone to be someone else …,” in fact “… they cannot,”<sup>18</sup> because their social being—as Plessner claims—produces a contradiction between structure and reality. 

 What is meant here is the discrepancy between two dimensions or levels of the “I.” There is the “I” connected with specific social functions it fulfils, taken in its abstract generality (the “I” as father, husband, official, professor, etc) and replaceable in this function by every other “I.” In this perspective, these other “I’s” are perceived by man as a threat or obstacle to achieving his goals. On the other hand, there is the one-time, unique “I” connected with particular actual being of the individual, endowed with self-consciousness and “personality” and as such irreplaceable. This gives the individual confidence that no one can deprive him of his own “personality” (individual “I”) and take his “place” in the literal sense, so he perceives other “I’s” as a necessary condition for social “games” he plays with them, sometimes winning, sometimes losing. 

6\. 

So in Plessner’s approach, we are dealing with a completely different aspect of the relation between the “I” and another “I” than the one studied by Lacan in *The Mirror Stage*, when he writes about infantile transitivism. Invoking the Kantian formula of “asocial sociability,” Plessner is interested in the ambivalence of the relation between a self-conscious, individual “I” to another “I” on the cultural-social plane. He assumes that this “I” is mature, fully self-conscious and capable of treating himself, his body, others and things of this world as objects. As for Lacan, he is interested in the “stage” preceding the formation of thus conceived mature “I.” At this stage the individual is not yet able to distinguish the mirror image of his own body from the image of the body of other. He also does not distinguish this mirror image of himself from what he is in reality, on “this” side of the mirror, therefore, when it turns out that this mirror image of

*[p. 138]* ^pg140

himself or another is a fictitious other “I” of himself, it produces frustration and aggression. The individual is faced with the problem of Narcissus, who has to resolve the following dilemma: either I, or him. Either my ideal image of myself in the mirror is the “I” taken in my real existence and hence I can identify with it, or this image is a fiction, because it belongs to someone else, so it is a fraudulent fake of my image. Therefore I have to reject it, while the other to whom this image refers has to be destroyed. Since the “other” in the mirror is a fictitious image of Narcissus himself, by destroying this fiction he destroys himself. This act is tantamount to an outbreak of psychosis, where wanting to destroy its “other,” the subject targets his aggression against himself. And this possibility, as a possibility of self-destruction—although a psychotic might as well direct his aggression against the other—is grounded in man’s notion of himself as the “I.” 

 Unlike for Plessner, steeped in the Kantian tradition, for Lacan man’s conceiving himself as the “I” originally has the form of a narcissistically cast notion of himself which has the status of a symptom. This symptom is, on the one hand, a defensive tool, a “fortress” shielding the “I” from the unbearable sense of fragmentation of his own body. On the other hand, the “I” as this kind of symptom is an illusion, an outcome of the individual becoming alienated from himself. Seen in the light of this “genealogical” perspective, the “I” has not yet become a self-justifying instance with a self-reflexive structure. Only when the individual realises that his own mirror image is a fiction devoid of any essential connection with himself (and it differs in that from the image of other “I’s”) and he accepts this “fiction,” we can speak about the situation noted by Kant and then invoked by Plessner. Forgetting about his genealogy, the individual may start conceiving his “I” as “replaceable” with other “I’s” in the social space in terms of the position he occupies, but at the same time irreplaceable by other “I’s” in his individuality. 

 It does not mean that Plessner’s approach is simply complementary to Lacan’s, as concerning a later development stage of the “I” notion. And that differences between them are simply consequences of the fact that these two concepts focus on different stages of the formation of the “I” notion. There is no doubt that the stage Plessner is interested in, when the individual shapes his notion of himself as an “I,” but also recognises the fact that some other “I” may take his social functions, to some extent is built over the “mirror stage.” But Plessner’s anthropological perspective misses not only the drama of man’s experiencing his body, preceding the formation of the “I.” It also does not take into account the possibility that this “stable” “I,” to some extent “interchangeable” with other “I’s,” may disintegrate and vanish under the impact of specific circumstances. This will result in a regression to the level of the quasi-psychotic experience of your own body as fragmented. This possibility is not just an event which may occur at the ontic level, but it is part and parcel of the manner the “I” relates to itself. It belongs to the ontological structure of “I.” In other words, the 

*[p. 139]* ^pg141

human “I” not only can take part in social games, confident that in the literal sense no one will take its “place.” It may as well disintegrate from the inside and this possibility is rooted in its structure. 

 This difference, which is already apparent once we set Plessner’s ideas against Lacan’s early works, results from the fact that for Plessner the paradigm of thinking about the human “I” is the “I” of a neurotic. That is someone capable of submitting to the rigour imposed on him by his own ideal “I,” and entering social space as an individual capable of taking part in the games forming this reality. This individual treats his “I” as replaceable with other “I’s” on the social level. So such an “I,” accepting the fact that it is just one of many actors in the social games, treats others “I’s” as potential partners in these games. Although others constrain it in its social being and they may take over its various functions, without them social games would not make sense. At the same time, this “I” is confident that the others are not able to replace it as “personality.” If this “I” treats others as endangering the achievement of its life goals, it is also self-confident as an individual “I.” 

 Meanwhile, in his early works Lacan points at the possibility of the emergence of a different structure of human identity, where the concept of the “I” as “interchangeable” with others, and hence the sense of its irreplaceability as an individual “I,” does not appear. Should such a situation occur, the “I” treats the very existence of other “I’s” as a mortal threat to itself. In no aspect of its social being can it accept them as “replaceable” with itself, because it relates to them according to a dualist, narcissistic logic of exclusion: either I or him. Its relation to them is the relation of deadly combat and may be resolved only through destruction of one of the parties concerned.
