Perhaps I can demonstrate that phenomenology is not identical with foundationalism. Think for a moment of the givenness of our life; the most telling form of this givenness is language. Of course, language is now of essential concern in modern philosophy. I think there are good reasons for this, but I am not convinced that philosophy of language or linguistics touches at all the decisive point of givenness. In language there is, first of all, both *langue* and *parole,* to use Saussure's distinction. The spoken word is something other than the system of symbols which constitutes language. Language *is not* itself a given, what is given is *parole,* the speaking word in its working reality. And that certainly involves a strange form of concealment. One should realize that it is a basic character of speaking that it is completely forgetful of itself. Nobody could utter one sentence if he were completely aware of what he was doing. If I were to do that, I would not find a second word after the first. And more than that: it would really prevent me from going beyond every utterance to the matters I would convey, and force me to keep to myself what I am saying. I would go mad if I were to make an attempt at complete thematization of saying in saying. I must say *something* in order to speak; when I do there is a forgetfulness of speech as a theme or topic. One could reply that speech exists in texts. Yes, certainly, but the texts are alien or brutal. How is this speech, the speaking word, really preserved in the written text? Is it completely the utterance of my mind? Are we not all acquainted with the alienation between what we said and what we had in mind? Is it not one of our leading experiences that the utterance is no longer mine? We must always look for the *real* meaning of an utterance. It is an error when our logician friends insist that we must "improve" Plato, in regard to what is contradictory or inconsistent, making his argumentations more coherent. That is a misunderstanding of what speaking is. Speaking is not logical deduction; it is, in a way, overcoming the word, and it produces something one has to interpret by the context in the boldest sense. The context here is not only the words but the whole life context. 

That context, of course, is never given in its full extent. So interpretation seems to me very demanding, and, of course, a field of philosophical and philological activity. I know just one instance in which the interpretation of speech is not an additional supplemental moment, and in which we go to the essence of the matters themselves: that is dialogue. In the dialogue we are really interpreting. Speaking then is interpreting itself. It is the function of the dialogue that in 

*[p. 322]* ^pg82

saying or stating something a challenging relation with the other evolves, a response is provoked, and the response provides the interpretation of the other's interpretation. In this way, we know (an old Platonic insight) that the real mode of givenness of speech starts with dialogue. It is no longer a system of symbols or a set of rules of grammar and syntax. The real act of work is appropriation in the common being of the speakers. I try in my own work to develop this point of view, on how language, not in the sense of *langue,* but in the sense of real exchange and work, manifests itself in the dialogue. In any form of dialogue, we are building up. We are building up a common language, so that at the end of the dialogue we will have some ground. Of course, not every dialogue is fruitful, but it should at least aim at being a dialogue. (Very often it is the opposite of that: two monologues following one upon the other.) Anyway, we are here describing language in its *givenness,* I mean in its actuality and not in the abstracting modes of a science of symbols. We are speaking about the *word* which is presenting itself to us in our exchange of speech, exactly what the Greeks called dialectic. It is the procedure of Greek thinking. 

Let me give you an outline of what I have in mind, i.e., dialectic as the common ground. Dialectic does not claim to have a first principle. It is true: Plato as he appeared to Aristotle developed two "principles," the One and the Dyad. The Dyad was an indeterminate Dyad and that meant openness for further determination. Is that "foundation" in the sense of Husserl's higher principle? These "principles" of Plato were not meant to yield an ultimate determinancy. I think Plato was well aware of this position when he said that philosophy is something for human beings, not for gods. Gods *know,* but we are in this ongoing process of approximation and overcoming error by dialectically moving towards truth. In this sense I could present a partial defense of the idea that the oldest heritage of philosophy is exactly its functionality, its giving an account, and that as such it cannot presume to have first principles. This suggests very well what I would have in the place of "foundation." I would call it "participation," because that is what happens in human life. That is, without any doubt, the excellence of the humanities, that we share a common world of tradition and interpreted human experience. The interpretation of the common world in which we participate is certainly not in the first place the objectifying task of methodical thinking. That may certainly be included, but it is not the *raison d'etre* of our activity. When we are interpreting a text, it is not to prove "scientifically" that *this* love poem belongs to the genre of love poems. That is objective and nobody can doubt it; but if 

*[p. 323]* ^pg83

that is the only result of the investigation of a poem, then we have failed. The intention is to understand *this* love poem, on its own and in its unique relation to the common structure of love poems. It is an absolutely individualized particular form, so that one participates in the utterance or message which is there embodied by the poet. Participation is indeed a better formulation of what is going on in our life experience than is the foundationalist account of the apodictic evidence of self-consciousness. 

Participation is a strange word. Its dialectic consists of the fact that participation is not taking parts, but in a way taking the whole. Everybody who participates in something does not take something away, so that the others cannot have it. The opposite is true: by sharing, by our participating in the things in which we are participating, we enrich them; they do not become smaller, but larger. The whole life of tradition consists exactly in this enrichment so that life is our culture and our past: the whole inner store of our lives is always extending by participating. 

I want to end with just one remark about what there is in the methodical character of such an approach, that I would call hermeneutic in its central sense. Let me refer to the practical philosophy of Aristotle. Aristotle asks, "What is the principle of moral philosophy?" and he answers, "Well, the principle is *that,* - the thatness." It means, not deduction but real givenness, not of brute facts but of the interpreted world. Is it not the question of Husserl's *CriSis,* how we can rejoin and reconnect our efforts at rigorous science, including phenomenology as a rigorous science, with the historical conditions of our own place in the course of history? And so my thesis is: exactly because we give up a special idea of foundation in principle, we become better phenomenologists, closer to the real givenness, and we are more aware of the reciprocity between our conceptual efforts and the concrete in life experience.

Introduction 

Many passages in Dilthey leave the reader with the impression that Dilthey's critique of historical reason, and his general theory of understanding and the construction of the historical work, is as such not a contribution to methodical hermeneutics. He distinguishes between a general theory of understanding and methodical hermeneutics. It seems to be the case that he himself has nothing to add to the latter which has as its last outstanding theorist for Dilthey in Boeckh. <sup>1</sup>Dilthey's concern as the philosopher of the human sciences is rather to deliver the critical groundwork which shows that the claims made in the. development of hermeneutics, beginning with Flacius and culminating in Schleiermacher and Boeckh, are valid claims. Likewise he seems to assume that Boeckh's own rudimentary reflections on the critical level and on the relation between philosophy and philology are consistent with his own approach.2 A closer consideration can, however, show that Dilthey has made - in the period in which the development of methodical hermeneutics attracted his special attention <sup>3</sup> proposals -

which imply significant changes in the system of methodical hermeneutics which are connected with Dilthey's general theory of understanding. Dilthey has in these passages, and nowhere else, given an account of Boeckh's system and his understanding of that system. With these proposals, he gives only a very brief and hidden hint that he wants to introduce a new level in hermeneutics, and to reinterpret the function of lower hermeneutics, excluding them, on the one hand, from the process of thought in understanding, but giving them, on the other hand, the function of determining methodologically what the first canon of hermeneutics really is about. Dilthey apparently presupposes that his readers are familiar with traditional methodical hermeneutics

*[p. 326]* ^pg85

, especially Boeckh, and can, hence, appreciate his innovations. Of course, an interpretation of the passages in question cannot be built on such a "psychological" assumption. <sup>3</sup>In order to formulate and ground the first thesis of this paper, namely that Dilthey indeed introduced significant innovations, I will first have to give an account of Boeckh's methodology in Part I, which will hopefully be sufficient for the purpose of showing in Part II exactly what Dilthey's innovations were. 

A reconstruction of Boeckh's methodology, and Dilthey's reaction to it both on the methodological level and on the level of his general theory of understanding, will, in addition, be a contribution to a re-evaluation of traditional hermeneutics which might require a reevaluation of recent criticisms of traditional hermeneutics. Such criticisms, grounded in phenomenological accounts of the process of interpretation, point out that, although "methodical discipline" is necessary,4 method is not the essential part in the process of understanding ultimately grounded in the application and the immediate quest for truth, unfolded in the dialogue between the interpreter and the text. Boeckh's conception of methodology is by no means a methodological absolutism, i.e. it does not imply the thesis that it is method and method alone which paves the way to truth. Method does not lead to truth in hermeneutics. Hermeneutics is based on canons and is in general canonical, i.e. it teaches how to avoid error. Methodology does one additional thing: it has to point out the limits of method. The limits of methodology are reached on the level of generic critique of the *works of the genius.* The problem of the appropriateness of such works leads to a vicious circle. The critical question for *truth* and the hermeneutical question for the generic whole of the work are inseparable. <sup>s</sup> With his proposal to go beyond Boeckh's highest level of hermeneutic and critique, Dilthey will not deny Boeckh's critical limitations of method. If he wants to give a critique of historical reason with some orientation in Kant's enterprise then this can only mean, and it does, that he also wants to indicate the limits of method together with the justification which the method has within these limits. The basic category introduced by Dilthey at this point is effective historical connectedness, *historischer Wirkungszusammenhang.* <sup>6</sup>Some methodical considerations, which are not made perfectly explicit by Dilthey, indicate that the limits of method are precisely the limits of a methodical treatment of the effective history, *Wirkungsgeschichte,* <sup>7</sup>within the totality of the whole of effective historical connectedness as a category which belongs to life as such in Dilthey's sense. 

*[p. 327]* ^pg86

If that is the case , then the criticism of methodologism in hermeneutics in recent literature might be quite appropriate, but it cannot be applied either to Boeckh or to Dilthey. The difference is rather the following: the so called New Hermeneutics, in its critique of methodologism, has never pointed out *why* methodological discipline is necessary and is even not interested in the question of *how* it is possible. Boeckh and then Dilthey tried to give an answer to both questions without every denying that there were limits for the method in the process of understanding. It is worthwhile to reconsider their positions especially because it cannot be denied that the critique of method in the New Hermeneutics has been understood as a license to roam about with *ad libitum* invented ideas in the interpretation of texts as well as in the theory of interpretation, perhaps very much against the 'original intention' of the authors who launched this critique. <sup>8</sup>

It will not be possible to discuss this problem in detail. Part III of this essay can only summarize the actual claims of traditional hermeneutics with respect to method and what especially Boeckh and Dilthey never claimed it to be. A complete reevaluation of the relation between the old and the new hermeneutics, which would have to emphasize also the real progress that has been made in the New Hermeneutics, must be left for future considerations. 

I. Boeckh's methodology 

It will be sufficient for the present purpose to consider three points: 

A. The relations between philology and history, philology and philosophy and other disciplines in general. 

B. The determination of the proper objects of hermeneutics and critique according to Boeckh. 

C. The theory of the levels of hermeneutic and critique which is the basis of hermeneutic methodology. 

A. History in the broadest sense, and philology are identical; the task of philology is to interpret the documents which represent the tradition. History in the broadest sense is the result of interpretation, i.e. the understanding of these documents and their place in the historical development. <sup>9</sup>

History in the narrower sense is knowledge of the *res gestae,* and philology is related to it in a double way. The *res gestae* have been 

*[p. 328]* ^pg87

known. This knowledge of the past is represented to the historian in documents. The general definition of philology is "knowing of what was known" *(Erkennen des Erkannten).lo* Thus the first task of the historian is the philological understanding of the documents. Furthermore the actions, reported in the documents, imply some knowing in the past. In order to understand it properly, further historical documents, reports about customs and values of the time, have to be consulted. The understanding of such documents is again a philological task. 

Philosophy is knowing the truth, *gnosis.* Philology is the knowing of what was known, *anagnosis.* Both are related to each other: 

1\. To know what was known implies some knowing of some object. 

Philology has, therefore, to refer to philosophical concepts. Otherwise it would be a mere knowledge of facts. It has, furthermore, the tendency to dissolve itself in the philosophy of history, where history is understood as the advent of truth. 

2\. To know something always implies an implicit knowledge of what was known. What was known is the presupposition of present knowing. Philosophy has to clarify its own presuppositions and has, therefore, the possible tendency to dissolve itself into the history of philosophy. I I 

What is said holds for the relation between philology and all other theoretical disciplines which, as special types of knowing, can be considered as branches of philosophy. It is philology which provides for all of them their history and the relation between the history of knowing, and actual knowing is the same for them as it is for philosophy. The same relation can also be found between history in the narrower sense and practical philosophy P 

It has to be kept in mind that Boeckh offers here a specific way of mediating philology and philosophy. The "mediating" term is philology as history in the broader sense. One has only to substitute "history" by "tradition" and "philosophy" by "truth" in order to be very close to modern conceptions. <sup>13</sup>The difficulty with Boeckh is, however, that he will offer a second "mediating" factor, without ever bringing the two together. This factor will be discussed under section I.e. 

B. The methodology of philology has to deal with the two, closely intertwined methods developed in philology, hermeneutic and critique. Methodology is a reflection on what is already practiced. Its purpose is critical in the philosophical, not in the philological, sense, i.e. it has, 

*[p. 329]* ^pg88

as already mentioned, to show how to avoid errors and to recognize the limits of a possible methodical distinction between truth and error.14 In order to determine the proper object of hermeneutic and critique, it is sufficient to determine the proper object of hermeneutics. 

It can be said in general that hermeneutics is about the understanding of signs. Regarding signs, one has to distinguish between: 

a. signs indicating an object known which is separated from the signs. 

All signs which are signs of a language in the proper sense are such signs. 

b. Signs which are in partial or complete material identity with the object to which they refer: buildings, tools, i.e. that which is called by historians a "silent witness" or a "trace." 

Only (a) is a possible object of philology and philological hermeneutics; (b), if taken as such, belongs to archeology and archeological hermeneutics. Is Boeckh does not enter into a discussion of the question of how both are interrelated in historical research and philology. He can do that because his methodology sets up very narrow limits for philology and philological hermeneutics with the next distinction. 

The objects of philological hermeneutics are texts, not oral speeches. Texts as signs have several referents: (a) the written text as a sign of the language as spoken language, the speech; (b) the language to which the speech belongs, which signifies; (c) the signified knowledge. Philological hermeneutics deals only with (b) in its relation to (c), not with (b) for itself and not with (b) in its relation to (a). This means, first of all, that the lowest level of the "art of grammer" in Hellenistic philology, the art of "bringing a text back to speech," i.e. reading, does not belong to hermeneutics, it is presupposed by hermeneutics. The process of hermeneutics presupposes that one is able to read and understand the text in a preliminary way. Since hermeneutics is also not about (b) as such, it follows that it presupposes grammatical, lexicographical and other linguistic knowledge which is about the language in general. 16 Such linguistic knowledge will be applied in hermeneutics on the level of grammatical hermeneutics, but research at this level does not belong to hermeneutics itself. Boeckh has with these restrictions excluded two disciplines from philological hermeneutics which are closely connected with archeological research techniques, namely the extreme task of deciphering a text in an unknown language and paleography or "the art of reading," bringing a text to speech, in more sophisticated cases. The restrictions have great significance for a proper understanding

*[p. 330]* ^pg89

of Boeckh's methodology and possible criticisms. 

The last distinction which is necessary in order to determine the proper object of hermeneutics is Boeckh's sense is the distinction between *interpretatio* and *explicatio.* In the process of doing philology, *hermeneia* occurs in three different functions. The first is the *hermeneia,* the written speech, represented by the text. It is the *elocutio* made by the text. The third function is the *hermeneia,* the *elocutio* made by the philologist about the text which is usually itself a new text and called *explicatio.* Between both and mediating the first *elocutio* into the second we have *hermeneia* as *translatio* or *interpretatio.* Hermeneutics as a method of philology is concerned only with the latter; that means it is not concerned with the methods of the explication of the knowledge gained in the process of interpretation, or with its representation in commentaries and other forms of the explication of philological knowledge. <sup>1</sup>7 A consequence is, of course, that history as a research process is closely connected with hermeneutics, but *historiography* is beyond the scope of hermeneutics. Since application, *applicatio,* is always either implied in the explication or something which is offered after explications are given in a separate speech, it does not belong to hermeneutics. Boeckh does not mention this point explicitly. It is, however, obvious enough. 

C. The theory of the levels of hermeneutics and critique. It is, as mentioned, the task of a methodology to analyze the possible sources of error and the limits of a method which is already practiced. The sources of error in philological hermeneutics are vicious circularities, logical circles, instances of a *petitio principii.* It holds in general that the circle according to which the part determines the meaning of the whole and vice versa is the leading principle of interpretation, but method is nothing else than the attempt to avoid possible vicious circularities in the application of this principle.1s The limits of the method are then all instances in which whole-part relations have to be used, and the material is not sufficient to decide whether a *petitio principii* is on the basis of the interpretation or not. 

The method of breaking the hermeneutical circle presupposes the distinction between hermeneutics and critique. Both are mutually dependent and can produce in this relation vicious circularities. The task of hermeneutics is the understanding of what was known in a certain text or part of it, the task of critique is to decide if what is understood is appropriate, i.e. fits into the context of other philological knowledge; if not, to make guesses about what would fit better and, finally, to decide what was there originally.19 Since critique does no more than to 

*[p. 331]* ^pg90

introduce wholes of a broader scope it is as such not sufficient for the discovery of vicious circularities. What has to be added is the distinction between different levels of hermeneutics and critique. 

Boeckh distinguishes levels belonging to the objective and subjective conditions of the text. The objective conditions are language and historical context. The wholes represented by them are constitutive for the levels of grammatical hermeneutics and critique and historical hermeneutics and critique. The subjective conditions entail references to the author. Individual hermeneutics and critique have as their whole the "individuality of the author," i.e. not his/her psyche but, as represented in the works of the author as his/her style. The level of generic interpretations has the text as such as a whole as its topic. The specific structure of a text as a whole is determined by its genre: poem, novel; but also legal text, mathematical text and so forth. The reference to the author is given insofar as the genre of the text indicates the leading subjective purpose, the scopus, which the author has had in mind. Since the genre question has to be decided by an analysis of the text alone, and/or perhaps other texts, no "psychology" is involved. <sup>20</sup>

How the hermeneutical circle is broken can be indicated only schematically; Boeckh himself gives lucid examples. <sup>21</sup>The main principle is that the different levels of hermeneutics and critique refer to different wholes, namely the whole of the language, the whole of the historical context, the whole of the opus of the author, and finally the whole of the text. A *petitio principii* is given in all cases in which a certain interpretation is carried over from one level to the other presupposing in both cases the *same content,* i.e. in the given schematic example: If the determination of the genre of a poem depends on the assumption of certain shifts in the meaning of certain words, and the only justification for including such shifts in lexicographical lists is the assumption that the poem belongs to that genre, one has a vicious circle. It will be broken if other material content, either from the levels involved or from other levels, can be found which backs at least one of the assumptions. 

The limits of the method can, hence, be given on the one hand accidentally by a lack of material which justifies the judgment: undecidable, *"non liquet."* But Boeckh has pointed out that there is also an essential case, and this is the work of the genius who, for him, is not only the genius of art but also the genius in other fields. There are, on the level of generic hermeneutics and especially generic critique, which considers whether the work is appropriate to its goal, no criteria outside which can answer this question. The question of the critique concerning appropriateness and the question for truth in the broadest sense merge for the interpreter in such a case. Consequently, there is no specific historical context in which the question of generic critique can be decided. The history of knowledge as a whole is at stake. It is, hence, the work of the genius, in general the eminent text, in which truth and method, philology and philosophy, mediated by history as a whole - *nota bene* it is a new "whole" which occurs here - will merge. It has to be kept in mind, however, that the question whether we really have an eminent text or not with regard to which such a merging is necessary is not left to the caprice of the interpreter. It has to be justified in a rigorous application of the method to the available material in which the *"non liquet"* of the method itself becomes apparent. This will be the case at the very moment in which the circle between hermeneutic and critique on the generic level, i.e. the interpretation of what was known and the question whether what was known is true knowledge, cannot be broken. <sup>24</sup>

Before turning to Dilthey two critical observations have to be made with respect to Boeckh's methodology and its presuppositions. 

1\. Theodore Birt has pointed out that Boeckh's definition of philology is insufficient. 25 Philological linguistics deals with contents 

*[p. 334]* ^pg93

which have, to a very high degree, not been known but remained "unconscious," as unconscious as the objective conditions of a speech pregiven by the structure of a language are. Boeckh is actually ambiguous in this respect. His definition undoubtedly fits the task of philological hermeneutics from which he has, to a very high degree, excluded philological linguistics and other disciplines. There are, however, enough passages in which he includes linguistics in philology. The philosophical problem is that he presupposes unanalyzed the whole problem of how we understand texts by simply reading them and, with it, how we understand life expressions in general, i.e. he presupposes a premethodical realm of understanding and even of the understanding of texts. Thus not only philological linguistics and its methods are not treated by him explicitly but other disciplines of the human sciences, which deal with life expressions and can play a significant role in philological arguments, like paleography, archeology, and 

ethnology could also be added. 

2\. The concept of history, and especially the history of human knowledge as a whole, as mediating between philology and philosophy, philological method and the question for truth, remains in a mythical darkness. It cannot be history in a more narrow sense nor can it be history in the broader sense which is identical with philology itself, because recourse to history as a whole as mediating between philology and the quest for truth takes place exactly at the moment in which the judgment *"non liquet"* is passed on the method, i.e. in which the limits of the method are reached. The same problem occurs in a modified version within the method itself. Birt has reduced individual interpretation to the interpretation of style and thus to the level of philological linguistics. <sup>26</sup>There is a tendency in Boeckh to do the same, i.e. to reduce the diachronical aspect of individual interpretation, which is concerned with the individual history of the author, to a level which can be, in principle, treated synchronical on the linguistic level. However, biographical development seems to playa role, though it is reduced to the level of historical interpretation. Historical interpretation in Boeckh uses an even more restricted concept of history. History is about "facts" in this context. It is not clear how the investigation about such facts and the interpretation of style can be brought together in a biographical history of the author which is a history of the development of meaning. What is thereby presupposed is an unanalyzed conception of history in general and individual historicity.

II. Dilthey's response 

Dilthey's latest and most systematical response to Boeckh's methodology can be found in the *"Aufbau der Geschichtlichen Welt."* Since Boeckh has here the historical function of being the keystone in the whole development of traditional hermeneutics we have here, as well, Dilthey's response to the whole development. Dilthey's account of the earlier phases of this development can be found in his essay *"Entstehung der Hermeneutik."* 

What is given is a general theory of understanding. Philological understanding and philological hermeneutics are treated in this general theory at the end as highly developed forms of methodical understanding. The analysis is grounded in the analysis of lived experience *(Erlebnis)* and expression, its temporal structures, of the objectifications of life and the realm of objective spirit. All these investigations belong to the realm of descriptive psychology and a descriptive account of social life. <sup>27</sup>It is such a general theory of understanding, envisioned by Dil they, which is able to deliver the philosophical clarifications of the presuppositions of Boeckh's methodology mentioned in 0.) at the end of the last section. Only if they are given will it be possible to determine the exact relationship between philology in Boeckh's sense and the disciplines which he partially or completely excluded from philology. It is not the task of this essay to give an evaluation of this general theory of understanding - which Dilthey himself never called hermeneutics, saving this term for methodically guided understanding (!). Though it is true that recent critics are often unjust with respect to Dilthey it might be as well the case that they have added considerable descriptive accounts to the ones given by Dilthey himself. The main thesis of this paper is, as mentioned, that though it is in general correct to consider Dilthey's theory as a philosophical "transcendental" grounding of Boeckh's methodology and that they are in general in harmony, it holds nevertheless that Dilthey has modified and transcended Boeckh's methodology and that these modifications themselves are grounded in certain aspects of Dilthey's general theory of understanding on the premethodicallevel. 

What is presupposed in Boeckh's methodology is the ability of a premethodical understanding of a language, of written language and even foreign written languages. Dilthey introduces a distinction between levels of understanding in the premethodical realm which will turn out to be decisive for his treatment of methodical understanding. We have the elementary understanding<sup>28</sup>which is an understanding 

*[p. 336]* ^pg95

of isolated expressions immediately followed by corresponding applications and actions. The whole of the *Lebenszusammenhang,* the web of connections of social life, is present as that which makes such an understanding possible and determines it, but it is not thematized. Elementary understanding is embedded in the whole of objective spirit, which is not conscious in this understanding. Dilthey introduces, in addition, on the level of premethodical understanding a "higher understanding." Higher understanding which is, of course, always onesidedly founded and inseparable from elementary understanding, is an understanding in which the *Lebenszusammenhang* is thematized in different degrees. <sup>29</sup>There are several dimensions in which that can take place: (I) higher understanding is possible with respect to my own life, thematizing it as a whole or parts of it as a whole; (2) the same can be done with regard to other persons; (3) with regard to social institutions of different kinds, including economical relations, political relations and so forth; (4) the understanding of literary works which represent themselves wholes of life expressions. 

What is required for the attitude in which higher understanding can take place from the side of the "interpreter" is a certain type of induction, based on empathy *(stch hineinversetzen,* verbally: to bring oneself into the situation), re-creation *(Nachbilden)* and reliving *(Nacher-* *\[eben).* Instead of following the directions and intentions of my own life as they are pregiven temporally in the process of elementary understanding, the "interpreter" takes some time in which he is not immediately involved in elementary understanding but thematizes his/her own or other's life in its connectedness. The perfect result is re-experiencing which is perfected if it has become itself the shape of a fixed and permanent work, a literary work in the most cases. Such a work is then itself a possible object of higher understanding of others. <sup>30</sup>

The process of higher understanding can be guided methodically by a knowledge of the general structures of life provided by descriptive psychology and a general descriptive theory of culture. Such guidance, however, cannot eliminate completely the irrational and "divinatoric" component which is characteristic of higher understanding from the very beginning, i.e. a logical analysis of understanding as re-living and re-experiencing has its limits. It can proceed beyond a general knowledge about the structures of life only with respect to fixed life expressions. Personal talent can be guided in this case by rules. The science of these rules is *hermeneutics.* The level of methodical understanding is reached. <sup>31</sup>

The following points which are essential for Dilthey's appraisal of 

*[p. 337]* ^pg96

hermeneutics have to be emphasized before discussing his critique of traditional hermeneutics, i.e. in its most developed form, the hermeneutics of Boeckh. 

1\. Traditional hermeneutics has shown that the concept of validity in the understanding of fixed life expressions is a justifiable concept and thus provided a defence against historical scepticism and subjective decisionism *(subjektive Willkuer)* with respect to interpretation. 

2\. Hermeneutics has a basic principle, which is the first canon of Schleiermacher, that the text has to be understood out of the situation of the contemporary reader or addressee and the intention of the author. The basic principle is, hence, not the second canon, the canon of the whole and the parts and the famous "hermeneutical circle" governing their relation. This canon is a principle of hermeneutics, but it is rooted already in the premethodical realm of higher understanding and can only be applied together with the method, which teaches how to avoid vicious circularities. 

3\. This principle has not been introduced arbitrarily. It emerges in a situation in which an eminent text is understood in a multiplicity of mutually exclusive traditions of its premethodical interpretations which all denounce each other mutually as "false." Such a situation was given with regard to the New Testament in the western tradition at the time of the reformation. The origin of hermeneutics is the introduction of the ftrst canon as a principle of the exegesis of the Bible as an alternative to universal decisionism. The further development, culminating in Boeckh, has shown "beyond any doubt" the validity of the theoretical foundations of philology and history and the human sciences in general. <sup>32</sup>

Dilthey raises two criticisms against philological hermeneutics in connection with this appraisal. The first, explicit, criticism is that hermeneutics has not yet fulfilled its task completely: "Hermeneutics has now to ftnd a positive relation to the general gnoseological task, namely to point out the possibility of the knowledge of the connectedness of the historical world and to find the means to realise such a knowledge, "33 i.e. according to Dilthey traditional hermeneutics does not provide these means and at the end of Section I of this essay I tried to show that Boeckh in fact does not provide them. What Dilthey offers in this respect can only be explained after an explication of his second criticism: "The preliminary grammatical and historical work which merely serves the purpose to place the interpreter who wants to understand a fixed expression which originates in the past or in a 

*[p. 338]* ^pg97

geographical distance or is linguistically foreign, in the position of a reader of the author's own time and environment, *has to be excluded from the thought process ofunderstanding."34* 

The passage implies a complete new viewpoint for the theory of the levels of hermeneutics and critique and has to be considered "in its context," i.e. methodical hermeneutics. When Dilthey excludes grammatical and historical hermeneutics from the process of understanding he does not suggest that they are excluded from methodical hermeneutics. On the contrary) they are essential because they are the tools for the realization of the first canon, namely to understand the text from the viewpoint of a reader of the author's own time and environment. Since grammatical and historical hermeneutics and critique offer a set of methodical rules in Boeckh which are applicable to groups of texts Dilthey has given an interpretation of the first canon which eliminates the suspicion that this canon demands some kind of a mysterious travel through time which has to reach the psychological states of readers in the past or even the author. Grammatical hermeneutics and critique allows us to select, methodically with the aid of comparatistic methods, a group of texts which use approximately the same language, e.g. classical Greek. Historical interpretation allows us to locate texts within this set of texts taking into account the hints given in the texts to historical events in the presence and the past of the text which include other texts to which the text in question refers explicitly or implicitly. Thus the context of a text is methodically determined, and "contemporary readers" have access only to this context and not to texts outside this context. What the preparatory wor:k does is to reconstruct a counterpart to elementary understanding on the methodical level. It reconstructs elementary understanding because it is not yet interested in the understanding of a foreign life which occurs as a category only on the level of individual and on the level of the text as a whole in generic interpretation. Since they reconstruct only elementary understanding, they do not belong to the thought process of understanding, i.e. higher understanding which is interested in wholes of foreign life expressions. Higher understanding on the premethodical level has the character of an induction which has, regarding content, a "divinatoric" element. Such a divinatoric element is essential for individual and generic hermeneutics,35 but it is now, with regard to fixed life expressions, methodically controlled. Higher premethodical understanding which is applied in the medium of the present stage of the development of objective spirit is guided by induction on the basis of the unthematized experience of the structures of objective spirit in 

*[p. 339]* ^pg98

elementary understanding. Certain projections toward wholes of meaning structures will be ruled out on the basis of this experience. Such a guidance by "induction" of divination is missing with respect to fixed life expressions which are located in a temporal or geographical-spatial distance which can also be a linguistic distance. However the two lower levels of methodical hermeneutics can define the context of fixed life expressions, and it is this context which allows us to exclude certain divinatoric projections as grammatically and/or historically false or doubtful. It has to be mentioned that this methodical procedure has in most cases the character of eliminating "hypotheses" projections, it does not guarantee a straight path to "true interpretations." 

Dilthey also moves beyond Boeckh with respect to the higher levels of methodical hermeneutics. As mentioned, individual hermeneutics already has in Boeckh the tendency to dissolve in to a linguistic analysis of style, a tendency which determines Birt's understanding of individual interpretation of individual hermeneutics throughout. The individuality of the author lingers homeless between historical and individual hermeneutics. It is homeless because "history" means nothing more than the sum total of the historical facts of the context of a text. What Dilthey supplies, already on the level of premethodical understanding, is the category of understanding a life process as a whole which requires empathy, re-creation and re-living. Such an understanding is represented on the level of fixed life expressions in autobiography and biography.36 Since these are categories belonging to the premethodical level of understanding, they can be thematized on the methodical level. Formal style analysis on the one hand and historical facts about the author can be united in this medium in which we have an "individual interpretation" which deserves its name. The understanding is methodical again because it is guided by the first canon, i.e. the result of the preparatory work of the lower levels of hermeneutics. 

Boeckh's hermeneutic leads, with respect to eminent texts, works of genius, on the level of generic critique, into an immediate confrontation with the "philosophical" quest for truth. He added that "history as a whole" is what mediates between philology and philosophy, but the whole of history remains a methodologically unanalyzed, mystical, "romantic" concept. Dilthey's demand is that a third level of higher hermeneutics should be introduced, which deals with the connectedness of the historical world. Dilthey is not precise in his instructions about the application of methodical hermeneutics to the analysis of the connectedness of the historical world as a whole of higher order 

*[p. 340]* ^pg99

which does not occur as a *methodological* concept in Boeckh's system. It is, however, possible to construct such instructions and recognize their limits on the basis of what is said explicitly. The first model is that of a methodically re-created biography. The re-creation is methodically guided if each document is understood in its proper context as determined by the preparatory work of lower hermeneutics and critique. Each document has then to be taken as representing a certain aspect in a certain temporal phase of the whole of foreign life. In order to grasp its connectedness it will be necessary to determine the influence, the "efficient history" which this aspect of a certain phase has in phases in its *future horizon* represented by other documents. These documents reveal, analyzed in this way, certain interpretations and applications of the meaning contents of the past phase. Thus step by step the methodical understanding of the whole of a foreign life, represented in fixed life expressions, can be grasped. 

The whole of a foreign life is pregiven in a formal and external manner as the whole of the biological life of the person in question. The problem of the understanding of the connectedness of the historical world is that an external determination of the whole of the connectedness is not possible. Even wholes which are parts of this whole cannot be determined without a foregoing interpretation of their development in its connectedness. The question where the beginning and the end of the Middle Ages can be located historically depends on the interpretation of the connectedness of life in this period. 

It is, however, possible to transfer certain methodical procedures used in the methodically guided re-creation of a foreign life. It is possible to determine the degree to which a text is an eminent text or an action to which texts refer is an eminent action "historically." They are eminent to the degree that other texts represent interpretations and applications of such texts, i.e. to the degree in which they unfold in their future horizon an effective history. If the methodical principles implied in the first canon are applied from case to case to all of them and if from case to case the question is asked what the efficient history of texts which are interpretations of the eminent text is, e.g. of Luther's interpretation of the New Testament, the methodological idea of a completed efficient history of an eminent text is given. The methodical idea of a methodically guided understanding of the connectedness of the historical world is reached if it is taken into account that texts which refer to past eminent texts and actions represent interpretations and applications in which the proper context of the text in question is transcended. Texts and actions which belong to the context of the 

*[p. 341]* ^pg100

in terpreter will be taken in to account, first of all different interpretations of the primordial eminent text. The universal application of the first canon and methodical hermeneutics to all textual "nodes" of this web of connectedness is the methodological representation of the connectedness of the historical world. 

The limits of the methodical approach are obvious and have to be fixed critically. The merger between interpretation and the critical quest for truth and appropriateness indicated by Boeckh is not ruled out in developing the efficient history of the text. Efficient history will determine the attitude of the interpreter to a certain degree, but it cannot determine the final decision. Since efficient history is embedded in the connectedness of the historical world it will reveal possible interpretations which emphasize different aspects of the text. Confronted with the critical question for truth the interpreter has to make a choice or, better, develop a new aspect himself as a response to the text and its efficient history. But this new interpretation of higher order will itself belong to the efficient history of the text because it stands itself in the connectedness of the historical world. Dilthey's proposal to extend the realm of method does not supercede Boeckh's insight that method has only the power of a careful preparation but not the power of a complete determination in the process of the interpretation of texts which are the work of the genius. 

The connectedness of the historical world has to be re-created as the connectedness of the connectedness of the efficient history of eminent texts and actions. If the leap from method to the immediate quest for truth cannot be avoided for the latter, it cannot be avoided in the interpretation of the connectedness of the historical world. It can only be prepared by method, i.e. the application of method is necessary for the development of a *responsible* answer, it does not have the power of determining the answer. 

Is Dilthey aware of the limits of the methodical approach? The following quote shows that he is: 

Life and knowledge of it are the ever freshly-flowing sources of the understanding of the social-historical world, starting from life, understanding penetrates into ever new depths; only by reaction to life and society do the human studies achieve their highest significance which is constantly increasing. But the road to such effectiveness must pass through the objectivity of scientific knowledge. <sup>37</sup>

*[p. 342]* ^pg101

The constitution of the concept of historical connectedness must be properly understood. The general theory of understanding points out that it is present, unthematized and unconscious, already on the level of elementary understanding as the connectedness of life. It is in pre-methodical higher understanding in which it is thematized. The analysis of such an understanding on the level of descriptive, "phenomenological" investigation can reveal its general formal structures. This is the medium in which methodical hermeneutics has to be "criticized," i.e. in which it can be justified and in which its limits can be shown. The connectedness of the historical world is not an object for the method. It functions as a regulative idea in the Kantian sense. The thesis of speculative idealism according to which the whole of history can be grasped not by method but only in a philosophical speculative approach as well as the antithesis of the historical school according to which it can be grasped by method and that the direct quest for truth can be replaced by method, must both be rejected by the "critique of historical reason." 

. 

Conclusion 

Following Dilthey one has to distinguish between (a) hermeneutics as methodically guided understanding and (b) a general theory of understanding which he himself did not call 'hermeneutics." The critique of historical reason belongs to the latter and includes the critique of methodical hermeneutics. The critique points out that methodical hermeneutics is onesidedly founded in premethodical understanding and, furthermore, that it is restricted to the understanding of *fixed* life-expressions.<sup>38</sup>An account for the first canon of hermeneutics can be given only with respect to fixed life expressions, because only with respect to fixed life expressions can the preparatory work of grammatical and historical hermeneutics and critique set the stage for methodical understanding, i.e. determine the context of the text out of which the text has to be understood. The process of higher understanding in the framework of methodical hermeneutics is methodically guided understanding only because whatever is understood can be checked critically with respect to the context. The vague and dubious concept of the "contemporary reader" is thus defined in terms of contexts of texts, i.e. not psychological in any sense. Empathy is of significance for Dilthey's general theory of understanding. It has a function in higher understanding on the premethodical level and then on 

*[p. 343]* ^pg102

the methodical level, but not with respect to method. It is premethodical empathy, which has to be checked methodically. 

Already Boeckh pointed out that the methodical approach has limits. They occur with respect to eminent texts. The critical question for truth leads us on this level into a circle which cannot be dissolved. Boeckh's mediation of philology and philosophy, interpretation and the quest for truth, remains on the speculative level and requires a speculative concept of history as a whole. Dilthey's general theory of understanding gives an explication of this concept as historical connectedness and efficient historical connectedness, *historischer Wirkungszusammenhang.* He proposes, going beyond Boeckh, a methodical approach for the understanding of historical connectedness. A methodological analysis of this proposal shows, however, that the gap between the whole of historical connectedness as presupposed in the general theory of understanding and determining the process already of pre-methodical understanding and a methodical analysis of historical connectedness cannot be closed. The quote at the end of Section II indicates that Dilthey is aware (a) of the limits of the methodical approach and (b) of the fact that the unity of interpretation and application, which is suspended in the methodical approach nevertheless governs the process of understanding. Dilthey emphasizes, however, that an understanding which "penetrates into ever new depths" must pass through "the objectivity of scientific knowledge," i.e. must pass through a phase in which the unity of interpretation and application, which determines elementary understanding throughout, is suspended. 

What is the difference between the old hermeneutics and the new, provided the latter does not make any proposals with respect to method and even recognizes the necessity of methodological discipline? In order to ask this question properly one must be aware of a terminological trap. The New Hermeneutics is, seen from Dilthey's viewpoint and his terminology, hermeneutics qua general theory of understanding, not hermeneutics proper qua methodical hermeneutics, and it does not deal with a detailed analysis of methodical understanding per se in the framework of the general theory of understanding. What is analyzed in the New Hermeneutics is exactly what Dilthey tries to analyze as premethodical understanding. The thesis developed with respect to method is that method cannot escape the general framework of premethodical understanding and generate a separated and independent realm of objectivity and "truth" in the sense of methodologically correct interpretations. The absurdity of such a claim is shown in pointing out that it is impossible to escape the frame of 

*[p. 344]* ^pg103

premethodical understanding and its specific historicity. Neither Boeckh nor Dilthey will deny that. Boeckh in his methodical investigations reaches the same result. The methodical breaking of circularities has limits, and these limits are reached at the point in which the interpretation of a text and the interpreter's quest for truth begin to merge. Dilthey does not deny it because for him the methodically guided analysis of historical connectedness can occur only on the background of the premethodical experience of the historical connectedness of life and will finally merge with it. The difference is that both reach this result in the analysis of methodical understanding as such. The difference is, hence, with respect to methodical hermeneutics, only a difference of approach and emphasis. 

Boeckh does not offer a general theory of understanding, however Dilthey does. If there is a difference between the old and the New Hermeneutics in principle then it will be found here. What is required to determine such differences is a *tertium comparationis.* Gadamer, who does not deny the possibility and the merits of a methodically guided *Wirkungsgeschichte,* efficient history, points out that this type of methodical interpretation as well as every interpretation is grounded in a primordial consciousness of efficient history, *wirkungsgeschichtfiches Bewusstsein,* as consciousness of situatedness. What corresponds to this in Dilthey is the difference between the methodically guided investigation of the connectedness of the historical world and the immediate, originally not thematized consciousness of the historical connectedness of life. The question whether Gadamer's critique of the idealistic and speculative elements of Dilthey's concept of life and its historicity is justified in every respect must be left open. It is, however, obvious that Boeckh assumes for his methodology such a philosophical background. The question which has to be directed to Gadamer from a Diltheian point of view is: is it possible to reach a consciousness of efficient history as a consciousness of situatedness without passing through a stage of methodically grounded interpretation? The situatedness of understanding remains unthematized in elementary understanding. Higher understanding on the premethodical level will not provide the universality for the reflexion which is required for such a consciousness. If so then a complete analysis of the consciousness of efficient history as a consciousness of situatedness requires also a critical analysis of methodical understanding as such, its justification and its limits.

THE LIMITS OF LOGOCENTRISM (ON THE WAY TO 

GRAMMATOLOGY) 

HUGH J. SIL YERMAN 

*State University of New York at Stony Brook* 

In the discourse of the history of metaphysics, Logos occupies a variety of contexts. In order to demonstrate where the thesis of the prevalence of Logos reaches its limits an articulation of the operations of deconstruction is needed. In this way, logo centrism can be situated along with the other principal centrisms, that is, phonocentrism, ethocentrism, phallocentrism and egocentrism. The examination of logocentrism departs from the instance of Heidegger's "Logos" essay. By juxtaposing Heraclitus' account with that of Heidegger, it becomes evident that Heidegger has transposed the place of Logos. Logos is no longer the universal structure of what is said apart from we who listen to it. With Heidegger, I show that we are situated alongside Logos in the ontological difference. In the process, it becomes clear that Logos *qua* language is both the house and the name of the Being of beings. On this basis, Logos is taken to its limits where language itself occupies the place of the indecidable or hinge at the edge of the discourse of metaphysics. 

Unlike Michel Foucault's discourse of epistemic spaces and knowledge frameworks, where every couple of centuries a new episteme supplants the previous one, Jacques Derrida elaborates only two basic "epochs" - that of metaphysics and that which arises at the closure of metaphysics. Metaphysics began, at some point - though Derrida does not date its *arche.* He only remarks that it developed out of the exteriority of writing and the exteriority of the signifier. Along with the *destruction* of this exteriority comes the *construction* of interiority. With the construction of interiority comes the *appurtenance* of metaphysics. 

Metaphysics pertains as a whole in the form of a metaphor. Writing in the epoch of metaphysics is "a sign signifying a signifier itself signifying

*[p. 348]* ^pg107

an eternal verity."1 Writing is taken as that which is literal (itself a sign). As a sign, writing serves as the signified for a signifier, i.e. the letter. The letter is that which forms the word. In the literal sense, writing is the concept associated with the letter. Metaphorically, however, the letter is the signifier for another signified. This metaphorical signified is the "eternal truth," the signified "truth," which goes under the name of *Logos* and is inscribed in the Book of Scripture. This metaphorical scheme was most prominent in the medieval system. 

In the medieval appurtenance of metaphysics, the Book of Nature, which is the place of the letter as signifier, is signified by its association with the Divine Word *(Logos)* which speaks as a voice. The Divine Word leaves its sign in the form of the letter. Writing is the exteriorization of the Divine Word. When the *Logos* speaks, the intelligible is made sensible. The transcendental signified leaves its mark in the empirical signifier. When external and empiricized, the letter is elaborated as a multiplicity of signs which are read literally within the Book of Nature. The signified of the letter is the sign itself, and as a signifying system, a literal writing is proliferated. Since, however, the signifier-signified relation is itself a sign, it can also be interpreted allegorically - recalling the metaphorical, scriptural sign. 

Rousseau reiterates the medieval system by substituting natural writing for the Divine Word. Natural writing is given its form by an arche-speech, an interior holy voice, which provides the "beginning word." The beginning word, however, is readily corrupted in the form of ordinary writing as it becomes "representative, fallen, secondary, instituted." This writing (as a type of social contract) is the replacement for medieval literality. What was a metaphorical sign in the Book of Scripture is now a natural sign formed by the articulation of the beginning word, and what was literal writing in the Book of Nature is now ordinary (Le. conventional) writing in social commerce. 

Similarly Heideggerian thought "would reinstate rather than destroy the instance of the logos and of the truth of Being as *'primum signatum:'* the 'transcendental' signified .. .implied by all linguistic sign ifiers."2 Thus entities or beings *(Seiendes)* respond to the call of Being *(Sein),* that is, they correspond to the *primum signa tum* of which they are only the *signans.* Their ontic character is always conditioned by an ontological voice. Dassen as a sign leaves its signature in the ontological difference between Being and beings, while the ontic distinction between beings indicates the place occupied by Rousseau's social sign and the medieval Book of Nature. 

The history of metaphysics in Derrida's terms is the history of a 

*[p. 349]* ^pg108

series of ruptures or breaks. These include the break between the Divine Word and the Book of Nature, between speaking and writing, and between Being and beings. Such oppositions, crystallized as binary oppositions in semiology, form the center of the epoch of metaphysics. One could add other pairs such as intelligible/sensible, mental/physical, interior/exterior, etc. Together they constitute the metaphysical discourse, which begins to achieve its closure with the advent of writing (arche-writing), the *differance* which *deconstructs* the text of metaphysics. 

I have now appealed to the earmark of Derridian discourse: "deconstruction." Deconstruction is the praxis which is employed in the movement to the limit, border, or hinge, and by which *differance* is inscribed as arche-writing. Deconstruction is structured. It situated itself at the intersection of the inside and the outside, the word and the concept, ordinary writing and speaking. Deconstruction is neither destruction, a tearing apart, analyzing into atomic units nor construction, a bringing together, synthesizing into a unified totality. Deconstruction involves both destruction and construction. It operates at the juncture which Merleau-Ponty described as the chiasm or intertwining between the visible and the invisible, between philosophy and non-philosophy; it fills out the Heideggerian "in-between" *\[Inzwischen\]* as indicated by the crossing out of Being *(Sein)* in the *Seinsfrage.* In its own right, it determines the literal meaning of writing as metaphoricity itself. The citing of writing as literally metaphorical places Derrida's texts at the interface between the closure of metaphysics which Nietzsche, Heidegger, and Merleau-Ponty inaugurate and the post-closure which remains pure *differance.* 

At the edge of the epoch of metaphysics, deconstruction takes its place. Now thoroughly proliferated, deconstruction, - the mark of Derridean discourse, - has achieved both the renown and the imprecision of a celebrated event. Although the task here is to demonstrate the space in which a certain type of discourse reaches its limits, it would be unwise to simply put deconstruction to work. This caveat becomes especially important when attempting to situate Heidegger near the border of metaphysical language. Thus a deconstruction of Heideggerian discourse would hide the respect in which Heidegger has taken the history of metaphysics to its final moments and thereby established himself as one of the last outposts on the way to grammatology. We must therefore reconstruct the Heideggerian discourse in order to deconstruct it. 

*[p. 350]* ^pg109

Deconstruction involves the examination of texts taken to their extreme form. Here texts or discourses can neither build models nor tear them down. Theory construction and objections to theses have no place. Deconstruction establishes itself at the interface between thesis and anti-thesis, between history and post-history, between truth and lie. <sup>3</sup>It should not be supposed that deconstruction has any *proper* place nor for that matter any *improper* one. One might expect therefore that deconstruction is either Kantian or even Hegelian in character. In the first instance, the function of *critique* is to establish the conditions of the possibility of, for example, metaphysical questions. Kantian critique however delimits what can be known and how it can be known. It would seem that critique avoids assertions and only establishes conditions of knowledge. However, it does not recognize that even critique itself is conditioned by a certain play and limit-setting; it does not consider that it builds theses - particularly that of human action created against a backdrop of a transcendental and empirical formation. Furthermore, it does not admit that its very procedure has become the foundation for a whole era of metaphysical thinking. Similarly, the Hegelian *Aujhebung* permits the reestablishment of higher truths constructed on the basis of prior oppositions. It does not go to the tear between one type of discourse and another. Rather it attempts to surpass both. Hence the Hegelian enterprise is a paragon of theoretical knowledge: of destruction and construction. 

Kant and Hegel, luminaries of the metaphysical establishment, give credence to the idea that critique and *Aujhebung* can provide tools for the fulfillment of a *telos.* All that is can be known or at least known in a certain way. Deconstruction however can inscribe neither teleology nor archaeology. It can only work with (and play at) the traces, supplements, and edges of discourses which presuppose and incorporate metaphysical features. In short, deconstruction looks for the crack, the hinge, and the tear in the manifold of a metaphysical texture. Deconstruction situates itself there at the place where placement spills over. 

Heidegger operates within the metaphysical texture which calls for origins and announces ends. Yet if we could speak of horizons, Heidegger would approach the one beyond which metaphysics no longer functions. What would it be like to stand on the other side? If Heidegger has a vision of that possibility, he is nevertheless firmly entrenched on this side. Though he may offer an account of the end of metaphysics, the end of philosophy, the version itself is provided from within metaphysics. This end is not the same as that which Kant offered when he 

*[p. 351]* ^pg110

admonished us to never treat man merely as a means, or which Sartre proposes when he describes our fundamental project. It does however ressemble the type of end which Michel Foucault notes when he indicates that man is at an end. <sup>4</sup>Metaphysics will achieve its completion, metaphysics will be fulfilled and will no longer stand on its own. But Heidegger the metaphysician brings this about within a metaphysical discourse. Here is a philosophy which announces the end of philo sophy.5 

Where is the edge between philosophy and non-philosophy?6 At what place can we say that philosophy turns into non-philosophy? What are the parameters for identifying the difference between them? Is there a location in which the indecidable operates? A topology in which the *topos* is botha *locus* and a topic will help, but only in that we can employ geographers and rhetoricians. Heidegger is neither of these. He can of course look for the clearing *\[Lichtung\]* or for country paths and he can point out the place of speech, but neither of these activities will help us to discriminate between philosophy and non-philosophy, to interpret the signs at the edge of metaphysics where metaphysics can no longer identify itself as such. Nevertheless Heidegger does offer an account of difference, particularly the ontological difference, which introduces the question of limits - the limits ofthat-whichis (beings), the limits of that which is present, and even the limits of truth (at the place where truth comes *out* of concealedness). While Heidegger falls decidedly *on this side* of the history of metaphysics, he also moves quite close to the deconstructive machinery. However, advancing with care, he keeps himself from becoming enmeshed in the interface. 

Derridean deconstruction takes to task the five great centrisms which characterize the epoch of metaphysics: (1) phonocentrism, (2) ethnocentrism, (3) phallocentrism, (4) egocentrism, and (5) logocentrism. Phonocentrism is particularly prominent in the medieval appeal to the Divine voice and in Rousseau's substitution of an original speech (an arche-speech) for the Divine Word. Levi-Strauss places ethnocentrism sufficiently in question for Derrida to examine its closure. The purloined texts of Lacan and Nietzsche indicate where the deconstruction of phallocentrism can occur. Since I have entered the fray elsewhere on the question of egocentrism, I shall not belabor it in this context. 7 Rather the discourse which is of concern here is that of logocentrism, for logocentrism is precisely the domain in which Heidegger gives form to his own language. It is also where the deconstruction of metaphysics must predominate. 

*[p. 352]* ^pg111

Logocentrism takes Logos as central. But what sort of centrality can it have when it is regarded as that which permeates everything? Can that which is all, or one, be central? Centering announces a circumference, an encompassing domain for which there is a center. De-centering the self (as transcendental ego), for example, conforms to this model. In logocentrism, however, the centrality of Logos is due to its all-pervasiveness, for it is, in that way, the point of reference and return for all that is said, expressed and meant. 

It would not be suitable to retierate (any more than I have already done) the diachronic account of the changes in the conception of Logos within the history of metaphysics from Logos as universal structure to Logos as Divine Word to Logos as natural speech to Logos as language. Rather we shall turn to the place where Logos is most prominently in question and where Heidegger has attempted to demonstrate how he moves it to the edge of the history of metaphysics. Here is where Heidegger allows for the beginning of a de-Iogocentralization of language in grammatological arche-writing. I refer to a passage from the essay entitled "Logos" in Part Three of Heidegger's *Vortrage und Auf-siitze.* This text itself questions another text - Heraclitus' Fragment B 50 - which reads: "When you have listened not to me but to the Logos, it is wise within the same Logos to say: One is All." *(OVI( €Iloii aAh€ TOU A orov al( ovO'allTas* OIlOhOrE'" *O'OCPOII EO'TLII ifll 1fallTa).* The passage from Heidegger goes as follows:

Here Heidegger has moved away from his own equating of *Logos* with *Rede.* He has located *Logos* where language speaks and removed it from its privileged ontological state. 

Heidegger asserts that Logos (language) is the "Laying that gathers." This would suggest that Logos is both inside the Being of beings and outside it, both its inner structure and its outer shell. But how can language be both inside and outside? Or would it not be better to say that language (Logos) is the hinge between the inside and the outside, that language itself is at the juncture between the container and the contained? If we could accept the deconstruction, it would mean that language itself is the indecidable, wavering between the inclusion of the Being of that-which-is and its inner necessity. 

In this respect, language is not only the rubric under which all of metaphysics (in its history) must be thought but also its identity and meaning. In grammatology, writing has priority - as arche-writing, where neither phonocentrism nor logocentrism prevails. Arche-writing is the textualization of experience, the traces of the letter and the voice which vie for the lead position. But neither written language nor spoken language can succeed, for at their juncture is language itself, cultivated by metaphysics - a metaphysics which calls for its own end. Therefore, to what does language belong? It cannot belong *(gehoren)* uniquely to the Being of beings any more than it can uniquely hear *(horen)* the call of Being. Language has lost its center when it incorporates itself into a disseminated self whose meaning is wherever the Logos can be said. REINER SCHURMANN 

*New School for Social Research* 

What if there were, lodged within the heart of law itself, a law of impurity or a principle of contamination? What if the condition for the possibility of law were the *a priori* of a counter-law, an axiom of impossibility, maddening its sense, order and reason? 

Jacques Derrida <sup>1</sup>

With these rhetorical questions Jacques Derrida casts general suspicion on what is primarily constitutive of the transcendental tradition: the authority of the "I think" as the source of laws for both knowing and acting. In this paper I wish to show some presuppositions under which the legislative ego can appear as traversed by "the *a priori* of a counter-law," by a condition of "impossibility"; how an element of transgression can be seen to "contaminate" transcendental legislation atits very heart. I will then point out some consequences of the formal identity between legislation and transgression for the status of the social and human sciences today. 

A ftrst set of such presuppositions can be stated briefly, as just so many hints that the reading I propose of that tradition is not disinterested. 

*Reading modern philosophy backwards.* What is the text in which transcendental legislation may eventually appear as formally identical with transgression? It is not anyone text by Kant, Nietzsche or Heidegger taken in isolation, but rather a discursive network within which each of their books is inscribed, a network that undergoes incessant shifts. Thus to the question, What are we reading?, I answer: a diachronical concatenation of texts, in which elements of continuity and 

*[p. 362]* ^pg120

discontinuity have to be isolated. This cannot be done from some fictitious, neutral vantage point. The inherited issues appear in their historical transmutations when unfolded from the fold in which we live. To situate any given text is to appropriate it, whether explicitly or implicitly, from our own site in that history, a site that I will describe as closing modernity. To read the transcendental tradition backward, with the explicit interest in elucidating our own locus, it is not enough to trace mere developments of concepts *(Begriffsgeschichte)* nor the progressive enrichment of a text by generations of its readers *(Wirkungsgeschichte).* When a text from another moment in history is placed into our own context so as to enlighten it, that text's topics will necessarily be de-naturalized, deprived of their original citizenship within the datable discursive network from which they were born, and thereby de-natured, altered in their nature. 

*Topical Strategies.* The subject matter for a retrospective reading of the tradition consists not so much in philosophical topics as in their strategies. The topos of transcendental legislation will be "varied" along the line - the lineage - of modern, late modern and closing modern contexts. A topical strategy is the itinerary that an issue goes through as it appears and reappears, without a master strategist, in transient horizons. To speak of diachronical strategies also implies that the successive crises in modernity cannot be understood as utter ruptures or breaks (contrary to what Michel Foucault seems to hold): to account for the possibility of novelty in history means to sort out what endures from what undergoes change. Novelty results from a play of identity and difference in topical strategies. 

*The hypothesis of closure.* The interest in casting general suspicion upon the transcendental tradition is, as I have said, to better understand our own site. There could be no retrospective reading and no search for topical strategies, to say nothing of the *"a priori* of a counter-law," without the hypothesis that in the twentieth century a certain history is drawing to a close. That history has been characterized by such misleading phrases as "onto-theology" and "logo-centrism." They are misleading in that they seem to perpetuate the eschatological, apocalyptic tone introduced into philosophy in the early nineteenth century, a tone that indeed turns the claim of closure into yet another ideality. The thrust of that hypothesis is, however, more modest.
