### 21.1–8: New world and new Jerusalem

*[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-1|Text]], p. 572* ^21-1

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-1|1]]:** *New heaven and a new earth*, see [[Isaiah 65#^65-17|Isa 65.17]]; [[Isaiah 66#^66-22|66.22]], referring to the descent of the new Jerusalem. Some rabbis imagined Isaiah’s phrase to pertain to the rejuve-nation of the earth after the period of woes leading into the messianic era (e.g., *Gen. Rab.* 12). ^21-1-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-2|2]]:** *New Jerusalem*, see 19.7–9. *Prepared as a bride*, see 19.7–10n. ^21-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-3|3]]:** The descent of the heavenly city signifies God’s *home* (Gk “skene”; Heb “mishkan,” “tent/dwelling”) among people, following [[Ezekiel 37#^37-27|Ezek 37.27]] (see [[Torah 19 - Terumah#^25-8|Ex 25.8]]; *4 Ezra* 7.26). Cf. [[2 Corinthians 06#^6-16|2 Cor 6.16–18]]. ^21-3

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-4|4]]:** *Every tear . . . Death will be no more*, 7.17; see [[Isaiah 25#^25-8|Isa 25.8]]; [[Isaiah 35#^35-10|35.10]]. ^21-4

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-5|5]]:** John continues to interpose divine declarations reminiscent of Isa 65–66 and Ezek 37 with the distinctive divine attributes introduced in 1.8,17–18. *Seated on the throne*, God (see 20.11n.). ^21-5

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-6|6]]:** *Alpha and Omega*, see 1.8n. *Water of life*, the river that runs through the Edenic Jerusalem (22.1–2; see [[Ezekiel 47#^47-1|Ezek 47.1–12]]). Some amoraic rabbis imagined that the Garden of Eden would reappear at the end of time (*b. Pesah*. [[Pesachim 54#54a|54a]]). ^21-6

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-7|7]]:** *Conquer*, referring back to promises to individuals in the seven congregations (2.7b,11b,17b,26; 3.5,12,21). ^21-7

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-8|8]]:** Such an assortment of evildoers may be meant to characterize the range of deviants of the Greco-Roman city. *Lake . . . with fire*, see 19.19–20n.; 14.10n. ^21-8

### 21.9–21: Walls and gates of the city

*[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-9|Text]], p. 572* · The systematic detailing of the city’s architecture recalls Ezekiel’s tour of God’s heavenly Temple (chs 40–42), likewise on a tall mountain (40.2). ^21-9

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-9|9]]:** That one of the *angels* with a bowl of the *last plagues* (see 15.1) now serves as an “angelus interpres,” or “tour-angel,” signifies that the heavenly ministry has shifted from execution of judgment to revelation of the pure world. ^21-9-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-10|10]]:** *In the spirit*, 1.10. ^21-10

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-11|11]]:** *Glory*, (Gk “doxa”; Heb “kavod”) of God, an aspect of the enthroned presence ([[Ezekiel 01#^1-28|Ezek 1.28]]; [[Ezekiel 43#^43-4|43.4]]; see [[Revelation 15#^15-8|Rev 15.8]]; [[Revelation 21#^21-23|21.23b]]). ^21-11

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-12|12]]:** Names of the twelve tribes inscribed on *gates*: cf. [[Ezekiel 48#^48-30|Ezek 48.30–35]]. ^21-12

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-14|14]]:** An odd addition, perhaps by a later editor (see [[Ephesians 02#^2-20|Eph 2.20]]), since the verse introduces ideas nowhere else in Revelation: the *twelve foundations*; the reference to *twelve apostles*; and the use of *Lamb* to refer to the earthly Jesus (cf. 11.8). ^21-14

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-15|15–17]]:** As in Ezek 40–42, measuring with an angel’s help reveals the heavenly symmetry and incalculable enormity of a divine structure. Unlike most ancient Mediterranean cities, God’s city is a perfect square (v 16). *One hundred forty-four cubits*, 12 x 12 cubits, symbolizing the completeness of God’s people (see 7.4–8n.); a cubit was approximately 18 inches. The same interest in the eschatological Jerusalem’s perfect dimensions, based in Ezekiel, appears in the Qumran New Jerusalem fragment (5Q185); see also 7.4–8n. ^21-15

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-18|18–21]]:** Perhaps inspired by Isaiah’s verses about a bejeweled Jerusalem (54.11–12), John reimagines the panoply of jewels from the breastplate of the high priest ([[Torah 20 - Tetzaveh#^28-17|Ex 28.17–21]]; [[Torah 23 - Pekudei#^39-10|39.10–13]]) now on the walls of the heavenly city, as if to combine two spectacles of divine perfection. The spectacle of the bejeweled Jerusalem derives from an older Jewish motif of the bejeweled bride: see, e.g., [[Isaiah 61#^61-10|Isa 61.10]]; *Jos. Asen*. 18.6. Rabbinic tradition also imagined a new Jerusalem in the world to come as a bejeweled spectacle: *b. B. Bat.* [[Bava Batra 75#75a|75a]]; *b. Pesah.* [[Pesachim 50#50a|50a]]; *Midr. Tehilim* 87. A “City of Gold” or “Jerusalem of Gold” referred in amoraic times to an elaborate gold crown worn by wealthy women: *b. Sot.* [[Sotah 49#49b|49b]]; *b. Shabb.* [[Shabbat 59#59a|59a]]–59b; *b. Ned.* [[Nedarim 50#50a|50a]]. ^21-18

> [!note] A Holy City without a Holy Temple *(p. 573)*
> Given God’s inextricable connection to the Jerusalem Temple in biblical tradition (Ps 24; 84; [[Torah 16 - Beshalach#^15-17|Ex 15.17–18]]), it is no surprise that texts from Ezekiel and Deutero-Isaiah to *1 Enoch* and the Dead Sea Scrolls all imagined a great, eternal Temple as the centerpiece of God’s new creation ([[Isaiah 66#^66-20|Isa 66.20]]; Ezek 40–48; *1 En*. 90.28–29; *Jub*. 1.27–28). In deliberately noting the absence of an eschatological Temple, John perhaps imagines that such a structure will not exist. Does he think that Rome’s destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE means that such a structure would no longer be part of God’s plan? Or, as Christian interpreters often suggest, does John mean that with Christ’s death the Temple, its barriers and priestly privileges, had been superfluous obstacles to the divine presence (cf. [[Mark 15#^15-38|Mk 15.38]])?
>
> Viewed in isolation, John’s temple-less Jerusalem seems strange, but in the context of the many images of eschatological cities and divine presence in early Jewish literature, it is more comprehensible. Much like Ezekiel’s eschatological Temple, John’s holy city stringently excludes all impurities (21.27; 22.3,15); its holiness is manifest in its divisions and gates (21.12–21). Indeed, when we read these details in light of Ezekiel and Qumran’s Temple Scroll, both of which imagine temples (or temple rules) covering virtually the entirety of their eschatological cities, it appears that it is the very boundaries of John’s holy city, not a particular building in it, that constitute the perfection that allows the divine presence to dwell there.
>
> The infrastructure of the eschatological Jerusalem was a topic of quite diverse thinking in apocalyptic literature: from meticulous reestab-lishments of the Temple cult (Ezekiel, see *2 Bar.* 6.7–9), to far more abstract notions of a space for God’s eternal dwelling ([[Isaiah 66#^66-1|Isa 66.1–2]]; *4 Ezra* 10.27, 44–54; *T. Benj.* 9.2; *T. Levi* 18; see [[LXX 2 Maccabees 05#^5-19|2 Macc 5.19]]). Even in the Psalms YHWH is celebrated as dwelling in a city or a mountain, not just a physical sanctuary (Ps 48; 87; see [[Jeremiah 03#^3-16|Jer 3.16–17]]). Later rabbinic literature took a more concrete approach: an eschatological Jerusalem with a Temple (*b. Hag*. [[Chagigah 12#12b|12b]]; *b. B. Bat.* [[Bava Batra 75#75a|75a]]; *b. Pesah*. [[Pesachim 50#50a|50a]]; 54; *Midr. Tehilim* 87; *Lev. Rab*. 9). But here in Revelation the divine presence is imagined as dwelling in the heavenly Jerusalem by virtue of its purity and architecture, not a temple per se. Thus John’s combined interests in purity, architecture, and divine presence can be situated easily in this continuum of Jewish ideas.

### 21.22–27: The holy city

*[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-22|Text]], p. 574* ^21-22

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-22|22]]:** *No temple*, that John noticed the absence of a temple means that the Temple would have been expected in the eschatological Jerusalem (see “A Holy City Without a Holy Temple,” p. 573). ^21-22-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-23|23–26]]:** Inspired by the light produced by the glory of God in Ezekiel’s vision (43.2), John extends the image of eternal intimacy between God and the holy city through the metaphor of light. As with most biblical and Second-Temple depictions of the eschatological city (esp. [[Jeremiah 03#^3-17|Jer 3.17]]; [[Isaiah 60#^60-1|Isa 60.1–11]]; [[LXX Tobit 13#^13-11|Tob 13.11]]), here it serves as a beacon to all nations. This tradition continues, in different permutations, with the rabbis (*t. Sanh.* 13.2–3; *b. Pesah.* [[Pesachim 118#118b|118b]]; *Pesiq. Rab.* 1). John’s imagery of universal devotion in the new, sanctified Jerusalem contrasts with the judgment scenes (19.17–20; 20.7–9) that depict the destruction of Gentile kingdoms. ^21-23

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-25|25]]:** *Gates will never be shut by day*, inspired by [[Isaiah 60#^60-11|Isa 60.11a]],[[Isaiah 60#^60-19|19–20]], and [[Zechariah 14#^14-7|Zech 14.7]], the verse refers to the perpetual light the city enjoys, not to an elimination of boundaries. *There will be no night*, the elimination of this division of times from creation ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-3|Gen 1.3–5]],[[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-14|14–18]]) allows perpetual light, a sign of God’s presence. ^21-25

**[[NRSVue Revelation 21#^21-27|27]]:** The city’s heavenly purity is reasserted through the exclusion of the *unclean* (lit., “common”; Gk “koinon”) and polluting (see 21.8; 22.15; Lev 15; 21; [[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^23-10|Deut 23.9–14]]; [[Zechariah 14#^14-21|Zech 14.21]]; 11QSTemple 47.3–18). Rabbinic images of the eschatological Jerusalem maintained this interest in the maintenance of purity, especially if Gentiles were included (*b. B. Bat.* [[Bava Batra 75#75a|75a]]-b). ^21-27
