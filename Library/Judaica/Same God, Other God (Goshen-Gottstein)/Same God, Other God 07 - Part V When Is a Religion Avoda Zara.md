## Part V When Is a Religion Avoda Zara?

 Overview 

 The final part of the book returns to posing the lead question in terms of Avoda Zara. Having journeyed from Avoda Zara to the “same god” question, we can now return to a more nuanced way of inquiring about another religion as Avoda Zara. The suggested new formulation assumes that all religions can be Avoda Zara and that the discernment of whether a religion is or is not Avoda Zara runs within religions, and not between them. Accordingly, the question is posed: When is a religion considered Avoda Zara? This formulation can be applied to Judaism and is studied here with reference to the test case of Hinduism. Rather than assume that “Hinduism” is or is not Avoda Zara, the discussion suggests a calculus of factors that together add up to forms of the religion that can be evaluated as Avoda Zara or as beyond the charge of Avoda Zara. 

 The factors that make up the calculus represent the sum total of all the criteria that have served our discussion of Avoda Zara, especially in Part III. Rather than assuming that only one criterion determines a religion’s status, it is suggested that a convergence of factors informs the decision. This, it is argued, has always been the case with reference to other religions, and the procedure is being made explicit in our discussion. 

 The factors that converge include awareness of God, morality, spirituality, overall orientation of the religious life, and more. Different sections of a religion—groups, communities, philosophies, may be judged differently. Moreover, it may be that the determination should itself be context-specific and relate to the context within which the question is posed. What is required to be Avoda Zara-free in terms of diplomatic or business relations is much less than what is required for purposes of spiritual sharing and inspiration. 

 The approach to Avoda Zara from this perspective takes it from the realm of clear-cut halachic determination to the realm of spiritual discernment. Moreover, pronouncement on another religion as Avoda Zara is not a one-time event. Rather, Avoda Zara functions as a dynamic category, calling us to be mindful and asking us to consider all expressions of the religious and spiritual life in terms of their ultimate value and quality. While we may lose some of the certainty that comes 

*[p. 184]* ^pg187

with application of the category to large religious entities such as “Hinduism,” we also gain much by this method. It allows us to approach Avoda Zara as a dynamic concept that has meaning in our religious lives and that informs our view of self and other in light of our highest spiritual ideals. What we lose in terms of othering and the ability to label others as Avoda Zara we gain in the viability and vitality of a religious category that lies at the foundations of our religious history and life.

### 14 Beyond a Global Approach to Hinduism

 We have journeyed from global condemnation of anything that smacks of Avoda Zara and that touches upon ancient Jewish sensibilities to 

other forms of worship to a more nuanced and complex view of Hinduism. In the process, we have moved from the domain of certainty and of absolute categories—wrong and right, idolatrous and true worship—to the realm of increasing complexity and uncertainty. One of the effects of increasing knowledge and understanding of Hinduism is the greater uncertainty involved in making a pronouncement on the question “Is Hinduism Avoda Zara ?” It is now time to consider whether the question has been framed in the optimal way, and whether in light of all that we know we must adopt a different way of framing the question. 

 There are several reasons why we should consider reframing the question. It has become apparent that complex factors are at play, with pride of place going to the tension between philosophy and ritual. Any pronouncement on the question as posed hitherto forces us to prefer one dimension over another, thereby leading us to ignore or minimize the importance of either the philosophical or the ritual dimension. We have also seen that the problem of Avoda Zara extends beyond what we associate with it at first sight, namely the worship of images. Accordingly, it is an internal Jewish problem as well as an external problem, in relation to others. While the manifestations of Avoda Zara and the rhetoric that accompanies them are of different magnitude when it comes to internal Avoda Zara, we cannot ignore this dimension when we seek to develop a comprehensive understanding of Avoda Zara, in light of which we might view other religions. But beyond these considerations, there is a fundamental problem that pertains to all religions, but that is particularly relevant to Hinduism. Religions are not monoliths and their believers are not all of a kind. To ask whether Hinduism, or for that matter any religion, is Avoda Zara is to ignore all the complexity of a religious tradition and all the varying nuances among its believers, whether they stem from different creeds and practices or from different levels of spiritual understanding. 

*[p. 186]* ^pg189

 In light of all these considerations, I would like to propose the following question as the revised lead question: When does the practice of a religion become Avoda Zara ? Or: When should a religion be considered Avoda Zara ? The new formulation assumes all religions have the potential of being or not being Avoda Zara and that the verdict on the matter is not pronounced on a religion-by-religion basis, but based on other criteria. The novel question allows us to apply the question within, as well as without. And it allows us to integrate the results of our “same God” discussion within a theory of what is Avoda Zara. Following this new formulation we can then ask “When does Hinduism become Avoda Zara ?” Or: “When should we consider Hinduism as Avoda Zara ?” 

 It is noteworthy that some of the fundamental rabbinic discussions are not framed as global pronouncements regarding a religion in its entirety. Meiri speaks of nations that are bound by ways of religion, rather than of specific religious traditions. The proponents of the permissibility of Shituf similarly do not make a blanket statement about Christianity, even though it is implied. Significantly, Maimonides does speak in broad terms about Christianity versus Islam, considering Islam nonidolatrous and Christianity as Avoda Zara. <sup>1</sup> The less nuance one brings to the discussion, the easier it is to address the topic through sweeping generalizations. Some contemporary rabbinic voices have realized, after nearly 2,000 years of engagement, that Christianity is not a monolith and that a halachic view of Christianity requires nuancing. <sup>2</sup> However, little is made of this distinction in practical terms, and it seems more like a nod to their awareness of the heterogeneous nature of the society within which they live than a serious considered view of Christianity that might have practical and theoretical consequences. Nevertheless, the very raising of the recognition that Christianity is not a monolith is significant in terms of intellectual honesty and in terms of its potential to establish a different kind of discourse with other religions. To argue that, at least in theory, some parts of a religion, construed as broadly as “Christianity,” “Hinduism,” etc., are Avoda Zara, while others are not, forces us to study and engage those traditions. It challenges us implicitly to define where the problem with Avoda Zara lies and what kind of otherness we seek to cultivate in relation to the other religious tradition, whether one of distinction or disdain. 

 The reason that those few halachists who have attempted a distinction between the various strands of Christianity have not gone very far with it is related to the workings of the halachic system. The declaration that a religion is Avoda Zara automatically entails a series of prohibitions and attitudes. These cannot be applied partially or selectively. One either enters a church or does not. One cannot enter a church a little bit, because Jews and Christians recognize the same God *in part* . The nuances of the discussion of Shituf, and even more of the previous discussion of the same God, cannot be translated easily into the halachic system. That system applies wholesale patterns of behavior to large entities, or at least has done so to date. Any nuancing that might lead to a weakening of the sense that a religion is Avoda Zara would imply, from the perspective of halacha, a loosening of the stringencies and prohibitions associated with Avoda Zara. Very few halachists would be comfortable with applying practical changes in Laws of Avoda Zara on account of a more nuanced understanding of another religion. <sup>3</sup> 

*[p. 187]* ^pg190

Thus, it requires a wholesale revision of the criteria that define and that inform Avoda Zara to lead to any significant change in action. This is precisely the kind of wholesale redefinition of tradition undertaken by Meiri. Anything short of such wholesale revision will leave us struggling with the question of how much and under what circumstances we may draw practical consequences from a more nuanced understanding of another tradition. <sup>4</sup> 

 While historically and theologically the time may have indeed come to reexamine our tradition and its attitudes to other traditions in a more systematic way, applying these new understandings to the halacha is far more challenging and likely to be received with much greater reservation. Consequently, the present attempt to develop a more complex view of what Avoda Zara is in general, and how it pertains to Hinduism in particular, should not be considered a halachic discussion. <sup>5</sup> Whether or not several decades from now the way will have been found to apply these insights to the halachic process and what effect the decisions of individuals, following their own instincts not always under the guidance of halachic teachers, will have on the system are matters that we can only speculate about. 

 What follows, then, is an attempt to ask the question of when a religion ought to be considered Avoda Zara, with special reference to Hinduism. In order to do so, I would like to first review the various dimensions that we have surveyed. I refer to the different dimensions in light of which Hinduism was considered, but even more so to the different perspectives through which Avoda Zara was defined and approached. The following discussion deviates from conventional discussions in one major point. Historically, discussions of Avoda Zara offer one specific criterion, in light of which a practice, a practitioner, or a religion are declared to be Avoda Zara, or free of the charge of idolatry. Historically, one either opts for the criteria adopted by Maimonides (or purported to him), or accepts those suggested by Meiri. Or rather: one either accepts the authority of the one or the other. One rarely comes across a discussion that seeks to approach Avoda Zara by incorporating multiple views, perspectives, and criteria. In terms of conventional halachic practice, the reason is evident. One either declares a given religion or practice to be Avoda Zara and draws the appropriate practical consequences or one does not. In terms of proscribing behaviors for the public at large, through the medium of halacha, one cannot say that a religion is simultaneously Avoda Zara and non-Avoda Zara, depending on the criteria applied. As one seeks certainty, one must adopt one criterion, one opinion. In light of this choice one declares a religion to either be or not be Avoda Zara. The following discussion seeks to problematize the method by which we chose a single criterion and a single opinion. Our tradition contains multiple criteria in light of which other religions are viewed. To choose one criterion at the expense of others is to ignore some important dimension of tradition and its wisdom. I will consequently explore what it could mean to uphold multiple criteria in relation to other religions as Avoda Zara and how one might construct a robust sense of what Avoda Zara is, taking into account the wealth of criteria and understandings that have informed earlier discussions of Avoda Zara and of other religions. Let us then review what these criteria have been. 

*[p. 188]* ^pg191

 A. It seems to me the most important definition, hence criterion, for recognizing Avoda Zara is that offered by Maimonides in his terse definition in Chapter 2 of Laws of Avoda Zara : the worship of any being other than God. Thus the basic definition of Avoda Zara is related to recognition of the existence of one single God, to whom worship is due. The basic understanding of Shituf conforms to this definition of Avoda Zara, while recognizing the permissibility of worshipping another being, so long as worship of God is maintained alongside it. According to Nachmanides, awareness of God may be sufficient to permit worship of other beings for non-Jews. As I have suggested in our discussion earlier, even the seemingly alternative definition offered by Meiri should be understood as an alternative means of establishing the identity of the God worshipped. It seems to me that both in terms of substance and in terms of the authorities who employ this definition, this remains the most fundamental definition. 

 The encounter with Hinduism forces us to consider not simply the definition of Avoda Zara, but more specifically the criteria by means of which the otherness of the worshipped god is established. The complex relationship between philosophical recognition of one God and the multiple forms through which worship of the Divine takes place challenges us to revisit the criteria by means of which the otherness of the other god is established. Awareness of the historical evolution of the affirmation of exclusive faith in Israel’s God makes the challenge even more complex. Once only one God is recognized as real, what does it mean to worship beings other than God? On the face of it there is no meaning to competition with what amounts to nothing. <sup>6</sup> Hindu theology pushes the question one stage further: If only one being exists, and all beings are manifestations and expressions of that one being, what is the meaning of worshipping another god? Thus, Hinduism’s challenge to a Jewish view of Avoda Zara is more significant and fundamental than the selection of appropriate criteria for determining the otherness of the worshipped god and getting past the knee-jerk reaction of identifying the worship of forms with the otherness of god. Hinduism, or its philosophical and theological spokesmen, challenges us to think through the very notion of the otherness of another god, once it is affirmed that only one God exists and that all beings manifest His being. Hindu metaphysics undermine, to a large extent, the notions of agency and worship of intermediaries that have informed most Jewish thinking on Avoda Zara since the Middle Ages. Far from being a cut-and-dry example of Avoda Zara, Hinduism provides possibly the greatest and most complex challenge to any contemporary attempt to construct a meaningful definition of Avoda Zara. 

 B. While the definition of worshipping another god may be quite simple, its proper application may be far more complicated. In the framework of biblical covenantal thinking, which is grounded in a henotheistic or monolatric worldview that may recognize the existence of other gods but prescribes worship to one God alone, it is quite simple to recognize when another god is being worshipped. Part of what makes such recognition possible is the method of worship, resorting to images and forms that depict what might be recognizably other gods. Thus, the external or practical criterion of the worship of images emerges as the most immediate way of recognizing that another god is worshipped. For the greater 

*[p. 189]* ^pg192

part of Jewish history, this criterion has operated to a large extent exclusively, providing the needed discernment as to whether God or another being is worshipped. Were we looking for a single criterion for defining Avoda Zara, it would be proper to not consider the worship of images as that criterion, as much of the preceding discussion has made clear. Nevertheless, if we consider multiple criteria for determining what Avoda Zara is, we should recognize the power that the worship of images has in suggesting that the worship of another religion is Avoda Zara. Given the deep-seated attitudes toward the worship of images and how they have shaped Jewish attitudes to other religions for millennia, we should certainly acknowledge the use of images as one criterion in the determination of whether a given religion is Avoda Zara. It is certainly a powerful psychological factor and depending on how one understands or constructs the halachic materials, it could also be argued to be a self-standing halachic criterion. <sup>7</sup> 

 C. One often juxtaposes Meiri’s moral criterion with the theological criterion of recognition of the single God to whom alone worship is due. Meiri’s criteria certainly do function as an alternative to exclusive reliance on the worship of images as determining the status of a religion as Avoda Zara. Meiri was, after all, willing to overlook Christian image worship in light of his alternative criteria and definitions. As our discussion has suggested, Meiri’s criterion does not simply replace one criterion with another. Rather, it assumes that a moral life both matches and points to knowledge of God. Whereas most authorities rely either on theological affirmations or on methods of worship, Meiri relies on moral behavior. This allows Meiri not simply to identify other religions as worshipping the same God, but more importantly, for him, as being instances of true, hence valid, religion. While theological criteria for proper faith are loosened, Meiri’s alternative criteria provide another means of affirming that the same God is worshipped in different religions. 

 D. Meiri provides a helpful departure point for some additional criteria for defining Avoda Zara and conversely for recognition of the same God. In our discussion of Meiri we sought to broaden the criteria from basic moral life to the special life of “friends of God,” saints and holy people, whose lives show traces of the touch of God and of a relationship with Him. Once we admit phenomenological criteria, derived from the observations of a lived religious life, into our definition of Avoda Zara, these may be broadened from the foundations of the religious life—healthy moral living—to its summits—the spiritual lives of special individuals. This criterion is particularly relevant to Hinduism. Hinduism as a religious culture is largely constructed around such virtuosi. The organization of the religious life around teachers and saints suggests their centrality in phenomenological, sociological, and theological terms. They also point to the overall pattern of a religious culture that Meiri could recognize as true religion. 

 E. Reference to gurus may allow us to develop a deeper appreciation for the texture of Hindu religious culture, and recognition of the centrality of the quest for God may be helpful for an overall evaluation of the religion in terms of Avoda Zara. Nevertheless, the attitudes shown to gurus also present us with challenges that are specific to the question of Avoda Zara. Even if we assume that the offering of worship to teachers and saints is ultimately but another form of respect, 

*[p. 190]* ^pg193

reverence, and approach to God, and that it can be reconciled, in relation to non-Jews, through appeal to the category of Shituf and a robust theological understanding of how it extends to non-Jews, there is still something disturbing in the worship and consequent empowerment of human beings. <sup>8</sup> While the caricatural application of Avoda Zara relates to it as the worship of wood and stone, in some ways such worship is more innocuous than the worship of living people. The dangers of power abuse, psychological dependence, and other potentially problematic consequences of the uncritical devotion and worship of religious teachers are more immediate in the lives of followers than any harmful consequences that the worship of images might produce. Recent years have brought to light serious charges against some of India’s leading teachers and gurus , from murder charges, against one of the leading Shankaracharyas, to accusations of pedophilia against one of India’s most celebrated gurus , Satya Sai Baba. But must the dangers of guru worship lead us to the conclusion that such worship ought to be considered as Avoda Zara? After all, similar difficulties, whether moral or psychological, exist in power relations within authoritarian, hierarchical, or other kinds of student-teacher relations in other traditions that do not proffer divine status on the teacher. We come back to the question of exclusive versus accumulative criteria for declaration of Avoda Zara . It seems to me that if we apply one exclusive criterion in light of which we judge whether guru worship should be considered Avoda Zara, we would be forced to admit that it need not be viewed as such, given the arsenal of halachic and theological possibilities made available by the history of halacha. However, if we consider accumulative criteria, we may do well to keep this as one nuance that may be added to an eventual calculus that would apply as we seek to reflect on a given religion or practice as Avoda Zara. 

 F. Exploring further the implications of approaching another religion through its religious phenomenology, rather than through its theology, we are led to consider the significance of the overall orientation of Hindu religious culture. While the following may be true of other religious cultures as well, it seems that it comes to particular and heightened expression in the religious life of India. Indian speakers and theoreticians, who seek to articulate what is unique about Indian or Hindu culture and its contribution to the religious life of humanity, often highlight the intensity of the spiritual quest in the religious life in India. <sup>9</sup> Admitting the witness of religious life as a testimony to the quality of a religion, to the god it worships, and to its status as Avoda Zara forces us to consider all that takes place within Hindu religious life against the backdrop of the overall purposes of the religion. Its self-stated goal of living a spiritual life, <sup>10</sup> beyond the fulfillment of various human needs and desires, provides the context for assessing its methods of worship and its approach to God and His many manifestations. <sup>11</sup> The broader framework of the spiritual life may thus function as one additional criterion in light of which the validity of a religion and its status as Avoda Zara may be assessed. 

 G. The criteria we have introduced have taken us gradually from the objectivity of acts (worship of images) and beliefs (affirmations of belief in God or in beings other than God) to the more nuanced domains of attitudes, orientations, and personal and subjective spiritual life. While the criterion I am about 

*[p. 191]* ^pg194

to suggest may seem elusive, it is in fact fundamental to the attempt to address questions of Avoda Zara in relation to the practices of another religious culture. The following criterion emerges as an interesting point of confluence between Nachmanides and Meiri, despite apparent differences in the broader worldview of these two thinkers. Meiri contrasted the worship of God with the worship of astral forces, designed to bring material benefit to those who practice such worship. <sup>12</sup> This contrast may be understood as implying that worship bonds one and establishes some real connection between the worshipper and the object of her worship. Worship or faith are not simply personal, interior, or subjective. Something transpires through the attitudes and actions of religion. These enable a connection, a flow, a bonding between the worshipper and the object of her worship. We may think of this connection in energetic terms. Through worship one bonds with the object of worship, thereby drawing energies from the object of worship, possibly even becoming a vehicle for that being’s reality. For Meiri, a moral life is the guarantee that one bonds with God and not with other beings, thereby avoiding the consequences and implications of a religious life or practice that are deemed lower, in terms of metaphysics, the quality of the spiritual life, and its attendant energetic level. 

 Nachmanides is even more explicit about this “bonding” aspect of the religious life. <sup>13</sup> Nachmanides presented Avoda Zara according to a threefold division that is based on a clear metaphysical hierarchy. The hierarchy extends from the celestial ministers, appointed by God to oversee the nations, to demons and spirits whom one can worship in order to attain various benefits. If the issue were purely theological, as it is for Maimonides, and its sole concern was with the question of whether one does or does not worship God, Nachmanides’ threefold hierarchy would be superfluous. The reason Nachmanides can differ from Maimonides regarding the permissibility of the worship of celestial ministers for the nations over whom they have been appointed by God is precisely because Nachmanides’ criterion is not strictly theological. Nachmanides’ thought, here as well as in other contexts, is informed by the assumption that contact and “bonding” with spiritual beings are real. <sup>14</sup> For Maimonides it would matter little whether one is worshipping a celestial minister or a demon. For Nachmanides it makes all the difference, hence his threefold presentation. His hierarchical presentation of Avoda Zara implies that whom you worship really matters. 

 Looking at the “bonding” or energetic principle, we are led to concerns that seem to me fundamental to any discussion of Avoda Zara. The question touches on the identity of the being receiving worship, but even more fundamentally on the very heart of the concern for Avoda Zara, namely on the question of what is really wrong with Avoda Zara. The following questions arise: Who is being approached through a given worship or practice? What contact and “bonding” take place through the spiritual exchange? What or whom has one become a vehicle for, through the exchange of ritual, worship, or the approach of faith? In other words: tell me whom you worship, and I will tell you who you are. What matters in worship, hence in Avoda Zara, from this perspective is the consequence of “bonding” with a being other than God. These may be energetic, moral, or related to impact on the will and actions of the worshipper. From this perspective 

*[p. 192]* ^pg195

Avoda Zara really does matter. Avoda Zara is a way of saying that one has bonded with undesirable forces, and such bonding is sure to have negative and harmful consequences for the individual and society. 

 Some might consider this criterion as taking us deep into subjectivity. Surely it is the least measurable of criteria. One can measure objective acts and examine statements of belief. It is harder to ascertain what harmful energetic consequences undesired spiritual liaisons may carry. But our difficulty in measuring these does not invalidate this criterion. It only makes it harder to gauge. It is reasonable to suggest that for Meiri the moral life may function as a way of gauging the quality of spiritual contact, thereby distinguishing the effects of “bonding” with God with those of “bonding” with other beings. But there may be other ways of making such an evaluation. Some gifted individuals may be able to appreciate the energetic qualities associated with different forms of religion. While this criterion appeals to the intuition and to spiritual senses, rather than to the intellect and to the faculties that measure what is observable and measurable, in terms of the spiritual life it may be as real. Because of its subjectivity, this criterion may be easily misapplied and corrupted in both directions. It may be dismissed by some as unreal, and it may also be applied too readily. <sup>15</sup> Still, for all the difficulties in applying this criterion, we must not discount it. It must be used as a complement to other criteria, in light of which we judge whether another religion is Avoda Zara. Perhaps even more to the point, applying this criterion might lead us to consider whether individual instances of the practice of another religion are Avoda Zara or not. Applying this criterion may lead to the paradoxical, but still very coherent, conclusion that some instances of what is called Hinduism are Avoda Zara, while others are not. <sup>16</sup> 

 H. One way in which we might be able to complement what might seem completely subjective in the energetic criterion is by considering the purposes of religion. The realm of intention is purely subjective and is not subject to the same objective verifications as acts of worship and creeds of faith. Nevertheless, it does provide a measure of objectivity, inasmuch as the intention of the worshipper or practitioner can be stated. Moreover, intention is a fundamental criterion of religious thought, both Jewish and non-Jewish, and an important axis along which spiritual education, growth, and transformation take place. *Kavana* , intentionality, is a formative category of halacha and rabbinic thought. Actions, both good and bad, are assessed by their intentionality, and one’s spiritual evolution is a function of the intentionality brought to bear within one’s sphere of activities. Broadly speaking, the religious life can be presented as a movement from imperfect to perfect intention, from *shelo lishma* to *lishma* . The former is characterized by self-serving intentionality, variously defined as seeking material or spiritual gain in this world or the next. The latter is defined as acting for the sake of the inherent good of the deed or for the sake of God’s love, fulfillment of His will, and the attainment of closeness to Him. Growth along this axis reflects fundamental processes of the spiritual life and points to a hierarchy of purposes that motivates the practice of religion. 

 At its lowest, religion is a means for attaining certain ends that are identified with fulfillment of one’s interests. At its highest, religion is a means of transcending 

*[p. 193]* ^pg196

self-interest, in the service of God and others. The attempt to make the energetic dimension of Avoda Zara more palpable and quantifiable may benefit from a consideration of the correspondence between intentionality and the “bonding” dimension of religious practice. It is worth noting that the Torah associates worship of foreign gods with practices of fortune telling, sorcery, necromancy etc. <sup>17</sup> This association points to the energetic link that is made with undesirable, “lower” forces. But it simultaneously points to the “lower” dimensions of intentionality. Religion practiced for the sake of attaining knowledge of future things, of gaining control over menacing forces, and for that matter of gaining power and control over any force that might bring with it success, prosperity, and this-worldly gain is religion of a lower kind. Higher religion seeks to raise the person to attunement with the Divine, to knowledge, liberation, and love for all. While the term “magic” has become increasingly suspect as a neutral and descriptive category, it does retain a certain suggestivity with regard to the purposes of religion. 

 Meiri’s presentation of what the idolatry of old consisted of suggests that a “religious” life that is self-serving, in that it uses worship for purposes of material gain and fulfillment of pleasure or, more broadly, of human interests and desires, is closely related to and might even define Avoda Zara. Magic and fortune telling may be extreme instances of the nexus between practices, intentions, and the energetic “bonding” with other forces that we might consider Avoda Zara. But similar considerations may apply *wherever* the purposes of religion are associated with self-gain and satisfaction. What is important to note concerning the *wherever* of the previous sentence is that the concern for purity of intention is not a function of one religion over and against another, but an internal concern that relates equally to all religions. If Avoda Zara is understood as a function or equivalent of different levels of the religious life and their energetic correlates, this consideration applies equally within and outside Judaism. If so, all religions have, or are subject to the danger of, Avoda Zara within them, just as all religions have the potential for self-transformation and for raising their followers and believers to the heights of the spiritual life in a true relationship with the living God. What this would mean is that one cannot pass a blanket judgment upon any religion and its status as Avoda Zara. As we move from the realm of the objective act to the domain of the subjective intention and its energetic and spiritual consequences, we move to a realm that is not only harder to measure but that also shifts the focus from the religion, conceived as a broad entity, to the individual worshipper and his or her intentions. Intentionality, motivation, magic, seeking personal gain, etc., would then complement other criteria discussed above in an attempt to portray the spiritual life or spiritual level of a practitioner or practice. Avoda Zara would then apply to such clusters of faith or groups and types of practitioners, rather than to religions. Perhaps one could put things as follows: the move from larger wholes to the individual and his practices takes us from the realm of halachic declaration to the realm of spiritual discernment. The tools for spiritual discernment are much finer. Typically, spiritual discernment is accompanied by a concern for the well-being of others and their spiritual growth rather than by the disdain that often accompanies the halachic proclamation of an entire religion as Avoda Zara. 

*[p. 194]* ^pg197

 Hinduism may be much better approached through a process of spiritual discernment rather than through the process of halachic ruling and its inevitable clumsiness, as it negotiates large and complex realities. Hinduism itself is very conscious of competing purposes of religion. <sup>18</sup> The distinction between self-serving and self-transcending purposes is an internal distinction that emerges from its own teachings. The distinction between different aspects and degrees within the religious practice of Hinduism is a distinction with internal validity. The hierarchy it implies permits criticism, but also invites growth. It echoes concerns we find within Judaism, thereby allowing the two systems to recognize each other in structural terms, suggesting that the same spiritual drive informs both. If the two religions are engaged in similar processes of orientation of the individual and his will toward greater spiritual transformation, we may more readily recognize similarities across the religions. 

 Having listed various possible criteria for Avoda Zara, we may now return to the guiding question of the present discussion: Should a consideration of Avoda Zara be founded upon one single criterion, leading to a debate on which of the above is the most suitable criterion, or should it be founded upon multiple criteria, which would then present us with the challenge of how to successfully negotiate these criteria and how they might relate to one another. In reality, I doubt that one single criterion ever applied, when prophets, sages, or halachists approached another religion. Prophetic mockery of idol worship cannot be divorced from their understanding that another god is worshipped and possibly from a critique of the moral life of surrounding religions. Positive recognition of Christianity as worshipping the same God was similarly based on some intuitive recognition that the same God is worshipped and the consequent application of several criteria—moral and theological—to support such recognition. Our reading of Meiri also suggests the simultaneous application of moral and theological criteria in order to determine that today’s religions are different from the religions of old. Nevertheless, I would submit that *formally* most authorities in the history of halacha have relied on one single criterion in declaring a religion Avoda Zara. 

 The determination that a religion is Avoda Zara relies on a series of complex factors. Some of these grow out of an analysis and appreciation of the religion under evaluation. Others reflect the needs of the party undertaking the evaluation. The need for distinguishing one’s identity from that of the other is as prominent as various financial and social needs growing out of common living. The pronouncement that another religion is Avoda Zara is but the external or final expression of a more complex process that leads to that conclusion. Much, perhaps most, of the factoring that leads to this final declaration remains out of sight, while the actual halachic declaration usually appeals to a single criterion. 

 But even appeal to a single criterion raises fundamental questions. Do we seek to find one single criterion in light of which a religion would be declared to be free of Avoda Zara or a single criterion in light of which it would be proclaimed as Avoda Zara? Highlighting the moral dimension of Meiri’s reasoning would lead to the conclusion that one single criterion might suffice to declare a religion non-Avoda Zara. Applying this criterion alone would make Avoda Zara nearly extinct, as indeed Meiri considered it to be. If, on the other hand, our working 

*[p. 195]* ^pg198

assumption is that fault in one single criterion suffices to consider a religion as Avoda Zara, then we will be led to apply the criterion of worship of idols as the exclusive criterion, thereby leading one to the conclusion that most present-day religions, with the possible exception of Islam and its offshoots, should be considered Avoda Zara. 

 Those familiar with contemporary halachic practice realize that there is a large measure of indecision, possibly a stalemate, concerning how other religions are viewed. In terms of halachic precedents, it remains torn between Maimonides and Meiri, with appeals to one or the other in order to justify one’s position, and with little to no discussion of the actual criteria upon which the decision is made. Often the halachic discussion of other religions is *formally* boiled down to choosing between one of these authorities. But even here, beneath the surface, even unconsciously, lies a far more complex mechanism of reasoning, choosing, and applying various considerations that ultimately find expression in the end decision of accepting the authority of one or another of the rabbinic precedents. It seems to me that as a way of moving forward a serious discussion of Avoda Zara, conceptually and maybe even halachically, we must bring to light the processes of reasoning, the choice among competing criteria, and the ways in which they come together that inform the decision of whether a religion is viewed as Avoda Zara or not. Classical halachic process may not be able to sustain the attempt to make conscious and explicit the coming together of multiple factors, inasmuch as it requires one single criterion. But what if we considered Avoda Zara as something other than a “yes or no” determination? What if we considered the very pronouncement of Avoda Zara as something graded? What if we thought of Avoda Zara in adjectival rather than nominal terms. In other words: What if a religion or its practices were “idolatrous” rather than “idolatry”? Surely we are able to tolerate some degree of Avoda Zara. We know this because Avoda Zara functions as much as an internal category as an external evaluation of other religions. Our own spiritual literature teaches us that there are many applications of Avoda Zara that are internal to Judaism, to the faith processes of its believers, and to their ongoing quest to attain perfect faith. <sup>19</sup> We can tolerate Avoda Zara as a form of internal religious discourse that seeks to purify our faith. In fact, it is my impression that internal application of Avoda Zara as a category for critiquing internal practices is quite widespread. <sup>20</sup> It is only when the category of Avoda Zara is coupled with the attitude to the other, and quite possibly with concerns for identity and the definitions of self and other, that Avoda Zara takes on a charge that puts it beyond the boundary of the tolerable. Such Avoda Zara is no longer worthy of attention, dialogue, or respect. 

 As we seek to make the processes of reasoning behind the declaration of Avoda Zara more explicit, we are led to ask: How could the coupling of multiple criteria lead to a graded and more nuanced approach to Avoda Zara? Within this graded approach, when is a religion considered Avoda Zara, and is it possible to point to some definite coming together of factors that make it so? And on the more positive side: How can we put the various criteria together in a way that extends our internal capacity to tolerate Avoda Zara to the religious other? To this end, we must seek to bring to light the hidden processes that inform our approach 

*[p. 196]* ^pg199

to other religions and to move the discussion from a single-value discussion of Avoda Zara to a more complex approach to other religions. This is best described as a calculus. 

 As suggested, we already apply multiple criteria in our attitudes to other religions, except we do so with limited self-awareness, thereby limiting the attitudinal changes that a more nuanced and graded approach to the topic might bring about. Let me illustrate this with regard to attitudes to Christianity, as elaborated in one of the more important modern treatments of its status as Avoda Zara. We have already encountered Rabbi Herzog’s discussion of Christianity, articulated prior to the founding of the state of Israel, in anticipation of the need to accommodate other religions within the about-to-be-founded state. Rabbi Herzog seeks to establish the permissibility of the practice of Christianity for Christian minorities in the future state. To this end, he offers one of the more detailed halachic analyses of Christianity’s status. An examination of his discussion reveals how a calculus of factors operates. His explicit position relies on the permissibility of Shituf for non-Jews. At the same time, also implicit in his discussion is the recognition of the moral standing of Christians, compared to the paganism of old. <sup>21</sup> And yet, having declared Christianity to be permissible for non-Jews, and hence not strictly Avoda Zara, he nevertheless makes the point, in passing, that Jews should refrain from entry into a church . <sup>22</sup> His reasoning is that Shituf is permissible to non-Jews only, hence entry into a church means entry into a space that is considered Avoda Zara for Jews. As noted before, his reasoning is by no means conclusive, as the rulings of other halachic authorities suggest. What Rabbi Herzog is doing is, in fact, applying a calculus, according to which Avoda Zara might be different things to different people, under varying circumstances. The notion of Shituf already suggests that much: Avoda Zara is defined differently for Jews and non-Jews. But then a further distinction is drawn. For purposes of public and political recognition, Christianity is affirmed as non-Avoda Zara. For purposes of its own rituals and the legitimacy of contact with its religious life, it is considered as Avoda Zara. <sup>23</sup> Multiple distinctions are implied here, such as the distinction between public and private or between the political and ritual. It may be that concerns for identity or the need to protect the faithful from harmful exposure add further nuance to his discussion, leading him to forbid entry into the churches whose construction in the State of Israel he permits. The application of the different criteria to varying situations remains implicit, a function of the intuition rather than of the reasoned halachic process. What would it look like if we sought to make this conversation more explicit and to reflect on the varying criteria and their application in different contexts? Let us enter this thought exercise in relation to Hinduism, bringing to bear the various criteria discussed above, in an attempt to develop a robust, as well as flexible, notion of Avoda Zara. In so doing we seek to advance discussion of Hinduism beyond the simple “yes or no” declaration, typical of the few discussions that it has received in halachic literature. 

 A graded application would begin with Meiri and his moral criteria. Realizing that Hinduism fits within the definition of “nations that are bound by ways of religion” permits a fundamental degree of recognition of Hinduism as non-Avoda 

*[p. 197]* ^pg200

Zara. Applying Meiri’s basic criterion does not take away the sense of strangeness and otherness that the encounter with Hinduism’s practices might engender. Nor does it tear down all possible boundaries, psychological and halachic, between the two religions. But it could go a long way both in terms of attitude and in terms of their practical consequences. For one, various strictures and instructions found in the literature in relation to idolaters would be made nonapplicable in relation to Hinduism. While many of these do not apply in practice, they continue to inform attitudes. Consequently, providing a baseline attitude founded on Meiri goes a long way in bringing about attitudinal change. Let us imagine for a moment how the sheitel crisis would have been negotiated if its starting position was not the otherness of worship, as occasioned by encounter with Hindu images of divinity, but the commonality of legitimate religion, based on its moral practices. Surely, a different attitude would have applied, which would have made the halachic consideration of the use of hair shorn at Hindu temples more nuanced. The fact that halachic discussions of the sheitels completely failed to take into account the religious nature of Hinduism or to engage the fundamental question of whether it is or is not Avoda Zara, but relied instead exclusively on the technicalities of whether hair shearing was or was not to be considered an offering, may be better understood in light of the present discussion of criteria and their applications. To declare that the religious practices at Tirupati were not Avoda Zara would be counterintuitive, if one assumed that such a declaration amounted to recognition of the legitimacy of the religion practiced there, as encountered by the uninitiated observer. However, if the declaration was limited in scope, in view of one guiding criterion, one among many that figure in a broader calculus, it may have figured in halachic discussions. 

 Contemporary interreligious relations are as much a matter of good diplomacy as they are of proper theology. Diplomacy involves establishing formal or semiformal relations, which have representative and symbolic value. In the case of Judaism, interreligious relations take on a distinct diplomatic dimension, in view of the close association between Judaism and the State of Israel. This accounts for the establishment of a dialogue track between the Israeli Chief Rabbinate and a notable group of Hindu religious leaders. <sup>24</sup> Now, in terms of their own ability to enter such diplomatic relations, rabbinic authorities need to overcome the difficulties associated with the declaration that Hinduism is Avoda Zara. <sup>25</sup> In such a context, the recognition that Hinduism is not Avoda Zara plays an important role, which accounts for why this has been an agenda item tackled in this dialogue. While the strategy that was used to arrive at this conclusion did not appeal to Meiri, he could very well have provided precisely such a baseline exoneration of Hinduism from the charges of Avoda Zara. It seems to me that once we are willing to think of Avoda Zara in terms that are both graded and contextual, not only does Meiri provide the needed baseline; his position becomes indispensible and one that we cannot continue ignoring. 

 A general “diplomatic” recognition of Hinduism paves the way to doing business, both in financial and in diplomatic terms. But more is involved in relations, and as additional dimensions enter our calculus, we are confronted with further subtleties in our approach to Hinduism. Recognition of morality may help us 

*[p. 198]* ^pg201

to overcome the urge, or the challenge, to apply to Hinduism some of the worst attitudes, reserved for idolaters. But it does not yet pave the way for a positive appreciation of Hinduism. Such a positive appreciation would have to be built on one of two foundations—a positive recognition of the spiritual life of Hinduism and a recognition that despite appearances and all visible differences, Jews and Hindus do worship the same God. Attitudinal changes will be impacted most profoundly by our ability to move on both fronts, while advances along even one of these fronts is obviously significant. 

 I think it is fair to claim that recognition of the spiritual heritage and overall spiritual orientation of Hindu religious life must be central to a Jewish appreciation of Hinduism. Spirituality may not be, in and of itself, a halachic criterion. But it does shape overall attitudes, and these in turn find expression in halachic rulings. The case of Rabbi Daniel Sperber provides an excellent illustration for this. In a public lecture on the status of Hinduism as Avoda Zara, <sup>26</sup> Sperber began with a testimony of his personal encounter with pious Hindus and the great impact that encounter with their spiritual lives has had on him. In response to a question, Sperber affirmed that the drive behind his willingness to consider Hinduism as non-Avoda Zara can indeed be traced to the inspiration that he derived from meeting religious Hindus. 

 One dimension that may be helpful here is the historical recognition of India as a land of wisdom. Our presentation of the history of Jewish attitudes to India and its religious life suggests that Avoda Zara has not always been the dominant concern. For many centuries, the reigning paradigm was the view of India as a land of wisdom. <sup>27</sup> This paradigm is doubly useful, when thinking of India in terms of Avoda Zara. First, it allows us to uphold a positive attitude to Hindu religious culture. It also points our attention to the Hindu wisdom tradition, which in this case is the internal Hindu philosophical understanding, which is helpful to a consideration of Avoda Zara. While the appeal to spirituality and wisdom may condition the overall tenor of our approach to Hinduism, in order to make significant advances in our relationship to Hinduism as Avoda Zara we have to tackle the “same God” question. Our discussion above has suggested various options that would allow us to affirm that ultimately Judaism and Hinduism worship the same God. 

 Positive appreciation of another religion opens the door to positive reception of its ideas, to transformative spiritual encounters, and to the possibility of overall spiritual sharing. The present encounter with Hinduism takes place precisely within the social context of broad sharing. <sup>28</sup> How should such sharing and, in particular, receptivity to the spiritual example, testimony, and teaching of Hinduism be viewed in light of the concern for Avoda Zara? This brings us back to our calculus. If for the initial, diplomatic, recognition it may suffice to have one guiding criterion that exempts Hinduism from being Avoda Zara, for more advanced purposes, such as spiritual sharing, we may require a larger number of converging factors, maybe even an accord on all factors, with perhaps a single exception. <sup>29</sup> 

 Because it is difficult to quantify how many factors are needed for what purpose, I refer to the processes described above as a calculus, whose application 

*[p. 199]* ^pg202

remains intuitive, hence somewhat imprecise, by definition. We might therefore consider a complementary approach, one that could stand on its own or become part of the calculus. In presenting the factors that add up to a consideration of other religions as Avoda Zara I raised the qualitative or energetic dimension, in light of which we seek to discern what it is that one is bonded to upon entry into the religious life of any religion. This qualitative dimension may be considered the experiential counterpart of the philosophical discussion of the “same God.” Accordingly, if it is God who is at the heart of another religion, then the energetic, experiential, conscious “bonding” takes place with the self-same reality that we seek to approach through our own spiritual efforts. If something other than God is approached, that is, lower forces and beings, then these fall within the purview of the original biblical battle against idolatry, understood as following forces and beings other than God. 

 The present encounter with Hinduism places us before novel challenges, not the least of which is the fact that broad spiritual sharing and influence already take place. While some sections of the Jewish people struggle with wig hair, others, including many who consider themselves religiously observant, are taking up practices, experiences, and personal relationships that go to the heart of Hindu religious experience and practice. If for the former, the external categories, provided by Meiri, might be sufficient for recognition, the latter require an approach that would help guide them through the challenges and opportunities they face. As the stakes rise and as halachic ruling shifts to spiritual discernment, we require other tools for evaluating Hinduism. These are not provided by a superficial view of alternative forms of worship; even the philosophical recognition of the ultimate unity of the Divine as known in all religions may be inadequate. We require criteria for discernment that will allow us to appreciate the spiritual quality, the “bonding” effect, that exposure to various forms of Hinduism might have. The need for cultivating the capacity for spiritual discernment seems ever more vital as contacts become deeper and more diverse. Setting such expectations is appropriate, even if they can only be fulfilled in part. 

 I would accordingly like to raise the question of how one evaluates the overall spiritual quality of a religion, the spiritual atmosphere or vibrations it establishes and its potential beneficial or harmful spiritual impact on those who are exposed to it. Framing the question in this way already suggests that different criteria might apply to passive encounter, such as a touristic visit to a Hindu temple, and to active encounter, such as sharing spiritual practices and teachings. As the encounter becomes more intensive, the need for discernment, based on the spiritual quality of that which one contacts, increases. 

 In this context we must ask how much we may trust our feelings and intuitions. If something “feels right” or “feels inspiring,” can one take that as indication that one is in an atmosphere that is fundamentally good? Of course, “feeling right” is only meaningful if it corresponds to “feeling wrong” and if there is a counterpoint of negative feelings that provides the complementary pole to one’s positive feelings. The present argument assumes that within the same religion one may come across expressions of the religious life that feel right, inspiring, and take one toward God, as well as expressions that have the opposite effect. 

*[p. 200]* ^pg203

Even within Judaism we might find such different realities. Spiritual bipolarity is a function of all religions. 

 The capacity to “tune in” to the energetic component of any spiritual reality is something that some possess and others do not. One way of dealing with this fact is to trust those who do. In theory, one would be evolving a kind of alternative mode of authority to the classical learning-based authority. Such intuitive authority and discernment are already in effect in various contexts. <sup>30</sup> If this criterion were applied within an open and dialogical context, rather than within an essentially isolationist and protective context, it could provide helpful guidance to those who seek to engage other religions with spiritual responsibility. 

 Given that not many are equipped with the spiritual feelers to judge the qualitative energetic differences between forms of religion, we must approach the concern for discernment of spiritual quality by various other methods that would be suggestive of the overall spiritual beneficence of a religious community, lifestyle, or practice. In this context, I would suggest a combination of four factors:

 A. Morality would be the first, and instances of its absence are critical to a discernment, as I shall presently suggest. 

 B. A consideration of the overall purposes of religion would be second. A religious milieu that seeks to gratify personal desires, to fulfill material needs and gains, to provide knowledge of things hidden, and to tell future fortunes must be judged differently from a religious milieu that seeks self-transcendence, personal purification, selfless service, cultivation of love and compassion, and knowledge of and union with God. While religion, any religion, may contain all of the above, it seems fairly easy to discern whether a community, a set of practices, and the goals that are placed before one seek to address the former or the latter concerns. 

 C. The existence of a robust body of spiritual teaching. Teaching plays a crucial role in orienting the overall religious life and setting its goals. But in relation to the concerns of Avoda Zara it is particularly significant, inasmuch as it is through the teachings of a religion that one understands how God is understood, and therefore whether one can affirm a “same God” position, vis- à -vis another religion. 

 D. The existence of models of spiritual excellence who can inspire and guide others toward the higher goals of the religion. 

 The sum total of these factors could provide a helpful indication by means of which the overall qualitative or energetic appreciation of a given section within a religion may be made. Thus, an ashram that is organized around the presence, or memory, and teaching of an outstanding individual, wherein teachings on the nature of God and the spiritual life are offered, is likely to be a safe spiritual place, where the fundamentally positive spiritual vibrations point to the same God and open the way to spiritual sharing. Let me suggest two examples of what might be the opposite. We have already mentioned one recent Indian spiritual teacher who came to fame, or notoriety, in the West. This was Rajneesh, who in what might be a clever public relations move, changed his name later to Osho. Rajneesh was 

*[p. 201]* ^pg204

apparently an excellent teacher and philosopher and was able to put the finest of Hindu philosophy to the use of his own brand of Hindu teaching. However, reports of practices from his ashram suggest that his teaching violated fundamentals of morality. Free, and at times violent, sex was encouraged as part of the pursuit of the spiritual life. To this day, someone visiting his ashram in Poona is required to undergo an HIV test, in a matter as routine as handing in a passport for registration. To the best of my knowledge, such testing is unique to this ashram, and makes sense in light of what takes place in the ashram, following Rajneesh’s teachings. In terms of discernment, violation of basic morality might be sufficient for making us averse to this brand of Hinduism. But let us consider one additional fact about this teacher: Rajneesh had himself addressed by his devotees as bhagwan , that is, Lord. He thus attributed to himself divine status and had his disciples approach him not simply as guru , but as god incarnate. This approach to the guru as god raises all the problematics associated with the deification of the person. Seeing Rajneesh as god himself draws upon and reinforces a teaching and lifestyle, branded as Hinduism, that is repugnant. Here abuse of power and moral laxity are closely intertwined with Avoda Zara. Theological error, moral imperfection, and an overall degeneration of the religious life come together in a way that might justify, even necessitate, application of Avoda Zara as a critical category that provides us with discernment into the true quality of these spiritual practices. 

 Visitors to Rajneesh’s ashram today are offered instruction in Tantra. As mentioned, this is a branch of Hinduism that plays up sexual relations, often illicit ones, as a means of transcending conventional norms in an attempt to attain mystical heights beyond good and evil. Tantra is only coming to light in recent decades as a subject for careful study, and facts and myths still govern much of common attitudes to Tantra. <sup>31</sup> But for purposes of the present attempt to construct a method for discernment between different expressions of Hinduism, we might find in tantric practices, or some forms of Tantra, a combination of elements that might lead us to consider it as Avoda Zara. Needless to say there are the many gods and their images, but these, as the reader of the present work already realizes, are not conclusive in and of themselves. It is the broader philosophical, moral, and energetic framework that provides them with meaning. In theory, the same set of deities and images may serve a unitive spiritual approach that could be recognized as having value from a Jewish standpoint. In the case of Tantra, breaking conventional moral boundaries, in the quest for ultimate liberation, raises the question of whether we should consider its practices as Avoda Zara. 

 The present argument seeks to shift the ground from judging by external worship or even philosophical declaration to the energetic quality of a particular religious lifestyle. It suggests that the question of Avoda Zara should be approached as a kind of discernment that takes place as one circle contained in another. If we think of the interior circle as the worship of images, these are dependent on the meaning provided by the next circle, that of theological interpretation. That in turn is dependent on the next circle, defined as quest for spiritual life and the overall orientation it offers. And this too is contained by the most fundamental of circles, the moral criterion, posited by Meiri as the foundation for the recognition 

*[p. 202]* ^pg205

of other religions. While it would be convenient to consider Hinduism in a neat pattern, created by these circles, in reality we will encounter the various dimensions in more messy combinations. The challenge will remain constant—assessing that it is really God at the heart of the religion and discerning the spiritual quality of the form of religion that is encountered. While this is a universal challenge that applies within Judaism as it does outside it, the challenge posed by Hinduism is unique. For one, because of the sheer diversity of forms of the religious life within Hinduism. From another perspective, the challenges is unique due to the intensity of its spiritual life and the manifold forms it takes, wherein spiritual practice and the structuring of society around spiritual concerns intersect with other concerns of life, served by religion. Because within Hinduism we find such an accent on the spiritual life and its attainment, the need to confront it in its full complexity and to develop tools for spiritual discernment is heightened, possibly more so than in relation to any other religion. 

 What then of the straightforward criterion of worship of images as an indication of Avoda Zara? What room is there for it within the proposed calculus? It is clear, in my view, that we cannot rely on the use of images to provide a final answer as to the identity of God, independently of Hinduism’s theology, morality, and spiritual life. But we can affirm its otherness and strangeness. It is hard for a person brought up in Judaism to relate positively to the manifold images of the Divine found in Hindu culture. Swami Vijayananda was aware of this when he counseled his Western disciples to take from Hinduism only the philosophical teaching and to leave aside the worship of images. Forms of worship are culturally specific. Hindu worship is an expression of Hindu mind, being, and culture. In this sense, Hinduism is *culturally* , rather than theologically, incompatible with Judaism. But cultural incompatibility need not be translated into knee-jerk condemnation of idolatry. Rather, it provides the mechanisms for maintaining distinctive identity, which are as much a concern for the encounter with another religion as are the concerns for the appropriateness of worship. 

 It is perfectly legitimate to feel ill at ease with the worship of Hindu images, or for that matter of Christian or any other image. We should be able to express our discomfort by stating just that: worship of images is not compatible with Jewish sensibilities. However, we need not declare another religion as Avoda Zara simply in order to express our discomfort. Otherness is a matter of identity. We may state our identity in just those terms, without absolutizing it in the theological or philosophical terms of Avoda Zara. I have found myself over the years much more comfortable in the spiritual home of Chandra Swami, who rejects the worship of forms, than in the atmosphere of other ashrams that practice such worship. At the same time, I have also taught myself to see beyond the forms to the intentions and spiritual recognitions of those people who worship God through form. Both options are legitimately Hindu. Both approach God, the same God. I may feel safer or more at ease with one rather than the other. But it is important for me to distinguish between my own level of spiritual comfort and an objective judgment placed upon another religion. Avoidance of images is fundamental to Jewish identity. It should not be the governing criterion for determining what is Avoda Zara. 

*[p. 203]* ^pg206

 Cultural encounters require responsibility. Responsibility amounts to a fine balancing act of how to view the other and how to negotiate varying circumstances involving the other. Responsibility involves more than simply putting up a protective fence against religious otherness. Insularity may be irresponsible, in its own way, even as it seeks to be responsible. Proper application of responsibility calls for applying different criteria to different circumstances. At the heart of interreligious theological responsibility is the recognition that God alone counts and that we must seek God and His reality in the encounter with others. The difficulties in approaching other religions stem from the complications involved in ascertaining God’s presence and from the need to simplify procedures. Our encounters with others are already too extensive and too complex. We know about Hinduism more than before, and still so much less than what we ought to know. Boundaries have already broken down and we are called to establish a method that would allow us to proceed in a relationship that affects individuals, collectives, and ultimately the entire people, through some of its representative bodies. What I have suggested is the conscious cultivation of a complex process that takes into account multiple factors and applies them according to changing circumstances. Such a path is necessarily tentative and subject to changes in our knowledge and understanding over time. But it is a path that seeks the truth where it may be found and recognizes the greater values of the spiritual life—peace, love, and harmony—wherever they are found, as expressions of a true spiritual life. 

 There is one important consequence to the move suggested in this last section. The common approach to Avoda Zara sees it as something cut-and-dry, easy to define, demanding unequivocal response. For such a view, Avoda Zara is not much of a challenge. Once it is defined, it simply has to be avoided. Once it is avoided, it is out of sight and out of mind, no longer challenging the religious system in any meaningful way. The proposed calculus leads to a dynamic view of Avoda Zara, where neither practices, nor faith, let alone an entire religion, can be globally defined as Avoda Zara. According to the possibility here developed, Avoda Zara would apply variously, according to context, need, and the spiritual understanding of the performer. This seems to have the disadvantage of confusion and lack of clear categories. But its disadvantage may also be its great advantage. Rather than relegating Avoda Zara to the margins of our consciousness and our religious observance, <sup>32</sup> leading us to focus only on the center of our religious life, Avoda Zara becomes an ongoing presence. The battle against Avoda Zara, both within and without, is a constant one, as we continually seek to improve our spiritual lives. Static definitions of Avoda Zara leave us only the option of avoiding it, thereby making it meaningless in our lives, at best an identity marker. An active engagement with Avoda Zara, seeking to articulate its meaning, categories, presence, and boundaries, makes it into a meaningful category that intersects with other major categories of meaning—intentionality, morality, spirituality, and more. Avoda Zara and going beyond it thus help define our spiritual lives internally, orienting them toward the highest ideals. In this way we do greatest justice to this formative category, by engaging it seriously and giving it the recognition and attention it deserves.
