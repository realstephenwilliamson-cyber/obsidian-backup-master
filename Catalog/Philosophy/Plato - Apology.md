## About

Plato's version of the speech Socrates gave in his own defence at his trial in Athens in 399 BC, on charges of impiety and of corrupting the young. Socrates speaks throughout, apart from two passages in which he cross-examines Meletus.

- **People:** Socrates; his accusers Meletus (the only one who speaks), Anytus and Lycon; and a jury Wikipedia puts at perhaps 500 men. Plato names himself as present (34a, 38b).
- **Dramatic date:** the trial itself, 399 BC; the SEP (Nails and Monoson) places it in May–June of that year.
- **Shape:** the defence; a second speech after the guilty verdict, on the penalty; and closing words after the death sentence. Socrates first suggests free meals in the Prytaneum. Wikipedia says he then offered 100 drachmae and his friends 3,000 (30 minae); the SEP says he offered thirty minae. Socrates says thirty more votes would have acquitted him. Wikipedia cites Diogenes Laërtius for 280 votes for death and 220 for a fine; Wikipedia adds that Plato cites no total numbers of votes.
- **Composition and accuracy:** no date is known. Wikipedia says most defences of Socrates appeared within a decade of the trial; the IEP puts it among the early dialogues, after Socrates' death and before Plato's first trip to Sicily in 387 BC. Its authorship is not doubted: the IEP names the *Hippias Major* as the only early work still sometimes called spurious. Its accuracy is debated. The IEP says scholars commonly treat it as the most reliable ancient source on the historical Socrates; Wikipedia sets Kahn (1996), who agrees, against Morrison (2000), who doubts it.
- **Place among the dialogues:** Thrasyllus put it second in his first tetralogy, between the *Euthyphro* and the *Crito*, with the *Phaedo* last; the four cover Socrates' last days.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia notes that Aristotle praised the questioning of Meletus, and that the work inspired a 2007 play by Andrew David Irvine and Rossellini's 1971 film *Socrates*.
