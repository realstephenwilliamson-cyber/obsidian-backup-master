## About

A dialogue by Plato on the question "What is knowledge?" Socrates tests three definitions offered by the young mathematician Theaetetus, and the dialogue ends without an answer.

- **People and frame:** Socrates, about 70; Theaetetus, who resembles him; and Theaetetus's geometry teacher, Theodorus of Cyrene. In the frame, set as Theaetetus returns wounded and sick from a battle at Corinth, Euclides of Megara and his friend Terpsion have the conversation read aloud from Euclides' written record.
- **Dramatic date:** the main conversation falls shortly before Socrates' trial in 399 BC, and ends as he leaves to face it. The frame battle is usually put in 369 BC; Wikipedia reports Debra Nails's case for 391, while the SEP gives 394 as the alternative.
- **Composition:** the SEP says it probably dates from about 369 BC; the IEP places it in the late-transitional group (c. 360–355 BC). These sources raise no doubt about authorship, though the SEP notes that a double prologue circulated in antiquity, leading some to posit two versions.
- **Structure (Stephanus 142a–210d):** knowledge as perception (151e–187a), linked to Protagoras' "man is the measure"; a digression on the philosopher (172c–177b); knowledge as true judgement (187b–201c), with the aviary image; and true judgement with an account, logos (201d–210a). Socrates likens his method to his mother's craft of midwifery.
- **Place among the dialogues:** its aporetic ending recalls early dialogues such as the *Euthyphro*, but it also resembles later ones, and the *Sophist* and *Statesman* continue the conversation the next day.
- **Why it matters:** the SEP calls it arguably Plato's greatest work on epistemology and notes close engagement by Epicureans, Stoics and Academics. Wikipedia says Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper on justified true belief investigated the account of knowledge it examines.
