While the BraYā certainly contains multiple sections, the notion that it consists of sūtras appears contrived, and perhaps belongs to a late phase of its redaction. Indeed, references to the BraYā consisting of sātras belong to the concluding sections of the text, probably its final textual stratum. Moreover, there can be little certainly concerning the names and intended demarcation of the five sötras envisioned in the Bra Ya’s final verses (104.3 1—32). Two factors might have contributed to this conception: a smattering of vague references in the text to sütras, and the stra model of the Niśvāsa corpus. More specifically, the latter could have led some redactors to an unlikely interpretation of the former. In turn, the statement in Bra Ya 104 that the text consists of five sūtras appears to have stimulated further speculation within the tradition, for such is evident in the Jayadrathayāmala. As notes SANDERSON, chapter forty-four of the latter's first satka, called the Brahmayāmalanirnaya (“An Exposition on the Brahmayāmala”), claims that texts of the Brahmayāmala cycle contain five sūtras: a müla-, guhya-, naya-, uttara-, and uttarottarasūtra.™\*" This appears to be an attempt to reconcile several things: the claim that the Bra Yā consists of five sütras, the five-sütra model of the Nisvāsa corpus, and the actual sectional divisions of the Bra Ya—for unlike the Nisvāsa, the BraYā possesses both an Uttara- and Uttarottaratantra/sūtra. It is possible that the BraYa’s vinaya section was in this schema considered analogous to the Nisvāsa’s Nayasūtra. The Jayadrathayāmala does not, however, conceive of the BraYa’s sūtras strictly as sections: on the contrary, it describes the mülasütra, for instance, as the words “athātah,” with which begin many of the Bra Ya’s chapters and sections, while the guhyasütra is said to consist of teachings on secret matters, such as the yogic body’s channels and the clans of yoginzs.™\*

1.7.4 Has the Brahmayāmala incorporated independent tantras?

Several chapters of the second half of the BraYā appear remarkably self-contained. This is in part because a number belong to the kalpa genre: treatises setting out the practices of mantra-propitiation connected with a particular deity, many of whom do not feature in the BraYa’s core pantheon. However, at least two lengthy chapters arouse the suspicion of being independent or at least quasi-independent tantras subsequently redacted into the Bra Ya. This is a phenomenon attested, for instance, in case of the Jayadrathayāmala, a Vidyāpītha scripture even more extensive than the Bra Ya.'® The two chapters in question are BraYā 62, the Tilakatantra; and BraYa 85, the Utphullakamata. Both of these titles match those of texts quoted by Abhinavagupta.

1.7.5 Redactional discontinuities and textual strata The evidence reviewed above concerning the structure of the Bra Yā provides some indication that this is a heterogenous text which incorporated new material over time. A number of discrepencies in the Bra Ya’s content also suggest redactional discontinuity. One of these, elucidated below (§1.9.3) in discussing the Buddhist Laghusamvara’s debt to the Bra Ya, concerns typologies of the sādhaka: while a threefold classification of the sādhaka is presupposed in the earlier chapters, especially Bra Yā 24—45, the Addendum Tantras explicate a fourfold typology that appears to be a subsequent innovation. <sup>162</sup>Such development seems evident in the area of yoga as well, with the BraYa’s latter chapters introducing novel teachings which may belong to a later period.'® Some other discrepancies suggest heterogeneity but not necessarily historical development. For instance, the BraYā has two chapters—56 and 74—treating the subject of chommā, coded communication by means of verbal and non-verbal signs. Strikingly, these chapters overlap in ritual context and in many particulars, but give no indication of belonging to the same system.\*%4 As mentioned earlier, Satka II also introduces systems of mantra-derivation alien to the first fifty chapters. These kinds of heterogeneity may in some cases indicate redactors separated in time, while in other cases suggest the integration of disparate sources.

Important to the question of the BraYa’s stratification is the conception of the text containing twelve-thousand verses—hence its epithet, Dvaādaśa-sāhasraka, the Tantra of Twelve Thousand. This idea first appears in the opening of the revelation narrative (Bra Yā 1.2—3d), and features prominently in the text’s self-representation, as explored in §2.5. 1.8 Parameters for dating the Brahmayāmala

Although its redactional process and stratification remain in many respects opaque, the Bra Ya certainly has the appearance of a layered and composite text. Its production must have involved multiple individuals potentially separated in time and place. Nonetheless, while its language is utterly non-classical, it is more or less consistently so, betraying no jarring linguistic or stylistic discrepancies from section to section. This relative stylistic uniformity might point toward production within a more or less continuous textual community. Reconstruction of the chronological, geographic, and social parameters of the text's redactors and redactional process presents formidable challenges which I can only begin to address here.

No specific evidence I am presently aware of makes it possible to date the BraYā with precision. A variety of factors, however, points towards the seventh to eighth centuries as the period within it would most plausibly have been composed, though somewhat earlier and later dates cannot definitively be ruled out. Quotations of the BraYā in the Tantrāloka of Abhinavagupta, who flourished in the decades before and after the turn of the eleventh century, establish the existence of the text by this period, at the very latest. The distribution of Abhinavagupta’s citations, which are drawn from most sections of the Bra Ya, suggests that he knew the text in a form close to that preserved in the Nepalese manuscripts, as demonstrated in appendix B. Manuscript A was in any case copied not long afterwards, in 1052 C.E.\*®! 1.9.2 The Brahmayāmala and early non-Saiddhāntika literature

While the earliest Siddhāntatantras surely predate the BraYa, less clear is its chronology relative to other early extant works of non-Saiddhāntika literature.
