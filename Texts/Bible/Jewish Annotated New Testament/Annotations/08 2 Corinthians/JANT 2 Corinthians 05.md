# 2 Corinthians 5 (JANT notes)

[[JANT 2 Corinthians 04|← 2 Corinthians 4]] · [[Jewish Annotated New Testament|JANT index]] · [[JANT 2 Corinthians 06|2 Corinthians 6 →]]

Text: [[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05|NRSVue]] · [[2 Corinthians 05|BSB and SBLGNT]] · Introduction: [[JANT 2 Corinthians - Introduction]]

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-1|5.1]]:** The temporary–permanent contrast continues with the *earthly tent*, i.e., the body (see [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 09#^9-15|Wis 9.15]]), which is only a tabernacle and not meant to be everlasting, contrasted with the *building from God*, which is *eternal in the heavens*. After death we will acquire an immortal body, suited to the soul’s dwelling forever. *House not made with hands*, unlike anything earthly, which is subject to decline and death. *Heavens*, that God dwells in heaven is familiar from the Tanakh, e.g., [[Torah 50 - Ki Tavo#^26-15|Deut 26.15]]; cf. [[Psalms 002#^2-4|Ps 2.4]],[[Psalms 011#^11-4|11.4]]; [[Psalms 014#^14-2|14.2]]. ^5-1

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-2|2]]:** *Tent*, the impermanent, earthly body ^5-2

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-3|3]]:** *Taken off. . . ,* a different metaphor, the body as clothing. Following death, the soul does not exist *naked*, without a body. It immediately receives a perfect, eternal, heavenly body. ^5-3

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-4|4]]:** *Unclothed*, we wish not to escape our bodies but to acquire superior, immortal ones. ^5-4

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-5|5]]:** *Guarantee*, see 1.22n. ^5-5

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-6|6–8]]:** *While . . . in the body*, see [[Philippians 01#^1-21|Phil 1.21]],[[Philippians 01#^1-23|23]]; the body is a barrier to being fully with Christ. Rabbinic Judaism is more positive about bodily life (see 4.4n.). ^5-6

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-10|10]]:** *Judgment* seat, see [[Romans 14#^14-10|Rom 14.10]]. Rabbinic sources commonly speak of God’s seat, or throne, of judgment (see, e.g., [[Arakhin 10#10b|*b. Arak*. 10b]], *Lev. Rab*. 29.3,4,6,9). *Receive recompense*, the implication of the body/soul dichotomy for post-mortem judgment is treated by the rabbis; *Mekhilta de R. Ishmael Shirata* 27.2.7 states that, properly to judge an individual’s record, God will reunite the body and soul. ^5-10

### 5.11–21: Reconciliation with the Corinthians

*[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-11|Text]], p. 360* ^5-11

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-11|11]]:** *Fear of the Lord*, see 7.1n. *Consciences*, see 1.12; 4.2. ^5-11-2

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-12|12]]:** *Commending*, see 3.1n. *Boast*, see 1.12n. *Boast in outward appearance* may refer to opponents who advocated circumcision for Gentile church members. Early Jewish writings also distinguish between internal disposition and outer appearances: “one whose inside is not as his outside may not enter the school house” ([[Berakhot 28#28a|*b. Ber*. 28a]]). ^5-12

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-13|13]]:** Paul’s opponents may have derided him for an apparent lack of ecstatic experiences (12.1,12); Paul responds by pointing out that he experiences occasions of being *beside ourselves* (lit., “we are ecstatic”) as well as being at times *in our right mind*, that is, not in a state of spiritual ecstasy. Ecstatic experiences reflect the glory of God; but Paul says that he eschews an ecstatic life-style so as to conduct his ministry to the Corinthians and others; cf. [[1 Corinthians 14#^14-2|1 Cor 14.2–5]],[[1 Corinthians 14#^14-18|18–19]],[[1 Corinthians 14#^14-27|27–28]]. Paul’s thinking parallels rabbinic Judaism’s generally negative attitude toward ecstatic experiences (*m. Hag*. 2.1; [[Chagigah 14#14b|*b. Hag*. 14b]]) and recalls the rabbis’ focus upon leadership that promotes communal welfare (e.g., *b. B. Kamma* 80b; [[Sanhedrin 24#24b|*b. Sanh*. 24b]]; [[Arakhin 2#2b|*b. Arak.* 2b]]). But other perspectives also existed, represented, e.g., in the Merkavah and Hekhalot mysticisms of this same period. ^5-13

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-14|14–15]]:** *Love of Christ*, Christ’s love for us. *All have* died, to be resurrected like Christ, one must have died with him. Those *who live . . . was raised*, [[Romans 07#^7-4|Rom 7.4]]; [[Romans 14#^14-7|14.7–9]]; [[Galatians 02#^2-19|Gal 2.19–20]]. As the source of life, the Christ serves the role that Torah serves for the rabbis; one “eats its fruits in this world even as the principal remains for the world-to-come” (*m. Qidd.* 4.14). ^5-14

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-16|16–17]]:** *Knew Christ from a human point of view*, lit., according to the flesh, that is, in the previous human ways that estranged people from each other and God. The reality of *anyone* being *in Christ* documents the onset of the *new creation*, the eschatological reversal of human sin and estrangement from the divine. The *old* way of looking from a human point of view *has passed*. For *new creation*, see [[Galatians 06#^6-15|Gal 6.15]]; [[Ephesians 02#^2-15|Eph 2.15]]; [[2 Peter 03#^3-13|2 Pet 3.13]]; [[Revelation 21#^21-1|Rev 21.1]]; cf. [[Romans 08#^8-19|Rom 8.19–21]]; see [[Isaiah 43#^43-18|Isa 43.18–19]]; see also [[Isaiah 65#^65-17|Isa 65.17]]; [[Isaiah 66#^66-22|66.22]]. Rabbinic Judaism, by contrast, reads Isaiah to mean that former things become secondary to new things. All history will be eclipsed, but not forgotten, at the end of time ([[Berakhot 13#13a|*b. Ber*. 13a]]). ^5-16

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-18|18–19]]:** As a result of the Christ’s undoing the damage caused by human sinfulness, *trespasses* are canceled (see [[Romans 04#^4-8|Rom 4.8]]), and human beings are *reconciled* to God, that is, saved from God’s wrath and from death; [[Romans 05#^5-10|Rom 5.10–11]]; [[Colossians 01#^1-20|Col 1.20]]. Rather than seeing the trait of human sinfulness as a result of Adam and Eve’s rebellion and so as revocable, the rabbis understood sin, and especially what they describe as an inclination toward sin, as an innate and even necessary part of human nature, since sexual urges and the desire for wealth and power push people toward productive activity (e.g., *Gen. Rab*. 9.7). Still, the rabbis held that sin is not indelible, that true repentance allows complete reconciliation with God. Additionally, the sins of fathers are in no way accounted to their children. Thus, e.g., the grandchildren of Haman, Sisera, and Sennacherib became great teachers among the Jewish people ([[Gittin 57#57b|*b. Git.* 57b]]). *Ministry of reconciliation*, reconciliation is initiated by God, through Christ’s death. ^5-18

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-20|20]]:** As an *ambassador* of Christ, Paul’s ministry is to proclaim what God has done. This differs from the rabbinic perspective, in which, while God longs for and accepts human atonement, people must act to restore their relationship with God. ^5-20

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 05#^5-21|21]]:** *Made him to be sin*, a difficult phrase perhaps referring to Christ’s identification with humanity or to his having been made a sin-offering, to atone for human sin. As a result, those who live in Christ can be embraced by God’s reconciliation so that they *might become the righteousness of God*, that is, sharing God’s characteristic of righteousness; see [[Romans 01#^1-17|Rom 1.17]]; [[Romans 03#^3-5|3.5]],[[Romans 03#^3-21|21–22]],[[Romans 03#^3-25|25–26]]; [[Romans 10#^10-3|10.3]] (twice); [[Philippians 03#^3-9|Phil 3.9]]; also [[Matthew 06#^6-33|Mt 6.33]]; [[James 01#^1-20|Jas 1.20]]; [[2 Peter 01#^1-1|2 Pet 1.1]]. Based on [[Torah 47 - Re'eh#^13-5|Deut 13.4]], “The Lord your God you shall follow,” the rabbis decreed an obligation to imitate God’s righteous behaviors, e.g., clothing the naked, visiting the sick, comforting mourners, and burying the dead ([[Sotah 14#14a|*b. Sot.* 14a]]). More generally, [[Shabbat 133#133b|*b. Shabb.* 133b]] states: “As he is merciful and gracious, so you be merciful and gracious.” *himself* ^5-21
