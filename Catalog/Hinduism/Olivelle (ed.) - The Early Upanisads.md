## About

The Principal (Mukhya) Upanishads are the oldest and most widely studied Upanishads, the concluding portion of the Vedas. All Hindus accept them as revealed scripture (śruti). Olivelle's volume gives the Sanskrit and an English translation of twelve of them.

- **The canon:** most Hindu traditions count ten: Īśā, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Taittirīya, Aitareya, Chāndogya and Bṛhadāraṇyaka, each tied to one of the four Vedas. Some scholars add the Śvetāśvatara, Kauṣītaki and Maitrāyaṇīya; John Arapura (2012) calls the resulting thirteen the canon favoured by most present-day scholars.
- **Why these ten:** Shankara and Madhva wrote commentaries on them. Ramanuja quoted hundreds of their passages in his *Sri Bhasya*, and Rangaramanuja commented on almost all of them around the 1600s.
- **Date:** Wikipedia gives 800 BCE to the start of the common era in its introduction and, in a later section, probably 600–300 BCE, citing Joel Brereton (1990).
- **Form:** Wikipedia classes the Taittirīya, Aitareya, Chāndogya and Bṛhadāraṇyaka as prose, the Īśā, Kaṭha and Muṇḍaka as verse, and the Māṇḍūkya as prose in classical Sanskrit.
- **Olivelle's edition:** *The Early Upaniṣads: Annotated Text and Translation* (Oxford University Press, 1998), in a joint series of the University of Texas at Austin and Oxford. UT Austin lists its twelve texts: the ten above plus the Kauṣītaki and Śvetāśvatara, so the Maitrāyaṇīya is left out. Oxford calls it the full edition of the early Upanisads, "the central scriptures of Hinduism." Olivelle also published *Upaniṣads* (1996).
- **Other translations:** Robert Ernest Hume, *The Thirteen Principal Upanishads* (1921); Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan, *The Principal Upanishads* (1953).
- **Why it matters:** William K. Mahony, quoted by Wikipedia, defines the major Upanishads as those interpreted by most classical Indian philosophers; Wikipedia counts them among Hinduism's most important scriptures.
