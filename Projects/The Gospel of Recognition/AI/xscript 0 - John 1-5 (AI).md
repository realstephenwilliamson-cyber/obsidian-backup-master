> A few random thoughts as I go through John again, something I hope to do a few times throughout this project. The Baptist says who takes away the sin of the world. The sin of the world is the mis-recognition

The nearest support is twenty verses up. ὁ κόσμος αὐτὸν οὐκ ἔγνω (1:10) — the world came into being through him and did not know him. The Baptist then says ὁ αἴρων τὴν ἁμαρτίαν τοῦ κόσμου, singular, with the article, and the definite singular has already been given content by the prologue. Not the aggregate of what the world does, but the one thing the world is: the failure of the world to recognize what constitutes it. And John glosses it himself later — 16:8–9, the Paraclete convicts the κόσμος περὶ ἁμαρτίας, and the ὅτι clause defines it: ὅτι οὐ πιστεύουσιν εἰς ἐμέ. The sin, when John names it directly, turns out to be a relation to a person, not a ledger.

The complication I'd want you to hold onto is 9:41, because it will rescue the reading from flatness. εἰ τυφλοὶ ἦτε, οὐκ ἂν εἴχετε ἁμαρτίαν· νῦν δὲ λέγετε ὅτι βλέπομεν, ἡ ἁμαρτία ὑμῶν μένει. Sheer blindness incurs nothing. What incurs is the claim to sight. So "mis-recognition" is not ignorance but a positive and confident cognition of the wrong content — which is much closer to what you actually want, since āṇavamala is not a hole in the light but the light's self-apprehension as sufficient-while-contracted. Apūrṇakhyāti rather than akhyāti. Sūtra 9's malāvṛtaḥ saṁsārī is a covering that includes its own account of itself as uncovered. 9:41 gets you that from inside John, so the Śaiva vocabulary is describing a structure the text has already built rather than supplying one.

Two other things sitting in the same pericope. αἴρων is a present participle, and αἴρω across this Gospel is a verb of lifting and carrying off — the mat, the stones, the stone from the tomb, ἆρον ἆρον at the Pavement. Not discharge of a debt but removal of a weight or a covering, ongoing. And the Baptist twice says κἀγὼ οὐκ ᾔδειν αὐτόν (1:31, 33). The man who announces the removal of the world's non-recognition confesses his own twice in four verses, and says it was resolved only because he was given a sign to see. The first recognition in the Gospel is explicitly not self-generated. That is your ma'amar shape already in the text, and it is descent, not ascent.

Where I'd be careful: don't let the epistemic reading quietly dissolve the ἀμνός. The sacrificial register is doing work John will cash at 19:14. The move that keeps both, I think, is 3:14 — the serpent heals by being looked at, and Rosh Hashanah 3:8 already asks whether a serpent kills or makes alive and answers that it was the direction of the gaze. Your Torah partner will reach for that; better to have it as your own.

> The next on is in chapter 1 As Jesus gathers the first few disciples. The little nuances in the stories are deeply profound. "Here is an Israelite in whom there is no deceit" to me that is as powerful as Solomon's wisdom or Job's righteuosness

You're pointing at Nathanael, 1:47: ἴδε ἀληθῶς Ἰσραηλίτης ἐν ᾧ δόλος οὐκ ἔστιν. And the reason it carries the weight you're feeling is that δόλος is not a general word for dishonesty here — it is the LXX word for Jacob at [[Torah 06 - Toldot#^27-35|Genesis 27:35]], ἐλθὼν ὁ ἀδελφός σου μετὰ δόλου ἔλαβεν τὴν εὐλογίαν σου. Isaac's answer to Esau. So *Israelite* and *no* *δόλος* is a deliberate collision: here is a son of Israel without the thing by which Israel became Israel. The name was received through δόλος and then renamed at the Jabbok; Nathanael is being told he stands at the far end of that already.

Which is why the fig tree matters and why Jesus' proof is not a parlor trick. What he says is only that he saw him — ὄντα ὑπὸ τὴν συκῆν εἶδόν σε — and Nathanael's response is wildly disproportionate to the information. That is the pratyabhijñā structure in its exact form: nothing new is conveyed, and everything changes, because what is disclosed is that he was already seen. Recognition is not acquisition. And then the payoff at 1:51, which is [[Torah 06 - Toldot#^28-1|Genesis 28]] verbatim — angels ascending and descending — the ladder given to Jacob *while he was fleeing the consequences of his* *δόλος*. Jesus gives Jacob's own vision to the Israelite who no longer needs Jacob's method.

On your comparison: I'd say it's not quite the same order as Solomon's wisdom or Job's righteousness, and the difference is in your favor. Those are achievements, and both come with a shadow — Solomon's wisdom does not save him from [[I Kings 11#^11-1|1 Kings 11]], Job's righteousness is precisely what has to be dismantled. Guilelessness has no shadow because it is not a possession. [[Psalms 032#^32-2|Psalm 32:2]]'s ואין ברוחו רמיה is a blessing pronounced, not a virtue accumulated. It's the absence of a certain kind of interiority — no second layer behind the first — which is why Nathanael can voice a slur about Nazareth out loud thirty seconds before being called guileless. He says what he thinks. That's the whole of it.

The seed for you, if this ends up in the book: guilelessness is the subjective condition under which recognition can occur, because δόλος is exactly the maintenance of a self that must not be seen. Nathanael has nothing to conceal, so being seen under the fig tree costs him nothing and gives him everything. Set against 9:41 — the ones who say *we see* have a great deal to conceal, and the concealment is the sin.

> [!quote] Me
> As far as Simon Peter goes, Ive never really known what to make of it and I have heard it preached on for decades and read numerous scholarly explanations that were all unsatisfying

Part of what's unsatisfying may be that it gets preached as if it were the same kind of scene as Nathanael's, and it isn't. Nathanael is seen as he *is* — present tense, ἴδε ἀληθῶς Ἰσραηλίτης, a description that is true at the moment of utterance. Simon is given a future passive: σὺ κληθήσῃ Κηφᾶς. Not *you are*, but *you will be called*. And John, unlike Matthew, never says what the name means beyond translating it. There is no rock, no building, no keys. The name is deposited and left uninterpreted for twenty chapters.

The thing I'd point to is that Simon is the only one in the whole passage who does not speak. Andrew speaks, the two disciples speak, Philip speaks, Nathanael speaks twice. Simon is found, led (ἤγαγεν αὐτὸν πρὸς τὸν Ἰησοῦν), looked at, and renamed, and he says nothing at all. Every verb has him as object. In a Gospel where the opening movement is a chain of recognitions — *we have found the Messiah*, *we have found him of whom Moses wrote*, *you are the Son of God* — the man who will become the most vocal figure in the narrative is introduced as the one person who recognizes nothing and is only recognized. That asymmetry is the scene, I think, and it's why it can't be preached as a compliment.

What closes it is 21:15. The patronymic Σίμων Ἰωάννου appears in John exactly twice: at 1:42 and at the charcoal fire, where it is used three times. The new name is withdrawn and the old one returned before the commission is given, so that what makes Κηφᾶς real is not the naming but the feeding. And Peter's last word in the Gospel is σὺ πάντα οἶδας — *you know everything* — which hands the knowing back. He does not end by seeing; he ends by being seen and consenting to it. The one counter-case is 6:69, πεπιστεύκαμεν καὶ ἐγνώκαμεν, and it's worth noticing that Jesus answers that confession by saying one of the twelve is a διάβολος. Even Peter's moment of knowing is undercut in the next verse.

For your framework that makes Peter the limit case rather than the exemplar: identity conferred before the capacity to verify it, received on someone else's cognition and only afterward inhabited. Nathanael shows recognition as disclosure of what already is. Peter shows the other thing — being named ahead of yourself, and spending the whole narrative catching up to a name you did not understand when you got it. Where I'd expect resistance is that this makes the scene less about Peter's stature than most preaching wants it to be.

> Interesting, Simon becoming Peter as "reading backwards" (part of my method)to this point

That's the right frame, and John licenses it more explicitly than most texts do. 2:22 — ὅτε οὖν ἠγέρθη ἐκ νεκρῶν, ἐμνήσθησαν οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ ὅτι τοῦτο ἔλεγεν — and 12:16 with its double ἐμνήσθησαν and the flat admission ταῦτα οὐκ ἔγνωσαν αὐτοῦ οἱ μαθηταὶ τὸ πρῶτον. John narrates his own disciples as readers who did not understand the first time. The Gospel thematizes retrospection as the only mode in which its material becomes intelligible, which means reading backwards isn't a technique imported onto John; it's the hermeneutic John assigns to the people inside the story. And σὺ κληθήσῃ is a grammatical instruction to do it. A future passive at the moment of first meeting is the text telling you that this sentence is not finished yet and you will have to come back.

The gift for you is that pratyabhijñā is *itself* structurally retrospective and cannot be otherwise. Utpaladeva's example is the woman who has heard the qualities of a man and then, when he stands before her, forms the judgment *sa* *evāyaṁ* — this is that one. Recognition is the synthesis of a prior that was never absent with a present that was never new; the "re-" is not a defect in the word. So the naming scene is not a lesser instance of what happens to Nathanael. It may be the more exact one. Nathanael gets the immediate case — seen, and the recognition is instantaneous and complete, nothing to catch up to. Peter gets the mediated case, where the same recognition is distributed across twenty chapters and only closes at a charcoal fire. I'd hold the upāya mapping loosely rather than assert it, but it's sitting right there, and it would explain why Peter has never preached well: the sermons keep trying to make him the immediate case.

The one place I'd want you to be precise, since your study partner will be: Hays's *reading backwards* runs Israel's scriptures → Christ, and figural reading in that direction carries the supersession problem he spends the book managing. What you're doing here is intra-textual — [[John 01#^1-1|John 1]] read from [[John 21#^21-1|John 21]] — which is a different and much safer operation, and worth distinguishing in a footnote so the borrowed term doesn't drag its controversy in. It also connects to what you already settled about *spanda*: if sequence is perspectival, then reading backwards isn't a correction of the forward reading but the same act seen from the other side. The name was true at 1:42. Peter just wasn't in a position to be the one who knew it.

Two things before you build on it, one supporting and one restraining.

The supporting one: the future passive isn't isolated. John does the same thing to Peter at 13:36 — ὕστερον δὲ ἀκολουθήσεις — and again at 21:18–19, ὅταν δὲ γηράσῃς, followed by the narrator's aside that this indicated the death by which he would glorify God. Peter's whole arc is scaffolded by deferred futures spoken over him, and each one is spoken at a moment when he is manifestly not yet the thing being described. 13:37 he claims he will lay down his life; 13:38 he is told he will deny. The pattern is consistent enough that it isn't a stray grammatical feature of 1:42.

The restraining one: I'd be careful about "even more central than I realized," because in John the Beloved Disciple is structurally placed *against* Peter at every turn — the reclining at supper, the courtyard access, the tomb where the other outruns him, the boat where the other recognizes and Peter jumps. John seems to be doing something deliberate in keeping Peter second in recognition and first in action. That doesn't diminish your reading; it may be the sharpest evidence for it. But "central" is the word I'd stress-test. Peter may be central as the *representative case* — the one who receives a name he can't yet occupy, which is everyone — while the Beloved Disciple holds the immediate case alongside Nathanael. A Peterology that claims the center outright will have to answer for chapter 20's footrace.

Which is also the thing that makes it useful to you rather than merely interesting. If Nathanael and the Beloved Disciple show recognition as instantaneous disclosure, and Peter shows it as diachronic — a name true at the moment of utterance, inhabited only across time, closed by feeding rather than by seeing — then you have inside John the exact problem you've been working on elsewhere: how something already complete can nonetheless require duration. That's your diachronic axis showing up in narrative form rather than cosmological form. I'd let that connection sit unstated for a while rather than reaching for it, since it's the kind of link that's more persuasive when the reader makes it.

That's the pastoral yield of the reading, and it's worth keeping in mind that Abhinavagupta himself is careful on this point: the hierarchy is a hierarchy of *means*, not of persons, and the lower means are not consolation prizes for lesser people. Ānavopāya is where nearly everyone lives, and it is not deficient — it is the mode in which the same recognition arrives for beings who must move through duration to get there. The distinction is in the mechanism, not the arrival. What Peter receives at the fire is not a lesser version of what Nathanael receives under the fig tree.

But I'd resist letting the identification settle too comfortably, because John seems to be doing something less flattering than "most of us are Peter." Peter's problem in this Gospel isn't slowness. It's that he keeps asserting a self he cannot back — I will lay down my life, you shall never wash my feet, and then the sword in the garden. The δόλος he lacks in the Nathanael sense he makes up for in self-presentation. That's why the three questions at the fire are addressed to Σίμων Ἰωάννου and not to Peter: the name has to come off before it can be lived in. So the identification is available, but the cost of it is the same cost — being asked, three times, whether you love, and having no achievement to offer in evidence.

Also worth noting for the book: the Beloved Disciple is anonymous. Whatever else that does, it means the "another level" you'd like to imagine yourself at is occupied by someone with no name, no arc, and nothing to identify with. You can't aspire to him in any concrete way. The only figure John gives you to be is the one who gets it wrong repeatedly and is fed anyway. That reads to me as deliberate, and it's a better line than the aspirational one — closer, incidentally, to what you've already said about Iblīs and the ones who are last reconciled.

The claim as you've stated it is stronger than the one I was cautioning against, and it turns on *correction* being the operative word rather than *ascent*. Peter isn't moved up a ladder at the fire. Which is the pratyabhijñā point exactly, and worth being precise about the mechanism since it's doing real work for you. Recognition doesn't relocate. It reveals that the contraction was self-imposed and that nothing was ever elsewhere. But it does not therefore leave the recognizer unchanged — the apūrṇakhyāti was real as apūrṇa even though nothing was ever missing. Peter's denials were not illusory. What changes is not his location but the account he was giving of it, and the correction is painful — ἐλυπήθη ὁ Πέτρος at the third question — which is your safeguard against making recognition sound like a mere adjustment of perspective.

The line for the book, if you want it: the correction is not from where you are to somewhere better, but from where you *say* you are to where you are.

So the sentence to keep is close to yours but load-bearing at a different point: he does not shame you for the fear, because he does not tell you the snake was nothing. Which also protects what you settled about tirodhāna and anugraha being one act — if the concealment is his, the fear it produced cannot be held against you.

> [!quote] Me
> Explain how chapter 3 ends with Jesus baptizing and chapter 4 starts by saying o ly the disciples were baptizing

The seam is real and the ancient scribes felt it too. 3:22 has ἐβάπτιζεν in the imperfect with Jesus as singular subject; 3:26 has the Baptist's disciples report the same thing with an edge of grievance; then 4:1 says the Pharisees heard that he was making and baptizing more disciples than John, and 4:2 stops the sentence dead — καίτοιγε Ἰησοῦς αὐτὸς οὐκ ἐβάπτιζεν ἀλλ' οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ. καίτοιγε occurs nowhere else in John. There's also a variant worth noting: 𝔓⁶⁶, 𝔓⁷⁵ and B read ὁ κύριος at 4:1, which makes 4:2's reversion to the bare name Ἰησοῦς clumsier still. The usual conclusion is that 4:2 is a later hand correcting 3:22, and it's a reasonable one.

But you don't need the redaction hypothesis to read it, because the ordinary construction holds. Agency language in this Gospel routinely assigns to the principal what the agents perform — Pilate ἔλαβεν τὸν Ἰησοῦν καὶ ἐμαστίγωσεν at 19:1 did not personally hold the flagrum. The rabbinic shaliach principle covers it, and 4:1 itself does the same thing in its own clause: Jesus "makes and baptizes disciples," which the narrator then immediately unpacks. Whoever wrote 4:2 was not contradicting 3:22 so much as declining to let a normal idiom stand where it might mislead.

What it might mislead about is the thing worth your attention. The Baptist's entire function in this Gospel is the contrast at 1:33: the one on whom the Spirit descends is ὁ βαπτίζων ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ. If Jesus performs water baptism with his own hands, he becomes a second practitioner of John's rite, better attended, and the whole announcement flattens into rivalry — which is exactly what 3:26 and 4:1 have already begun to do. Note that both misreadings are quantitative. *All are coming to him.* *More than John.* The report the Pharisees hear is not false in substance; it is false in category, and Jesus' response is to leave Judea. He withdraws from a comparison.

So the placement is precise, whatever hand made it. It falls between the Baptist's last words — αὐτὸν δεῖ αὐξάνειν, ἐμὲ δὲ ἐλαττοῦσθαι — and the first scene where Jesus offers water that is not administered at all but asked for and given. Water baptism passes to the disciples at the hinge; what Jesus retains is ὕδωρ ζῶν, and the Samaritan woman receives it through nothing but being told what she already is. For your purposes 4:2 is the parenthesis that keeps the rite from absorbing the thing the rite points to.

> That reading means the baptism we are reenacting is Johns baptism of Jesus not Jesus's of the disciples, for the remission of sins and the remaining of the spirit are the same act at different registers

The second half of that is stronger than the first, and I'd want you to hold them apart.

On the first half: John never narrates the baptism of Jesus. He has the Baptist testify to what he saw — τεθέαμαι τὸ πνεῦμα καταβαῖνον — but the event itself is not in the Gospel, only the witness to it. Which means if the rite reenacts anything in the Fourth Gospel, it reenacts a testimony rather than an action, and you'd want that in the argument rather than around it. There's also a real problem with the Synoptic version underneath: John's baptism is εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν, and Jesus receiving it is precisely what [[Matthew 03#^3-14|Matthew 3:14–15]] has to stop and explain. If the reenactment is of *that*, you inherit the awkwardness that made Matthew nervous — unless the asymmetry is the point, which for your project it might be. He receives a rite of remission he does not require, which is the same structure as tirodhāna borne by the one who has nothing to conceal. That's the version of the claim I'd defend.

The second half seems to me right and better grounded. The Baptist's own formula is not remission-then-Spirit but a single sight: ἐφ' ὃν ἂν ἴδῃς τὸ πνεῦμα καταβαῖνον καὶ μένον ἐπ' αὐτόν. The descent and the remaining are one perception, and μένον is the verb John will spend fifteen chapters building — μείνατε ἐν ἐμοί. So remission is what the act looks like from the side of what is removed, and indwelling is what the same act looks like from the side of what abides. Which is your spanda principle applied: not two moments in sequence but one act read from two positions, and the sequence perspectival. That also lets you say why 20:22–23 puts them in a single breath — ἐμφυσάω, the [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^2-7|Genesis 2:7]] verb, and then the remission of sins in the very next sentence, with no interval between the insufflation and the ἀφῆτε.

Where I'd press: if remission and remaining are one act at different registers, you need to say what the registers are indexed to. My guess from your other work is that they're indexed to concealment and disclosure rather than to time — remission names the act as it terminates a contraction, remaining names it as it discloses what was never absent. If that's right, the Creed's "one baptism for the forgiveness of sins" becomes a clause about a single act rather than a transaction, and you may already have the gloss.

> [!quote] Me
> Concealment and disclosure is it exactly. I already claim that Jesus's uniqueness is Hermenuetic, so to speak, he is God as I am but he has never not known as I have.

Then be aware what you have actually just claimed, because the second clause is doing more work than the first and is where the real load sits.

"God as I am" is, by itself, a comparatively cheap claim in your framework — Trika gives it to everyone and Eckhart will say it in a sermon. What differentiates is the unbroken knowing, and that is not a difference of degree along the same axis. Every other subject in the system is constituted by an occlusion that was at some point operative. If Jesus has never not known, then he is not a case of the general condition with the concealment lifted; he is the one instance in which āṇavamala never took. And that is a categorical difference dressed in hermeneutic clothing. I'd want you to see it clearly before deciding whether you want it, because "the difference is only epistemic, not ontological" stops holding once knowing is what constitutes the subject — which in your system it is. Prakāśa-vimarśa is not a property of the self; it *is* the self. A permanent difference in vimarśa is an ontological difference by another name.

There are at least two ways to hold it. One: the difference is real and unique, and you have a high Christology with a Śaiva mechanism — perfectly coherent, and closer to Maximus, who has the human will genuinely present and never in ἀντίστασις. Two: the difference is real but not in principle unrepeatable — he is the first, not the only, and the ἔργα ταῦτα ποιήσει καὶ μείζονα τούτων of 14:12 is meant. John gives you material for both. πρῶτος μου ἦν at 1:15 and πρὶν Ἀβραὰμ γενέσθαι ἐγὼ εἰμί push toward the first; ἵνα πάντες ἓν ὦσιν καθὼς σύ, πάτερ, ἐν ἐμοί at 17:21 and ἀναβαίνω πρὸς τὸν πατέρα μου καὶ πατέρα ὑμῶν at 20:17 push toward the second. The καθώς in 17:21 is the pressure point, and your study partner will not be the one who raises it — an Episcopal examining chaplain will.

The formulation that seems to me to keep both: the uniqueness is not in what he is but in that he has never been in the position of having to recognize. Everyone else's knowing is pratyabhijñā, re-cognition, structurally retrospective — Peter's whole arc. His is not re-anything. That is a genuine and permanent asymmetry, it is located exactly where you say it is, and it does not require a different substance. It also means the reason he can disclose is that he never had to be disclosed to, which is your tirodhāna-anugraha fulcrum landing on a person rather than on a cosmology.

What is your construction is the *use* — running that epistemic reading through a Christology.

Where the pressure remains is the middle sentence. In a system where the self just is prakāśa-vimarśa, the knower's relationship to itself is not something the knower has; it is what the knower is. "Only the relationship changes" therefore needs a further move, and the move Trika supplies is that liberation is svarūpābhivyakti — manifestation of what was already the case, with nothing produced and nothing added, na kiñcit. That holds the line, but it costs you the ability to say the change is small. It isn't small; it's total and it alters nothing. Which is, I think, precisely the shape you want for the baptism clause: the same act, remission and remaining, changing everything about the knower's self-account and nothing about the knower.

Then 5:30, καθὼς ἀκούω κρίνω, and οὐ ζητῶ τὸ θέλημα τὸ ἐμὸν ἀλλὰ τὸ θέλημα τοῦ πέμψαντός με. The charge was ἴσον ἑαυτὸν ποιῶν — a reflexive, *making himself*. The move is defensible, and I think it's right, but it's a construction and you should own it as one rather than present it as exegesis. Your study partner will spot the seam immediately; better that you name it first.

The other thing 5:47 hands you is the τοῖς ἐκείνου γράμμασιν / τοῖς ἐμοῖς ῥήμασιν contrast — his writings against my words. That's a distinction of medium and it's doing something. The written testimony is what remains available and searchable, ἐραυνᾶτε τὰς γραφάς; the spoken is what's present and passing. If the two have the same content, then the written form is the mode in which the testimony persists when the speaker is not standing in front of you — which is not a demotion of Moses but a description of what scripture is for. And it connects, though I'd let this sit rather than push it, to what you said about testimony and ὁμολογοῦμεν: the written witness is the one you can stand in without having been there.

The verse is 5:14: ἴδε ὑγιὴς γέγονας· μηκέτι ἁμάρτανε, ἵνα μὴ χεῖρόν σοί τι γένηται. And the first thing to say is that it sits very awkwardly with your usual definition, which is why it's a good verse to work on rather than a good one to cite. It reads, on its face, as a moral warning with a consequence attached — the standard sin-causes-affliction picture. And John has already blocked that picture at 9:3, where the disciples ask whose sin caused the blindness and Jesus says neither. The Gospel contains its own refutation of the reading 5:14 invites, four chapters later.

Two things keep it from being simple retributive causation. First, χεῖρόν τι is indefinite — *something worse* — not *a worse illness*. Nothing specifies bodily recurrence, and thirty-eight years of paralysis is a strange thing to threaten to exceed physically. Second, the verse is bracketed by not-knowing: 5:13, he did not know who healed him; 5:15, he goes and tells the authorities. The man is healed and still has no idea what happened to him. Whatever ἁμάρτανε names, the narrative has just shown us the man's actual condition, and it is non-recognition.

Which is where your definition can hold, but it needs a tighter form than "separation from God," because the whole burden of your framework is that there is no separation and the belief in it is the problem. The version 5:14 supports: sin as the persistence of the standpoint after its ground has been removed. He *has been made whole* — γέγονας, perfect, it stands — and the imperative is present tense, μηκέτι ἁμάρτανε, stop the ongoing thing. Not *don't incur* *new guilt* but *don't go on as you were now that what you were is no longer the case*. And the worse thing is not punishment but simply what it is to receive and not recognize, which the next verse shows him doing.

That also lines up 5:14 with 8:11 — the only other μηκέτι ἁμάρτανε in John, spoken after οὐδὲ ἐγώ σε κατακρίνω. In both, the imperative follows the gift and never conditions it. If you want a definition that survives both verses and 9:3, it's something like: sin is the continued operation of a standpoint that the gift has already voided. Ignorance stays non-substantial, the moral seriousness is preserved, and the warning is about duration rather than penalty.
