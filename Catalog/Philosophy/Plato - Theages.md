## About

A short dialogue in the Platonic corpus, probably not by Plato. Demodocus brings his son Theages to Socrates for advice on his education; the talk turns to wisdom, rule and Socrates' divine sign.

- **People and setting:** Socrates, Demodocus and Theages, meeting near the Stoa of Zeus. Mark Joyal's 2000 edition, as BMCR's review reports, sets the dramatic date at 409 BC.
- **Authenticity:** accepted in antiquity and kept in Thrasyllus's canon, but doubted since the early 19th century. The IEP lists it among the dubia and says that, if genuine, it would probably go with the early or early transitional dialogues. W. R. M. Lamb, cited by Wikipedia, judged it "inferior and un-Socratic". Copleston counts it among works generally rejected, though probably fourth-century and no forgery.
- **Date of composition:** Joyal: about 345–335 BC, by a member of the Academy's first generation after Plato. Klaus Döring (2004): the last two thirds of the fourth century BC. John Dillon, reviewing Luc Brisson (2014), notes that some date it as late as the first century BC.
- **Subject:** whether wisdom can be taught. Theages wants the wisdom to govern men; Socrates links this to tyranny and suggests that such arts are learned by keeping company with those who have them. He then describes his daimonion, an inner voice heard since childhood that only warns him off. Theages proposes a trial period as his pupil, to end if the sign objects.
- **Place among the dialogues:** first in Thrasyllus's fifth tetralogy, with *Charmides*, *Laches* and *Lysis*. Diogenes Laertius (tr. Hicks) titles it *Theages, or On Philosophy* and classes it as obstetric; Wikipedia gives the subtitle as On Wisdom. Stephanus pages 121a–131a.
- **Why it matters:** the BMCR reviews of Joyal and Döring say that it turned Socrates' sign into a daimon of traditional religion, a treatment Joyal sees as typical of most of antiquity, and that it seems, with the *Alcibiades*, to be an important source for the Platonic tradition's picture of Socrates.
