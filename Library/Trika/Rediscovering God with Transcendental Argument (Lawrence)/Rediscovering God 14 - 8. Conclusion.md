## 8. Conclusion

TOR

What answer can be given to the question “Who are you?” by one possessed by a demon, who, not having recognized Himself, desires to study something else?

—IPV 4.1 concluding verse 3, 2:317

In the endeavor to understand the foreign and complex Pratyabhijñā system, this book has been concerned with different aspects of the problem of interpretation. The problem presented itself from the outset in its contemporary radicalization as the difficulty of cross-cultural understanding. The first chapter argued against cultural relativism and in support of realism in cross-cultural interpretation by adducing the insight embodied in the Liar paradox’s along with the theories of Davidson and Apel. On this basis, I contended that paradoxically foundationalist and open-ended philosophical dialogue is the best approach to arbitrating conflicting accounts of the world. I formulated this conception of philosophical methodology both as a program for the contemporary discipline of comparative philosophy and as a model for understanding expressions of philosophical apologetics in other cultures, such as the Pratyabhijñā.

This conception of philosophy was confirmed by Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta’s understanding of the Pratyabhijñā discourse. The Pratyabhijñā thinkers endeavored to make their spiritual tradition available to all humanity by rationalizing it in terms of the inferential methods and the epistemological and ontological concerns of the medieval Sanskritic academy. At the same time—illustrating what I described as the hermeneutic boundedness of the specifics of philosophical methodology with substantive orientation—they attempted to “encompass” or

*[p. 156]* ^pg176

“overcode” their project of universally intelligible discourse by making it into a complete actualization of tantric myth and ritual spiritual exercise. Indeed the Pratyabhijñā articulates in a philosophically rationalized form the very syntax of Śaiva myth and ritual.

As a project of universally intelligible discourse, the Pratyabhijñā system demands to be taken seriously by a response in kind. Granting a competent philological exegesis, the most “neutral” and “critical” approach we can take to a system such as this is to consider its plausibility and make a decision about it. In respect of the Śaivas’ intentions, I believe we may understand the process as a contemporary translation and elaboration of their discourse corresponding to the Nyāya categories structuring dialogue—the prima facie position expressing doubt (pūrvapakṣa) and the ostensible refutation of it articulated in the doctrinal conclusion (siddhānta). To the extent that the conclusions of the Pratyabhijñā are accepted, they may be integrated into a further-expanding philosophical theory. To the extent that they are rejected, the Pratyabhijñā may be reductionistically interpreted in

terms of contesting viewpoints.

The analogies that I have suggested between Indian and Western ideas are by no means intended as simple equations, but rather as similarities-in-differences. Nor are the suggestions made on the premise that foreign ideas are of significance to us only as offering solutions to our own conundrums. My purpose is to facilitate the consideration of plausibility that I have argued is necessary.

I have emphasized the analogies of the Buddhist logic pūrvapakṣa with our class of interpretive skepticism and, most relevantly, the varieties of postmodern interpretive skepticism/relativism discussed in chapter 1. As affirming the necessity of an ultimate cosmic agent, the siddhānta has commonalities with the various Western arguments for the existence of God, granting of course the disagreement on whether or not God and the individual are identical! However, we have already been pursuing a more pointed analogy between the Pratyabhijñā and the Christian philosophical theology of logos. I maintain that the Pratyabhijñā enables us better to understand the presumptiveness of this philosophical theology against skepticism, by elucidating in it moments corresponding to the prakāśa and vimarśa arguments, divine self-recognition and its associated narrative syntax.

This chapter will more fully develop this analogical approach to plausibility through considering the strongest contemporary arguments that have been made for and against the affirmation of God as grounding our judgments through His logos. I believe these to be found in the grammatology of Jacques Derrida and the transcendental Thomism

Jacques Derrida’s Effort to Deconstruct Logocentrism

Derrida’s most frequent targets of deconstruction are not explicit advocates of the philosophical theology of logos.? Rather he elucidates this conception, which he calls “logocentrism,” as one of the deepest historical presuppositions of even secular Western thought; and he correlates it in an ad hoc fashion with such other themes as phonocentrism, phallocentrism (combined with our theme as “phallogocentrism”), masturbation, and so on.

In making allegations of such deep propensities, Derrida endeavors to criticize any absolutized claim about the nature of reality, especially as articulated in conceptions such as truth as correspondence, or the reference of the linguistic signifier to an objective signified or presence.’ For this reason Derrida may heuristically be characterized along with other kinds of skepticism as denying the validity or groundedness of recognition. For him, along with other proponents of cultural relativism, Buddhist logic, Mādhyamika, and Humean phenomenalism, conceptual/linguistic memory has no applicability to a

separate intelligible reality.‘

What are Derrida’s most important skeptical arguments?" Crucial to Of Grammatology is a reworking of the linguistics of Ferdinand de Saussure. While Derrida views Saussure as perpetuating logo-centrism and phonocentrism, he uses some of his ideas on speech and writing to subvert Saussure himself along with Rousseau, Lévi-Strauss, and others.

*[p. 158]* ^pg178

Most important for Derrida is Saussure’s conception of the linguistic sign. According to this view, the linguistic signs of both speech and writing are arbitrary, and given their significance only by their relations to each other. The relationships between signs are based on their differences from each other. Saussure states the idea clearly:

No particular configuration of sound is more aptly suited to express a given message than any other such configuration. So it is clearly the case—indeed, it must be the case—that no linguistic item can ever be based, ultimately, upon anything other than its non-coincidence with the rest. Here the terms arbitrary and differential designate two correlative properties.ć

Derrida’s move is to radicalize this conception of the systematicity of language to exclude the possibility of there ever being any referential

connection to, or recognition of, reality or presence.”

At this point we may observe a significant analogy between Derrida and the Buddhists beyond their skepticism per se. Both conceive semantics in terms of the negation between words/concepts to eliminate the need to posit an isomorphism between language and reality. The most important distinction between Derrida and the Buddhists in this regard is that the latter do not advert to such difference as a primary means of criticism, but only to support their skepticism

in epistemology.

Another key feature of Derrida’s skepticism is his argument that all language has the character erroneously attributed merely to writing of being a system of signs of signs. Since they are not symbols of initial presences, linguistic signs can only refer to other linguistic signs. A useful statement of this idea is found in the following passage from a section discussing Peirce and Lambert:

There is thus no phenomenality reducing the sign or the representation so that the thing signified may be allowed to glow finally in the luminosity of its presence. The so-called “thing itself” is always already a representamen shielded from the simplicity of the intuitive evidence. The representamen functions only by giving rise to an interpretant that itself becomes a sign and so on to infinity. The self-identity of the signified conceals itself unceasingly and is always on the move.’

The referential, or we may say recognitive, connection of language with presence is endlessly deferred. Derrida coins the term “différance”

While Derrida is cognizant of the great range of Western understandings of logos, from the Greeks through Hegel and Heidegger, he treats the theistic interpretation as most important. The Pratyabhijñā, Bernard Lonergan, and the Defense of

Logos as a Way of Affirming the Reality of God

I believe that the most focused and cogent development of the Christian understanding of logos as a way of affirming the reality of God—or what we may call a “logos argument”—is that made by Bernard Lonergan. This argument is presented by Lonergan in chapter 19 of his magnum opus, Insight, as one of his culminating syntheses of themes propounded throughout the work." Lonergan’s project in this regard is to develop the conceptions of logos of Thomas Aquinas and Augustine to formulate a transcendental argument that invokes God as necessary to account for the processes of human cognition evinced in the progress of science and other academic research." Like the examples from ancient and medieval Christian logos theories discussed in chapters 5 and 6, Lonergan’s argument may be elucidated by the analogous intellectual moments of the Pratyabhijñā epistemology.

Before considering Lonergan’s proof, I will appropriate one of his rhetorical strategies for my own argument. Lonergan designates the findings of the transcendental inquiries throughout his work “positions” and calls views that deny these findings “counter-positions.” He claims that the insights articulated in positions can be further and further developed. The counter-positions may be expressed along with various enduring insights. However, insofar as they deny what is necessary to their very expression, they are self-contradictory and invite reversal.™ć

These ideas usefully thematize what appears essential to the presumptiveness of any transcendental argument. The subversion of opposition is evinced in the Śaivas’ response to the Buddhists’ denial

*[p. 160]* ^pg180

of the validity of recognition with their theory of Śiva’s primordial self-recognition, and in the arguments for interpretive realism and against relativism that were adduced in chapter 1. With Lonergan’s terms I may express my hope: that the Pratyabhijñā system will enable us further to develop the positions articulated in Western understanding of logos to demonstrate the conception of the self-recognition of God against the counter-position of the skeptical denial of recognition—whether expressed by relativists, Buddhists, Humean phenomenalists, or any others.

Though Lonergan’s argument is difficult and complex, it admits

of simple expression:

The existence of God, then, is known as the conclusion to an argument and, while such arguments are many, all of them, I believe, are included in the following general form. lf the real is completely intelligible, God exists. But the real is completely intelligible. Therefore, God exists.”

Now, I suggest that this proofs minor and major premises respectively develop considerations analogous to and complementing the Śaiva arguments centering around the terms vimarśa and prakāśa.

### The Intelligibility of Reality and the Self-Contradictoriness of Skepticism

Lonergan’s minor premise that the real is completely intelligible may thus be related to the Pratyabhijñā vimarśa arguments for the transcendental character of recognition, along with the correlated epistemological and ontological arguments for the necessity and primitiveness of universal categories and relations. Whereas the Śaivas demonstrate their views through examining the most elementary features of the human construction of experience, Lonergan focuses on the critical process of academic research. Lonergan defines reality as being, that is, what is affirmed to be, as the objective of the human eros of an “an unrestricted desire to know.” The complete intelligibility of reality is a necessary truth or position. Whenever a state of affairs is affirmed, it is affirmed to be and placed within the generic sphere of intelligibility. The denial of this fact is a counter-position inviting self-reversal:

Might there not be an unknowable? If the question is invalid, it is to be ignored. If the question is valid, the answer may be “Yes” or “No.” But the answer, “Yes,” would be incoherent, for then one

*[p. 161]* ^pg181

would be knowing that the unknowable is; and the answer, “No,” would leave everything knowable within the range of being.”

In this way Lonergan demonstrates his minor premise.

As explained in chapter 6, the Śaiva vimarśa and correlative arguments, along with their precursors in Bhartphari and their Western analogues, articulate the transcendental entailment of the creation of the world from a metaphysical principle of interpretation. The primordial unity of the source pervades and interrelates both poles of ordinary interpretations and the states of affairs to which they are recognized to apply. Interpretation must be the way to reality and reality must be what is interpreted; the denial of either results in internal contradiction. The antirelativist arguments given in chapter 1 assert the impossibility of a theory of interpretations that does not refer to a noncontingent reality. Lonergan’s contention that reality is nothing but intelligible being, and that to deny this demonstrates it, is only a particularly clear and direct expression of a basic insight of the Western understanding of logos.” In the perspective of the Pratyabhijñāă, reality is only what we know through the recognitive application of

interpretations.

These considerations may be brought against the skeptic and antagonist of logocentrism, Jacques Derrida. I note that Derrida in many ways does address the problem of the self-referential contradiction for deconstructionism. More than claiming that the contradiction is not a problem, he frequently asserts that deconstructionism itself must be deconstructed. He claims that the différance, arche-writing, and so on, he invokes do not exist, and thus cannot be viewed as objects of a science.” He attempts to belie rational systematicity by deconstructing in an ad hoc fashion, largely commenting on the works

of others, and multiplying his terminologies.

I do not believe that all of this is satisfactory, although 1 am sympathetic with many of Derrida’s views and objectives, and the way in which his work may function as an agent of intellectual modesty. Despite his rhetorical devices, Derrida in both his supporting considerations and conclusions implicitly is recognizing as presences, or positing as intelligible being, a number of complex states of affairs. The Śaivas’ contention against the Buddhists that difference itself is a recognitively synthesized universal category applies also to Derrida’s ironically negated difference. Even if Derrida does not mind contradicting himself, this contradiction means that he is wrong in an old-fashioned, hard-headed rationalist sense.” Just as there can be no satisfactory phenomenalist account of “atomic” sensual objects in the

*[p. 162]* ^pg182

manner of either the Buddhist logicians or Hume, there can be no satisfactory grammatological non-science of nonexistent différance, nor any other satisfactory form of relativism. All kinds of interpretive skepticism are counter-positions.™\* Thought is essentially essentialist.

### The Idealistic Move: From Intelligibility to Intelligence

We may now turn to Lonergan’s major premise. How does the full intelligibility—or intrinsic recognizability—of reality or being entail the existence of God? Does the failure of skeptical positions, such as those of the Buddhists, Derrida, and other relativists, due to self-contradiction demonstrate the necessity of a Supreme Agent of the cosmos? The close association Derrida makes between the conception of the signification of presence or linguistic nonarbitrariness on the one hand, and the ontotheological logos on the other—would appear to grant that if deconstruction does not succeed, the existence of God as presumed by logos theology must follow. Of course many of self-content modern scientific and agnostic perspectives, as well as of theological viewpoints, would acknowledge the truth in Lonergan’s minor premise, but not grant that thereby God must exist.

Lonergan’s major premise, which culminates much of the laborious study of cognitive processes in Insight, is a theistic formulation of the ultimate grounds of intelligibility, of how we know that we know.” This is the intellectual moment explained in chapter 6 to correspond to the prakāśa arguments within Christian logos metaphysics. The transcendental grounds of our interpretations of the world are affirmed to have their existence within God's subjective experience, or self-recognition, rather than in features of external material objects, through a logic to the effect that esse est percipi. The same reasoning is evident in Lonergan’s demonstration of his major premise.

I must acknowledge that throughout his works, Lonergan actually opposes a doctrine he calls idealism, which would reduce cognition merely to contingent human interpretations. Against this he argues rigorously for objective scientific, philosophical, and theological inquiry. For Lonergan, the historic diversity of cognitive methods are animated by a drive to know that which is. Nevertheless, Lonergan perhaps even more vehemently repudiates the nāive realist or objectivist conception of knowing as “taking a look.” For him insight emerges within the laborious processes of critical inquiry itself and not as the contemplation of an uninterpreted datum.

Furthermore, these processes by which human subjects learn of a reality transcending their own subjectivity, are grounded in a higher

*[p. 163]* ^pg183

subjectivity. While a particular inquirer may be satisfied by reaching a “virtually unconditioned judgment” about a topic, each such judgment depends upon the “formally unconditioned judgment” of God. Lonergan explains intelligibility per se as the “idea of being.” This final interpretation—or we may say fully sufficient rationality—grounding all other interpretations is neither an actual human accomplishment nor a Platonic Idea existing in a realm of pure abstraction. It is the content of an act of an unlimited intellect:

An idea is the content of an act of understanding. As a sense

datum is the content of an act of sensing, as an image is the content of an act of imagining, as a percept is the content of an act of perceiving, as a concept is the content of an act of conceiving, defining, supposing, considering, as a judgment is the content of an act of judging, so an idea is the content of an act of understanding.

Being is the objective of the unrestricted desire to know.

Therefore, the idea of being is the content of an act of understanding that leaves nothing to be understood, no further questions to be asked.

According to Lonergan this unlimited act of intellect grasping the idea of being must necessarily exist:

Particular inquiries solve some questions but not all. Only an unrestricted act of understanding can meet the issue. . . . Correlative to an unrestricted desire to understand, there may be posited either an indefinite process of development or an unrestricted act of understanding. But the content of developing understanding never is the idea of being, for as long as understanding is developing, there are further questions to be answered. Only the content of the unrestricted act of understanding can be the idea of being, for it is only on the supposition of an unrestricted act that everything about everything is understood. It follows that the idea of being is absolutely transcendent. For it is the content of an act of unre-

stricted understanding.”

In order to flesh out further the analogy between Lonergan’s argument and that of the Pratyabhijñā, we must further examine his understanding of the interrelation of subjectivity and objectivity at this level. According to Lonergan, the idea of being has both “primary” and “secondary” components, which he also calls primary and secondary intelligibles. These are respectively being or reality in itself and all the contingent components of reality. Now Lonergan holds that the primary component of the idea of being is none other than the

*[p. 164]* ^pg184

unlimited act of the intellect Himself. For by understanding Himself, this unlimited act of intellect understands all contingent things as coming from Himself:

Jf an act of understanding is unrestricted, it understands understanding; it understands not only restricted acts but also the unrestricted act; understanding the unrestricted act it must understand its content, otherwise the understanding of the unrestricted act would be restricted; but the content of the unrestricted act is the idea of being, and so if the unrestricted act understands itself, it thereby also understands everything else... .

The primary intelligible is by identity the unrestricted act of understanding. It is intelligible in the profounder sense, for it is an intelligible that is identical with intelligence in act. It is a unique intelligible, for it is identical with the unique act of unrestricted understanding. On the other hand, the secondary intelligibles are what are also grasped inasmuch as the unrestricted act understands itself. They are intelligible in the ordinary sense, for they are understood; but they are not intelligible in the profounder sense, for the unrestricted act is one understanding of many intelligibles, and only the unique, primary intelligible is identical with the unrestricted act.”

Now, according to Lonergan, this idea of being in its primary component, or unlimited act of intellect has the attributes of God. Therefore Lonergan considers his proof to have been accomplished.?

I believe that Lonergan has posited—through arguments paralleling the Śaivas’ arguments about prakāśa and vimarśa—as the necessary grounds of human cognition what is in effect a conception of divine self-recognition. We even see an analogy between Lonergan’s theory and the Saiva conception of Pure Wisdom, in that he contends that all limited recognized things are understood by God as contained within and derived from His self-recognition.

Again, for Lonergan as a Thomist, since humans are potentially what God is in act, what we really are is in God.” So for him as well as the Śaivas, our recognitions are grounded insofar as they in some way participate in the self-recognition of the deity. I believe that Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta would heartily agree with Lonergan’s statement that “God is the unrestricted act of understanding, the eternal rapture glimpsed in every Archimidean cry of Eureka.”

However, there is a crucial difference between Lonergan and the Śaivas here as well. For the latter philosophy enables us to utilize this

*[p. 165]* ^pg185

glimpse, present in every experience, as a path toward full participation in the self-recognition of God. I recall a passage by Abhinavagupta

that I quoted in chapter 5:

Thus the \[lower\] condition of conceptual construction \[vikalpa\] should be understood in the recognitive synthesis \[anusamdhāna\] of the pot, etc., \[expressed\] “This is that pot.” However, in these levels of recognitive synthesis \[anusamdhāna\], the Wisdom Power \[vidyāsakti, a.k.a., śuddhavidyā, Pure Wisdom\] flashes strongly, like lightning. Thus the gurus admit them to be first means to the

Supreme State.?

At the end of the chapter I will offer my suggestions about how to assess the Pratyabhijñā in its divergence from Christian logos theology about whether or not God and the person are identical. At this point we must further consider their common idealistic premise.”

### The Tenableness of Idealism at the End of the Twentieth Century

If I am right that there is an idealistic supposition, that esse est percipi, essential to the Pratyabhijñā as well as the Western philosophical theology of logos, it may seem that I have discredited them. Within the recent past of philosophy, only isolated voices such as John Findlay and Nicholas Rescher have openly defended idealism. The last advocates of idealism who had a dominant influence in the Western academy were the nineteenth-twentieth century Neo-Hegelians or Absolute Idealists, such as Green, Bradley, Royce, and Gentile. As all argumentation for a theistic superagent has been intellectually marginalized,

Neo-Hegelian idealism has virtually disappeared.

Idealistic systems were driven into academic disrepute earlier in this century by attacks from a diverse group of philosophers reforming the discipline in conformity with modern patterns of rationality. It is notable that two of the sharpest critics, G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell, had at one time been idealists. Philosophy for them was a linguistic or quasi-mathematical tool assigned the role of defending and analyzing the empirical data of both common sense and science.™\* An early formulation of Moore typified much of the criticism of idealism, and also plays into our reflections on narrative syntax: the anti-idealists wished to separate the acts of any subject's awareness from its objects.” They thus held that it is a logical fallacy to conclude on the basis of the tautology that we only know what we know that it is

*[p. 166]* ^pg186

impossible for things to exist that we do not know. Moore, in particular, is famous for arguing the complete undeniability of the common belief in external objects.

The “metanarrative” of human progress in scientific and technological thinking advocated by such philosophies may be broadly characterized in a comparative narrative-syntax framework by an emphasis on two factors. First is the object or what Burke interpreted as the material “scene.” Second is the instrument, which includes the causes identified by science as well as technological artifacts exploiting them.” The same emphases typify efforts to understand and control the political, social, and economic spheres.

Such “objectivist” and “instrumentalist” thinking endeavors to neutralize interpretive subjectivity in the recognition of an independent, predictable reality. The role of the agent in the modern story of rational progress has been correctly to observe and manipulate this

reality.

In the developing postmodern consciousness discussed in chapter 1, through appreciation of the increasing pluralism, we may say that there has been a greater sensitivity to the role of various forms of individual or cultural “agency” /subjectivity in interpretively structuring experience. Moreover, I claim that a doctrine of idealism, of an “illusionistic” variety, is very widely accepted in our postmodern situation, although its proponents would not accept the appellation of what modern thinking superseded. That is, systems of thought that are skeptical in the sense of reducing reality to interpretations are in effect asserting that esse est percipi. This is true of some of the central theses of thinkers such as Whorf, Barnes and Bloor, the Kuhn of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, Feyerabend, Derrida, Lyotard, Foucault, and countless others. I am not alone in labeling much of skeptical postmodern thought idealist. Others including Richard Rorty and Juliet Sychrava have already pointed out how in the reduction of reality to textuality, in their “sentimentalism,” and in other ways these philosophies continue the themes of nineteenth-century idealism and romanticism.”

Of course the postmodern vision of paralogical narratives does not reduce reality to an expression of divine subjectivity /agency. Such thought repudiates the intersubjectivity, universality, or objectivity of reality guaranteed by divine authorship. Postmodern skepticism rather reduces reality to the production of a fragmented human subjectivity. Paradoxically, this fragmented subjectivity is delimited by postmodernists in terms exhibiting the modern syntax of: What can be

*[p. 167]* ^pg187

objectively contextualized, whether the locus be personal-psychological, cultural-linguistic, subcultural, textual, etc.; and what can be causally /
functionally described, for example in terms of the sexual drive, the politics and economics of power, or the grammar or structural/
”poststructural” characteristics of language. In this respect the skeptical aspect of postmodernism represents a kind of nihilistic development out of modern thought. However, as has been shown repeatedly, the skeptical view is incoherent and must be rejected.

Summing up, I suggest that the philosophical plausibility of the conception of the Lord's self-recognition we have been considering depends on two conditions: First, that we accept various contemporary analyses of the interpretive agency, imaginative character, linguisticality, and so on, integral to all experiences of the world as supporting the idealism of the prakāśa arguments. We never cognize a reality outside of our interpretations. Second, that we accept the vimarśa-type arguments that reality must be recognizable or intelligible. The skeptical form of idealism, whether propounded by Dharmakirti, Nāgārjuna, Bradley, Feyerabend, or Derrida, is self-refuting. Granting these points, it should still be reasonable to invoke God’s self-recognition as the source of whatever presence we achieve in the recognitions by which we live our lives. Even if this way of thinking is not entirely persuasive as a proof of the existence of God, I hope it may at least be seen as a way of making the idea of God coherent with our daily lives.”

### Divergences and Convergences: Monotheism and Panentheism

Having thus argued for a mode of thought in which the Pratyabhijñā converges with Christianity, I must stress that there are still substantial divergences. These are found in numerous areas—in religious myths including conceptions of theodicy and salvation, in ritual and ethical practices. We can only consider what I view as one of the most important divergences related to the argument that I have made. The Śaivas aim to lead us to complete participation in Siva’s self-recognition. Monotheistic orthodoxy on the contrary stresses the ultimate difference between God on the one hand, and the soul and the world on the other. I will close with a few suggestions about how I believe this divergence should be assessed.

First of all, despite what I have just said, it must be granted that there is a dialecticity in both traditions. There is, paradoxically, a dualistic moment in the Śaiva notion that the multiplistic experience

*[p. 168]* ^pg188

of the world and the individuals in it are real, as the emanation of Śiva.” Much of the Śaiva devotional literature in fact has a dualistic, supplicatory quality.® Likewise a moment of identity between God and the world is found in much of Christian theology, particularly when it makes use of features of Neoplatonic emanationism; this is seen also in the Thomist conception of the act of the divine intellect. The understanding of identity in difference found in both traditions

permits a degree of rapprochement.

Second, in the philosophy of logos/recognition that I have expounded, the moment of identity is supported precisely by the premise of idealism, the reduction of human experience and its objects to contents of the divine mind. We see the power of this monistic logic in Lonergan’s dialectically qualified formulations of the subject-object unity in the divine mind:

The secondary intelligibles are conditioned. For they are what is to be understood, if the primary intelligible is understood.

It follows that they are distinct from the primary intelligible,

for they are conditioned and it is unconditioned.

Still, though the secondary intelligibles are distinct from the

primary, they need not be distinct realities. For knowing does not consist in taking a look at something else and so, though the secondary intelligibles are known, they need not be something else to be looked at. Moreover, the primary being is without any lack of defect or imperfection; but it would be imperfect if further realities were needed for the unrestricted act of understanding to be restricted.“

If one grants some validity to the prakāśa arguments, the Thomist conception of the subject-object unity in the divine mind, and other such conceptions—one can establish difference only by limiting the degree of identification in the logic of esse est percipi. In this context, both traditions are faced with the paradox of unity and multiplicity raised by the Buddhist reductios introduced in chapter 4. Perhaps the matter can be resolved by such conceptions as the Aristotelian theory of the relation of act and potency. Anyway, though the Śaivas also need to explain difference as intrinsic to the existence of the world, this problem does not have for them the theological urgency that it does for Christianity.

Conversely, if by the idealistic premise it is God who is the source and inner reality of the recognitions that make up our lives, then it is easy to say that we are nothing but God and His self-recognition is

our self-recognition. The Pratyabhijñā apologetic objective of leading us to the Śaiva soteriological realization would follow naturally.

Third, I observe in favor of the Pratyabhijñā that a number of thinkers in the contemporary period have argued that the traditional Western understanding of God places too much emphasis on God’s transcendence of the world. It has been argued that this emphasis denies various modern valuations of this worldd—including the scientific and historical, the ethical or socially progressive, and the sexual. A number of thinkers have attempted to sacralize these values by developing a new conception of the deity stressing His immanence in the world as complementing His transcendence. Following the initiative of the process philosopher Charles Hartshorne, we may use Karl Christian von Friedrich Krause’s term “panentheism” to classify these doctrines. These include: Hegelianism; theological Heideggerian thought, for instance, of John Macquarrie;® the process thought of Whitehead, Hartshorne,“ Ogden, and others; the religious theories of evolution of Teilhard de Chardin and Sri Aurobindo Ghose; the phenomenological dialectic of the sacred as within and transcending history of Mircea Eliade; and the dialectic of manifestation and proclamation of Paul Ricoeur and David Tracy.

One of the ideas most emphasized by Abhinavagupta and much of Hindu tantrism is that God/Śiva-Śakti is both transcendent (viśvottīrna) and immanent (viśvamaya). Therefore I think that these systems may be placed within the class of panentheism. I also believe that a panentheistic approach to the problems of tradition and modernity has inspired many contemporary retrievals of Hindu tantrism, by both Indian scholars such as Gopinath Kaviraj and Western scholars such as Sir John Woodroffe. I also recommend the Pratyabhijñā as a valuable intellectual and spiritual resource for the development of

contemporary panentheism.

### Recognition and Retrieval

Abhinavagupta’s explanation of the soteriological recognition describes a kind of hermeneutic retrieval, in the sense of a very deep personal appropriation. It will be recalled from chapter 3 that he places the knowledge of God from scriptures in the counter of what is in memory and re-experienced. In our contemporary dialogical retrieval of the significance of the Śaiva understanding of recognition, the tradition-bearing aspect of memory may be taken to include analogous expressions of spirituality—such as the Christian logos theology, panentheism,

and so forth—as well as similar conceptions that may be found in

other religions.

To facilitate this retrieval I have endeavored to elucidate the orientations in the Western academy supporting and denying the claims of the Pratyabhijñāa. If the reader accepts at least some of my interpretation of its cogency, there will still be reasons for further arguments about a variety of philosophical and theological points, regarding the nature of God, theodicy, ethics, ritual, and so on.

The considerations adduced here as well as countless possible others regarding the acceptance of Śiva’s self-recognition as one’s own are extremely difficult to assess, and will be assessed in different ways by different people. Wrestling with this difficulty is both the most rational approach we can take and the one most respectful of the intentions of Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta.
