## Time and the Hindu Experience

*Anindita Niyogi Balslev* 

An outstanding characteristic of the conceptual world, which is the abode of Hindu religious thought and philosophy, of myths and symbols, is its vast time-consciousness. Bound by a few shared convictions regarding the nature of the self and the world, the Hindu mind plays with various alternatives with regard to time. While here one breathes an atmosphere where there is no pressure of achieving uniformity of views and dogmas, there are surely a few broad and general insights that are common and which form the backdrop to the competing paradigms for interpreting time in a widely divergent manner. [^1]

The richness and the enormity of the material available for exploring the many-layered meaning that the theme of time has in this complex and highly articulate tradition, calls for a gigantic scholarly enterprise. [^2]In this paper my objectives will be: a) to draw the attention of the reader to the wide range of views on time that has nourished this culture by referring to some of the texts whose authenticity has been unquestionably accepted in the tradition and b) examine some cliches about the Hindu time-experience in a cross-cultural and inter-religious context. I. THE IDEA OF ATMAN 

If the term 'self' is far from being obsolete in religious and philosophical literature across cultures, it must be emphasized that in the Hindu context the notion of *atman* is absolutely crucial,-hence the other name of the Brahmanical tradition is *atmavada.* How the self is to be conceived in contrast to the not-self has been a preoccupation for centuries for the Brahmanical thinkers. As one begins to delve into the depth of Hindu religious consciousness, one captures the centrality of the idea of Atman as that which in the midst of the flux of existence remains ever-identical, untouched by 'change'. <sup>4</sup>There are different approaches to the disclosure of the atman in the U pani~ads, such as in the Brhadaral).yaka which exposes it as *svqyaajyotih-the* light that invariably illuminates the fleeting states-or as in the Taittiriya which unsheathes the inner self through the idea of *ko,fa-where* embodiment is explained in terms of 'sheaths', which are external to the *atman.* In Hindu understanding of death and deathlessness, of time and timelessness, this notion of the unchanging and unchangeable self plays a role of capital importance. 

Disregarding for the moment all internal differences in the tradition concerning the idea of the self, it may be noted that there is no disagreement that in the last analysis, the self *(atman)* is seen as unsublatable *(abadhya),* as that about which it cannot be said-it was not, it is not or it will not be *(nirya).* The Bhagavad Gita 2.20 describes it in a nutshell5 thus:

II. ON KALA (TIME) 

Before going into the details of some aspects of the question of Hindu time-experience in the Indian as well as in an inter-cultural context, I will attempt briefly a general survey of some ideas that are recorded in some of the earliest sources. Concern with time, as a distinct theme, is clearly seen in so old a document as the Atharva Veda. III. THE IDEA OF ANADI-SAMSARA 

One of the basic tenets of Hindu thought is the notion that no absolute beginning can be attributed to the world-process. Whether in theological symbolism or in cosmological speculations, the Hindu mind strongly adheres to the view that 'being cannot come out of nothing' (cf. *Bhagavad-Gzta* 2/16, *Nasato vidyate bhavo* etc.). In many classical philosophical texts one comes across elaborate discussions about the difficulties and the absurdities that a transgression of this principle of ex nihilo nihil fit would entail. This is one of the major shared convictions that runs through the entire tradition. However, although the idea of absolute beginning i.e. creation out of nothing is rejected, the notion of repeated creation *(Punar-S'(,f(i)* and recurrent dissolution *(Punar-pralaya)* is widespread. This is a very ancient idea and it can be traced back to the *Rk-veda Sainhita* ( 10-190-3): The Lord created the sun and the moon like before ( *Surya-candra-asaudhata-yatha-purvam-akalpayat).* These oft-quoted words suggest that prior to creation there has been a state of dissolution. This idea of cosmological cycle is widely accepted in the tradition, so much so that the idea may well be considered to be a characteristic of the Hindu conceptual world. 

The question has been asked whether the *anadi* (beginningless) *sainsara* is also *ananta* (endless) or not. It is interesting, in this connection, to observe the intimate relation between Hindu cosmology and soteriology. There are examples of controversies amongst the different schools of Hindu thought regarding the question of dissolution *(pralaya).* Only those who accept the idea of universal salvation *(Maha-pralaya),* such as Advaita Vedanta, admit the possibility of a state of final dissolution. V. AVATARAVADA AND THE IDEA OF FOUR YUGAS *Avataravada* is a distinctively Hindu religious doctrine. It is widely accepted in the tradition. This is the idea of periodic divine descent in order to restore righteousness and vanquish the evil. In the *Bhagavad-Gzta,* a text revered by all denominations of the Hindus, Kri~r:ia says to Arjuna: 

Whenever there is a decline of righteousness and rise of unrighteousness, 0 Bharata (Arjuna), then I send forth (create, incarnate) Myself. For the protection of the good, for the destruction of the wicked and for the establishment of righteousness, I come into being from age to age. 

The *Purar;as* weave endless stories about Divine incarnations, assuming diverse earthly forms. The spectacular manner in which each *Avatara* accomplishes the task of crushing the evil and establishing the supremacy of goodness and righteousness form the basis of colourful legends that have fed the imagination of all stratas of Hindu society, such as the well-known stories of the ten divine *incarnations-Dafavatara.* 

In the *Bhagavad Purar;a* there are detailed renderings\_ of the various forms in which the Lord appears in each yuga. The idea of four yugas, it is interesting to observe, is very much a part of traditional Hindu ideology concerning *Dharma* or moral order of society. The epic *Mahabharata* contains rich and elaborate descriptions of the various characteristics of the four yugas viz. *krta, treta,* *dvapara* and *kali.* The moral order is said to be degenerating, the standard of human conduct is seen in a state of gradual deterioration. *Kali,* the *yuga* in which we are, is the last in succession of four yugas. It is precisely the time when unrighteousness is more a predominant feature than it has been in any of the three earlier yugas. The *Avatara* who is not yet incarnated is called *Kalki.* All these form a very important part of Hindu mythology which provide an excellent document of how the Hindu mind has imbued its vast time-consciousness with religious significance.

The Vaisqika view of *mahiikala* is a model of absolute time. Time is a distinctly independent *(svatantra)* category of reality. It is all-pervasive ( *vibhu),* ubiquitous *(niramfa),* unitary *(eka).* It is without beginning *(anadi)* and without end *(ananta). Moreover, time-divisions are not to be taken as integral to time, these are seen as nothing but conventional practices which are made possible with the help of any standard motion, usually it is the solar motion that furnishes the standard. A detailed study of the system shows that it is causality which is considered the key problem for understanding change and becoming. The dynamic *Prakrti* in Sankhya combines space, time, matter in the same principle. VII. TIME, SELF AND CONSCIOUSNESS 

A study of time in any intellectual tradition is inevitably related to philosophical reflections on the themes of consciousness and the self. For understanding the role that a specific view of time plays in diverse schemes of Hindu soteriology, one has to look carefully into how the view is interwoven with the notion of *atman,* a fundamental tenet of Hindu thought. The dominant pattern is the ultimate disclosure of *atman* as free from the influence of time *(Kalaprabhava-mukta).* Arduous spiritual practice is recommended for the attainment of this saving knowledge. The various and divergent views on time are all knitted to the one basic shared conviction, inspired by the exegetical texts of the U panisads that the self is 'unborn' and 'indestructible' *(ajara, amara)* . [^13]

The idea that the world is beginningless, or that the self is unborn are based on the same philosophical insight that being cannot come out of nothing and that the human situation is incomprehensible without the idea of *karma* and rebirth. 

The most familiar paradigm in theological traditions across cultures is perhaps where the self is conceived as transcending, overcoming time ( the idea comes in many versions, the model of the immortal soul is only one example). The theological concern with death is intimately related to it. Time stands for death, self for immortality. Various concepts and symbols, doctrines and mythologies show this. A specific time-concept, it becomes evident, is so central to a given system that it cannot be isolated from other major issues or be substituted by an alternative model without seriously disturbing or even demolishing the conceptual structure. An excellent example of this can be found in the polemics of the Brahmanical and Buddhist philosophers. The Brahmanical tradition, as we have seen, has

been a citadel of the idea of the immutable self. The drastic Buddhist understanding of universal flux, where the separation between time as moment and being as momentary was attributed only to an arbitrary linguistic convention, projected a world-view where nothing is exempt from change. The idea of the so-called permanent entity was shown to be fraught with logical contradictions. The implication of this view was obviously shattering for any notion of an identical and abiding self, as in this conceptual frame there can be no room for any notion of an unchanging self in the changing stream of consciousness. The Brahmanical philosophers were fully aware that the idea of *atman* could be reinstated only if the Buddhist interpretation of time (and being) were rejected. Centuries of exchanges bear witness to the massive philosophical disputes which stimulated the Indian mind. 'That which is, is momentary' *(Yat sat tat kwrJikam),* the central idea implied in *KimJabhangavada* becomes a challenge to the Atmavadins. The Buddhist replies and Brahmanical counter-questions form elaborate documents of controversies that are results of arduous philosophical exercise on the questions of time, self and consciousness. IX. TIME-METAPHORS AND ENCOUNTER OF 

WORLD-RELIGIONS 

One may well wonder whether in any other context the major time-metaphors have come to play such a decisive role as in the context of encounter of world-religions. Metaphors of cyclicity and linearity are used for the schematization of different cultural experiences of time, a practice which has thwarted inter-religious communication. 

It is commonplace to come across the appellations of 'cyclic' and 'linear' time in the literature, wherever some attempts have been made to situate the time-experience of major traditions in a global setting. These epithets are assumed to represent radically different time-experiences. It has become almost a cliche to say, for example, that the ludo-Hellenic time is cyclic as opposed to the Judeo-Christian understanding of linear time. In order that a productive discourse may ensue in the area of inter-religious and cross-cultural studies, it seems to be specially important to examine these general and broad distinctions that are made, which are repeated endlessly and hardly ever questioned.

X. SAMSARA-CAKRA: THE METAPHOR OF THE WHEEL One of the most profound metaphors of the Hindu (as well as the Buddhist[^25]) conceptual world is the *cakra* or the wheel. It pervades the literature and the arts, the life and culture in a variety of ways. Consider, for instance, the idea of *Sainsara-cakra* which is profusely used in Hindu religious and philosophical literature. One authentic example of the function of the symbol can be seen in the Yoga literature. The commentary by Vyasa on the *Yoga-Sutra* 4/11 describes the idea of *Sadadam sainsara-cakram* (six-spoked wheel of existence) by pointing to how from 'virtue arises happiness, from impiety pain, from happiness attachment, from pain aversion; attachment and aversion both lead to efforts, resulting in actions, which in turn again give rise to virtue or impiety, happiness or pain, attachment or aversion. Thus, it is said, revolves 'the six-spoked wheel of existence'. To the participants of this culture, the wheel captures the pole of experience which allows to arrive at a notion of a law, focusing on the recurrent generic pattern. The metaphor conveys a message which is to be deciphered in the specific context where it is used.
