### 1.1–17: The genealogy

*[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-1|Text]], p. 11* · The genealogy is unusual in citing women and non-Jews, and in alluding to morally questionable circumstances. ^1-1

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-1|1]]:** *Genealogy*, Gk “geneseōs,” perhaps an allusion to the book of Genesis. *Messiah,* from the Heb “anointed one”; Gk “Christos” (see 1.16–17; 3.14n.; 8.20n.; 11.14–15n.; 16.16; 17.12–13n.; 26.53–54; see also [[Daniel 09#^9-25|Dan 9.25]],[[LXX Daniel (Greek) 09#^9-26|26]] \[LXX\]; 11QMelch 2.9–13). This term is never used in Tanakh (the HB) of the future ideal Davidic king, though it is used that way in rabbinic literature (e.g., *b. Sukk*. [[Sukkah 52#52a|52a]] on “Messiah, the son of David”; see also 9.27; 12.23; 15.22; 17.15; 20.30; 21.9; cf. *T. Sol*. 20.1). *David*, traditional Jewish belief held that the Messiah would descend from King David ([[Isaiah 11#^11-1|Isa 11.1]]; [[Jeremiah 23#^23-5|Jer 23.5]]; *b. Sukk.* [[Sukkah 52#52a|52a]]; *b. Sanh.* [[Sanhedrin 97#97a|97a]]), perhaps based on the idea of an everlasting line of Davidic kings in [[II Samuel 07#^7-12|2 Sam 7.12–16]]. ^1-1-2

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-2|2]]:** *Abraham*, see 3.9, was regarded as the father of Judaism, as Jewish literature indicates (e.g., *b. Ned.* [[Nedarim 52#52a|52a]]; “Our father Abraham”). Luke (3.23–38) traces Jesus’ genealogy to Adam; from David to Jesus, the genealogies of Matthew and Luke are inconsistent. ^1-2

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-3|3]]:** *Tamar*, ancient sources variously consider her a Gentile, a proselyte to Judaism, or an Israelite (Gen 38; [[Ruth 4#^4-18|Ruth 4.18]]; [[I Chronicles 02#^2-4|1 Chr 2.4]]; *L. A. B.* 9.5). She conceives twins with her father-in-law Judah ([[Torah 09 - Vayeshev#^38-18|Gen 38.18]]; *T. Jud*. 12.8). ^1-3

> [!note] Genealogy ([[Matthew 01#^1-1|Mt 1.1–17]]) *(p. 11)*
> Genealogies appear throughout early Jewish, Greek and Roman literature (e.g., [[Torah 02 - Noach#^10-1|Gen 10.1–32]]; [[Torah 02 - Noach#^11-10|11.10–26]]; [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-12|25.12–18]]; [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^6-16|Ex 6.16–25]]; [[Ezra 07#^7-1|Ezra 7.1–5]]; [[I Chronicles 01#^1-1|1 Chr 1.1–9]]; [[LXX Tobit 01#^1-1|Tob 1.1]]; [[LXX Judith 08#^8-1|Jdt 8.1]]; [[LXX Baruch 01#^1-1|Bar 1.1]]; 2 Esd 1.1–3; Quintilian, *Inst*. 3.7.10; the *Aeneid* 7.1–4). They served to praise heroes and heroines by glorifying their past as well as adding legitimacy to their narrative. The gene$alogy in [[Matthew 01#^1-1|Mt 1.1–17]] (cf. [[Luke 03#^3-23|Lk 3.23–38]]) locates Jesus as the heir of Abraham and David and the epitome of Israel’s history. Matthew’s opening words (Gk *biblos geneseos*, “account of the genealogy,” convey a textual link to the creation narrative, [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^2-4|Gen 2.4]], as well as the account of the descendants of Adam in [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^5-1|Gen 5.1]].
>
> Matthew traces Jesus’ descent from the patriarch Abraham (see [[Luke 03#^3-23|Lk 3.23–28]]), a symbol of Jewish identity and piety (3.9; 8.11–12; [[LXX 4 Maccabees 06#^6-17|4 Macc 6.17]],[[LXX 4 Maccabees 06#^6-22|22]]; *t. Ned*. 2.5; *Pesiq. Rab*. 11.4). Yet Abraham is also the first “convert” in that he turned from idol worship to heed God’s call ([[Torah 03 - Lech Lecha#^12-1|Gen 12.1–4]]). Thus he represents both Jewish and Gentile fidelity to the God of Israel.
>
> Matthew’s genealogy also depicts four women whose symbolic value is complex. First, several of the women have Gentile connections. Tamar (Gen 38) may be an Aramean or Canaanite, although she may also be within the family of Israel; one rabbinic legend claims she was an orphan who converted in order to marry into Judah’s family (*b. Sot*. [[Sotah 10#10a|10a]]). Rahab (Judg 2–6) is a Canaanite; Ruth is a Moabite; and “the wife of Uriah,” Bathsheba, is married to a Hittite (2 Sam 11). Therefore, the women, like Abraham, may suggest the unity of Jews and Gentiles among the followers of Jesus. The unexpected sexual relationships of the women — Tamar has relations with her father-in-law; Rahab is a prostitute; Ruth, who has Moabite ancestry (Gen 19), places Boaz in a potentially compromising position (Ruth 3); Bathsheba commits adultery with David — are not indications of sinful behavior. To the contrary, Jewish tradition praises these women (*L. A. B.* 3.14; *Avot de R. Natan* 45 B; *Pesiq. Rab*. 40.3–4). In addition, they anticipate the surprising conception of Jesus as well as Joseph’s initial reluctance to marry his pregnant fiancée.
>
> Matthew’s genealogy may also contain an example of gematria, a literary technique that recognizes that Hebrew letters have numerical equivalents. The genealogy centers upon the number 14, the sum of the Hebrew consonants contained in the name “David” (*dalet* = 4, *vav* = 6, *dalet* = 4). Fourteen is also 2x7, where seven, the number of days in the week, may symbolize completeness. To keep the generational pattern intact at fourteen, Matthew is forced to omit five kings: Aha-ziah, Joash, Amaziah, Jehoahaz, and Jehoiakim. In addition, the third and final set contains thirteen names, which may suggest that the fourteenth and final generation is that of the church.

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-5|5]]:** *Rahab*, the Canaanite prostitute faithful to Joshua’s spies; she and her family are spared from Jericho’s destruction ([[Joshua 06#^6-25|Josh 6.25]]; *Ruth Rab.* on 1.1). *B. Meg*. 14b lists her as the wife of Joshua. *Ruth*, a Moabite woman regarded as a proselyte in postbiblical texts (*Gen. Rab.* 59.9; *b. Sukk.* [[Sukkah 49#49b|49b]]; see [[Ruth 1#^1-16|Ruth 1.16–18]]). ^1-5

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-6|6]]:** *Wife of Uriah*, Bathsheba, who committed adultery with David ([[II Samuel 11#^11-2|2 Sam 11.2–12.24]]; [[I Kings 15#^15-5|1 Kings 15.5]]; *b. Shabb*. [[Shabbat 56#56a|56a]]; *b. Qidd*. [[Kiddushin 43#43a|43a]]). ^1-6

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-7|7]]:** *Abijah . . . Asaph*, according to [[I Chronicles 03#^3-10|1 Chr 3.10]], Abijah’s son was Asa, not Asaph. ^1-7

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-10|10]]:** *Amos*, the name evokes the biblical prophet; the son of *Manasseh* was Amon ([[II Kings 19#^19-18|2 Kings 19.18]]; [[I Chronicles 03#^3-14|1 Chr 3.14]]). ^1-10

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-11|11]]:** *Deportation to Babylon*, in 597, a decade before the destruction of the First Temple and exile in 586 BCE ([[II Kings 24#^24-10|2 Kings 24.10–17]]). For *y. Ber.* 2.4, the destruction of the Second Temple (70 CE) is the beginning of the period awaiting the Messiah. ^1-11

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-12|12]]:** *Zerubbabel*, Davidic descendant considered the builder of the Second Temple, and a messianic figure according to some biblical sources ([[Haggai 2#^2-23|Hag 2.23]]; [[Zechariah 04#^4-6|Zech 4.6–10]]). Only the names Jechoniah, Salathiel, and Zerubbabel, in the third group, are mentioned in the Tanakh. ^1-12

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-16|16]]:** *Jacob the father of Joseph*, see [[Torah 08 - Vayishlach#^35-24|Gen 35.24]]; Matthew evokes Israel’s ancestors. *Joseph . . . of whom Jesus was born,* although not Jesus’ biological father, Joseph is his legal parent. ^1-16

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-17|17]]:** *Fourteen*, see “Genealogy,” p. 11. *From the deportation*, Matthew lists only thirteen generations for the last set. ^1-17

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-18|1.18–25]]:** **The nativity** ([[Luke 01#^1-26|Luke 1.26–2.40]]). ^1-18

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-18|18]]:** *Before they lived together*, *m. Ketub*.1.2; 5.2 note the betrothal period prior to the marriage. *B. Yebam*. 62b suggests betrothal took place around the age of twelve (see *m. Ned.* 10.5; *Gen. Rab.* 95), although the rabbinic ideal may not reflect first-century practice (see “Jewish Family Life,” p. 604). The betrothal was formalized with a marriage contract (Heb “ketubah”) ([[LXX Tobit 07#^7-14|Tob 7.14]]; *m. Ketub.* 5.2; *m. Ned.* 10.5). *Holy Spirit*, God’s creative and enduring presence ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-2|Gen 1.2]]; [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^2-7|2.7]]; [[Torah 02 - Noach#^8-1|8.1]]; [[Torah 16 - Beshalach#^13-21|Ex 13.21–22]]; [[Joshua 02#^2-16|Josh 2.16]]; [[Psalms 051#^51-13|Ps 51.11]]; *b. Hag.* [[Chagigah 12#12a|12a]],b). ^1-18-2

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-19|19]]:** *Righteous*\[ness\], a Matthean theme (3.15; 5.6,10,20; 6.33; 9.13; 10.41; 21.32; 25.37,46; cf. [[Luke 01#^1-6|Lk 1.6]]). Righteousness was linked to justice, ethics, and Torah observance ([[Torah 02 - Noach#^6-9|Gen 6.9]]; [[Torah 29 - Achrei Mot#^18-5|Lev 18.5]]; [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^6-25|Deut 6.25]]; [[Psalms 085#^85-12|Ps 85.11]]). *Dismiss*, divorce; see e.g., *m. Ned*. 11.12; *b. Git*. [[Gittin 9#9a|9a]]-b; 32a-b; 62b; 64a. ^1-19

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-20|20]]:** *Angel*, angels often mediate prophecies in apocalyptic literature of this period (see “Supernatural Beings,” p. 682). *Dream*, evoking the Joseph of Genesis and Amram, the father of Moses (Josephus, *Ant*. 2.210–17). ^1-20

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-21|21]]:** *Jesus*, Heb “Yehoshua,” a common name in the first century, meaning “God saves.” Philo notes that “Moses also changes the name of Hosea into that of Joshua; displaying by his new name the distinctive qualities of his character . . . the name of the most excellent possible character” (*Names* 121). Name changes were generally reserved for important biblical characters, including Abraham (from Abram; [[Torah 03 - Lech Lecha#^17-5|Gen 17.5]]), Sarah (from Sarai; [[Torah 03 - Lech Lecha#^17-15|Gen 17.15]]), and Israel (from Jacob; [[Torah 08 - Vayishlach#^32-29|Gen 32.28]]). The Tanakh records four men named “Joshua” ([[Torah 37 - Sh'lach#^13-16|Num 13.16]]; [[I Samuel 06#^6-4|1 Sam 6.4]]; [[II Kings 23#^23-8|2 Kings 23.8]]; [[Haggai 1#^1-1|Hag 1.1]]). Jesus’ mission is to save people from their sins (9.1–8; 20.28; 26.28). Jews traditionally saw salvation as a part of the covenant ([[Psalms 130#^130-8|Ps 130.8]]; [[II Chronicles 07#^7-14|2 Chr 7.14]]; *m. Sanh* 10.1, “All Israel has a share in the world to come”), and understood continuing divine presence to be part of the ideal future (e.g., [[Ezekiel 48#^48-35|Ezek 48.35]]). ^1-21

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-23|23]]:** *Virgin*, Matthew quotes the Greek translation (LXX) of [[Isaiah 07#^7-14|Isa 7.14]], which reads “parthenos” (see “Scripture Fulfillment,” p. 99). Often, Matthew claims that Jesus fulfills Hebrew prophecy. These sayings, labeled “fulfillment citations,” are designed to prove that Jesus is the culmination of Israel’s Scripture. *Emmanuel*, lit., “God with us,” frames the Gospel (28.20). ^1-23

**[[NRSVue Matthew 01#^1-25|25]]:** *No marital relations*, the phrase does not preclude their having relations after Jesus’ birth. The view of Mary’s perpetual virginity develops in the second century, where it is first alluded to in the *Protevangelium of James* (see 9.2; the brothers of Jesus are explained as sons of Joseph from another wife); see “Mary in Jewish Tradition,” p. 744. ^1-25
