**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-11|11]]:** *Left everything*, including families (5.28; 8.21; 9.61; 18.29 and cf. [[I Kings 19#^19-20|1 Kings 19.20]]). ^5-11

> [!note] Pharisees in Luke (Lk 5.17–39) *(p. 122)*
> The Gospel’s presentation of Pharisees is puzzling, inconsistent, and complex. Positively, Luke mentions no Pharisees in the Passion Narrative, and does mention Pharisees as members of the Christian community ([[Acts 15#^15-5|Acts 15.5]]). Yet the first words attributed to Pharisees—“Who is this who is speaking blasphemies” ([[Luke 05#^5-21|Lk 5.21]])—show them at best misunderstanding Jesus. They next appear grumbling at Jesus’ disciples, where they accuse Jesus of inappropriate table fellowship (5.30; a point repeated in 15.2) and question the disciples for their failure to fast (5.33). By 6.1–5 “some” Pharisees are accosting the disciples for violating the Sabbath, and in 6.6–11, Pharisees seek to accuse Jesus directly of Sabbath violation. [[Luke 07#^7-29|Luke 7.29–30]] depicts the Pharisees as rejecting John’s baptism, which for the Gospel means rejecting the plan of God. The story continues in 7.36–50, which, together with 11.37–54; 14.1–24, depict Pharisees hosting Jesus at table. In each case, Jesus verbally attacks his hosts, for example, by the accusation that they “neglect justice and the love of God” (11.42). Teaching his disciples, but within the hearing of several thousand listeners, Jesus advises, “Beware of the yeast of the Pharisees, that is, their hypocrisy” (12.1).
>
> In 16.14–15, Luke has added that Pharisees are self-righteous (a point potentially reinforced by 18.9–14) and “lovers of money.” Conversely, Josephus, who is himself no great admirer of Pharisees—admits that the Pharisees “simplify their standard of living, making no concession to luxury” (*Ant*. 18.12), “while the Sadducees are able to persuade none but the rich, and have not the populace obsequious to them, but the Pharisees have the multitude of their side” (*Ant*. 13.298). Given these and other excoriations, potentially neutral or even friendly actions of Pharisees take on more ominous hues. When “some Pharisees” warn Jesus that Herod seeks to kill him (13.31), they can be seen as attempting to turn Jesus from his mission. When a Pharisee inquires about the timing of the kingdom of God (17.20–21), he could be seen as misunderstanding Jesus’ program. In their last appearance (19.37–40), “some” Pharisees exhort Jesus to rebuke his disciples for hailing him as a king. They can be seen, positively, as fearing that the acclamation would prompt Roman reprisal; they can equally be seen as rejecting the disciples’ claims.

### 5.12–16: Healing the man with leprosy

*[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-12|Text]], p. 123* · ([[Matthew 08#^8-1|Mt 8.1–4]]; [[Mark 01#^1-40|Mk 1.40–45]]). See Lev 13–14; [[Torah 36 - Beha'alotcha#^12-9|Num 12.9–15]]; [[II Kings 05#^5-1|2 Kings 5.1–14]]; [[Luke 04#^4-27|Lk 4.27]]; [[Luke 17#^17-11|17.11–19]]; Josephus, *Ant*. 3.264. ^5-12

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-12|12]]:** *Leprosy*, generic skin ailment, not Hansen’s disease. *Make me clean*, cured, thus ritually pure. ^5-12-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-13|13]]:** *Touched*, Josephus, *Ag. Ap*. 1.281, suggests that one who touches a person suffering from leprosy would be “esteemed unclean”; this is implied, but not stated in biblical texts, and explicit in rabbinic literature (*m. Zabim* 5.6). But no law forbids such contact; ritual impurity is a normal part of life. By curing the man, Jesus is reinforcing purity laws rather than abrogating them: there will be no impurity in his kingdom. **14**: *Tell no one*, this “messianic secret” is more prevalent in Mark’s Gospel. Suggestions for its rationale include emphasizing teaching over miracles; avoiding Antipas’s attention (8.56; see also 7.22; 22.70; 23.3); an explanation for why most Jews did not follow Jesus. *Show yourself to the priest*, the priest needs to pronounce him cleansed (Lev 13). *Moses commanded*, see [[Torah 28 - Metzora#^14-1|Lev 14.1–57]]. ^5-13

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-16|16]]:** *Pray*, see 3.21n. ^5-16

### 5.17–26: Healing the paralyzed man

*[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-17|Text]], p. 123* · ([[Matthew 09#^9-1|Mt 9.1–8]]; [[Mark 02#^2-1|Mk 2.1–12]]). ^5-17

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-17|17]]:** *Pharisees*, see, “Jewish Movements of the NT Period,” p. 614. *Teachers of the law* ([[Acts 05#^5-34|Acts 5.34]]; [[1 Timothy 01#^1-7|1 Tim 1.7]]), a Christian rather than Jewish term. *Power of the Lord*, see [[Torah 37 - Sh'lach#^14-7|Num 14.7]]; [[II Kings 03#^3-15|2 Kings 3.15]]; see also Judg 14; such power may not always be present, and it is manifest in various ways, particularly unusual action. ^5-17-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-19|19]]:** *Tiles*, Mark describes a mud-plaster roof; Luke gives the home an upgrade. ^5-19

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-20|20]]:** *Sins are forgiven*, the comment implies a connection between infirmity and sin ([[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^34-7|Ex 34.7]]; [[John 09#^9-2|Jn 9.2]]). Jews also recognized illness as caused by demons (see 4.39n.), as tests of the righteous (especially Job), and as part of life. ^5-20

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-21|21]]:** *Scribes*, Heb “soferim.” *Blasphemies*, capital offense ([[Torah 31 - Emor#^24-14|Lev 24.14–16]]), although Jesus does not here blaspheme by using the divine name. Had he blasphemed, his opponents would do more than question *in \[their\] hearts* (v 22). In 4QPrNab, a Jewish exorcist forgives the sins of an ailing man. ^5-21

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-24|24]]:** *Son of Man*, Jesus’ self-designation, variously indicating a mortal ([[Ezekiel 02#^2-1|Ezek 2.1]]; [[Psalms 008#^8-5|Ps 8.4]]) or an apocalyptic redeemer ([[Daniel 07#^7-13|Dan 7.13–14]]; *1 En*. 71). ^5-24

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-25|25]]:** *Glorifying God*, or giving glory (praise) to God; see18.43. Luke does not directly equate Jesus with “God,” but readers could make the connection (see 4.8n.). diseases b Gk *he* c Gk *him* d Other ancient authorities read *was present to heal them* e Gk *into the midst* f Gk *Man* ^5-25

### 5.29–39: Table fellowship

*[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-29|Text]], p. 124* · ([[Matthew 09#^9-14|Mt 9.14–17]]; [[Mark 02#^2-18|Mk 2.18–22]]). See “Food and Table Fellowship,” p. 650. ^5-29

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-29|29]]:** *Banquet*, settings for instruction (7.36; 10.38; 11.37; 14.1,7,12,15) and suggesting formal Greek banquets known as symposiums. ^5-29-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-33|33]]:** *Fast*, here, for spiritual discipline; Tacitus (*Hist*. 5.4) suggests fasting is a distinctly Jewish activity. ^5-33

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-34|34–35]]:** *Bridegroom*, Jesus’ frequent (self-)designation ([[John 03#^3-29|Jn 3.29]]); see [[Isaiah 62#^62-5|Isa 62.5]]; the term connotes creation of new families, celebration, and precedence over other obligations ([[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^24-5|Deut 24.5]]). ^5-34

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-35|35]]:** *Those days*, after Jesus departs. ^5-35

**[[NRSVue Luke 05#^5-36|36]]:** *Parable*, see “Midrash and Parables,” p. 707. ^5-36
