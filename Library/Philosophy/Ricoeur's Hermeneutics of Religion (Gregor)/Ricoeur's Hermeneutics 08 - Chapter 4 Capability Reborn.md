## Chapter 4

## Capability Reborn

*The Hermeneutics of Religion*

One of the pivotal autobiographical sections of Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn’s monumental work *The Gulag Archipelago* bears the evocative title “The Ascent.” In that section Solzhenitsyn makes his oft-cited observation that “the line separating good and evil passes not through states, nor between political parties either—but right through every human heart.” This is not to deny that states and parties do commit evil, but instead to make the point that we cannot root out evil by dualistically separating the heroes and the villains—in other words, *us* versus *them*. In terms of Kant and Ricoeur, radical evil afflicts the self at a more fundamental level. Solzhenitsyn argues that this struggle takes place within all human beings: “Even within hearts overwhelmed by evil, one small bridgehead of good is retained. And even in the best of all hearts, there remains . . . an unuprooted small corner of evil.” And yet it is possible to work against this radical evil. Solzhenitsyn makes a point very similar to both Kant and Ricoeur: “I have come to understand the truth of all the religions of the world: They struggle with the *evil inside a human being* (inside every human being). It is impossible to expel evil from the world in its entirety, but it is possible to constrict it within each person.”<sup>1</sup> For Ricoeur, this constriction is part of the liberation of the will that is bound by radical evil. On his reading, the religious aims to recover contact with an originary goodness that lies at the active core of the self and has not been entirely eliminated by evil. This liberation and regeneration is the topic of the present chapter.

In chapter 3 we examined the development of Ricoeur’s poetics. This chapter will show how his hermeneutics of religion continues the abandoned project of the *Poetics of the Will*. Ricoeur never assembles a full treatment of his hermeneutics of religion, and his work on the topic is scattered over a host of articles. There is, however, a direct continuity from Ricoeur’s early 

*[p. 80]* ^pg97

sketches on transcendence and his more mature hermeneutics of religion. The common theme is the reconstitution, or rebirth, of the *I can*.

Just as we need a hermeneutics of symbols for understanding evil, so we need a hermeneutics of symbols to understand how religion regenerates human capability. As Ricoeur writes: “Religion brings about this regeneration by specific symbolic means that reawaken fundamental moral capacities . . .” Religion provides “a symbolic order” that is vital to the liberation of the will bound by what Kant calls radical evil. This order of religious symbolism is vital to the rebirth of capacities afflicted by the incapacity “classically designated as fault, sin, moral evil” (PA 276).

For Ricoeur, religious discourse is a special instance of poetic language. By figuring the “new being” as it does, religious discourse is ontologically transformative. According to Ricoeur, “all religions are different attempts in different language games to recover the ground of goodness, to liberate, so to say, the enslaved freedom, the enslaved capability.”<sup>2</sup> Ricoeur appeals to a created goodness that is “rooted in the ontological structure of the human being” and has not been entirely erased by sin.<sup>3</sup> Religious discourse aims to recover this originary goodness,<sup>4</sup> thereby regenerating and liberating the bound will through the power of poetic discourse. 

Ricoeur’s philosophy of religion continued to develop through the 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s. He incorporated the work of new philosophers and theologians as he continued to read, but the thinker who provides the programmatic statement for Ricoeur’s mature philosophy of religion is Kant. Ricoeur differs from Kant on numerous points, but the basic structure of Kant’s philosophy of religion—the liberation of the will bound by radical evil—is the source for Ricoeur’s own interpretation of religion as the restoration of human capability. Ricoeur is not, however, strictly or narrowly Kantian. As we will see, his reading of Kant is also mediated by other thinkers, such as Spinoza and Heidegger, and especially Hegel, such that Ricoeur describes himself as a “post-Hegelian Kantian.” Thus our journey in this chapter will follow the itinerary of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, taking us from Kant to Hegel, and back again.

### Kant on the Regeneration of the Will

As we saw in chapter 2, while Kant does not want to reify radical evil, he also maintains that it cannot simply be rooted out or eliminated. It can, however, be “overcome” (RBMR 83). This overcoming is possible by restoring the original predisposition to good. This means recovering “the *purity* of the law, as the supreme ground of all our maxims.” The power of choice (*Willkür*) must incorporate the moral law in its pure form, unadulterated by other 

*[p. 81]* ^pg98

incentives, so that the *Willkür* is powered solely by the pure moral law as its “self-*sufficient* incentive” (RBMR 91). This also means regaining contact with one’s original created goodness. “The human being is created good,” Kant writes, “created for the *good* and the original *predisposition* in him is good; the human being is not thereby good as such, but he brings it about that he becomes either good or evil, according as he either incorporates or does not incorporate into his maxims the incentives contained in that predisposition” (RBMR 89). 

How does this incorporation of pure moral incentives work? From the perspective of Christian theology it requires divine grace, but for Kant, grace cannot be a proper object of theoretical reason, which operates according to categories of cause and effect (RBMR 96–97). As Ricoeur puts it, Kant is trying to identify the condition of possibility for regeneration, “without alienating freedom either to a magical conception of grace and salvation or to an authoritarian organization of the religious community” (FS 215). It is hard to tell just how polemical Ricoeur is being with this remark. Is he describing a distorted view of grace—superstition, *schwärmerei*—or is his critique broader? As Ricoeur’s philosophy of religion becomes more Kantian, he becomes more reticent than in earlier work to identify the status of grace and its role in regeneration. To be sure, both Kant and Ricoeur leave open the possibility of grace or supernatural assistance. Kant, however, stipulates that “the human being must nonetheless make himself antecedently worthy of receiving it” (RBMR 89). He concedes that it is unclear how an evil tree can bear good fruit, but ought still implies can: “The command that we *ought* to become better human beings still resounds unabated in our souls; consequently, we must also be capable of it . . .” The most we can do is “make ourselves receptive to a higher assistance inscrutable to us” (RBMR 90). Kant is at pains to rule out any appeal to cheap grace. If (and only if) we do what is in our own power, making use of our “original predisposition to the good in order to become a better human being,” then (and only then) can we hope for the cooperation of grace from above (RBMR 95).

How does the self become worthy of grace? Kant maintains that gradual habituation or reformation is not enough. A genuine change of heart requires a revolution in the mode of thought—*metanoia—*so that the inner ground of our maxims comes into conformity with the moral law (RBMR 95). In biblical terms, one becomes a new man, a rebirth, a new creation. In a “single and unalterable decision” the revolution occurs, so that the subject becomes receptive to the good. This revolution sets in motion an “incessant laboring and becoming,” a striving toward the good. But one’s actual moral goodness remains hidden from view, so only God can truly assess the “intelligible ground of the heart.” Phenomenally, we can assess our relative progress “from bad to better,” but our noumenal righteousness is hidden and known 

*[p. 82]* ^pg99

only to God—for us, an object of hope rather than knowledge (RBMR 92). This phenomenal/noumenal dialectic is Kant’s approximation of the Lutheran dialectic, *simul justus et peccator*. The difference, of course, is that Kant rejects the radicality of Luther’s doctrine of justification by faith, which holds that it is precisely as sinners that we are justified, and not insofar as we are making moral progress. That radical idea of grace is a scandal to reason and likewise a scandal to Kant’s rational religion.

The majority of Kant’s *Religion* explores how the subject regains contact with the original predisposition to the good. Religious representation, the topic of Part Two, is vital to this transformation. As Ricoeur explains: “We have no direct access to this existential conflict” between the good and bad principles. Instead, we need the indirect representation of dramaturgy—a “quasi-theatrical” representation of cosmic conflict, superhuman powers doing battle in the human heart (FS 82–83). This is Kant’s philosophical approximation of apocalyptic literature and the biblical imagery of spiritual warfare, such as Ephesians 6. The evil principle is represented as an “invisible enemy,” an evil spirit. The good principle is personified in the ideal of moral perfection, which is Kant’s approximation of Christology. The ideal is a prototype presented by reason, and it gives power to our moral transformation (RBMR 101, 103–4). We receive power from the prototype by seeing how he performs all duties, spreads the good in word and deed, overcomes all temptations, suffers, and dies a shameful death for all the world, including his enemies. The representation also enables the human to “hope to become pleasing to God,” and “to *believe* and self-assuredly trust that he, under similar temptations and afflictions . . . would steadfastly cling to the prototype of humanity and follow this prototype’s example in loyal emulation” (RBMR 104–5).<sup>5</sup>

Kant’s Christology seems like blunt exemplarism, but in Part Three he also offers a philosophical interpretation of justification by faith. How can the human being hope for moral regeneration, given his past transgressions and the fact that “he still stands in the power of the evil principle and can find no capacity in him sufficient to improve things in the future?” (RBMR 148). Kant tries to approximate the Lutheran teaching on vicarious satisfaction and extrinsic righteousness, in which the self trusts in the alien righteousness of Christ, yet Kant is also at pains to show that this righteousness is not received passively, but depends on our prior activity as a condition of grace. There can be no cheap grace in rational moral religion (RBMR 147–49). The real issue for Kant is not God’s justification of the sinner, but the liberation of the will from evil for the good.<sup>6</sup> Hence Kant’s later claim that true religion does not concern what God has done to save us, but rather how we become worthy of salvation (RBMR 160). Likewise, the closing words of *Religion*: “The right 

*[p. 83]* ^pg100

way to advance is not from grace to virtue but rather from virtue to grace” (RBMR 215). We can deduce no rational system that guarantees a fit between virtue and grace, moral worth and happiness. Instead, the proper posture is one of hope. This hope is paradoxical, Ricoeur notes, since it involves “our effort and the externality of the gift,” but this gift can also serve “as an injunction to mobilize the most repressed resources of the disposition toward good” (FS 86–87).

Finally, in Part Three of Kant’s *Religion*, he offers a philosophical ecclesiology. Pure moral religion requires an ethical community, a “people of God” (RBMR 135), where belief takes practical and public form (FS 88). Political community is insufficient here. Although political institutions can establish and maintain public peace, this is not the same as genuine virtue. For that, we need religious institutions that employ public means to regenerate the will (FS 89).

In sum, the overarching concern of Kant’s hermeneutics of religion is twofold: (1) the liberation of the will bound by radical evil, and (2) the initiation of hope. These ideas are programmatic for Ricoeur, who agrees with Kant that radical evil binds capability but does not eliminate the human capacity for goodness altogether. Like Kant, Ricoeur believes there is an original, created goodness “rooted in the ontological structure of the human being,” but that this is bound by radical evil—what Ricoeur calls fault. The purpose of religion is to recover contact with this originary goodness and set the will free.<sup>7</sup> Religion offers a unique efficacy—at the level of the symbol, of belief, and of community—to reorient the power of choice.

### How Kantian is Ricoeur’s Hermeneutics of Religion?

Since Ricoeur often cites Kant’s *Religion* as his inspiration, just how Kantian is his hermeneutics of religion? Like Kant, Ricoeur insists on the disciplinary boundary between philosophy and theology. One of the persistent questions for such a boundary is whether it can be consistently maintained. Despite Kant’s assurance that his rational religion will not encroach on biblical theology, it is clear from the details that he sees it as normative for any faith that is rationally and morally acceptable. The pure religion of reason provides an *a priori* basis to interpret all positive faiths and their scriptures. This is a crucial feature of Kant’s hermeneutics of religion: Universal moral reason provides the measure for interpreting the phenomena of faith. 

For example, the pure religion of reason provides the interpretive measure for scripture. Consider Kant’s interpretation of [[2 Timothy 03#^3-16|2 Timothy 3:16]]: All scripture 

*[p. 84]* ^pg101

is inspired by God and profitable for the “moral improvement of human beings” (RBMR 143). According to Kant, this is how “rational and thoughtful teachers” in all the world’s faiths have interpreted their scriptures, bringing their “essential content” into “agreement with the universal principles of moral faith.” This is also how the ancient philosophers interpreted Homer, Hesiod, and other poets—learning to interpret their crude elements symbolically. Kant finds similar moral interpretation in Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Hinduism. For instance, faced with the violence and vindictiveness of the imprecatory Psalms,<sup>8</sup> he suggests we read the prayer for revenge, first, in terms of spiritual struggle against invisible evil powers rather than flesh and blood, or against one’s own evil inclinations, and second, as a warning against private vindictiveness (since vengeance belongs to the Lord, not us). Likewise, in the New Testament, the symbol of the kingdom of Heaven and the cosmic conflict of the Apocalypse can stimulate hope and courage (RBMR 161, 163), and the biblical narratives of Christ’s resurrection and ascension can signify new life and one’s entrance into “the society of all the good.”<sup>9</sup>

Kant’s philosophical interpretation is very much a project of Enlightenment rationalism. I call his philosophical interpretation of religious phenomena a *proto*-hermeneutics because its interpretations are guided by rationalism rather than the more thoroughgoing philosophical hermeneutics of Heidegger, Gadamer, and Ricoeur. This is evident in the way Kant views the historical content of positive religion—that is, existing faith traditions. In his approach, the historical is the vehicle by which pure religion advances, but it is ultimately “indifferent” and dispensable (RBMR 143, 146). Kant grants special status to Christianity, since he sees its true purpose as “none other than the introduction of a pure religious faith” (RBMR 159),<sup>10</sup> but this too is historically accidental rather than essential. That is why Kant can assert that “every human being” is capable of knowing with full certainty, “without the slightest scriptural learning,” what we must do to be well-pleasing to God (RBMR 160).

Ricoeur is critical of Kant’s rationalism, arguing that Kant underestimates the alterity of the “Christ” prototype, and fails to recognize that this alterity is essential to its efficacy (FS 87). For Kant, the idea of the prototype is “revealed” insofar as we cannot account for its origins nor for human receptivity to it. This revelation is not, however, empirical, contingent, or historical. Instead, it arises out of moral reason<sup>11</sup> and is therefore “permanently taking place within all human beings” (RBMR 152).

External experience is never adequate to the inner rational purity of the idea, but we nevertheless need an external example because of our unbelief. Human reason can only think moral goodness when it is portrayed in human form; it always needs a “*schematism of analogy.*” Analogical figures help to 

*[p. 85]* ^pg102

elucidate ideas by representing the supersensible according to a schematism of the sensible. In other words, analogy gives an example corresponding to an idea. Kant insists that analogy must not be taken as expanding our knowledge of the supersensible, determining the thing in itself as a sensible object. That, after all, is the way to anthropomorphism and other illusions (RBMR 107n).

We might wonder why morality needs religious representations or symbols at all. As Ricoeur observes, practical reason could make do with its own depictions of heroes of duty. What does religion add to this? On Ricoeur’s reading of Kant, religious symbolism designates “the mediation between the confession of radical evil and the confidence in the triumph of the good principle.” In other words, the religious symbol does not merely give us an example of how to fulfill our moral duty regardless of the outcome; it gives us the means to hope for the ultimate triumph of the highest good, in which goodness and happiness coincide. In this regard, the schematism of analogy is a schematism of *hope* (FS 85).

Despite this schematism of hope and the crucial role of imagination in Kant’s critical philosophy, Ricoeur objects that Kant lacks a philosophy of religious imagination, which would help to make sense of the efficacy of the schematism (FS 87–88): “The influence of this archetype on our will seems to require that the accent be placed not just on the side of representation but also on that of this schematism” (FS 88). How then does religious representation become effective, in Kant’s view? After all, as Ricoeur observes: “Representation has to clothe itself with power” (PA 284, FS 85–86). Kant’s answer is that representation receives its efficacy from *belief*. Kant’s appeal to belief at this point is a rather flat, one-sided account of the possibility of regeneration. The subject’s own belief is what is transformative. Ricoeur sees this as a key shortcoming in Kant’s hermeneutics of religion, which recognizes the importance of religious representation but fails to grasp the transformative power of the symbols themselves. In Ricoeur’s hermeneutics, the liberation of the will involves an intermingling of “the alterity of outside aid and the ipseity of an admission” (PA 284). This shortcoming in Kant, Ricoeur suggests, is due to his lack of a philosophy of the religious imagination. A fuller account of religious imagination would illuminate the productive power of religious *poiesis*, that is, the way the image can *give* a creative, transformative power to belief. Granted, Kant’s account of the productive imagination is groundbreaking, but he “had no idea of a language which would not be *empirical*,” which is why “he had to replace metaphysics by *empty* concepts” (BH 142).<sup>12</sup> This is where the poetics of religious discourse is so important to the philosophy of religion, describing via symbols, metaphors, myths, parables, and narratives not what the Unconditioned *is*, but what it is *like* (BH 143).

### Testimony

By neglecting this aspect of religious discourse, Kant falls prey to the illusion of a pure rational religion. As Ricoeur argues, there is no pure “Religion,” only a plurality of concrete existing religions, just as there is no such thing as “Language,” only concrete existing languages (ELRD 130). Part of this concreteness is the contingency of historical actuality. The challenge for philosophical reflection is to understand how the Absolute can manifest itself in historical events and experiential moments—words, works, actions, and lives—that transcend history and experience (H 119), for example, the exodus of Israel from Egypt, and likewise the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus. The “*historical contingency*” of such events “directly assaults . . . one fundamental characteristic of the idea of autonomy; namely, not making the internal itinerary of consciousness depend on external events” (H 145). This is a serious challenge for Kant, who wants to maintain rational autonomy at all points. 

Kant is not the only one to recognize the challenge, though. Lessing’s ugly broad ditch is emblematic of the problem, that “accidental truths of history can never become the proof for necessary truths of reason.” Kierkegaard tackled a slightly different formulation of the problem in *Philosophical Fragments*: “How can a historical point of departure be given for an eternal consciousness; how can such a point of departure be of more than historical interest; can an eternal happiness be built on historical knowledge?”<sup>13</sup> Nabert poses yet another formulation in *L’Essai sur le mal*: “Does one have the right to invest with an absolute character a moment in history?” (EBI 122).

Ricoeur takes up Nabert’s formulation as well as his proposal of *testimony* as the appropriate mode of discourse for these historical signs of the Absolute. Testimony has more “historic density” than examples or symbols. For Kant, religious representations are examples (like the Christ prototype) and symbols (like the cosmic conflict between good and evil), but neither of these really expresses the singularity that is essential to so many religious traditions. Examples introduce a degree of particularity to the reflection, but they are always subordinate to the universality of the idea, just like the case is subordinate to the rule. As Kant writes in the *Groundwork*:

Imitation has no place at all in matters of morality, and examples serve only for encouragement, that is, they put beyond doubt the practicability of what the law commands and make intuitive what the practical rule expresses more generally, but they can never justify setting aside the true original, which lies in reason, and guiding oneself by examples.<sup>14</sup>

This also means that examples cannot introduce anything truly new, anything that conflicts with the authority of reason, nor anything that would undermine 

*[p. 87]* ^pg104

the moral and epistemic autonomy of the self. Thus “even the Holy One of the Gospel must first be compared with our ideal of moral perfection before he can be cognized as such.”<sup>15</sup> In short, even Jesus must measure up to the standards of pure moral reason.

Ricoeur observes that where examples are easily reducible to the moral ideal, symbols are “not obliterated so easily” because they have a surplus and opacity of meaning that cannot be reduced to concepts. Yet, with symbols, meaning still matters more than historic reference. This is where testimony is different. Like examples and symbols, testimony gives to thought, but what it gives is unique because it involves a dialectic of meaning and event. The event is the contingent, self-manifesting and self-initiating disclosure of the Absolute (EBI 144, 148–49). 

Testimony “confers the sanction of reality on ideas, ideals, and ways of being,” which means it can be true or false (H 147–48). As such, testimony is more fragile than examples or symbols, and is subject to testing or *trial*. There is “no manifestation of the absolute without the threat of a false testimony, and without the decision that separates the sign from the idol” (H 148). This requires what Nabert calls the “criteriology of the divine,” which Ricoeur takes as a model of hermeneutical discernment. The philosophical interpretation of testimony in fact entails a double trial: of historical judgment and reflexive judgment (EBI 143, 148). On the one hand, this means reflection on the act of understanding those historically contingent signs in which the Absolute gives itself, and on the other hand, reflection on the judgment in which the self undertakes a criteriology of the divine in order to distinguish between the true symbols and the idols. Testimony thus involves an “alliance” between the exterior sign and the interior act.<sup>16</sup> 

This alliance of exterior and interior serves what Nabert calls “originary affirmation.” Testimony is an assertion of conviction, an act of attestation (EBI 152), in which the self expresses its desire to be: Here I am! This conative drive is essential to the liberation of the bound will. Ricoeur writes that testimony “is the expression of the freedom that we desire to be.” The external sign moves from the historical to the ideal, so that “I recognize as existing what is only an idea for me.” This idea recollects the conatus and allows for the self to affirm an external ideal that initiates its liberation.<sup>17</sup> This external ideal is effective from outside insofar as it is met by an internal movement of affirmation. Once again we see Ricoeur’s insistence on transcendence and immanence, alterity and ipseity.

Reflection never achieves a unitary intuition of these two acts, no absolute knowing “in which consciousness would become aware of the absolute and of itself” (EBI 147, H 149). That is why the concrete singularity of testimony remains a difficult pill for rationalist philosophies of religion to swallow. It requires reflection to lose itself, to surrender its claim to be a sovereign, 

*[p. 88]* ^pg105

self-positing, constituting consciousness by “confessing its total dependence on the historical manifestations of the divine” (H 150). This far Kant is unwilling to go. One modern philosopher who does, however, give testimony a chance is Hegel. Unlike Kant, Hegel rejects the idea of a Pure, Moral, Rational, or Natural Religion that can be abstracted as the essence of religion. Instead, Hegel gives us what Ricoeur calls a “hermeneutics of testimony.”

### Hegel on Religious Representation

Hegel is important for Ricoeur because he helps push Kant’s proto-hermeneutics of religion in a more concrete direction. As Ricoeur observes, Hegel breaks with the Enlightenment tradition of deducing a Pure, Moral, Rational, or Natural Religion that would be distinguished from the various positive religions—what Kant calls “faiths.” As we have seen, for Kant the pure form of religion is present in reason and is “permanently taking place within all human beings.” This claim is highly suspect because Kant’s pure religion looks a lot like Lutheran Christianity, yet is supposedly not dependent in any essential way on its particular content. For Kant, the rational form is separable from the content. Hegel has a different take. On the one hand, he agrees with Kant that the True Religion is already contained immanently within reason. He disagrees with Kant, however, by insisting that this truth can only be discovered through concrete religions. These particular religious traditions (their beliefs, practices, and institutions) are essential moments in the grand narrative of Spirit—that is, the historical process by which Spirit comes to absolute self-knowledge.

The *telos* of Spirit is to achieve self-knowledge, to know its own essence as freedom, and Spirit only accomplishes this goal at the end of a long historical process. Spirit manifests itself in successive stages—in ethics, law, politics, art, religion, and philosophy. It needs these concrete manifestations to actualize itself, to actualize the potency that lies within it in germinal form so that its implicit truth becomes fully explicit. For Hegel, therefore, religious traditions are not just dispensable content to be separated from the essential, but rather manifestations of the essential itself. The task of philosophy is to show how these stages of religion manifest Absolute Spirit. Religion and philosophy have the same content, but religion only possesses that truth in penultimate form—namely, representation (*Vorstellung*), which includes images, symbols, narratives, creeds, and doctrines. The task of philosophy is to realize the truth of religious representation in its ultimate form—the Concept (*Begriff*). This requires a movement of sublation (*Aufhebung*): the representation is denied its status as the ultimate truth of Spirit, but what is true in the representation is preserved and lifted to a higher level in conceptual 

*[p. 89]* ^pg106

form. Religious representation is therefore revealed to be indispensable, and yet not the final word about Spirit.

This is a significant departure from Kant. For Kant there is no self-presentation (*Selbst-Darstellung*) of the Absolute, which is why religious representations cannot be brought into the domain of theoretical reason. To do so would only lead to transcendental illusion. For Hegel, on the other hand, representation (*Vorstellung*) is just such a self-presentation of the Absolute, which is why it “is potentially speculative” (SV 71). Speculation turns out to be a kind of *anamnesis*—a recollection or recapitulation of each moment of truth in the career of Spirit (SV 86).

Hegel’s hermeneutics of religion is consequently “the description of the inner dynamism of the figurative mode” (SV 73) in which concrete, historical religions make up the movement of Absolute Spirit toward its *telos*. Despite being the philosopher of absolute knowing, Hegel’s philosophy of religion is still a hermeneutics, a work of interpretation. The manifestation of the Absolute—the “immediacy of its ‘being there’”—does not preclude interpretation but in fact initiates it (SV 78). Ricoeur notes that for Hegel, “\[r\]evelation, as immediate as it may be, requires a believer, a witness. It is never a brute fact, open to public observation and description” (SV 83). At the same time, “there would be nothing to interpret if there were not, as at the beginning of the *Phenomenology*, an absolute presence, a substantial given, which calls for mediation” (SV 78).

What Ricoeur takes from Hegel’s hermeneutics of revelation is the indispensability of *Vorstellung*. Without representation, philosophy of religion remains too abstract. In this regard Hegel makes the same criticism of Kantian religion as he does of Kantian morality. Ricoeur agrees with the Hegelian verdict that Kant’s focus on moral duty “separates the form from its content, duty from desire, coherence from life, universality from history, legality from effectiveness, rationality from reality” (FS 209). Hegel’s attempt, in the *Philosophy of Right*, is to show the unity of these opposing poles. The same spirit of abstraction is present in Kant’s philosophy of religion: it is too abstract because it conceives of religion in terms of moral duty and fails to recognize the desire to know and be reconciled to God. Kant also fails to recognize the necessity of concrete religious traditions (faiths). While Hegel distorts both points by situating them within a teleo-logic of absolute knowing, he does recognize their importance more clearly than Kant. One of the lessons Ricoeur takes from Hegel is that “\[p\]hilosophical hermeneutics will henceforth have to come to grips with this resistance of the historical to the conceptual” (83). This is a lesson that Hegel himself did not fully embrace, but on Ricoeur’s reading, Hegel did move somewhat in this direction in his development from the *Phenomenology* to his *Lectures on the Philosophy of Religion*.<sup>18</sup>

*[p. 90]* ^pg107

For Ricoeur, then, the significance of Hegel for a hermeneutical phenomenology of religion “is to have attempted to trace the stages through which religious ‘representations’ point toward their speculative fulfillment.” As we have seen, “the world of religion is the everlasting process of giving forms and abolishing forms” (BH 140). Religious representations are meaningful in the sense that they are the manifestation of the Absolute, giving it “substantial plenitude of expression” (PA 173–74). This representation is dynamic rather than static, insofar as it moves toward its own sublation in the concept. This means two things: first, that interpretation arises from the movement of the representation itself, and second, that this movement of sublation is a movement of mortality; the representation dies in order to achieve new life in the concept. All of this works toward a philosophical interpretation in which the Absolute inhabits consciousness itself (PA 174). 

Ricoeur approximates Hegel with a more modest version of absolute thought, which is less a state of finality than the process by which representations “remain thoughtful.”<sup>19</sup> From his reading of Hegel, Ricoeur reinterprets the hermeneutics of religion “as an endless process thanks to which representative and speculative thought keep generating one another”—an inner dynamism leading to speculative thinking. The symbol gives to thought. At the same time, philosophical thinking must always preserve its roots in figurative thinking, since it cannot subsist without symbol and narrative. This also means, first, that the hermeneutics of religion must take the lived experience of religious phenomena as its starting point, and the point to which it continually returns. That is Ricoeur’s approximation of the immediacy of subjective spirit. Second, the hermeneutics of religion is likewise attuned to the “stories, symbols, and interpretations” in which these religious phenomena are figured in communities of faith. That is Ricoeur’s approximation of the mediation of objective spirit. And third, from this rooting in immediate experience and figurative mediation, the hermeneutics of religion “keeps aiming at conceptual thought.” That is Ricoeur’s approximation of the thinking of absolute spirit.

Ricoeur is a mediating thinker and does not want to choose between Kant and Hegel. He admits that there is an inescapable choice to be made between a philosophy of limits and one of absolute knowledge,<sup>20</sup> but he also admits to finding the choice “embarrassing” (PA 170). In the end, though, Ricoeur remains a thinker of limits and will not go all the way with Hegel: “The mutual promotion of reason and faith, in their difference, is the last word for a finite consciousness” (EBI 153). This is the sense in which he returns to Kant after Hegel—thus warranting his self-description as a “post-Hegelian Kantian.”<sup>21</sup> That is an accurate description of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, since it is the domain of the understanding (*Verstehen*) rather than reason (*Ver-nunft*), the conditioned rather than the unconditioned. This self-limitation of reason need not be merely negative, though. In Kantian terms, the denial of 

*[p. 91]* ^pg108

direct, objective knowledge of the Absolute or unconditioned makes room for faith—in this case, the indirect presentations in the figures of religious language: symbols, parables, myths, and so on (BH 142–43). 

Beyond the standoff between Kant and Hegel, Ricoeur also appeals to Heidegger to help articulate his hermeneutics of religion. In this formulation, the task of the hermeneutics of religion is to “disentangle from the ‘world’ of the texts, their implicit ‘project’ for existence, their indirect ‘proposition’ of new modes of being,” and to display for our imaginations “the figures of our authentic existence” (BH 144). And as we have seen, Ricoeur sees the goal of this imaginative display not merely as intellectual self-understanding, but as the liberation of the servile will. In this sense it has an ethical momentum—ethical not in the sense of a narrow Kantian moralism based on duty or obligation, but in the Spinozistic sense of “our travel from bondage to freedom.” Or, in terms Ricoeur borrows from Nabert, ethics “is the theory of the mediations through which we fulfill our desire to be, our effort to exist.” Ethics in this sense is “the whole set of conditions for the realization of freedom,” and therefore “all self-understanding is ethical and we can affirm that symbolic consciousness is the long detour by which the self discovers the conditions of its own establishment” (BH 144).

### Ricoeur’s Ontology of Human Capability

This combination of Spinoza, Kant, Hegel, and Heidegger forms the philosophical core of Ricoeur’s mature hermeneutics of religion. Like Kant, Ricoeur sees religion in terms of the regeneration of human capability. At times Ricoeur sounds very Kantian, describing the “extraordinary capacity” of religion to “make the ordinary person capable of doing the good” (RB 30). Unlike Kant, however, Ricoeur does not want to reduce religion to moral duty. Beyond this narrow moralism, Ricoeur appeals to Spinoza’s ethics as the liberation and realization of our freedom. Ricoeur also appeals to Hegel, who shows that the philosophical interpretation of religion must attend to the concreteness of religious representation rather than working with an abstract rational idea of “Religion.” Following Heidegger, religious discourse calls the self to a new world and new possibilities. Religious texts are revelatory because they redescribe reality, proposing “a world I may inhabit and wherein I can project my ownmost possibilities” (HIR 138). But this Heideggerian lingo does not tell us all that much about what this religious possibility actually looks like. In less Heideggerian, more biblical, terms, Ricoeur describes this proposed world as the “new creation, a new Covenant, the kingdom of God . . .” (H 139). These are new ways of being-in-the-world, and the biblical texts disclose these new possibilities of self-understanding 

*[p. 92]* ^pg109

and action. The self is constituted—or *re*constituted—according to the new being revealed in the biblical texts. This regeneration registers across all the fundamental capacities of the self: our capacities to speak, act, narrate and impute ourselves as responsible. 

If religious discourse is addressed to the capable human being, we should not imagine this means religion is for the few, the proud, the capable. Instead, it addresses those who are poor, broken in spirit, and cannot do the good they want. Religion is addressed to those whose capability is bound by evil, to those selves who are fragile, vulnerable, and whose capacities are far from stable or certain. It aims to restore or regenerate the capability of people like these—namely, all of us.

If Ricoeur’s mature thought revolves around the capable human being, one of his unfinished projects was to work out the ontological basis for this philosophical anthropology. What kind of being is the capable self? Ricoeur’s reflections on this topic mostly exist in sketches, but he did set forth some basic principles. In contrast to traditional metaphysics of substance or mechanism, Ricoeur prefers a dynamic conception of being as potency and action.<sup>22</sup> Ricoeur suggests this in his re-reading of Aristotle’s *Metaphysics* in *Oneself as Another*, where he argues that *ousia* cannot be reduced to substance. He also challenges the traditional priority of actuality (*energeia*) over potentiality (*dynamis*) (OA 305–6), proposing to understand the ontology of the self with the pairing *energeia-dynamis*, which Pamela Sue Anderson helpfully renders as “the \[actual\] power to act.”<sup>23</sup> Ricoeur envisions this as an ontological ground of the self as acting and being-able to act (OA 308). It is the basis for Ricoeur’s “*phenomenology of being able*”—that we have the capacity to “realize a task,” to speak, to act, to narrate, and to designate ourselves as imputable.<sup>24</sup> At the same time, this being able always corresponds to a being *un*able, for the self is always a mix of acting and suffering, capable and incapable, potent and impotent.

Ricoeur finds other cues for thinking about this dynamic power of being able in Leibniz and Schelling, and especially in Spinoza’s idea of *conatus essendi*. Ricoeur was impressed by the fact that Spinoza did not differentiate *energeia* and *dynamis*, but instead saw them as one and the same.<sup>25</sup> For Spinoza, the conatus means life, which in turn means power (OA 315). This life is not a self-possessed power, but rather a participation in the divine Life. All finite beings have their being in God, who is “an infinite power, an *acting energy.*”<sup>26</sup>

The aim of religious discourse, then, is to regenerate our being-able—not merely to fulfill our moral duties, but to employ all our capacities in new ways. Religious discourse liberates and transforms the fundamental capacities of the self. We will see these different ways over the next several 

*[p. 93]* ^pg110

chapters, but for now it is worth outlining how religion impacts the different capacities of the self.

1\. Religious discourse liberates the capacity for speech, making me capable of speaking in new ways: I can name God, since God has already been named in the text. Biblical texts allow the vulnerable self to speak in new ways, since the God named there is a God who sees the plight of the widow, orphan, and stranger, the poor, the sick, and the lost. The different genres of the biblical text open up other capacities of speech: I can pray. I can worship. I can lament. I can forgive—that is, perform the speech act of forgiving, due to the logic of superabundance I encounter in the text. I can make promises, vows, and confessions of faith. Religious texts give me the grammar and vocabulary to do this.

2\. Here we can see Ricoeur’s point that speech and action are not entirely divisible—for instance, in the case of forgiveness. Religion also makes me capable of acting in new ways: of responding to the divine call, and of inhabiting the world proposed by the text, loving my neighbor, serving others who are vulnerable and in need. It makes possible liturgical acts and other acts of worship, sacrifice, and renunciation.

3\. Religion liberates the capacity for narration: I can narrate myself in a new way, insofar as I see my own story within the larger story of the kingdom of God. I can tell my story because it is already in some way told *to* me and *of* me, prior to my own narration. This is a story of good news and unforeseen possibilities of hope.

4\. This also liberates the capacity of imputability, making me capable of assuming responsibility—in the confession of sin, as well as in response to the divine call. Religion locates us within a symbolic order—perhaps *the* symbolic order *par excellence*—and this enables us to assume responsibility for ourselves, for our past acts, to ask forgiveness. It also opens us to the possibility of forgiveness, in which we are unbound from our past acts and set free for the future. 

In brief, religion registers a change in all the fundamental capacities of the self. This is why Ricoeur finds Kantian morality too narrow a hermeneutic for understanding religion. Kant’s moral religion is simply not grounded deeply enough in concrete, particular *faiths* but instead interprets Christianity according to the *a priori* concepts of moral reason, leading to a purportedly pure moral religion that can be normative for interpreting and evaluating the phenomena of concrete religious faith. That is why Kant finds it so easy to conclude that religion is all about recognizing our moral duty as divinely commanded, and about becoming morally pleasing to God. He has set up a hermeneutical circle, the vice or virtue of which is open to debate. 

*[p. 94]* ^pg111

By interpreting religion in terms of morality, he can conclude that religion is all about morality. What is clear, for our purposes here, is that Ricoeur does not want to limit religion to the moral level, since it discloses a wider spectrum of possibilities.

If the text reveals new possibilities for being, what exactly is the ground of this ontological novelty? Where do these possibilities come from? They are given in the world in front of the text, but *what gives* this ontological donation? Are these possibilities immanent or transcendent? Is some sort of divine agency or grace active in the regeneration of the self? These questions reveal a tension in Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, dependent as it is on Kant and Spinoza. As Ricoeur observes, Kantian philosophy of religion is “mute about the divine origin of the process of regeneration” (PA 282). This is a theological question, and Kant assures his colleagues in the Faculty of Theology that he is not encroaching on their territory. Yet this assurance is a bit disingenuous, because Kant’s philosophical interpretation of religion has obvious theological consequences. His claim that we must become “worthy” of grace and salvation is hardly theologically neutral. According to the traditional doctrine of justification by faith, Kant gets it exactly backward. Kant, of course, knows this, and he is consciously reinterpreting the doctrine.

Thus we might ask again: How Kantian is Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion? Ricoeur is clear that he follows Kant in conceiving of religion as the regeneration of our human capability to do the good. Ricoeur is also Kantian in his attempt to maintain both the alterity of religious symbols and the ipseity of human capacity. Ricoeur wants to push this alterity further, in such a way that destabilizes the rational autonomy of the self, but, like Kant, he wants to keep questions about the origins of this alterity in brackets. Our question in the chapters ahead is whether Ricoeur still ends up making theological moves, or at least moves with theological consequences. We will consider this regarding Ricoeur’s views of revelation and Scripture (chapter 5), the call and response of faith (chapter 6), desire and eschatology (chapter 7), the relation between God and Being (chapter 8), and the question of religion and violence (chapter 9).
