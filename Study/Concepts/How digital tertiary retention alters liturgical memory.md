# How digital tertiary retention alters liturgical memory

> [!abstract] Definition
> Liturgy is a communal memory practice built around tertiary retentions that hold still: a canon, a calendar, a rite repeated in the same form. Digital tertiary retention re-sorts itself for each person at machine speed, and removes the stillness such practices were built around. The sources establish the two halves of this claim separately; how they meet is not yet written.

## What the sources establish

**Tertiary retention in its digital form.** The frame is set out in [[Tertiary Retention]]. In the Thoughts fragment (31 Dec 2020) of [[Leviathan fragments 2018-2024|Leviathan fragments 2018-2024]], Stephen quotes *The Age of Disruption* on individuals "transformed into data-providers" whose own retentions "enable them to be dispossessed of their own protentions — that is, their own desires, expectations, volitions, will", and writes in his own voice that the memories we retain form our *Principium Individuationis*. Externalised memory reaches into what a person can expect and want.

**Liturgy as a practice built around an inert retention.** Part I of [[Second Domestication outline 2026-07-07|The Second Domestication]] says writing was domesticable because it held still, and names the practices that grew around it: "recitation and commentary, *lectio* and disputation, the paired study of the study-house, marginalia, the canon itself." Its general law: every memory technology is poison and cure at once, "and the prescription is written by the surrounding practice." The word *liturgy* is not used; recitation and *lectio* are liturgical, disputation and paired study are not. Part IV calls deliberately re-lengthened reading "the modern *lectio*".

**Liturgy as memory that outlives its substrate.** In Conversation 4 of [[xscript fragments (AI)|xscript fragments (AI)]] Stephen observes that "the Seder of today (the entire Jewish liturgical calendar really) is also doing something the temple sacrifice once did", and that for religious people the model of their relationship to God "takes the form of scripture, liturgy, and tradition", which commandments and rituals help them "work through".

**Liturgy as remedy.** [[Cybernetic Anamnesis (concept)]] reports that *Cybernetic Anamnesis* concludes by calling for "educational and liturgical habits that restore [[Engineered Cognitive Friction|cognitive friction]] and deliberative latency to digital encounters" (the text itself was not available to this draft).

## Where the alteration bites

Part II of the Second Domestication outline: the feed "does not hold still long enough for practice to form around it, it adapts faster than practice can form, and it is maximally profitable precisely in its undomesticated state"; among its losses is "the common narrative dissolved into a billion private streams." A rite is recited together, from one text, on one calendar. A retention that re-sorts itself for each person works against exactly that. Stephen's sentences supply both premises; the conclusion about liturgy is this draft's, not his.

## Open questions

- Is liturgy the "surrounding practice" that writes the prescription for digital memory, or the first casualty of a retention that will not hold still? Either reading fits the sources.
- The Seder outlived the Temple by carrying its function into a meal and a calendar. Is there an analogous move when the substrate becomes recursive rather than destroyed?
- Which "liturgical habits" does *Cybernetic Anamnesis* mean? The AI reply in Conversation 4 offered the phrase "liturgical anamnesis"; Stephen did not adopt it.
- The first three sources (the fragments and the outline) do not mention the Eucharist, and in them *anamnesis* is Plato's word. The vault has the liturgical sense twice: the Prayer Book's Eucharistic Prayer A ("Do this for the remembrance of me"; "We celebrate the memorial of our redemption", [[BCP - Holy Eucharist - Rite II#Eucharistic Prayer A|Rite II]]) and Gschwandtner on liturgy as anamnesis (pp. 97–100) and on liturgical memory (ch. 7). The Hub's two homilies (Proper 22A; Christ the King) were not in the copy read and may hold more.
- The portioned Tantrāloka reading cycle ([[Hub - Tantraloka]], "Liturgical Pacing") paces reading on digital tools. Is it the test case?
