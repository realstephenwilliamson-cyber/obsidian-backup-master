All these services had been written in traditional liturgical English, apart from adopting the modern form of the third person singular ('has', not 'hath'). But from 1966 onwards there was a steadily increasing demand for the use of modern English. The services had been authorized for only four years, so that work on their revision had to begin almost at once. It was planned at first to issue the revised Seri~s Two Holy Communion in both traditional and modern English; but before long it was decided that all future services should be in modern English only, and these services were described as Series Three. 

The first service to appear was Holy Communion, which had a relatively easy passage, and was authorized in 1973, with a completely new set of Bible readings also derived from the Joint Liturgical Group. It was followed in steady succession by Funeral Services (1975), Morning and Evening Prayer (1975), Marriage (1977), Ordination (1978), Initiation (1979), and the Calendar and Lectionary (1979)' After a final, massive revision of Holy Communion (1979), all the Series Three services were gathered up into an Alternative Service Book, which was authorized from 1 November 1980 until 31 December 1990. This also contained Holy Communion Series One and Two Revised (see p. 207), and a new translation of the Psalms. 

The Liturgical Commission's drafts of all these services were subjected to considerable revision, at first by the Church Assembly, then by its successor, the General Synod. In 1973, in order to save time, a procedure was devised of remitting the draft services to a revision committee. This led in practice to the amendments proposed becoming more and more numerous and extensive. It is unlikely that anywhere else has more prolonged and thorough consultation been achieved before the authorization of new services.

Morning and Evening Prayer (Revised) retains the framework of the Prayer Book offices. There is a generous provision of seasonal Sentences, as in 1928, though newly chosen. The penitential section 

*[p. 210]* ^pg233

begins with 1 John I: 8-9, followed by alternative confessions and absolutions, one pair taken from Series Two Holy Communion, the other from the Joint Liturgical Group office. *Jubilate* is now alternative to *Venite,* as in the South India rite, and *Benedictus* is placed between the Old and New Testament lessons, by analogy with *Magnijicat,* its previous place being taken by *Te Deum.* [[Psalms 134#^134-1|Psalm 134]] is placed before the Evensong psalms, by analogy with *Venite.* A new collect is provided as an alternative to the third collect at Mattins. Still following the JLG office, shorter versions of the two services are included: no confession and only one canticle at Mattins, only one lesson and canticle, and no creed at Evensong. In these versions every service of the week has its own canticle: the Easter Anthems are to be said every Saturday; *Gloria in excelsis* returns to its original horne in the office on Thursdays; and there are five new canticles, one from the Apocrypha, two from Revelation, the fourth-century hymn 'Hail, gladdening light', and a nineteenth-century composition, '0 Saviour of the world', attributed to Henry Allon, a wellknown Congregationalist divine. <sup>1</sup>

The Holy Communion service was much influenced by the patristic scholars E. C. Ratcliff and A. H. Couratin. I t was designed 'in terms of a parish communion, that is to say, a Sunday service, with a sermon, and the bulk ofthe congregation communicating'. I t was to last for an hour: 'thirty minutes for the word and thirty minutes for the sacrament';2 the sermon was envisaged as lasting a quarter of an hour. The Prayer Book order is retained at the beginning with the Collect for Purity and the Ten Commandments, though both are optional. In keeping with Parish-Communion practice, there are psalms and three lessons, though only the Gospel is compulsory. The intercessions are modelled on the *Orationes solennes* of Good Friday in the Roman rite. Petitions composed by the officiant are followed by silence, a versicle and response, and a set prayer; this pattern is repeated four times. The service so far constitutes the Ante-Communion, and may end here with the Grace. 

'The Communion' begins with the penitential section, which reproduces the the Prayer Book order with the addition of the Prayer of Humble Access, as in 1928, though both the invitation ('Seeing we have a great high priest .. .') and the confession are new. This section is rounded offwith the Peace, a feature which had been retained in 1549, but omitted in 1552, it is now in its primitive position, first mentioned by Justin Martyr (see p. I), and adopted 

*[p. 211]* ^pg234

in all Eastern liturgies, a good example of the Liturgical Commission's desire to get behind the Middle Ages to the earliest sourees. 

The next four seetions are given tides: 'The Preparation of the Bread and Wine', 'The Thanksgiving', 'The Breaking of the Bread' , and 'The Sharing of the Bread'. Here the influence of Dix's 'four-action shape' is obvious. The word 'ofTertory' is carefully avoided (offering comes later), but the Thanksgiving has a subtide, 'The Prayer of Consecration'. Even at their most adventurous, the compilers of the service could not resist glancing over their shoulders at 1662. 

But the Thanksgiving itself is quite unlike the 1662 prayer. To begin with, for the first time in an English Prayer Book, the Preface and *Sanctus* are reunited with the rest ofthe eucharistie prayer, as in the Eastern liturgies. The Preface rehearses the history of our salvation, but quite exceptionally carries the rehearsal down to Pentecost *be/ore* reaching the *Sanctus.* As a consequence, the bridge-passage from *Sanctus* to Institution Narrative is slight and fails to sustain the feeling of thanksgiving. It contains one of the ambiguities by which consensus was achieved in the phrase 'the sacrifice of praise', which is interpreted literally by Evangelicals, whereas Anglo-Catholics 'know of no sacrifice of praise other than the Bread of Life and the Cup of everlasting Salvation'. 3 The presence of manual acts, due to the Church Assembly, ignores the sequence of the four actions and the theology underlying the prayer, that the whole prayer consecrates, and not merely the words of institution. 

As originally drafted, the anamnesis ran: 

having in remembrance his saving passion, his resurrection from the dcad, and his glorious ascension into heaven, and looking for the coming of his kingdom, we oller unto thee this brcad and this cup. 

The final phrase is reminiscent of the eucharistie prayer in the *Apostolic Tradition* of Hippolytus, though in fact the word 'this (bread)' suggests rather the *Apostolic Constitutions,* a much less respectable ancestor. It was this phrase that caused the trouble. Since the removal of ofTering was, liturgically speaking, what the Reformation was about, it is not surprising that the phrase was rejected by the Evangelicals. The final version, <sup>4</sup>drawing to some extent on 1549, was generally welcomed as making the best of a very difficult situation. Ifit does not say all that either party would wish 

*[p. 212]* ^pg235

to say, it likewise avoids saying anything that they would not wish to say. The sentence following, based on [[Torah 18 - Mishpatim#^24-1|Exodus 24]], links the service with the worship of heaven, so that the prayer could have ended with the *Sanctus,* as the repetition of the phrase 'with all the company of heaven' suggests that it was originally intended to end. 

There is now, again for the first time in an English book, a separate form of words to accompany the Breaking of the Bread; and this is followed by the Lord's Prayer, placed after it, without precedent from Rome or the Prayer Book, as a final preparation for communion. The Words of Administration, 'The body ofChrist', were attacked as teaching transubstantiation, and defended as merely putting Jesus's own words into the third person. 

The service ends with an abbreviated version of the Prayer of Thanksgiving from 1662, and and a much more abbreviated version of the Prayer of Oblation. Most previous revisions had placed the latter before communion, as in 1549, but Dix had criticized this practice as Pelagian, and in the position after communion the new prayer was widely welcomed. The 1662 Blessing and a new Dismissal end the service. 

In 1976 a revised version was authorized in which the service was combined with Holy Communion, Series One; the opportunity was taken to introduce some improvements from Series Three. 

The 1958 services of Baptism and Confirrnation had been unfavourably received (see p. 199), but they were taken as the basis ofthe Series Two services. Here the chiefinfluence was exercised by E. C. Whitaker, at whose instance the Liturgical Commission adopted as their fundamental principle that the act of baptism should follow immediately after the profession of faith, without interruption by the Blessing of the Water. On the other hand, the renunciation, being a form of preparation, was to be kept separate from the profession, preferably taking pi ace in a different part ofthe church. 

As in 1958, the normative service comprises Baptism, Confirrnation and Communion. Baptism and Confirrnation both take place within the service ofHoly Communion, between the ministry ofthe word and that of the sacrament. Proper psalms and lessons are provided, and baptism follows the sermon. The renunciations are now termed 'The Decision'. The position of the first question, 'Do you turn to Christ?', aroused widespread criticism on the ground that it *shouldfollow* repentance and renunciation; but it has come to

be accepted with the passing of time. It has, after all, good precedent in the Prayer Book. 

The Blessing of the Water takes the form of a eucharistie thanksgiving. The baptism is followed by the signing with the cross, as in 1662, but now the people join in the accompanying words. Two new ceremonies are introduced: first, the giving of a lighted candle to the newly-baptized (or the parents or sponsors); and secondly, a welcome by the priest and the people on behalf of the whole Church. The newly-baptized adults leave the font to be confirmed. 

The Confirmation section begins with candidates who had been baptized in infancy renewing their baptismal vows. The main difficulty was to express the widely varying views held within the Church of England about the relation of confirmation to baptism. Either the Holy Spirit is given on ce for all in baptism; or baptism is primarily cleansing, and the Holy Spirit comes fuHy in confirmation; or the gift of the Spirit is made in baptism and increased in confirmation. The new prayer, 'Send forth upon them thy Holy Spirit', aIlows the holding ofany ofthese views. So does the formula of confirmation, 'Confirm, 0 Lord, thy servant with thy Holy Spirit'. The traditional use of [[Isaiah 11#^11-1|Isaiah 11]] is maintained; and finally the old formula, 'Defend, 0 Lord, this thy child', is said by the whole congregation. 

The derivative service ofInfant Baptism is preceded bya Preface setting out the Church of England's requirements for those who wish their children to be baptized. This arose from the prolonged controversy over baptismal rigorism. It is not part ofthe service, but presupposes some kind of pastoral preparation beforehand. The service proper begins with a catena of two pairs of biblical texts, each pair foIlowed by a prayer. The questions are addressed to the parents as weIl as the godparents, and are introduced by a sentence which aIlows two possible interpretations of what happens in the vows: 

Those who bring children to be baptized must affirm their allegiance to Christ and their rejection of all that is evil. 

'Their' may refer back to 'those who bring .. .' (in the present) or to 'children' (in the future). The service may end with the Lord's Prayer; a prayer made up of phrases from different parts of the Prayer Book service; and a prayer for the parents.

After 1971 all new services were written in modern English, a procedure which aroused considerable opposition and often distracted attention from the theological and pastoral merits of the services. The centre of controversy was the use of 'you' instead of 'thou' in addressing God. Apart from questions of reverence, this use also affects the structure of sentences, particularly in the collects. Undoubtedly there is a loss ofhallowed associations and the sense of mystery, but it remains to be seen whether these will not in time attach themselves to the new texts; and there is a corresponding gain in immediacy and 'cutting edge', which must be set against accusations of banality and ftatness. The twenty years up to 1980 have also been marked by several new translations ofthe Bible and a spate ofnew hymns and tunes. It is a general movement ofthe time. The Liturgical Commission's chief adviser in literary matters was Professor D. L. Frost, the author of, among other things, the much-loved post-communion prayer in Series Three. 

TheJoint Liturgical Group produced in 1967 a new calendar,5 in which the Church Year starts nine Sundays belore Christmas instead of four, and all Sundays are related to one of the feasts of Christmas, Easter or Pentecost. After repeated changes of mind at every stage of revision, the titles 'Sunday in Advent' and 'in Lent' were restored; the 'red-letter' holy days have been increased by the addition of St Joseph (19 March) and the Blcssed Virgin Mary (8 September); and the list of 'black-lctter' days was very thoroughly revised, and adequate provision of commons made for them. 

The Prayer Book's choice of Epistles and Gospels had for long been generally regarded as capable of improvement; and, with the Calendar, theJoint Liturgical Group produced an entirely new set of readings, based on . the principle of thematic relationship pioneered by the Church ofSouth India. Each Sunday one reading was selected as the controlling reading, and the other two were chosen to relate to it. Finally, the rclationship was summed up in a theme. The readings covered two years. This lectionary, with the addition of saints' day lessons, was appended to Holy Communion Series Three, and, after further revision, included in thc 1980 Book. Later, an additional set ofreadings, for use at a daily eucharist, was taken over from the Roman Catholic provision, with minor alterations to fit the Church of England calcndar.

Morning and Evening Prayer are a straight translation of the Series Two Revised offices, with the addition of the Litany and further prayers, including 'All conditions of men' and the General Thanksgiving. The versions ofthe canticles are those oflCET, the International Consultation on English Texts, an ecumenical and inter-continental body whose translations are now used by Roman Catholics, Anglicans, Lutherans, Methodists and other churches throughout the English-speaking world. 

The Communion service in use between 1973 and 1980 was a straightforward translation and revision ofSeries Two. The versions *ofGloria in excelsis,* the Nicene Creed, the *Sanctus,* the Lord's Prayer, and the *Agnus Dei* were again those made by I CET. One line in the Lord's Prayer, 'Do not put us to the test', was severely criticized, and was altered in Great Britain to 'Do not bring us to the time of trial'. The I CET versions cannot be sung to settings of the traditional texts, which caused a difficulty in the introduction ofthis service from which Series Two had been free, that offinding suitable music. At least twenty settings have been produced, but none of them has established itself as favourite. 

One innovation which was much disliked by scholars was the versicle 'This is the word of the Lord' after the Scripture readings. Borrowed from the Roman rite, it was one of a number of points at which it was alleged that Series Three was out of touch with recent scholarship. Certainly the choice of readings was inspired by the biblical theology of the 1940S and 1950s, with its stress on the unity of Scripture, whereas more recent scholars have emphasized the singularity of each writer. 

Another new feature was the frequent suggestion of silence: the rubric 'Silence may be kept' occurs thirteen times in the service. The structure ofthe intercessions was changed, so that the sequence was now: *ad hoc* petitions, silence, set prayer, versicle and response. In addition, thc opportunity was ofIered of giving thanks as weIl as interceding. In the penitential section, wh ich follows without a break, as in 1662, Series Two's 'Seeing we havc a great high priest .. .' was rcplaced by thc Comfortable Words 01' a new paragraph compressing them into a single sentence. A second new paragraph combined an invitation to confession with an allusion to the Ten Commandments, which had been transferred to follow the in tercessions, if used at all (see p. 2 1 7). The Series Two confession had at first been criticized as being too short and making no

mention of repentance; both these objections were now met. 

The second part of the service, 'The Communion', now star ted with the Peace, the first part being called 'The Word and the Prayers' and including penitence. The peace had been universally welcomed, and now became mandatory, though any suggestion of accompanying gest ures was still confined to the Notes, where 'a handclasp or similar action' was recommended. The presentation of the elements and the collection is marked by an optional sentence added in Synod (I Chronicles 29: I I a, 14b). 

The four actions were unmistakably set out in the subtitles of the Communion: 'The Taking of the Bread and Wine', 'The Thanksgiving', 'The Breaking of the Bread', and 'The Giving of the Bread and the Cup'. The celebrant is described as 'the president' (a term already found in Romans and inJustin Martyr), to make the point that the whole congregation celebrates; the priest presides as their representative. 

The Thanksgiving begins with a controversial versicle and response: 'The Lord is here. His Spirit is with us.' This originated in an attempt to translate *'Et cum spiritu tuo'* in the light of modern scholarship.6 In the end, it was decided to try and convey the spirit of the words rather than their literal meaning, adecision which aroused more criticism than any other change in the whole service. The Proper Prefaces (here called 'Proper Thanksgivings'), which were rather clumsily handled in Series Two, were increased in number, and more neatly inserted into the prayer. An attempt was made to carry on the note of thanksgiving after the *Sanctus,* and a reference was added in Synod to the work of the Holy Spirit in the eucharist. This addition met with virtually no opposition, in marked contrast to the controversies over the epiclesis in the 1920S. 

After the storms over 'We offer unto thee this bread and this cup', the Liturgical Commission was determined to produce a generally acceptable anamnesis, and the printed text was the result of long discussion between K. N. Ross and C. O. Buchanan, representing the two wings of the Church of England, and the Chairman of the Commission. Evangelicals had feit that Series Two laid too little stress on the cross at this point, and this was remedied, with the result that Anglo-Catholics feit that there was now too much. For them the anamnesis was less satisfactory than that ofSeries Two. It was followed by a completely new sentence ('Renew us by your Spirit .. .'), which helped to build up a convincing ending to the prayer, while the addition of 'acclamations' after the Institution

Narrative and as a final doxology gave the laity a chance to participate audibly in the consecration. 

The words accompanying the Breaking of the Bread were newly trans la ted to make them more suitable for congregational recitation; and the words of administration were slightly lengthened, to become a prayer rather than a statement. Sets of seasonal post-communion Sentences and blessings added further to the variable element in the service, which had been deliberately limited in Series Two. There are two post-communion collects, one (referred to above) by Dr Frost, the other an augmented version of the Series Two prayer, including thanksgiving for communion. 

The Ten Commandments had been gradually falling into disuse for many years, though some still wanted them to be mandatory every Sunday. A compromise was reached by putting them into an appendix and adding to each one a suitable quotation from the New Testament. 

Holy Communion was the only service to be fully revised before inclusion in the 1980 Book, though all the others underwent small changes in detail. In preparation for the final revision, the Liturgical Commission sent out a questionnaire to parishes which were using the service, whose replies revealed a general satisfaction with it as it stood. The Commission therefore submitted to the General Synod a draft in which there was little change but some new material. The members of Synod, however I were of a different mind, and tabled some 1200 amendments. When the revision committee had incorporated a number ofthese and had added not a few of its own, the result could fairly be regarded as no longer a revision of Series Three but a new service. 

The service as it appears in the 1980 Book contains eight eucharistie prayers, two of which are modern-Ianguage versions of two others. This multiplication gave the death-blow to the belief, already quite unrealistic, that the whole realm had, or even should have, one Use. It was hoped that it would remove, or at any rate reduce, the need for ambiguity; at the same time it was stressed that the prayers were not intended to be identified with particular brands of churchmanship. Many other parts ofthe service have up to four alternatives; and the seasonal provision, with its accompanying rules, has reached a point where Cranmer's remarks about the Pie come irresistibly to mind. Besides authorizing pluriformity (which was a matter of officially recognizing the existing situation), the revision committee also sought to adopt as much as possible of

the wording 01' the Roman *Ordo Missae* (which was not). 

The first notable change is in the title ofthe service, which is now 'The Order for Holy Communion also called The Eucharist and The Lord's Supper'. This recognizes the acclimatization ofthe word 'eucharist' in ordinary speech. A much bigger change, first proposed by the Liturgical Commission, is that the Prayers 01' Penitence may be said at the beginning 01' the service. A question about this was included in the Commission's questionnaire, and produced a 42 per cent vote in f~lVour, a surprisingly high figure considering that none ofthose concerned had any experience ofthe rearrangement. Three alternative confessions, none very different from the others, are included in the Appendix. The Creed incorporates some small changes made by ICET in its final version. In the intercessions, which were becoming generally less and less formal, the minister is finally given permission to use any 'suitable words'. A rewriting or the Prayer 01' Humble Access by Professor Frost is included in the Appendix as an alternative. 

Instead of the heading TBE COMMUNION, the second part or the service is headed TBE MINlSTRY 01' TBE SACRAMENT. The Peace is provided with one permanent and five seasonal alternatives, and the president may now suggest some gesture. Care is taken to distinguish the oflertory procession from 'The Taking' by heading it 'The Preparation ofthe Gifts'. The four actions are now grouped in two pairs: Taking and Thanksgiving, Breaking and Distribution. In pI ace of the single Thanksgiving, there are four eucharistic prayers in the text. The first of these is that or Series Three; the second and fourth are from Series Two and One respectively, in modern English. All three have been slightly revised; and Prayer 3 is quite new. Like the Roman Prayer 2, it is based on the prayer in the *Apostolic Tradition* 01' Hippolytus, provided with a consecratory epiclesis and the *Sanctus.* In the first prayer small adjustments have been made in the preface to produce a more logical order, and in the Institution Narrative of all four prayers to bring out the two pairs of actions: 'gave thanks' is emphasized by being a main verb and not a dependent participle. The anamnesis has been rewritten yet again, and this process was in fact the major concern ofthe whole revision 01' this service. 

It has been truly said that 'the anamnesis-oblation is the most sensitive point \[ol' the prayer\], for it will almost inevitably express a particular view of the eucharistic sacrifice'.7 In the Church of England the difficulty is that it has to express two different views.

For Evangelicals, the sacrifice concerned is that of Christ on the cross, made once for all; for Anglo-Catholics, it is the offering ofthe consecrated elements. The controversy over offering the bread and the cup in Series Two has already been mentioned, and the Liturgical Commission's effort to avoid its revival in Series Three. But the dissatisfaction of Anglo-Catholics with the Series Three anamnesis led to prolonged discussion within the Commission, between the Commission and the bishops, and (less prolonged) on the synodical revision committee. At least a dozen different drafts came into consideration. 

Anglo-Catholics had first to find a substitute for 'offer' which would be acceptable to Evangelicals, and then ensure that this was the main verb of the sentence, while the remembrance of God's mighty acts was placed in a subordinate elause. Evangelicals had to consider how fin such a procedure could remain faithfuI to Scripture. Various drafts and the final text will be found on p. 330. The difficulty 01' finding a single text which would be generally acceptable led to the submission of a new prayer (the present Prayer 3) and a modern English version 01' 1662. The addition 01' these two prayers to the three al ready proposed made it unnecessary to secure total agreement on any one anamnesis. The authorization ofseveral eucharistie prayers is a new concept for the Church ofEngland, but is a logical devclopment 01' the idea 01' alternative services and had already been done by the Roman Catholic Church and Episcopal Churches, in both ofwhich the composition ofunofficial eucharistie prayers had been carried much further. 

An important change folIows: the Lord's Prayer is said before the Fraction. This is the normal position outside the Anglican Communion, and has been so at least since the time 01' Gregory the Great. It has the advantage 01' bringing the Fraction eloser to the Distribution, and thus making elear its function as apreparation for communion. This function is obscured when the Peace is given after the Fraction. 

There is little to be said about the rest ofthe service. In the Lord's Prayer, the sixth petition had continued to be a source of dissatisfaction, and has now reverted to its familiar form 'Lead us not into temptation'. Baptism and Confirrnation are set out in the same way as in Series Two, though accommodated to the presence of older children. The chief change is that the signing with the cross may now take place immediately after the Decision and before the Blessing ofthe Water. This serves two purposes: first, to provide a ceremony to füllow the renunciations, as baptism follows the profession offaith; secondly, to make it clear that the signing is only an 'honourable badge' and 'no part of the substance of this Sacrament'.8 There is a widespread belief among the uninformed that the signing is the most important part ofthe service. The congregation is now associated with the profession of faith, and the welcome is deferred until after the confirrnation. In the Baptism ofChildren, apart from the changes already made in the normative service, the Preface has become acharge by the priest to the parents and godparents. 

There are two new services in this section ofthe book: Thanksgiving after the Birth of a Child, and the Renewal of Baptismal Vows (Thanksgiving after an Adoption is simply an adaptation of the former). The first derives ultimately from the desire for a service of blessing to be used instead of baptism when the parents are not practising Christians. By express instruction of Synod, 'both the desirability of, and distinction from,' baptism were to be made clear in the course ofthe service, wh ich was also to be suitable both when the child is to be baptized and when he is not. <sup>9</sup>Thus it remains the equivalent ofthe Prayer Book 'Churching ofWomen', though any suggestion of purification has been eliminated, and the rubrics always speak of the parents rather than the mother. From 1549 onwards, the Marriage service has always been more gently revised than any other; and Series Three is no exception. (By an accident ofhistory, there never was aSeries Two service, even in draft form.) In the modern-English form the structure ofthe main body of the service remains the same as in the Prayer Book, though

provision is now made by rubrics for the rite to take place either within the framework of Holy Communion, or followed by it, or on its own. In all cases it begins with a collect derived from 1928, though there it was only to be used when there was a commumon. 

The Preface has been completely rewritten, though the flavour of the old form has not entirely evaporated. It tri es to set out a more modern view of marriage than Cranmer's. There are still three 'causes for which matrimony was ordained', but they are now (I) mutual help and comfort; (2) bodily union; and (3) the bringing up of children. The relation al value of sex, the importance of family life, and the role of marriage in society are all insights new to the liturgy. 

The ancient wording of the questions and vows has been kept almost intact, even to the misleading 'Will you take .. .' (the original Latin means 'Do you wish to take', or even 'Are you willing .. .'). At the end of the vows, 'and thereto I plight thee my troth' is replaced by 'and this is my solemn vow', an unusually dear case of the advantages of modern English. There is a choice of two forms, with or without 'obey' in the bride's vow; in the former case, the bridegroom's vow indudes 'worship', to maintain a balance between the sexes. 

A prayer over the ring is added, and, after the bridegroom has placed the ring on the bride's finger, she either does the same for hirn, using the same words, or says them with the substitution of 'I receive this ring .. .' for 'I give you .. .'. The practice of giving two rings has become very frequent, and, even.where it is not adopted, the new service gives the bride an equal share in the ceremony, thus reflecting contemporary thinking, in which the status of the bride is seen as complementary rather than subordinate. 

The priest announces to the congregation that the couple are married, in phrasing carefully indicating that it is the couple who are ministers of the sacrament. He then pronounces the Church's blessing on their union. (The old mistranslation 'Those whom God hathjoined . . .' is corrected to 'That which .. .'.) A new feature is a set of acdamations derived from J ewish sources, which help to express the new warmth of feeling in the service. Contemporary practice is reflected in a rubric allowing the signing of the registers before the psalm; there is a choice ofthree psalms to accompany the couple's movement to the altar rails; and an abundance of prayers is provided. 

*[p. 222]* ^pg245

The Burial service is given the name 'Funeral', since the majority of funerals do not now end in burial, but in the disposal of the ashes. Like Marriage, it retains the traditional structure. The Series Two draft service was rejected because of its inclusion of prayer for the dead, and the matter was referred to the Doctrine Commission, who suggested the prayer at section 13. After the opening sentences, the service begins with a collect to be said by all, with an effective quotation from the Apostles' Creed. The choice ofpsalms has been much widened, in an attempt to avoid the overworking ofPsalm 23. So too there is a wide choice of lessons, and the familiar [[1 Corinthians 15#^15-1|1 Corinthians 15]] isjudiciously shortened. For the first time, provision is made for a sermon, which is followed by the middle section of *Te Deum.* A new prayer commends and entrusts the departed to 'the mercy ofGod'; this, taken with the following prayer that God will bring 'the whole church, living and departed in the LordJesus, to a joyful resurrection', represents the best compromise that can be achieved between conflicting views about prayer for the dead. 

This concludes the service in church; the next section is headed 'The Committal', and may be said either at the graveside or remaining in the church or crematorium chapel. The material is familiar, except for averse from [[Psalms 016#^16-1|Psalm 16]] and an ascription from Jude. As a whole, the service strikes the note of joyful resurrection much more clearly than the Prayer Book, though some relics of medieval gloom were restored by the Synod. 

New forms are provided for the interment of the ashes after a cremation, and for use when 'the body is brought to church the day before the funeral'. There is also aversion of the service in church and the committal for the funeral of a child. Propers are provided for use 'when the Funeral Service is combined with the Holy Communion Service' and for 'a service of Holy Communion not combined with the Funeral Service', thus recognizing the celebration of a requiem. Eleven additional prayers are printed, and also a set for use 'after the birth of a still born child or the death of a newly-born child'. A service for use in crematorium chapels was licensed by the two archbishops, and does not appear in the Alternative Service Book.1t is cast in a new mould, rising gradually from grief to Christian hope. The language is kept deliberately plain, and there are no psalms or responses. 

The Ordination services were based on the abortive Anglican~ Methodist Ordinal of 1 968, which in its turn owed a good deal to the 

*[p. 223]* ^pg246

Church ofSouth India Ordinal of 1959. They inelude, besides the individual services for the three orders, a service for the ordination of both deacons and priests. Although this is by far the most common method of ordination in England, it has hitherto been covered only by a rubric. 

All three services now follow the same pattern, with the whole rite inserted into Holy Communion after the Nicene Creed or, if that is not said, after the sermon. The candidates are presented to the bishop, who then presents them to the people, for their assent to the ordination. There follows a description of the duties of each order, quite brieffor deacons and bishops, but for priests prolonged by the addition of an abbreviated and updated version of Bucer's homily from the Prayer Book service. The questions put to the candidates, now called 'The Declaration', have also been lightly revised; and those for deacons and priests are identical, which avoids the infelicitous double questioning in the 1662 combined service. 

A section headed 'The Prayers' begins with silent prayer, goes on to the singing of 'Corne, Holy Chost' (except for deacons), and ends with the relevant sections ofthe Litany. The ordination prayer is in three sections: first, a thanksgiving like those of the eucharistic prayers in Holy Communion; secondly, a single sentence repeated over each candidate; and, thirdly, a petition for the 'grace and power' needed to fulfil that ministry. After the giving of the appropriate 'instruments' (New Testament for deacons, Bible for priests and bishops, with or without chalice and paten or pastoral staffrespectively), the Communion service is resumed at the Peace. Proper lessons, psalms, preface, post-communion Sentence, and blessing are provided, and also a post-communion collect for each order translated from the Roman rite. The lessons are a great improvement on those in the Prayer Book. 

The medieval imperative formula has been abandoned, and with it any trace 01' a 'pipeline' doctrine of apostolic succession. The ordination prayer now prays für a fresh descent 01' the Spirit upon each candidate. So too the giving of the instruments is clearly set out as an illustrative ceremony, like the candle in Baptism and the ring in Marriage. The whole service is much eloser to primitive models with the assent 01' the people, the prayer of the people, the ordination prayer, and the laying-on of hands brought into elose sequence.

In previous chapters it has been possible to give some account of all important revisions of the period throughout the Anglican Communion. When all the other types ofservice are taken into consideration, it is immediately clear that some kind of selection is necessary. Furthermore, the whole concept of Prayer Book revision has changed. Up to, say, 1960, revision was the appropriate word: it was a question of modifying the Prayer Book services in one way or another. More recent services have gone back to antiquity for their structure, and the prayers in them have been newly composed. Words, phrases, even whole prayers may be incorporated from the Prayer Book, but this becomes increasingly less so as the new services establish themselves. The precedent set in the eighteenth century by the Scottish and American rites has been followed in most modern services. Quite apart, then, from the question ofspace, there is also the question ofrelevance. In order to keep the length ofthis chapter within bounds, attention will be confined to two categories: *(a)* the other Anglican Churches ofthe British Isles; and *(b)* churches which have reached the degree ofstability indicated by the publication of a complete range of services in definitive form.

Epilogue 

In the writing of chapter 12 it became apparent that, if this work had been entitled 'A History ofthe Book ofCommon Prayer', there would ha ve been no need for a new chapter. The his tory of Anglican *liturgy* has continued since 1965, but the history of the Prayer Book ended with the authorization of Series One. By a pleasant irony, chapter lofthis book was being written while the first draft ofHoly Communion, Series Two, was at the printers. The appearance of the latter marked more of a watershed than was realized at the time. Elements of the Prayer Book are still to be found in the 1980 Book, but they have taken on the character of family heirlooms in a not wholly congenial setting. Other sources, other theologies .have inspired the liturgies produced since 1965. 

In future the liturgical forms of the Church of England will need to be studied on an equal footing with those of the other Churches and Provinces of the Anglican Communion. The appropriate metaphor is no longer mother and children, but cousins ofthe same generation. And all of these must be studied side by side with the rites of the Roman Catholic Church. History is still relevant, but it must include the New Testament and the great fourth-century liturgies as weIl as the Sarum Mass and Hermann's *Consultation.* It will derive its relevance from a comparative liturgiology which will embrace the whole contemporary scene.
