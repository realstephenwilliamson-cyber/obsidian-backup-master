Overview 

The *Vātulanāthasūtras* belong to the Kashmiri Krama tradition. The commentator presents them, no doubt as the tradition was in his day, as the oral teachings of Yoginīs. There is no way to fix a date either for the *sutras* or the commentator Anantaśaktipāda. But is no reason to doubt, as Anantaśakti affirms, that he text belongs to a Krama tradition that descends from Niṣkriyānandanātha a founder figure who belonged to the earliest phase of the development of an independent Krama school. Normally he appears in these sources as the founder, here instead we are told that he learnt from a certain Gandhamadana. Although they are Krama teachings of the sūtras are unusual. Indeed, some are unique, at least as far as the surviving texts allow us to judge. The terminology is also distinctive. A striking example is the key term *Mahāsāasa*. This term appears only once in all of Abhinavagupta’s works. And yet, the acme of practice taught here is centered on the operation of what is called *Mahāsāhasa* — the Great Audacity. This is a sudden extremely powerful irruption of expanding consuming pure consciousness. In his *Tantrāloka* Abhinavagupta defines and explains as follows: 

‘When this divine group of senses termed consciousness (*bodha*) and freedom (*svātantrya*) unfolds, it makes the universe one with its own nature. He who, united with the Great Audacity (*mahāsahāsa*), all the activity (of his senses) dissolved away, abides in a state of plenitude, the flux of the rays of his (senses) gathered together (within his own nature) and free of thought, there, (his) intuitive insight lucid, attains to that consciousness whose (mere) sparks are the sole means by which (the abode of) transmigration (*samsāra*) is burnt to ashes.’[^1] 

In his usual, extraordinary way Abhinavagupta sums up the essence of the practice taught here. Enlightenment takes place suddenly brought on by the most intense descent of grace. The senses (including the mind) and their activity are first divinized, and operate freely not just as instruments of perception but as channels through which consciousness makes the sphere of objectivity one with itself. Thus, along with that, in a flash of insight the senses also dissolve away into the expanse of the plenitude of the Void of consciousness free of thought constructs. The liberated state of perpetually expanding consciousness is as profoundly tranquil as it is immensely powerful. The process begins by withdrawal. Accordingly, the first seven aphorisms teach introverted transcendence by penetrating the center of universal consciousness discovered between the opposites represented by subject and object. They describe the movement towards inwardness and the intuitive insight of the Great Audacity - *Mahāsāhasa*. The most basic and essential feature of this instant of immediate insight is the opening up of the centre between the opposites. Expanding out into the Self, it consumes into its inner, devouring consciousness, all outer objectivity. This is the Great Void where all outer activity of the energies of consciousness fuse into the Great Oneness of their universal aesthetic delight

(*mahāsāmarasya*) in the Fourth state of consciousness (*turīya*) in which absolute reality is experienced directly. In this state of intense introversion the yogi is so deeply absorbed within himself that the activities of the senses and mind come to a halt. Then when he emerges from his inner ecstasy, the universe again appears in its diversity and temporality. However, he carries with him enough of the inner joy to perceive the outer world as a varied form of the indeterminate Brahman he has experienced internally although it is not firmly established. 

As a remedy for the yogi’s deficiencies, the following aphorisms from eight to eleven teach extroverted immanence, that is, the experience that the cycles of emanation and withdrawal are the energies of consciousness. The eighth aphorism teaches how subjectivity (*ahantā*) pours out into objectivity (*idantā*). In this way, ultimately the impermanence of the Fourth state is remedied and consolidated into the permanent stability of the Fifth liberated state. Now the universe appears to the liberated yogi as full of his own energies[^2]. The yogi now views the outer world and the activities of daily life as he abides in a state of inner absorption perceiving objects collectively as parts of objectivity (*idantā*) free of thought constructs. His insight illumines the world and transforms it as his energy that reigns master over all things pervades it. 

Aphorisms nine to eleven teach various aspects of this expansion and various ways it takes place. In aphorism nine the Self impels its interiority into the outer domains of the divinized creative energies called Hunger and Thirst etc. They are deployed externally and when all attachment has disappeared in the Fourth state into which it is consumed (*alaṁgrāsa*), the yogi establishes himself within himself fully and permanently. The tenth aphorisms goes a step further. The yogi maintains knowledge of his true nature not only when he exercises his powers and faculties but also when he rests within himself. The eleventh aphorism describes the opposite process in which the yogi replaces the activity inwardly and views the world within the Self. In other words objectivity (*idantā*) pours into subjectivity (*ahantā*) and disappears, dissolved away in the of the pure unconditioned Self[^3]. Penetration henceforth is instantaneous, not gradual (*akrama*). The twelfth aphorism teaches the same realization but from the point of view of action, that is, the yogi’s behavior which, no longer limited by the duality of ‘me’ and ‘other’, recovers its complete freedom. In the last aphorism, the yogi enters into the fully liberated state Anuttara — the Absolute Void beyond the Void represented by the letter A. 

Together these aphorisms teach *Kramamudrā*, which is the dynamism of the Skyfaring Goddess (*khecarīvṛtti*). Ksemarāja explains that it is the yogi’s experience of how ‘the outer penetrates the inner and then the inner penetrates into the true nature of the outer.’[^4] Kṣemarāja considered this Krama practice to be the highest and most fundamental of all. Accordingly, he concludes his *Pratyabhijñāhṛdaya* — the Heart of Recognition - with two aphorisms that describe it in more detail. There we read:

In his commentary on the first of these two aphorism Kṣemarāja quotes the *Kramasūtra*s for a full definition of *Kramamudrā*:. 

‘The yogi stamps (*mudrayati*) himself into the sequential (*krama*) rotation of the wheel of consciousness in its aspects of cosmic emanation, conservation and reabsorption and, by means of *Kramamudrā* he lays hold of what lies within himself (in the course of laying hold forcefully of his own consciousness). Such is the power of consciousness, the Fourth (state). The yogi is thus completely immersed in himself, even as he directs (his attention) outside. Even when he is engaged in the affairs of daily life, the deployment of the supreme power abides for him as an immediately perceivable reality. It is because of this that the yogi is very elevated. During this process, at first, (of objectivity) is consumed (into subjectivity) with progressively greater force. Outer objects plunge into the interior of this intensely dynamic consciousness (*parāciti*) and are consumed by it. Then, in the phase of emission (*vamana*), the penetration (of consciousness) continues (in reverse) extending from within the energy of consciousness out to the true essential nature of the outer (world), that is, the totality of objects that constitute objectivity (*idantā*). (Thus) this gesture (*mudrā*), which engages simultaneously the inner and outer (domains in this way), is a state of permanent penetration (of consciousness into consciousness).’[^6] 

Swami Lakṣmaṇjoo explains how he to practice *Kramamudrā.* ‘You experience the rising flow of *citkuṇḍalinī* as filling the whole channel from *mūlādhāra* to *brahmarandhra*. Here again you abruptly breathe out and your eyes are open. This lasts for only a moment and then you are again inside, without breathing, experiencing the rise of *Kuṇḍalinī*. Then again you breathe out and again your eyes are open and for a moment you feel that the outside world is full of ecstasy and bliss. This happens again and again. One moment you are inside experiencing the bliss of the rise of *citkuṇḍalinī* and then the next moment you breathe out and your eyes are open and you are experiencing the world filled with ecstasy. This process of coming out and remaining in continues and, each time it occurs, it is filled with more and more ecstasy. The process is called *Kramamudrā*.’[^7]

It has, as it were, (*upacārataḥ*) established itself (in that part of the body that) begins with the pit of the throat. 

3\) *Anāhatahata* - Unstruck — cum — Struck Sound — unfolds grounded in both (the two previous kinds of sound). It is not struck (sound) because the experience (*pratha*) (of it) has come forth from the Lord of the sense of hearing (*śravaṇagopana*) when the cavity of the ear is at rest (as there is no audible sound). But it is also ‘struck’ (sound) (that has come forth from) the space in the middle of the two ears of the body made of the reality levels (*tattvadeha*) reflected within it. 

4\) *Anāhatahatottīrṇa*. The sound that transcends the Unstruck — cum — Struck Sound is the initial point of the Great Vibration which is a subtle (motionless) motion. Devoid of though constructs, it pours forth in the great abode of unobscured (consciousness). Devoid as it is of contraction and expansion, (this) resonance (*svara*) is the supreme expansion (of consciousness) and its nature is the unfolding experience (*prathā*) of the Great Mantra that cannot be uttered and (the one reality) that is devoid of (all) contact (with anything else) (*asparśadharma*). 

Moreover, the sound that transcends the Unstruck — cum — Struck Sound is (also represented) in the form of the triangle. It is the Fourth (state) which is Raudrī’s nature. 

Unstruck — cum — struck Sound is Vāmā, the mouth (of the letter A), the energy of Unstruck Sound (*anacka*) and the dream state. 

Unstruck Sound is the power Ambikā who is described in the scriptures. (She is) the arms (of the letter A) and dream sleep is of that nature. 

Struck Sound is in the form of the weapon (of the letter A) and Jyeṣṭhā who is the waking state.

This is the teaching (*kathana*). It is a special teaching (*yukti*) transmitted by word of mouth through the lineage (of teachers), concerning (A) the first letter (of the alphabet) of which these four are its essential nature. 

Its ‘power’ (*bala*) is the vitality (*vīrya*) which is the vibrant effulgence (*sphurattā*) of the Sound that transcends the Unstruck — cum — Struck Sound that has come forth from the three, Struck Sound and the rest. Thus it is ‘the power of the revelation of the oral teaching (*kathā*) concerning (the letter) A (*akathana*)’. There in this way (from this perspective) the revelation of A (*akathana*) is ‘unutterable’ (*akathana*), that is, transcends the ongoing unfolding of speech (*vākprapañca*). ‘Revelation’ (*kathana*) is (undistracted) attention to the essential nature devoid of distinctive characteristics (*nirniketa*) (attained) by the process of direct transmission (*saṁkramaṇa*) (from the teacher). That itself is (its) power (*bala*) which is the essence of the expansion of (ultimate) uncreated (reality). Thus (the nature of this) direct transmission (*saṁkramaṇa*) (from the teacher) will be discussed here briefly. 

The most excellent teacher has firmly fixed (his) place in the Abode of Consciousness that shines intensely, (tranquil and) free of the waves (of activity) by virtue of the falling away of the false identification with the perceiver of the vital breath (in the waking state), the subtle body (when dreaming) and void (of deep sleep). Whatever (such a teacher) gazes upon with the power of the direct experience of the Supreme Void that is beautiful with unwavering bliss, perceiving (anything), whatever it be, independently of the act (of perception), its object and instrument, as his own omnipresent nature, it becomes the most supreme consciousness permanently (*satatam*). There can be no doubt about this. 

There is another (different) explanation, which is as follows. Revelation (*kathana*) manifests thus within (all the other) multitude (of teachings) of the six philosophies, four (Kaula) traditions and the process of union (between Siddhas and Yoginīs) by taking the support (of certain) fixed or undetermined assemblies of deities that arise in the course of the liturgy of worship. Here, however, on the contrary, according to the teaching of the oral tradition of the most excellent Siddhas such as the venerable Vātūlanātha, it shines majestically everywhere constantly as the expansion of the great rays (of consciousness) that are always successful (*siddha*), undivided from the all-transcendent (reality) by abandoning the relationship between the object of worship, the worshipper and worship. 

This is ‘the power of the revelation of the oral teaching (*kathā*) concerning (the letter) A (*akathana*) (that is unutterable)’. The ‘Great wonder’ is attained by that. This is the syntax (between the words of this aphorism). ‘The Great Wonder (*mahāvismaya*)’ is that from which ‘*smaya*’, that is, the pride of both the limited and unlimited ego has departed (*vigata*) and been destroyed (*vinaṣṭa*). It is entry into one’s own (true) nature (that takes place) by means of the (transcending) dynamic state (of consciousness) (*vṛtti*) that transcends (lit. ‘crosses over’) everything. 

Again, the Great Wonder is penetration into the dynamic state (*vṛtti*) of the perpetually unfettered Skyfarer. It takes place in an instant (*jhaṭiti*) by forgetting the duality of ‘self’ and ‘other’. As the state of penetration into the plane of silence it is this itself that is the (one unique) ‘seal’ (*mudrā*) because it includes all the (other) seals (within itself). 

 ‘The resonance of the Void’ has that as its cause. The resonance of the Void is the oneness of the revelation of the thirteen oral teachings (*kathā*). Its state is the experience of oneness (*sāmarasyaprathana*). This is the meaning.

The ‘resonance (*svara*) of the Void’ (is such because) it bestows (*rāti*) by itself (*svena*), the Void (of consciousness) and so too the absence of phenomenal existence that it pervades, appropriates and takes up (as its own). Such is (the meaning of the expression) ‘resonance of the Void’. 

The oral tradition, written in the form of aphorisms was revealed by the goddesses of the sacred seat during the Great Union (with them) to some realized Kaula renounciate (*avadhūta*) as the non-duality of the plane of Anuttara by means of the instruction (that engenders) the direct experience of the thirteen oral teachings (*kathā*) which is (the essence of the) oral teaching (imparted) in (the course) of all unions (between Siddhas and Yoginīs) and that of the four (Kaula) traditions and the six philosophies. I have made this brief commentary here of this very (teaching) in order to awaken the good (and the wise) (*sat*). (May this) be auspicious. 

Someone who is free of the error that divides up phenomenal existence (*bhava*) from the glory (of the Deity) (*vibhava*), has correctly (and loudly) proclaimed the incomparable and supreme mystery that is free of the flux of the thought constructs of speech by the dynamic modality (*vṛtti*) (of consciousness) which is the outpouring of the Audacity of undifferentiated Supreme Universal Being (that flashes forth all of a sudden) by the expansion of consciousness.
