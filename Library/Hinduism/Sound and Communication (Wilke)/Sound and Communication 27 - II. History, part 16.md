However projected and Western Berendt’ s “India” may be, the encounters and new experiences behind it were just as real, and the desire to “hear differently with Indian ears” was just as obvious. In Berendt one may see the prototype of the modern “spiritual wanderer.” What he “took on board” from or “heard in” his conversations with Indian musicians was “spirituality,” a new attitude to making and perceiving music, and a new systematization of the world. The Nāda-Brahman was the key to a new world-view and a different spirituality than the one of the European mainstream. It is the product of an interactive meeting between East and West, which changed the Indian musicians less than the Western recipients, and in a certain sense “Indified” the latter. It was a conscious, autonomous self-Indification because one found something in the “other” that was missing in one’s own culture and which brought Berendt to the formula “spirituality.” To him, spirituality meant an attitude and an altered world-view that was holistic, networked and monistic (he mentions the famous “tat tvam asi”), and consciously different from the rationality common in Europe. It put life in the more comprehensive context of a “sacred universe” that was not tied to any particular religion. Like other New-Age concepts, Berendts idea was a programmatic holism, a “spiritual” world-view uniting religion and science and at the same time representing a criticism and an alternative to both. It was a kind of third way discounting the scientific rationality and secularization of the world as one-sided and destructive, and also rejecting the traditional religious boundaries, in particular the dualistic Christian world-view. (It is true that he never criticizes Christianity, but interprets it in a non-Christian, “Nāda-like” manner.) Berendt’s Nāda-Brahman reception had the function of protest and of forming an alternative identity, and documents a shift in values and world-view that the sociologist Colin Campbell called “the Easternization of the West.”

However, the descriptive category of “Easternization” is not very apt. In contemporary Western societies several world-views, or elements of world-views, exist side by side — Christian, secular, civil religious, Eastern-inspired, transreligious, magic pagan, etc. — and not uncommonly a collage of all of them. Structural elements of Eastern world-views can certainly be identified and are extremely popular. Yet they mostly enter into new combinations and mix with religious elements, world-views and esotericism from all over the world, and with secular views, science and philosophy (or else sometimes are completely absent). Only a minority practice exclusively an Eastern tradition. Camp-bell was not analytical enough in his consideration of the religious composites and the hybrid nature of the new formations, the new semanticizations, extensions and shifts in function. This becomes apparent particularly in his central argument concerning reincarnation as spiritual evolution. This is a new category that is neither purely Eastern nor purely Western (and was presumably first formulated by the Hindu reformer Aurobindo, who himself was inspired by the West). The problem of thinking in cultural “blocks” occurs in Campbell in relation to several aspects and in a much more marked way than in Kurt. For example neither the East nor modern spirituality is solely mystic, intuitive and full of emotion. Nevertheless, we would like to argue that indeed there are noticeable structural similarities and correlations between modern spirituality and Hindu habitus forms to which Campbell’ s critics paid too little attention. Campbell is certainly correct in saying that the reception of Eastern teachings in the 19th century was practically restricted to intellectuals and literary products, whereas real-life encounters have taken place since the 1960s, and the 1970s were the main period when Indian thought became the general possession of a whole generation of Westerners. Yet in their time the actors mentioned (Müller, Vivekananda, and the Theosophists) were powerful “global players” with more than a little socio-political influence. They created a powerful image of India that became very real, penetrated into popular culture and also left its mark on Campbell’ ऽ representation. At the same time, their globalized concept of spirituality provided the foundation for the mixing of religions in the New Age. One can speak of the establishment of a non-Christian concept of religion that was also open to the non-religious and made it possible for this aspect to be linked with religion. Even if this new concept of pure spirituality was something that could effortlessly be separated from India, it still kept a vague touch of India. We share Campbell’s opinion that Eastern (in particular a selection of Hindu Indian) knowledge helped to fill a cultural vacuum for certain individuals and segments of the population and to bring about a kind of epistemological revolution because the range of transcendency and the interpretation of the world and of “I” were altered. The holistic frame of universal myth in its monistic pantheistic variations was especially suitable for de-culturation and the production of the new sign system of pure spirituality. Once in existence, this system became filled with completely new aspects. India offered several universal mythical models of holism, such as the Brahman concept of the Upaniṣads, Śañkara’ s tat-tvam-asi teaching, the dynamistic world-interpretations of the Tantra, and the Nāda-Brahman. The Nāda-Brahman in particular, in which so many traditions had merged, was a metaphor of holism whose vagueness meant that it could be especially well de-culturated and filled with world cultures. Moreover, the topic of “vibrations” could easily link it with popular and modern scientific discourses. 

“Easternization” does not appear to us to be a quite suitable term either for the phenomena mentioned by Campbell, or for the above-mentioned processes of globalization of Indian world-views. We would prefer to talk of inspiration and expanding one’s horizons, which led to new solutions to problems and altered forms of habitus, and not least allowed new holisms to arise whose function was to spiritualize life in

Yet we are of the opinion that the decisive turning point toward modern spirituality came in the 19th and early 20th centuries. At that time other inventors of modern spirituality also existed who had little or virtually nothing to do with India (such as propounders of Mesmerism or artists like Kandinsky). Yet we would postulate that the non-European extension of the religious and cultural world had an especially powerful impact and created new zones of contact and thought. One of the fascinating aspects is, for example, how easily urban reform Hindus like Vivekananda processed thoughts from the Western Enlightenment and were able to link them to their tradition, and also how willingly the Westerners (Americans) accepted his criticism of the Western lifestyle. India was often more an imaginary place of longing than a real one.
