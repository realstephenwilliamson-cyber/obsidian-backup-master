Chapter t. The Brahmayāmala and Tantric Literature I7

Since the completion of the doctoral thesis from which parts of this volume derive, there has been substantial growth in scholarly engagement with the BraYa. Its study received significant impetus from the 2008-12 Franco-German project “Early Tantra: Discovering the interrelationship and common ritual syntax of the Śaiva, Buddhist, Vaisṇnava and Saura traditions,” directed by Harunaga Isaacson and Dominic GooparL. Alongside the Nisvāsatattva-sambhitā, the BraYā was one of two Śaiva texts selected for collective study, for which purpose I contributed my provisional transcription of the old ms. (Nax 3-370, siglum A). Under the projects auspices, Csaba Krss commenced work as a postdoctoral fellow on what has now been published as The Brahmayāmala-tantra or Picumata, Volume U. The Religious Observances and Sexual Rituals of the Tāntric Practitioner: Chapters 3, 21, and 45. This erudite publication does much to clarify the Bra Yz’s fundamental rituals. The volume focuses on the practices of the sēdhaka, as expounded in the BraYæ’s abstruse and lengthy forty-fifth chapter (the sādhakādhikārapatala), which Kiss edits. In addition, Kīss provides careful critical editions of two more chapters: BraYa 3 (the mahāyāga-patala), which delineates the cults elaborate principal mandala; and Bra Yā <sup>21</sup> (the vratapatala), which concerns post-initiatory ascetic observances (vrata) for mastery of the BraYa’s principal mantra, the Nine-Syllable Vidya. Kiss’s contribution extends beyond the published volume, for he has made innumerable improvements to my draft transcription of the old manuscript, and has developed working critical editions of a number of chapters of the first half of the text. Another contribution is his insightful study of yantras in the BraYā and other early Śaiva tantras (Kiss 2014).

1.3 An overview of this volume

This book is divided into two parts. Part I contains an introduction to the BraYaā (chapters 1—2) and several topical studies (chapters 3—6). Part II consists of a critical edition and annotated translation of five of the BraYa’s chapters (patala): 1—2, 39—40, and 83. Spanning different sections, and perhaps distinct strata of the text, chapters 1, 39, and 83 nonetheless form a coherent group insofar as all three set forth narratives of origin—of the BraYa itself, of the Śaiva revelation, and of the accoutrements and practice of the “Great Vow” (mahāvrata), respectively. These narrative chapters stand apart from the rest of the text, which is otherwise almost entirely prescriptive in nature.’ Among these chapters, drafts of Bra Yā 1—2 were included in my doctoral thesis (HATLEY 2007), while the others have not previously been published.’\* An additional 300-odd verses of the Bra Yā are quoted as supporting evidence in various parts of this book, mainly in footnotes, with or without translation and with differing degrees of editing.

BraYaā 1, entitled “Chapter of the Connections” (sambandhapatala), narrates the text’s journey of descent (avatāra) into the world. Traversing multiple world-cycles in redactions of various length, the texts descent culminates in the Kali yuga with the present recension of twelve-thousand verses. An excursus on the cosmogonic supreme Śakti concludes the chapter (vv. 120-33). BraYā 2, “Chapter of the Extraction of Mantras” (mantroddhārapatala), continues this excursis, briefly delineating the pantheon of mantra-deities emerging from the supreme Sakti; these deities collectively comprise the Nine-Syllable Vidyā at the core of the BraYa’s system of ritual. Complementing BraYa 1's revelation narrative is Bra Ya 39, “Chapter of the Exposition on the Streams \[of Revelation\]” (srotranirnnayapatala’>). This situates the BraYā within the broader, Bhaira-vatantra-centric model of the Śaiva scriptural canon alluded to earlier (p. 9). Next is the edition of BraYa 40, “Chapter of the Razors Edge Observance” (asidhāravratapatala). Part I of this volume comprises six studies of the Bra Ya, which draw especially on the chapters edited in part II. Chapters 1—2 have broad concerns, and, along with Kiss’s (2015) introduction to volume II, serve to introduce the text as a whole. The present chapter serves as an introduction to the critical edition (\$1.1—6) and study of the text's structure, textual strata, provenance, and place in the history of Śaivism (§1.7—12). This involves, in part, determining the nature of the Śaiva canon known the BraYa’s redactors, primarily on the basis of BraYā 39. One of the key aims is to lay bare the Bra Ya’s redactional process, including its potential sources and indicators of distinct textual strata. Given the difficulty of dating the BraYā securely, an additional aim of this chapter is to situate the text in tantric literature through analysis of intertextuality and other indications of influence. For instance, I review indications of the Bra Ya’s adaption of material from the Nisvāsa corpus (91.9.1), especially the Džksottara section of the Niśsvāsakārikā, a supplement to the Nisvāsatattvasamhitā proper. Conversely, I provide additional evidence for SANDERSON’s (2001) contention that the Buddhist Laghusamvaratantra draws directly or indirectly upon the BraYa. I also suggest that the Bra Yā may itself have drawn upon an unknown Buddhist Kriyātantra, though the material in question may instead derive from a aiva medical tantra (51.9.4). An additional concern of this chapter is to review evidence for the text’s social and geographical horizons (§1.x11).

Entitled “To Name a Tantra: Genealogies of Scripture and the Canon of Śaivism in the Fpithets of the Brahmayāmala,” chapter <sup>2</sup>employs the text's titles and epithets as lenses into its redactional processes and construction of scriptural authority and identity. Subjects of discussion include the Yāmala-tantras as a genre, the role of “root scriptures” (mülatantra) in tantric revelation narratives (accounts of zantrāvatāra), the BraYas Nine-Syllable Vidyā, and the meanings of the obscure term picu. In addition, the chapter seeks to reconstruct what can be known of the Bra Ya’s only explicitly named source, the lost Ucchusmabhairava. Though wide-ranging, this chapter draws particularly on

the revelation narratives of Bra Ya <sup>1</sup>and 39.

Chapter 3 is entitled “Erotic Asceticism: The Razors Edge Observance (asidhārāvrata) and the Early History of Tantric Coital Ritual.”’ With Bra Ya 40 as its point of departure, this chapter examines shifting representations of the æsidhārāvrata across a range of Sanskrit literary and religious texts. Originally a Brahmanical ascetic discipline, an observance by this name appears to be the earliest ritual involving sexual contact documented in the corpus of Saiva tantras, with possible Atimārga roots. In its tantric adaptation, an orthodox practice for the cultivation of sensory restraint was transformed into a means for supernatural attainment (siddhi). Diachronic study of the observance in three early Saiva texts—the Niśvāsatattvasamhitā, Matangapārameśvara, and Brah-mayāmala—reveals changes in ritual emphases, women’s roles, and the nature of engagement in eroticism. Analysis of the vrata thus sheds light on the early history of tantric sexual rituals, which, as I show, by the end of the first millennium had become highly diverse. Within Śaivism, the observance became increasingly obsolete with the rise of sexual practices more magical, ecstatic, or gnostic in orientation.

The remaining chapters of part I (4—6) comprise an extended analysis of BraYā 83. In chapter 4, I examine the narratives of Bra Ya 83 in their cultic context, and alongside tellings of the Origin of the Lingam (Āñgodbhava) and Skull of Brahmā (brahmakapāla) myths in the purānas, Śivadharma corpus, and tantric literature. The chapter aims to identify Bra Yā 83’s sources, discuss their relative chronology and interrelationships, and to elucidate the ways in which the BraY?’s redactors reshaped Saiva myth to their distinctive cultic vision. A key question is the degree to which this narrative tradition may harken back to the elusive Atimārga Kāpālikas. Based on scattered evidence from sources such as the Skandapurāna and Āvantyakhanda, I argue that BraYa 83 could derive from a lost Atimārga source which integrated tracts on the paraphernalia and practice of the Great Vow with tales of Mahākāla's creation of the universe from a skull, his manifestation as a skull-bearing yogin from a luminous lingam, and Brahmā’s decapitation. Chapter 5, for its part, focuses on a lesser-known tale: the slaying of Khatvāsura and transfiguration of his corpse into Bhairava’s skull-staff (khatvānga). The principal aim is to demonstrate that this section of Bra Yā 83 was redacted into chapter 119 of the Devīpurāna, either directly or via chapter 68 of the \*Brahmayāmalasāra (Bra YāSāra). 1.4 Editing the Brahmayāmala

1.4.1 The manuscripts Five more or less complete manuscripts of the old Bra Yā survive: the eleventh-century palm-leaf ms. of the National Archives, Kathmandu (siglum A), and four Nepalese paper mss. (sigla B, C, D, and E). As intimated earlier, the paper MSS. all appear to descend from A, and thus have limited value for constituting the text. Among these, the pairs D and E, and to a lesser degree B and C, are closely related. While other possibilities cannot be ruled out definitively, I believe that the manuscript evidence for Bra Yā 1, reported fully in the critical edition, is sufficient to sustain the premise that neither B, C, D, nor E provide variants not attributable to scribal error or, in the case of B, editorial activity. This view has recently been corroborated by Kiss (2015: 58). After BraYa 1, the critical edition uses the paper mss. selectively: the editions of Bra Ya <sup>2</sup>and 39 report the readings of A, B, and D; BraYā 40 reports A and B; and Bra Ya 83 reports A and B, as well as, for approximately half of the chapter, ms. S, of the BraYāSāra. In addition to the aforementioned mss., a handful of others transmit smaller sections of the Bra Yā, though none include chapters edited in the present volume.
