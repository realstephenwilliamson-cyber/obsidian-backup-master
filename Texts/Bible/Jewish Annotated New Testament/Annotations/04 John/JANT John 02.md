### 2.1–12: Jesus’ first sign: wedding at Cana

*[[NRSVue John 02#^2-1|Text]], p. 178* ^2-1

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-1|1]]:** *Third day*, foreshadows the resurrection, just as the wedding alludes to the messianic banquet. *Cana*, village in Galilee, about 15 km (9 mi) north of Nazareth. *Mother of Jesus*, the Gospel never refers to Jesus’ mother by name. ^2-1-2

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-3|3]]:** Her statement implies that Jesus’ mother expects him to remedy the situation, though on what grounds is not explained. ^2-3

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-4|4]]:** *Woman*, an unusual and discourteous address to one’s mother, though Jesus uses it elsewhere to introduce a revelation (4.21; 19.26; 20.13,15). This mode of address implies a distance between Jesus and his mother, and it contrasts with the importance attached in this Gospel to the Father-Son relationship. *Hour*, of Jesus’ death and glorification (13.1); here it may also refer to the time when Jesus’ true significance for humankind will be fully revealed (as in 16.25). ^2-4

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-6|6]]:** *Rites of purification*, ritual hand washing preceding the meal (see *b. Ber*. [[Berakhot 53#53b|53b]]; *b. Shabb*. [[Shabbat 62#62b|62b]]). ^2-6

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-7|7]]:** *Fill the jars*, since they needed to be refilled, the washing likely had already taken place. The size of the jars indicates many guests. ^2-7

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-9|9]]:** *Bridegroom*, a double entendre referring to the bridegroom of the wedding and alluding to Jesus as the eschatological bridegroom (cf. 4.29). ^2-9

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-11|11]]:** *First of his signs*, possible evidence for a “signs source” (v 23; 3.2; 4.48,54; 6.14; 10.41; 12.18; 20.30). *Glory*, see 1.14. ^2-11

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-12|12]]:** *Capernaum*, town on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee. *Brothers*, see 7.3, 20.17; 21.23; [[Mark 06#^6-3|Mk 6.3]]. ^2-12

### 2.13–25: Jesus’ authority over the Temple

*[[NRSVue John 02#^2-13|Text]], p. 178* · See [[Matthew 21#^21-17|Mt 21.17]]; [[Mark 11#^11-15|Mk 11.15–19]]; [[Luke 09#^9-45|Lk 9.45–48]]. In the other Gospels, this incident occurs at the end of Jesus’ ministry. ^2-13

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-13|13]]:** *Passover*, a week-long spring pilgrimage festival celebrating the Exodus from Egypt and the barley harvest ([[Torah 15 - Bo#^12-1|Ex 12.1–18]]) and closely associated with liberation and salvation, past and future. *Of the Jews*, a redundancy likely indicating that some members of John’s audience lack acquaintance with Judaism. ^2-13-2

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-14|14]]:** *Cattle, sheep, and doves*, unblemished and therefore acceptable for sacrifice, were sold at the Temple. *Money changers*, converted foreign currencies into the required half-shekel for the Temple tax ([[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^30-11|Ex 30.11–16]]). ^2-14

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-15|15]]:** *Whip of cords*, the reference to this weapon is unique to John. ^2-15

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-16|16]]:** *Father’s house,* that is, the Temple. There may be a veiled polemic against the priests, as Jesus is here asserting his identity as Son of God and therefore the one who has jurisdiction over his “Father’s house.” *Marketplace*, literally, a “house of market,” to contrast with the “house of my father.” ^2-16

> [!note] Cleansing of the Temple (JN 2.13) *(p. 178)*
> The “Cleansing of the Temple” scene is present in all four Gospels. In the Fourth Gospel, the scene comes at the outset of Jesus’ ministry. It has two main points: to stake Jesus’ claim to the Temple —his Father’s house — and to identify Jesus’ body as the Temple, that is, as the locus for God’s abiding presence. The scene thus becomes an expression of Johannine Christology as well as a foreshadowing of Jesus’ death and resurrection. By contrast, the Synoptics place this event at the end of Jesus’ ministry. For these Gospels, the scene is the cat-alyst for the Jewish authorities’ plot against Jesus that culminates in his crucifixion (see [[Matthew 21#^21-10|Mt 21.10–17]]; [[Mark 11#^11-15|Mk 11.15–19]]; [[Luke 19#^19-45|Lk 19.45–46]]). Although some have argued that the author of the Fourth Gospel modified the Synoptic accounts, it is more likely that John and the Synoptics drew on a common source that each redacted to serve specific theological and narrative purposes.

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-17|17]]:** [[Psalms 069#^69-10|Ps 69.9]]; John’s quotation puts the verb in future tense, in line with the future understanding of the disciples; see 2.23. ^2-17

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-18|18]]:** *The Jews*, may be an example of the use of the generic plural to refer to a specific group, perhaps the Temple authorities; the Synoptic Gospels refer to chief priests, scribes, and leaders ([[Matthew 21#^21-14|Mt 21.14]]; [[Mark 11#^11-18|Mk 11.18]]; [[Luke 19#^19-47|Lk 19.47]]). *What sign*, a challenge to Jesus’ authority. ^2-18

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-19|19]]:** *Destroy this temple* . . . , alludes to the tradition of Temple critique (4.21; see also [[Jeremiah 07#^7-1|Jer 7.1–15]]; [[Jeremiah 35#^35-2|35.2–10]]; [[Ezekiel 10#^10-18|Ezek 10.18–19]]; [[Acts 07#^7-48|Acts 7.48]]) and continues the theme of Jesus’ authority over the Temple. *Three days I will raise it up*, a proleptic reference to the resurrection. ^2-19

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-20|20]]:** *Forty-six years*, Josephus states that construction began in the eighteenth year of Herod the Great’s rule (20/19 BCE; *Ant.* 15.380) and concluded under the Roman governor Albinus (*Ant*. 20.219) in 63 CE. Given that Jesus died some 30 years earlier, this reference contributes to the consensus concerning a post-70 dating of the Gospel. ^2-20

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-21|21]]:** *Temple of his body*, a proleptic reference to the crucifixion and resurrection as marking the destruction and restoration of Jesus’ body but also a reference to the Johannine theme that whereas the Temple in Jerusalem was formerly considered the site of most direct encounter with the divine, that role is now — since Jesus’ coming — fulfilled by Jesus. ^2-21

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-22|22]]:** *Scripture*, perhaps a reference to scripture in general, or to a special passage, such as [[Psalms 069#^69-10|Ps 69.9]] cited earlier. *The word*, the Gospel treats Jesus’ prophecies in the same way that it treats scriptural citations, implying their equal authority. ^2-22

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-23|23]]:** *His name*, expression of his true being and power. *Signs*, a general reference to Jesus’ deeds, not recounted in the Gospel (20.30 which indicates that the Gospel is a selective account). ^2-23

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-24|24]]:** *Entrust himself*, rely upon others to vouch for his authority. ^2-24

**[[NRSVue John 02#^2-25|25]]:** *Needed no one to testify*, forensic language referring to the testimony of witnesses evokes the theme of God as judge. *He himself knew*, Jesus’ foreknowledge (see 1.48; 4.17–18; 6.70). *M o u n t o f O l i v e s* S A M A R I A *Mt. > [!note] Jesus and the Jewish Festivals (JN 2.13) *(p. 179)*
> Jesus “goes up” to Jerusalem (“to go up” is the usual expression for travel to Jerusalem from any direction or elevation; see [[Zechariah 14#^14-16|Zech 14.16]], and the later rabbinic phrase “ascent for the pilgrimage festival”) for the pilgrimage festivals of Passover (2.13; 12.1) and Tabernacles/Sukkot/Booths (7.10), as well as for an unnamed festival that may have been Shavuot or the Feast of Weeks (5.1). On one Passover (6.4), Jesus remains in Galilee, and he feeds thousands of people from only a few pieces of bread and fish. This departure from
>
> Jewish custom, and Jesus’ own practice, constitutes a partial fulfillment of the prophecy he made to the Samaritan woman in 4.21 that the hour is coming when true worship of the Father will be tied neither to Mt. Zion (the Jews’ holy mountain) nor Mount Gerizim (the Samaritans’ holy mountain). Within the experience of the earliest audience, who heard this Gospel in the decades after the destruction of the Temple, this deviation from practice may reflect the view that Jesus has not only co-opted but also displaced the Jewish festivals.
