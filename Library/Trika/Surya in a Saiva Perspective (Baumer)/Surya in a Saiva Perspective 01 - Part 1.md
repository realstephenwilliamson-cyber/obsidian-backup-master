Sūrya in a Śaiva Perspective: 

The Sāmbapañcāśikā, A Mystical Hymn of Kashmir and its Commentary by Kṣemarāja[^1] 

No doubt one of the most poetic as well as mystical hymns to Sūrya is the *Sāmbapañcāśikā* or “Fifty Verses composed by Sāmba”. Its importance is further highlighted by its having received a commentary by Kṣemarāja (11th cent. AD), in the light of the Śivādvaita of Kashmir. André Padoux, who has translated this Stotra into French[^2], calls it “without doubt the most important hymn of adoration of the Sun that has come down to us”.[^3] This text has come down to us through the Advaita Śaivism of Kashmir, where it is still a part of the living tradition, whereas the Saura Tantric tradition has not survived, and there is not even a text to testify for it.[^4] My attention was drawn to this poetic as well as mystical hymn when I

In this paper I want to examine the question of the relationship between Sūrya and Śiva in the context of Kashmir Śaivism, and hence to discover the reason why this Stotra has become a part of the Śaiva tradition, at least since the time of Abhinavagupta (10th-11th cent.). It is because his gifted disciple Kṣemarāja, a prolific commentator of the Tantras and other basic texts such as the *Śiva Sūtras* and the *Spanda Kārikā*, has commented on this Stotra to the Sun-God which establishes the link between the two traditions: the Vedic-Pauranic-Tantric Saura and the non-dualist Śaiva schools. 

Five printed editions of this text are known to me: The first is a separate edition of the text with Kṣemarāja's Ṭīkā in the Kāvyamālā Series of Nirṇaya Sāgar Press (1889). This edition is based on two manuscripts found in Kerala and Jaipur, but originally from Kashmir (the script is not mentioned). The second is contained in the first volume of *Tantrasaṃgraha* edited by Gopinatha Kaviraja from the Sanskrit University Varanasi (1970). This edition also contains Kṣemarāja's Ṭīkā and is based on three manuscripts in Śāradā script from the Sarasvati Bhavan Library. The third is a separate edition of the Stotra without Ṭīkā but with a Hindi translation and copious notes by Swami Ishvar Svarupji (Brahmacari Lakshmanji), published from Ishvar Ashram, Srinagar (originally published in 1944, 2nd ed. 1976). This edition contains a verse 53 which is not found in every edition. The fourth is the same but with the commentary by Kṣemarāja.[^6] The fifth is an edition of text and commentary with Hindi translation and notes by Nilkanth Gurtoo[^7]. 

The age of the text can only be determined by its upper limit, because of the commentary by Kṣemarāja, a direct disciple of Abhinavagupta of the 11th century. This fact also shows the early inclusion of this Stotra in the advaitic Śaiva tradition

Apart from the number of verses (the first and last verses need not be counted, being *maṅgalācaraṇa* and colophon respectively), the attribute *vedaguhyapadākṣaraiḥ* would also be fitting. However, the purpose of this Stotra is primarily spiritual and the recovery of a healthy body by the praise of the Sun-God seems to be only a secondary effect (cf. v. 46). 

The hymn belongs purely to the Saura tradition, and is comparable to other Stotras to the Sun God.[^10] It contains only two references to Viṣṇu and none to Śiva. This process was surely facilitated in the case of the Advaita Śaiva tradition of Kashmir because the Sun became a symbol of the Supreme Light of Consciousness (*cit, saṃvit, prakāśa)* and was hence identified with Śiva. Besides, the Kashmiri *karmakāṇḍa* has preserved some Vedic traditions, and even today Sūrya worship is part of the daily ritual of Kashmiri Śaivas.[^12] Hence, unlike Viṣṇu, Sūrya is not considered to be a distinct deity, and his *mantras* and *stotras* are very much a part of the tradition. 

One of the most important symbolic links between the Vedic and Tantric traditions is certainly the triad of luminaries: Sūrya, Soma and Agni, or Sun, Moon and Fire. Whereas they are important divinities in the *Ṛgveda*, with a great number of hymns devoted to their praise, we find a symbolic shift from their cosmic and divine roles to the spiritual or interiorized interpretation in the Tantras. The *Sāmbapañcāśikā* (*SP*) shows precisely this transition by giving all the three dimensions of the Sun as a cosmotheandric reality, while emphasising the spiritual aspect which accords with the Tantric or Advaita Śaivāgama interpretation. We have to distinguish three aspects of Sūrya: one is considered as a form or manifestation of Śiva, e.g. in the concept of the *aṣṭamūrti*, the eight forms with which Śiva is identified;[^13] the second is his interiorised identification with *prāṇa*, as we shall see; and the third is the Sun as the central divinity, visible symbol of the Divine, in non-dualistic Śaivism identified with the pure Consciousness, hence called by Kṣemarāja *cidarka*, *citsūrya*, etc. All the three aspects are found in the *SP*, although it is Sūrya as the central divinity who is invoked throughout the Stotra. It is in Kṣemarāja’s commentary that the identification of Sūrya with Śiva takes place, insofar as both represent the pure and non-dual Consciousness. This identification is based on the philosophy of *prakāśa-vimarśa* of Trika or

After a brief survey of the themes and symbolism contained in the SP, I will present a translation of the verses with a summary of the commentary and a brief interpretation. 

We find much of the usual symbolism connected with Sūrya, both Vedic and Paurāṇic. Full use is made of the symbolism of Light as such, in its physical, spiritual and metaphysical aspects. Though the Stotra does not use expressions which are used by Kṣemarāja like *cidarka* or *cidbhānu*, it is obvious that the physical aspect of the sun is primarily a symbol of its spiritual aspect. The only reference to the iconography of Sūrya is the image of the solar chariot drawn by seven horses which appears immediately in the opening verse and is interpreted in terms of the mystical Word, where the seven horses are identified with the seven *svaras,* the rising of the sun with the manifestation of *Oṃ* (*udgītha*), and so on. Here the tone is set for the esoteric interpretation of both, the physical and the mythical Sun (v.1). 

In connection with the physical sun, moon and the divisions of time – day and night, the three *sandhyās*, the bright and dark fortnights, the seasons, *uttarāyaṇa* and *dakṣiṇāyana*, the equinoxes, and so forth – the Stotra also gives an 

*[p. 6]* ^pg10

esoteric and yogic interpretation of time (vv. 19,28,29,49).[^14] Besides, the Sun as the giver of life and light is praised in its biological as well as spiritual significance (*prāṇabhṛtsūrya ātmā,* v, 24). The mythical conception of Soma, the waxing and waning of the moon connected with the drinking of *amṛta* by the gods, ancestors, and men, is also given an esoteric interpretation (vv. 8,27, 43). At the human level we find the role of Sūrya in overcoming the darkness of sin and ignorance, fear of old age and death (vv. 37, 38, 40, 41) and as giver of health and happiness (v.46). The sense organs (*karaṇas*) are not seen negatively as in Vedānta, but as part of manifestation (v. 3) and having their important role to play in liberation (v. 47). The poet also makes his personal confession as a devotee (v. 33); he is very sincere when speaking of sin and attachment and praying for release (vv. 38-39). 

However, Āditya is not only a mythical deity, the physical sun and a grand symbol of light; it is the Divine itself, both transcendent and immanent (*parabrahman* and *antarātmā,* vv. 1,14,15), of universal form (*viśvamūrti*, v. 48), friend of the world (*jaganmitra*, v. 35), beyond all pairs of opposites (v. 25), father and saviour (vv. 39, also 37,38), the giver of liberation (*nirvāṇa*, also of *bhukti* and *mukti*, vv. 44,46), who can only be realized by grace (v.20), to whom devotion and surrender is highly recommended (vv.16,17,45). 

Among the symbolic elements we should also mention an important symbolism of numbers, mainly of the numbers three and seven connected with Sūrya, but also twelve (v. 3): For three we find the Vedas, the stages of the Word (i.e. *paśyantī, madhyamā* and *vaikharī),* the *guṇas*, the *lokas*, the *sandhyās*, *soma*, *agni, sūrya* in a yogic sense, *sṛṣṭi, sthiti* and *laya* in the macro- and microcosmic sense (vv. 7,16,22). For seven we find the horses of Sūrya's chariot, the *svaras*, the *lokas*, the *ṛṣis,* the islands, the mountains, the hells, the oceans, the seeds, the metres, and the mental afflictions (*ādhis*) (vv. 1,23).

From the very beginning the *Stotrakāra* establishes a connection between the Sun and the Word, Speech or Sound. “The supreme Energy of Knowledge shines in the image of the Sun.” In verse 2 he identifies the supreme Āditya with *Oṃ*, the *mantra* that is as central in the realm of speech as the Sun is in the cosmos: 

*omityantarnadati niyataṃ yaḥ pratiprāṇi śabdo vāṇī yasmātprasarati parā śabdatanmātragarbhā* /

Kṣemarāja identifies this Paramāditya with *parabrahmasvarūpa*, the embodied essence of the Supreme Brahman which, in its microcosmic form, is established in the body as the *prāṇa* which has entered the central vein (*dehasthaṃ madhyanāḍīgataprāṇa*..). He paraphrases the simple expression of adoration (*prapadye*) by the significant *samāviśāmi*, i.e. I merge completely with the object of adoration, by awareness on the juncture (of the two breaths), *samāveśa* being the highest stage of fusion, absorption or union with the Divine in Śaivism. 

He paraphrases *Oṃ* as that sound which contains within itself all words in condensed form (*kroḍīkṛtāśeṣaśabdanātma-nādarūpa*), and which resounds in all living beings as the Supreme Word. From *Parā,* the second stage of the Word, proceeds *Paśyantī*, the “visionary”, intuitive stage. 

Thus from the very beginning are stated the three domains in which the Divine Sun operates: the inner yogic domain of the *prāṇas*, the domain of sound and Word, and the Ultimate Divinity, of which the cosmic sun is but a symbol. The theme of the Word with its three or four stages will recur many times in the Stotra itself.

The microcosmic meaning of the Sun in the body with its organs is brought out in verse 3, where, again, the positive view of the senses agrees with the Advaita Śaiva view. Kṣemarāja introduces verse three with these words: 

“Having thus bowed to the Sun of Consciousness seated in the central domain by means of the great penetration (*mahāvyāpti*) and the subtle penetration (*sūkṣmavyāpti*) without pause or interruption, he (thereafter) also praises (him) by the physical penetration.” *yastvakcakṣuḥśravaṇarasanāghrāṇapāṇyaṅghrivāṇī pāyūpasthasthitirapi manobuddhyahaṃkāramūrtiḥ* /
*tiṣṭhatyantarbahirapi jagadbhāsayandvādaśātmā mārtaṇḍaṃ taṃ sakalakaraṇādhāramekaṃ prapadye* // (3) I worship the Sun (Mārtaṇḍa) who is the one support of all the senses (*karaṇa*), who in twelve forms manifests the world, being established internally and externally, of the form of the skin, the eye, the ear, taste, smell, the hands, the feet, speech, the anus and the generative organ (outside), and the mind, the intellect and the ego-sense (inside). 

The name Mārtaṇḍa is significant not only because the great Sun Temple of Kashmir is devoted to him, but because the composite form of Sūrya and Bhairava is called Mārtaṇḍa-Bhairava.[^16] Kṣemarāja provides a *nirvacana* explanation of this very name,[^17] and calls the Sun of Consciousness “one without a second, because he has pervaded all experients by way of his physical penetration.”

Verse 4 describes the arising of the Word in its three stages, which corresponds with the doctrine of *vāc* developed by Abhinavagupta. 

*yā sā mitravaruṇasadanāduccaranto triṣaṣṭiṃ varṇānatra prakaṭakaraṇaiḥ prāṇasaṅgāt prasūtān* /

Kṣemarāja quotes a definition of *paśyantī* as *svarūpajyoti*, the light of one’s own inmost nature, and he infers the fourth and highest stage (*parā*) of *vāc* as that from which the others proceed. He identifies it with the “central domain” (*madhyadhāma*), where the two deities, Agni and Soma, are unified. That his interpretation is not a forced one becomes clear in the next verse, where the supreme Āditya is identified with the central vein of the yogic body (*brahmanāḍī).* 

*ūrdhvādhaḥsthānyatanubhuvanānyantarā saṃniviṣṭā nānānāḍiprasavagahanā sarvabhūtāntarasthā* /
*prāṇāpānagrasananirataiḥ prāpyate brahmanāḍī sā naḥ śvetā bhavatu paramādityamūrtiḥ prasannā* // (5) 

May the pure white image of the supreme Āditya which has entered in between the upper and lower worlds be propitious unto us! That (Sun) who dwells within all beings, having entered all the manifold veins, is (verily) the *brahmanāḍī* which is attained by (the *yogins*) who are engaged in absorbing the inhalation and exhalation (between the two).

Kṣemarāja identifies the divine form of the Sun of Consciousness which is shining in its whiteness with the intensity of Consciousness-and-Bliss (*śvetā-cidānandaghanatayā sphurantī*). Swami Lakshman Joo’s commentary is revealing: “By the experience of *brahmanāḍī* one obtains the knowledge of all the worlds. This is the reason why it is said that all the worlds are contained within the *brahmanāḍī*.” (*Sāmbapañcāśikā*, p. 5, note) 

The same idea is developed in the following verse, the connection being established by repeating the last line: 

*na brahmāṇḍavyavahitapathā nātiśītoṣṇarūpā no vā naktaṃdivagamamitātāpanīyāparāhuḥ* /
*vaikuṇṭhīyā tanuriva rave rājate maṇḍalasthā sā naḥ śvetā bhavatu paramādityamūrtiḥ prasannā* // (6) 

Propitious unto us be that pure white form of the supreme Āditya which is concealed on the cosmic path, which is neither too cold nor too hot, which is not limited by night and day, which does not burn and does not fall prey to Rāhu. As a heavenly body, it dwells in the disk of the Sun. 

Commenting on this verse, Kṣemarāja develops the idea of the overcoming of all dichotomies: “What is extremely cold is Soma (the moon), and what is extremely hot is Agni (fire); by overcoming this duality in the bliss of non-duality, it (Paramāditya) is not like either, but is a harmonious fusion of Agni and Soma (*samarasībhūtāgniṣomātmā*).” 

The next verse refers to the two aspects of the Sun, the cosmic and the spiritual, in reference to the way to liberation. 

*yatrārūḍhaṃ triguṇavapuṣi brahma tadbindurūpaṃ yogīndrāṇāṃ yadapi paramaṃ bhāti nirvāṇamārgaḥ* /
*trayyādhāraḥ praṇava iti yanmaṇḍalaṃ caṇḍaraśme rantaḥ sūkṣmaṃ bahirapi bṛhanmuktaye’ haṃ prapannaḥ* // (7) For attaining liberation I take refuge in that orb of the Sun, in whose body consisting of the three *guṇas* is present Brahman in the 

*[p. 12]* ^pg16

form of *bindu*, and which is the supreme goal and the way to *nirvāṇa* for the great yogins. In the form of *Om* it is the support of the three Vedas; internally it is subtle, and outside it is great. 

For Kṣemarāja, the way of *nirvāṇa* means the way to attain liberation-inlife, which he describes as perfect immersion (*samāveśa-jīvanmukti*). In this context he mentions meditation on the supreme nature of Parameśvara (*vimṛśyate paraṃ parameśvaraṃ svarūpam*), one of the rare cases where an identification of Sūrya and Śiva as Parameśvara is expressed. 

Verse 8 shows the difference between Sūrya and Soma: 

*yasminsomaḥ surapitṛnarairanvahaṃ pīyamānaḥ kṣīṇaḥ kṣīṇaḥ praviśati yato vardhate cāpi bhūyaḥ* /
*yasminvedā madhuni saraghākāravadbhānti cāgre taccaṇḍāṃśoramitamamṛtaṃ maṇḍalasthaṃ prapadye* // (8) I take refuge in the unlimited nectar of immortality present in the orb of the Sun into which enters the moon, having been reduced as the gods, ancestors and men drink it, but which again increases (and becomes full), in which the Vedas shine since the beginning, just like bees on honey. 

Kṣemarāja explains Soma as the group of objects (*meya*), and the gods, ancestors and men as the classes of subjects (*pramātṛ*) who, after decreasing the objects of perception, again come back to fullness “by merging in the quietude of the heart” (*praviśanti hṛdviśrāntyantaraṃ nimajjanti*). Here again, the cosmic process of the waxing and waning of the moon is compared to the spiritual process moving from distraction to inner fullness and rest. 

The simile of bees and honey is used by Kṣemarāja to show the unity of the meaning of all the Vedas in the “nectar of supreme non-duality” (*tathā sarve vedā vākyaikavākyatayā parādvayamamṛtam āmṛśya bhrājante*.) 

Verse 9, addressing the Sun as *viśvāloka* (light of the whole world), alludes to the deed of Viṣṇu (Hari) who, as Vāmana or Trivikrama, traverses the cosmos with his three steps and banishes Bali into the netherworld. The solar nature of 

*[p. 13]* ^pg17

Viṣṇu is evident in that his steps are identified with the phases of the Sun’s rising and setting. (I skip the commentary). 

*aindrīmāśāṃ pṛthukavapuṣā pūrayitvā krameṇa krāntāḥ sapta prakaṭahariṇā yena pādena lokāḥ* /
*kṛtvā dhvāntaṃ vigalitabalivyakti pātālalīnaṃ viśvālokaḥ sa jayati raviḥ sattvamevordhvaraśmiḥ* // (9) 

May Ravi be praised, the Light of the whole universe, whose rays on high are pure, who, in the form of Viṣṇu (as the Vāmana *avatāra*) has ascended the seven worlds with his foot, after having crushed the body of Bali who disappeared into *pātālaloka*, and who gradually fills the eastern region with his young, luminous body. 

Verse 10 again relates to verse 2 while referring to the sound (the same verb *nadantam* is used), to *praṇava* (*Om*) and to the Gāyatrī mantra. 

*dhyātvā brahma prathamamatanu prāṇamūle nadantaṃ dṛṣṭvā cāntaḥ praṇavamukharaṃ vyāhṛtīḥ samyaguktvā* /
*yattadvede taditi saviturbrahmaṇoktaṃ vareṇyaṃ tadbhargākhyaṃ kimapi paramaṃ dhāmagarbhaṃ prapadye* // (10) Meditating on the bodiless Brahman in the beginning, which resounds at the root of *prāṇa* (*mūlādhāra*), and having seen it within (the heart), uttering at first the *praṇava* (*Om*) with the *vyāhṛtis*, and what is called *tat* in the Veda, uttered by Brahman as *saviturvareṇyam –* in that luminous domain, which holds within it the supreme glory, I take refuge. 

Kṣemarāja ‘translates’ the Vedic Gāyatrī into his own system: *savitur* is explained to be “of the Sun of Consciousness: because it engenders (*prasūtihetoḥ*) (it is called Savitṛ)". *Bharga*, splendour, is interpreted as the supreme brilliance of Energy (*śāktaṃ tejas*), and the word *vareṇya* in the Gāyatrī is explained as “the bestower of grace” (*anugrahakāri*). This is a very interesting Śaiva hermeneutics of the most important Vedic *mantra*. In the context of its recitation (*uccāra* and *japa*) 

*[p. 14]* ^pg18

he quotes the *Śivasūtra*: *kathā japaḥ* (III.27); at the highest level of the *śāmbhava yogī*, this means that even (his) ordinary speech becomes a *mantra*, because “it is sprinkled with the essence of non-duality” (*tadabhedarasaprokṣitām*…). 

Kṣemarāja refers to the *Bhargaśikhā* (Tantra) where Brahman (and by implication Sūrya) is called Vīreśvara[^18], and its phonic manifestation is *akāra* (standing always for *anuttara*). The same is also called “the luminous abode of supreme Energy” (*paraśāktaṃ dhāma*). 

The theme of the Word and sound, which pervades the entire Stotra, has its importance also in the very fact of singing the praises in form of a hymn, and the *Stotrakāra* is conscious of its implications, as expressed in verse 11: 

*tvāṃ stoṣyāmi stutibhiriti me yastu bhedagraho’yaṃ saivāvidyā tadapi sutarāṃ tadvināśāya yuktaḥ* /
*staumyevāhaṃ trividhamuditaṃ sthūlasūkṣmaṃ paraṃ ca vidyopāyaḥ para iti budhairgīyate khalvavidyā* // (11) 

If I say: “I will praise you with songs (*stuti*)”, this is a sign of duality (*bheda*); it is my ignorance. But this (kind of ignorance) is appropriate for destroying itself even more. 

Verily I praise (you), who are manifest in three forms, gross, subtle and supreme. The wise have said that the means to attain wisdom is ignorance. 

The thought of praising the Lord is a sign of duality, and hence ignorance, which means non-realisation of the non-dual nature of Consciousness. But even this ignorance is fitting for uprooting ignorance, for, one worships you by merging one's identification of the subject with the body. The one who is worshipped is found at the three levels of *sthūla-sūkṣma-para.* This is a pervasive scheme of interpretation in the Āgamas, and in the case of Sūrya it can be easily applied.

Kṣemarāja identifies them as follows: “The gross (physical) is the external form of the sun as life (*prāṇārkarūpam*); the subtle is Brahman as breath, situated in the central vein (*madhyanāḍīgataṃ prāṇabrahmarūpam*), and the supreme is the uninterrupted universal condensation of Consciousness-and-Bliss.” 

*Stuti* or praise is of the nature of the *parāmarśa* (meditative reflection) of *paramātmā* who is a mass of Consciousness-and-Bliss; it pacifies the seed of ignorance, the false conceit which consists in identifying the body with the self, created by the imaginary separation between the praiser and the praise, and it bestows the supreme union (*samāveśa*). 

Having justified his right to offer praise even within a non-dual background, the *Sotrakāra* gives free expression to his adoration in theological terms: 

*yo’nādyanto’ pyatanuraguṇo’ṇoraṇīyānmahīyān viśvākāraḥ saguṇa iti vā kalpanākalpitāṅṅgaḥ* /
*nānābhūtaprakṛtivikṛtīrdarśayanbhāti yo vā tasmai tasmai bhavatu paramāditya nityaṃ namaste* // (12) O supreme Āditya! You are without beginning and end, bodiless and free from qualities, subtler than the subtle and greater than the great, the whole universe is your form; you have also been conceived with qualities, you who manifest the nature and the transformation of the manifold beings: to you be perpetual adoration! 

There is a clear reference to the Upaniṣads where Brahman is described as *aṇoraṇīyān mahato mahīyān*,[^19] “smaller than the smallest and greater than the greatest”; but in the next verse there follows a criticism of the Upanishadic-Vedāntic description of the Ultimate Reality as *neti neti:* 

*tattvākhyāne tvayi munijanāḥ neti neti bruvantaḥ śāntāḥ samyaktvamiti na ca tairīdṛśo veti coktaḥ* /
*tasmāttubhyaṃ nama iti vacomātramevāsmi vacmi prāyo yasmātprasaratitarāṃ bhāratī jñānagarbhā* // (13) 

[^19]: Cf. *Kaṭha Upaniṣad* 2.20.

*[p. 16]* ^pg20

While trying to describe the true Reality, the sages exhaust themselves by repeating: “not this, not this” – but nobody knows you as you really are. Therefore, only by saying the word *namaḥ*: “Adoration to you!” does the Divine word proceed, pregnant with knowledge. 

Kṣemarāja stresses the indescribability of the Supreme, which even by negative statements such as *neti neti* cannot be described. Instead, the simple expression of praise *tubhyaṃ namaḥ* comes down from the supreme Word to the level of *paśyantī*, which is filled with the self-effulgent consciousness (*jñānagarbhā svāvabhāsa saṃvitpradhānā prasarati).* 

The question of how the Supreme Āditya can be known is carried further in the next verse: 

*sarvāṅgīṇaḥ sakalavapuṣāmantare yo’ntarātmā tiṣṭhankāṣṭhe dahana iva no dṛśyase yuktiśūyaiḥ* /
*yaśca prāṇāraṇiṣu niyatairmathyamānāsu sadbhir dṛśyaṃ jyotirbhavasi paramāditya tasmai namaste* // (14) 

O Supreme Āditya! adoration to you, the innermost self within the bodies of all beings. Just as fire hidden in wood is not visible, so you are also not seen by those devoid of discrimination. But when the wise churn the fire-sticks of breath, you become visible as light. 

Kṣemarāja uncovers the meaning implied in the simile of the fire-sticks (*araṇi*): inhalation and exhalation are the two sticks by whose friction as *yoga* or meditation the light of Consciousness shines forth. An identification of the Vedic *yajña* with the yogic meditation by means of *prāṇa* is also implied here. 

*stotā stutyaḥ stutiriti bhavān kartṛkarmakriyātmā krīḍatyekastava nutividhāvasvatantrastato’ham* /
*yadvā vacmi praṇayasubhagaṃ gopate tacca tathyaṃ tvatto hyanyatkimiva jagatāṃ vidyate tanmṛṣā syāt* // (15) 

*[p. 17]* ^pg21

You are the one who praises, you are the object of praise and you are the praise itself – being one (only), you play (the roles of) doer, action and its result. Therefore I am not able to offer praises to you. But, O Sun, whatever beautiful words I utter out of love for you, that is real. For what is there in this world other than you? (If there were anything else) it would be unreal. 

Kṣemarāja comments: “O Gopati, i.e. Lord over the circle of the rays of Consciousness! Being one without a second, he plays in the form of the hymn etc., i.e. shines in the respective forms out of his very own freedom.” Here he quotes his own *Bhairavānukaraṇastotra*, stating in a similar vein that there is no difference between the singer of praise, the one praised and the praise itself (*stotā stutyaḥ stutiriti yadapi vibhinnaṃ na kiñcidasti).* 

*jñānaṃ nāntaḥ karaṇarahitaṃ vidyate’smadvidhānāṃ tvaṃ cātyantaṃ sakalakaraṇāgocaratvādacintyaḥ* /
*dhyānātītastvamiti na vinā bhaktiyogena labhyas tasmādbhaktiṃ śaraṇamamṛtaprāptaye’haṃ prapannaḥ* // (16) For people like us there is no knowledge without an internal organ, but you are unthinkable, being completely outside the range of all the senses. You are beyond (the reach of) meditation, and you cannot be attained without the practice of devotion (*bhaktiyoga*). Therefore I take refuge in devotion for attaining immortality. 

Kṣemarāja quotes here a verse from the *Vijñāna Bhairava* extolling intense devotion (v. 121). 

*hārdaṃ hanti prathamamuditā yā tamaḥ saṃśritānāṃ sattvodrekāttadanu ca rajaḥ karmayogakrameṇa* /
*svabhyastā ca prathayatitarāṃ sattvameva prapannā nirvāṇāya vrajati śamināṃ te’rka bhaktistrayīva* // (17) 

O Sūrya! When you first arise, you drive away the darkness (*tamas*) from the hearts of the devotees, then you remove *rajas* (passion) due to predominance of *sattva* (purity). (Finally) you 

*[p. 18]* ^pg22

increase *sattva* by progressive *karmayoga*, which has been properly practiced. 

Devotion to you, like the three Vedas, leads those peaceful ones to liberation. 

*Nirvāṇa* is commented upon as liberation, consisting in the revelation of the unity of the soul and Śiva. 

*tāmāsādya śriyamiva gṛhe kāmadhenuṃ pravāse dhvānte bhātiṃ dhṛtimiva vane yojane brahmanāḍim* /
*nāvaṃ cāsminviṣamaviṣayagrāsasaṃsārasindhau gaccheyaṃ te paramamamṛtaṃ yanna śītaṃ na coṣṇam* // (18) Having attained that (*bhakti*), she is like the goddess Śrī (prosperity) in the house, like Kāmadhenu in a foreign place, like a torch in darkness, like fearlessness in a forest, like the central vein (*brahmanāḍī*) in *yoga*, and in the ocean of *saṃsāra* full of the crocodiles of the various sense objects, she is like a (saving) boat. May I reach your supreme immortality which is neither cold nor hot. 

Kṣemarāja paraphrases *brahmanāḍī* as: “Like *brahmanāḍī* or *suṣumṇā* in uniting, i.e. penetration in oneness with the being of the Supreme Śiva” (*yojane paraśivaikyāveśe*…). 

*agnīṣomāvakhilajagataḥ kāraṇaṃ tau mayūkhaiḥ sargādāne sṛjasi bhagavan hrāsavṛddhikrameṇa* /
*tāvevāntarviṣuvati samau juhvatāmātmavahnau dvāvapyastaṃ nayasi yugapanmuktaye bhaktibhājām* // (19) O Lord! Agni and Soma are the causes of the whole world. By your rays you create their emission and withdrawal, in the order of decrease and increase. 

You lead the two equally in the inner juncture (*viṣuvat*), offering them in the fire of the Self, and you dissolve them in an instant to give liberation to your devotees. 

*[p. 19]* ^pg23

Agni and Soma, the complementary powers, are identified with *prāṇa* and *apāna*; they 'cause the whole world' because they cause the appearance and stability of everything (*prakāśana-avasthānahetutvāt kāraṇam*). 

*sthūlatvaṃ te prakṛtigahanaṃ naiva lakṣyaṃ hyanantaṃ sūkṣmatvaṃ vā tadapi sadasadvyaktyabhāvādacintyam* /
*dhyāyāmītthaṃ kathamaviditaṃ tvāmanādyantamantas tasmādarka praṇayini mayi svātmanaiva prasīda* // (20) 

O Sun! your gross form is by nature impenetrable, as being infinite it cannot be attained, and your subtle form is unthinkable, because it is neither existing nor non-existing. How shall I meditate on you in this way, since you are not known, without beginning or end? Therefore, O Sun, grace me who pray to you! 

The subtle form of the Sun is identified by Kṣemarāja with the supreme Knower (*parapramātrātma*), and hence is unthinkable because it cannot become an object of thought. The necessity of grace (*anugraha*) would be fitting to Trika theology, but he explains it differently, as removing the impurity of mistaking the body for the real subject. 

*yattadvedyaṃ kimapi paraṁ śabdatattvaṁ tvamanta-statsadvyaktiṁ jigamiṣu śanairlāti mātrākalāḥ khe* /
*avyaktena praṇavavapuṣā bindunādoditaṁ sacchabdabrahmoccarati karaṇavyañjitaṃ vācakaṃ te* // (21) As far as your nature is concerned, you are the inner reality of the Word, which wishes to reach manifestation. That (*śabdatattva*) gradually brings about the parts and elements of speech in the space of consciousness. The Brahman-that-is-sound, arising from *bindu* and *nāda*, when uttered through the organs, is your expression. 

Kṣemarāja interprets this extraordinary verse which connects the Sun with the essence of the Word and of language (*śabdatattva*) in the light of the “unstruck sound” (*anāhata),* quoting the *Svacchanda Tantra*. He relates it to the spiritual state 

*[p. 20]* ^pg24

which 

is 

of 

the 

nature of unbroken light of interiority (*tadantarityantarmukhānavachinna prakāśātmani pada*). The Word or language is taken at its original, subtle stage, before any human expression or activity (*Svacchanda Tantra*: *yasya rūpaṃ śarīraṃ vā nāsti varṇaḥ kriyā tathā, sa kathaṃ gṛhyate sūkṣmo hyagrāhyo nityamavyayaḥ*). It is ungraspable, but it manifests in the space of consciousness (*khe*), which he interprets as the “abode or inner space of the *susumnā*”, where the inner unstruck sound resounds as *a-u-m*, whose *mātrās* are energies of *vimarśaśakti*. Thus the Sun-of Consciousness full of Energy appears in the form of *Oṃ*, which is the unmanifest level of the Word as *paśyantī*. This verse has to be connected with verses 2 and 4 on *Oṃ* and on *paśyantī* as expressions of the Sun-of-Consciousness. Kṣemarāja summarises: “This being the case, the Absolute-as-Sound (*śabdabrahman*) contains within itself the entire expansion of the words and their referents and manifests spontaneously, without being “uttered”. *Bindu* and *nāda* correspond to the light (of consciousness), *prakāśa* and its self-reflection (*vimarśa*). 

*prātaḥ saṃdhyaruṇakiraṇabhāgṛṅmayaṃ rājasaṃ yanmadhye cāpi jvaladiva yajuḥ śuklabhāḥ sāttvikaṃ vā* /
*sāyaṃ sāmāstamitakiraṇaṃ yattamollāsi rūpaṃ sāhnaḥ sargasthitalayavidhāvākṛtiste trayīva* // (22) 

At the morning twilight your reddish rays partake of the nature of *rajas*, they correspond to the Ṛgveda. Shining bright at noon, your white rays belong to *sattva*, they correspond to Yajurveda. In the evening your rays assume the form of *tamas*, (corresponding to) Sāmaveda. 

Thus your manifestation as the Day takes the form of creation, preservation and dissolution, like the threefold (Vedic Science). 

The verse relates the three junctures of the day to creation, continuation and dissolution, and to the three Vedas. Kṣemarāja gives an internalized interpretation by equating the day with *prāṇa (*as generic life energy). The morning in the form of awakening of the heart (*hṛdunmeṣātmani prabhāte*) corresponds to the juncture of 

*[p. 21]* ^pg25

*prāṇa-apāna,* which consists in the (internal) union of Śiva and Śakti. Noon (mid-day, *madhya*) he equates with the central vein (*madhyadhāmni*) in which the *kriyāśakti* predominates, of the nature of worship of the divinity of Consciousness. The evening twilight corresponds spiritually to the dissolution of the breaths along with their residual traces, hence overcoming both, duality and nonduality and attaining 

a 

dark 

state of repose of unitary essence *(tamovastuviśrāntāntayaikarasatvāt*..). 

The three junctures of the day also correspond to the three levels of gross, subtle and supreme. In summary, the morning sun is predominantly the time of praise of the divinities, hence *Ṛgveda*. The mid-day is dominated by ritual, and hence corresponds to *Yajurveda*, whereas the evening juncture, being a moment of rest, is dominated by song (*gītā*), and hence *Sāmaveda*. 

*ye pātālodadhimuninagadvīpalokādhibīja-cchandobhūtasvaramukhanadatsaptasaptiṃ prapannāḥ* /
*ye caikāśvaṃ niravayavavāgbhāvamātrādhirūḍhaṃ te tvāmeva svaraguṇakalāvarjitaṃ yāntyanaśvam* // (23) 

Those who have taken refuge in You, equipped with seven horses, which symbolize (lit. make resound) (all the groups of seven): the seven underworlds, the seven oceans, the seven sages, the (seven) mountains, the (seven) continents, the (seven) worlds, the (seven) mental afflictions, the (seven types of) seeds, the (seven) metres, and those who have ascended the One horse which is the undivided state of the Word, they attain You alone, free from sounds, qualities and fractions, without a horse (*anaśvam*). 

The verse plays with all the realities which are grouped in seven, depending on the symbolism of the chariot of Sūrya being driven by seven horses. Taking refuge in Sūrya thus implies its association with all groups of seven, foremost (as in verse 1) the seven notes of music (*saptasvara*). However, there are those who take refuge in the Sun of Consciousness which is “driven” by the one horse, the partless Word at the level of *paśyantī* (*ye ca niravayavā vāk paśyantī tasyā bhāvaḥ…)* But 

*[p. 22]* ^pg26

both, those who connect through the seven (horses) and those who surrender to the sun drawn by one horse (*ekāśvam*), attain ultimately the Sun-of Consciousness without any horse, which is the supreme Brahman, in union with Him. 

*divyaṃ jyotiḥsalilapavanaiḥ pūrayitvā trilokīm ekībhūtaṃ punarapi ca tatsāramādāya gobhiḥ* /
*antarlīno viśasi vasudhāṃ tadgataḥ sūyase’nnaṃ tacca prāṇāṃstvamiti jagatāṃ prāṇabhṛt sūrya ātmā* // (24) Heavenly light, having filled the three worlds with water and wind and having become one, you again attract their essence with your rays. Dissolving it within, you enter the earth, and from there you make food grow that brings forth life. O Sūrya! you are the giver of life of the world and its soul. 

The physical meaning is clear: it shows the ecological cycle of sunlight, rain and wind, producing plants and hence food, but Kṣemarāja is only interested in the esoteric meaning. He interprets light as the light of consciousness, water as the nectar of Divine Energy, and wind as *prāṇa*; the three worlds he identifies as the three states of waking, dream and deep sleep, and “in these three realms he becomes one, having attained harmonious fusion (*sāmarasyaṃ prāpya*), the Divine essence, full of Consciousness-and-Bliss… taking it within, i.e. merging in the inner state, you enter the earth, the supreme stage. i.e. you shine in oneness with it.” (*antariti antarmukhe pade līno, vasudhāṃ parāṃ bhūmiṃ viśasi tanmayatayā sphurasi).* This commentary is a classical example of the *sūkṣma* level of interpretation of a *sthūla* description.[^20] 

*agnīṣomau prakṛtipuruṣau bindunādau ca nityau prāṇāpānāvapi dinaniśe ye ca satyānṛte dve* /
*dharmādharmau sadasadubhayaṃ yo’ntarāveśya yogī vartetātmanyuparatamatir nirguṇaṃ tvāṃ viśetsaḥ* // (25) 

The *yogī* who enters within (the pairs of opposites such as) Agni and Soma, Prakṛti and Puruṣa, *bindu* and *nāda*, *prāṇa* and *apāna*, 

[^20]: In the Vedas it would correspond to the three levels of *adhibhautika, adhidaivika* and *ādhyātmika.*

*[p. 23]* ^pg27

day and night, truth and falsehood, *dharma* and *adharma*, reality and unreality, which are ever-present, being free from them in his mind remains within his self (in self-bliss), he enters in you (who are) free from qualities. 

This is one of the verses expressing an overcoming of dichotomies by the *yogī* “who enters within”. Kṣemarāja relates all the dualities to *prāṇa* and *apāna*, which are predominantly connected with the energies of knowledge and activity (*prāṇa* with *jñānaśakti* and *apāna* with *kriyāśakti*). Day and night correspond to unfolding and retraction (*vikāsa* and *saṅkoca*), an important pair of complementary terms in Trika. “Entering within” means “entering or merging, in the realm of the inner *udāna* breath”, i.e. the rising breath associated with *kuṇḍalinī.* 

*garbhādhānaprasavavidhaye suptayorindubhāsā sāpatnyenābhimukhamiva khe kāntayormadhyasaṃsthaḥ* /
*dyāvāpṛthvyorvadanakamale gaumukhairbodhayitvā paryāyeṇāpibasi bhagavanṣaḍrasāsvādalolaḥ* // (26) 

O Lord! Being established in the centre (of the universe), awakening the lotus faces of Earth and Heaven, which are lying in space, you make them as if facing each other, in order to increase the (cosmic) fertility. Being desirous of tasting the six flavours, you drink them gradually. 

Kṣemarāja interprets this highly symbolic verse in his usual esoteric way. He interprets Earth and Heaven and the space in between (*antarikṣa*) as the centres *dvādaśānta* in the yogic body, the lotus faces as the lotus energies (*śaktinaline*), the awakening occurs by the supreme rays (of the Sun of Consciousness), the drinking as the tasting of the extraordinary bliss of Energy (*asāmānyaśāktānandāsvādana…*). The centre means the establishment between Agni and Soma. He admits that the verse uses the poetic device of double meaning on *śleṣālaṅkāra*, containing a number of suggestions (*śleṣadhvani*). 

*[p. 24]* ^pg28

*somaṃ pūrṇāmṛtamiva caruṃ tejasā sādhayitvā kṛtvā tenānalamukhajagattarpaṇaṃ vaiśvadevam* /
*āmāvasyaṃ vighasamiva khe tatkalāśeṣamaśnan brahmāṇḍāntargṛhapatiriva svātmayāgaṃ karoṣi* // (27) 

Having made an oblation of the Moon (Soma), the fullness of the lunar nectar, by your heat, and having made an offering to all the gods with the world as oblation in the mouth of the same fire, then consuming the remaining portion of the moon as the New Moon (*amāvasya*), you offer the sacrifice of yourself within the cosmos (*brahmāṇḍa*), like a householder who performs the sacrifice of his own self. 

The verse compares the waxing and waning of the moon with a cosmic sacrifice. Kṣemarāja takes both meanings, the cosmic and the spiritual, as the two levels of interpretation. The Sun of Consciousness performs a self-sacrifice, like a householder. But the fire is the rising breath or *udāna*, in which all the *tattvas* are offered. He homologizes this process with the phases of the Krama system, where all the sense divinities are experienced in the self as the ultimate reality (*viśvadevatāparamārtha svātmani camatkurvan*). Both, externally and internally, this process of sacrifice and transformation goes on. 

*kṛtvā naktaṃdinamiva jagadbījamāvyaktikaṃ yat tatraivāntardinakara tathā brāhmamanyattato’lpam* /
*daivaṃ pitryaṃ kramaparigataṃ mānuṣaṃ cālpamalpam kurvankurvankalayasi jagatpañcadhāvartanābhiḥ* // (28) 

O Sun (lit. "Maker of the Day"), you create (natural) night and day, the seed of the universe, and within that (original day) the day and night of Brahman, of the gods, of the ancestors and of men, of decreasing magnitude. In this way you create the world in five evolutions. 

Kṣemarāja refers to the creation of the *tattvas* as manifestation (*ābhāsa*), in the stages of *sarga, sthiti, saṃhṛti, vilāpana* and *svātmaikyābhāsana* (creation, 

*[p. 25]* ^pg29

maintenance, dissolution, concealing and self-revelation). He shows the relativity of time. In all these phases “it is only the Supreme Lord who manifests himself, for nothing exists which is separate from the one glorious form of the oneness of Consciousness.” In the context of human time he quotes the *Svacchanda Tantra*, but we need not go into these details. 

*tattvāloke tapana sudine ye paraṃ saṃprabuddhāḥ ye vā cittopaśamarajanīyoganidrāmupetāḥ* /
*te’horātroparamaparamānandasaṃdhyāsu sauraṃ bhittvā jyotiḥ paramaparamaṃ yānti nirvāṇasaṃjñam* // (29) O Sun! Those who are fully awakened (*saṃprabuddha*) in the day illuminated by the light of Reality, or those who have entered the yogic sleep in the night of the quieting of the mind (*cittopaśama*), having pierced through the light of the Sun at the junctures between day and night, full of supreme bliss, they attain the supreme state of *nirvāṇa* (liberation). 

In this verse the Stotra itself suggests the spiritual meaning of *sandhyā,* the junctures between day and night. Ksemarāja makes it clear that such a penetration in the centre of the two states does not happen by the effort of *prāṇayāma* etc., but by mere awareness, when all ignorance has vanished (*vimarśayuktyā prabuddhā galitākhyātitāmīsrāḥ*). 

The yogic sleep (*yoganidrā*) also means the pacification of all thoughts (*praśamita*-*vikalpa*…). Finally, the penetration in the juncture between *prāṇa* and *apāna*, of the nature of supreme bliss, implies the merging into the harmonious union with Śiva (*vyāpti*-*śivasāmarasya*). 

*ābrahmedaṃ navamiva jagajjaṅgamasthāvarāntaṃ sarge sarge visṛjasi rave gobhirudriktasomaiḥ* /
*dīptaiḥ pratyāhārasi ca laye tadyathāyoni bhūyaḥ sargāntādau prakaṭavibhavāṃ darśayanraśmilīlām* // (30) 

O Sun! From Brahmā to the living and immobile beings, you create every creation anew with your bright rays full of nectar. At 

*[p. 26]* ^pg30

the time of dissolution you again withdraw them in their respective sources. At the beginning and end of each creation you reveal the glorious manifestation of the play of your rays. 

Kṣemarāja explains “creating” as “manifesting your rays in condensed form” (*visṛjasi āśyānībhūtaṃ nijaraśmirūpaṃ prakaṭayasi*). Withdrawal or reabsorption means to draw into the oneness of Consciousness the manifestation of everything that has been manifested by its own freedom (corresponding to the play, *līlā*).[^21] 

*śritvā nityopacitamucitaṃ brahmatejaḥ prakāśaṃ rūpaṃ sargasthitilayamucā sarvabhūteṣu madhye* /
*antevāsiṣviva suguruṇā yaḥ parokṣaḥ prakṛtyā pratyakṣo’sau jagati bhavati darśitaḥ svātmanātmā* // (31) 

(O Sun!) You are always you are established in the eternal glorious light of Brahman, free from (the states of) creation, preservation and dissolution, dwelling in the centre of all the beings, hidden by nature, but visible in the world manifesting yourself by yourself, like a good master to his disciples. 

Kṣemarāja expands beautifully on the comparison of the Sun with the Guru in relation to his disciples: “The Lord is not accessible to the sense perceptions, and is yet present at the heart of all beings, just as a master is manifest directly to his disciples who are recipients of his grace (which arises out of) his Self which is self-luminous.” He further explains this self-manifestation of the Guru: “What he shows to the disciples is the Self, the reality of the nature of pure Consciousness; he manifests himself within as the Supreme Self, and outside as glory, being of universal form.” (…*guruṇā ātmā darśyate cinmayī sattā prakaṭīkriyate, tathā tvayāntaḥ paramātmatayā bahiśca tejo viśvarūpatayā svayamātmā prakaṭīkriyate).* 

*lokāḥ sarve vapuṣi niyataṃ te sthitāstvaṃ ca teṣām ekaikasminyugapadaguṇo viśvahetorguṇīva* / 

[^21]: Cf. B. Bäumer, ‘‘the Play of the Three Worlds: The Trika Concept of Līlā’’, in: William Sax (ed.), 1995, *The Gods at Play. Līlā in South Asia*, New York/Oxford: Oxford University Press, pp. 35-49.

*[p. 27]* ^pg31

*itthaṃbhūte bhavati bhagavanna tvadanyo’smi satyaṃ kintu jñastvaṃ paramapuruṣo’haṃ prakṛtyaiva cājñaḥ* // (32) O Lord! All the worlds are ever established in your cosmic body, and you are the universal cause, being established in each of them simultaneously, though you are free from qualities you appear as if possessed of qualities. Since you are thus, O Lord, I am in truth not different from you — but you are the omniscient supreme Person, whereas I am ignorant by nature. 

Kṣemarāja explains why, despite an essential non-duality, there is still a vast difference between the Lord and the devotee or the individual. “O Lord, whose form (body) is of the nature of supreme bliss and of the Energy of absolute Freedom, you are embraced by the Supreme Word; in your body, dense with blissful Light, being the cause of the entire universe, all the worlds — i.e. all the visible objects and all the subjects, like the Rudras, Kṣetrajñas and others — are established…. 

You dwell in every body without sequence (i.e. at the same time); otherwise, how could they be manifest without your light?... 

Since you are described as universal, in truth I am not other. However, you are the Supreme Puruṣa, the knower, absolutely full and omniscient. But I, a subject dwelling in the body, am ignorant due to the power of your *māyā*. 

Therefore, end this (my) ignorance which is due to my identification with the subjectivity of (my) body etc., and make me merge forever into my Self so that (my non-duality with you) becomes true.” 

*saṃkalpecchādyakhilakaraṇaprāṇavāṇyo vareṇyāḥ saṃpannā me tvadabhinavanājjanma cedaṃ śaraṇyam* /
*manye cāstaṃ jigamiṣu śanaiḥ puṇyapāpadyayaṃ ta-dbhaktiśraddhe tava caraṇayoranyathā no bhavetām* // (33) I believe that by praising you my intention, my will (effort etc.), my sense-organs and my voice have found grace with you, that I shall find refuge in you in this life, and that my good and evil 

*[p. 28]* ^pg32

deeds will also slowly subside. Otherwise, how could I have faith and devotion to your feet? 

Kṣemarāja paraphrases *abhinava* by “by the power of praise consisting in meditative reflection” (*parāmarśātmastutivaśāt*). By such a praise the devotee attains the supreme grace (*parānugraha*) sanctifying all his sense organs, life breaths etc. His good and evil *karma* will melt like snow. Faith and devotion are the sure signs of having attained such a grace. 

*satyaṃ bhūyo jananamaraṇe tvatprapanneṣu na stas tatrāpyekaṃ tava nutiphalaṃ janma yāce tadittham* /
*trailokyaiśaḥ śama iva paraḥ puṇyakāyo’pyayoniḥ saṃsārābdhau plava iva jagattāraṇāya sthiraḥ syām* // (34) (O Lord!) It is true that those who have taken refuge in you are not again subjected to birth and death. Even so, I pray that you grant me another birth so I might praise you! May I become the Lord of the three worlds, endowed by miraculous birth with a pure and transcendent body, so that I may be like a steady boat to help the world cross over the ocean of *saṃsāra*! 

The meaning of this verse is so clear that the commentary adds nothing to it. The *Stotrakāra* expresses his desire to become a saviour for those suffering in *saṃsāra*, so that, instead of being liberated, he prefers another birth in a pure body, so that he can become a boat for ferrying others across. The idea is close to the *bodhisattva* ideal of Mahāyāna Buddhism, i.e. renouncing one’s own liberation for the sake of helping others. This ideal, along with the use of the concept of *nirvāṇa*, may indicate some Buddhist influence on the Stotra. 

*sauṣumṇena tvamamṛtapathenaitya śītāṃśubhāvaṃ puṣṇāsyagre suranarapitṛn śāntabhābhiḥ kalābhiḥ* /
*paścādambho viśasi vividhāścauṣadhīstadgato’pi prīṇāsyevaṃ tribhuvanamataste jaganmitrārka* // (35) 

O Sūrya! First of all you nourish the gods, men and ancestors by your peaceful rays, on the immortal path of *suṣumṇā*, in the form 

*[p. 29]* ^pg33

of the moon. Then you enter the water and various types of plants. Even dwelling there, you give delight to the three worlds. Therefore it is true that you are the Friend of the World! 

This verse shows that Kṣemarāja’s esoteric interpretation is not far-fetched and superimposed on a merely external meaning of the Stotra, but that it is intended by the *Stotrakāra* himself. Here the two levels are already contained in the verse itself: The Sun is called the Friend of the World insofar as he nourishes the gods, men and ancestors by his peaceful rays, both internally in the central vein of *suṣumṇā*, and externally as the physical sun without which the plants would not grow. Kṣemarāja interprets the “peaceful rays” as the energies of self-awareness, by turning within (*śāntabhābhir*-*antarmukhībhāvena…vimarśaśaktibhiḥ).* 

*mandākrānte tamasi bhavatā nātha doṣāvasāne nāntarlīnā mama matiriyaṃ gāḍhanidrāṃ jahāti* /
*tasmādastaṃgamitatamasā padminīvātmabhāsā saurītyeṣā dinakara paraṃ nīyatāmāśu bodham* // (36) 

O Lord! When the darkness (of ignorance) is slowly destroyed along with sin, and my mind becomes absorbed within, (even then) it does not leave its deep slumber. Therefore, O Maker of the Day, lead me quickly to the supreme awakening, when darkness has vanished by the light of the Self, just like the lotus (which opens at the end of the night and upon the appearance) of the first rays of the sun. 

Although the commentary does not mention it, but the first word of the verse also hints at the metre of the entire hymn (except verse 1): *Mandākrāntā*. The slow movement of the vanishing physical and mental darkness is contrasted with the prayer to the Sun to remove it quickly, but these aspects do not attract the attention of the commentator. He also does not elaborate on the word *bodha* or relate it to the three states of awakening mentioned in the *Spanda Kārikā*[^22]. But the

comparison of the coming of the day (hence *dinakara*) and of the awakening from the slumber of darkness of the mind with the blooming of the lotus is well known.[^23] *yena grasīkṛtamiva jagatsarvamāsīttadastaṃ dhvāntaṃ nītvā punarapi vibho taddayāghrātacittaḥ /* *dhatse naktaṃdinamapi gatī śuklakṛṣṇe vibhajya trātā tasmādbhava paribhave duṣkṛte me‘pi bhāno* // (37) 

O all-pervading Lord! You have destroyed the darkness which has devoured the whole world. But again, out of compassion for it, you have divided it in two courses, night and day, bright and dark. Therefore, O Sun! be the saviour, and protect me too, a sinner and miserable. 

Kṣemarāja interprets darkness as ignorance which has covered the whole world. And by grace of the Sun of Consciousness the darkness of ignorance is destroyed. The division into the bright and dark halves (of the day and of the month) corresponds spiritually to the states of *vyutthāna* (externality) and *samādhi* (absorption). 

*āsaṃsāropacitasadasatkarmabandhāśritānām ādhivyādhiprajanamaraṇakṣutpipāsārditānām /* *mithyājñānaprabalatamasā nātha cāndhīkṛtānāṃ tvaṃ nastrātā bhava karuṇayā yatra tatra sthitānām* // (38) O Lord! You are our saviour, out of compassion protect us who are bound by good and evil actions collected from the beginning of the world, and who are afflicted by pain and sickness, birth and death, hunger and thirst, being blinded by the darkness of ignorance. Be our protector, wherever we are (or: in whichever state we are)! 

Here Kṣemarāja is extremely brief, since the meaning of the verse is clear (*spaṣṭam*). He only explains “wherever (in whichever state) we are” by: “in any place, time and condition be our saviour and protector by revealing a state of absorption” (*samāveśaprathanena*).

*satyāsatyaskhalitavacasāṃ śaucalajjojjhitānām ajñānānāmaphalasaphalaprārthanākātarāṇām* /
*sarvāvastāsvakhilaviṣayābhyastakautūhalānāṃ tvaṃ nastrātā bhava pitṛtayā bhogalolārbhakāṇām* // (39) O Saviour! Protect us like a father, we whose speech has been corrupted by speaking truth and untruth, who are deprived of purity and shame, who are ignorant and given to useless and useful desires, who in all states are attracted by numberless objects of the senses, and who are fickle like children wishing some enjoyment. 

The meaning being clear Kṣemarāja does not add anything. 

*yāvaddehaṃ jarayati jarā nāntakādetya dūtī no va bhīmastriphaṇabhujagākāradurvārapāśaḥ* /
*gāḍhaṃ kaṇṭhe lagati sahasā jīvitaṃ lelihānas tāvadbhaktābhayada sadayaṃ śreyase naḥ prasīda* // (40) O you who bestow fearlessness on your devotees! As long as old age, the messenger of death, has not consumed my body, nor the terrible noose encircled my neck tightly, like a fierce three-hooded serpent in order to forcefully take my life, be graceful to us with compassion for our salvation! 

Here too Kṣemarāja does not feel the need to comment, the meaning being clear. 

*viśvaprāṇagrasanarasanāṭopakopapragalbhaṃ mṛtyorvaktraṃ dahananayanoddāmadaṃṣṭrākarālam* /
*yāvaddṛṣṭvā vrajati na bhiyā pañcatāmeṣa kāyas tāvannityāmṛtamaya rave pāhi naḥ kāndiśikān* // (41) 

O Sun, eternal nectar of immortality! Protect us in our flight before this body dies out of fear from the sight of the terrible face of Death with his fierce fangs, with his fiery eyes, full of terror, with his wide open mouth ready to devour all living beings! 

*[p. 32]* ^pg36

Kṣemarāja paraphrases the Sun of Consciousness as “of the nature of a dense unity of supreme bliss which never sets”, and the prayer for protection means, “lead us to a state of absorption without returning to external consciousness” (*nirvyutthānasamāveśa*). 

*śabdākāraṃ viyadiva vapuste yajuḥsāmadhāmnaḥ saptacchandāṃsyapi ca turagā ṛṅmayaṃ maṇḍalaṃ ca* /
*evaṃ sarvaśrutimayatayā maddayānugrahādvā kṣipraṃ mattaḥ kṛpaṇakaruṇākrandamākarṇayemam* // (42) Your sonic nature is like empty space, your glorious body is the abode of *yajus* and *sāman*. The seven metres are your horses. Your (solar) disk is the *Ṛgveda*. Since you thus consist of the entire revelation (Śruti), out of compassion for me listen without delay to my cries of misery asking for mercy. 

The body consisting of space means a body that is pervasive like space (*śarīraṃ viyadiva vyāpakam*), and space is the support of all sounds (*samastaśabdāśrayaḥ*). Since the Sun of Consciousness contains all the Vedas, he is full of compassion (*karuṇarasapradhāna*). 

*nāśaṃ nāsmaccaraṇaśaraṇā yāntyapi grasyamānāḥ devairitthaṃ sitamiva yaśo darśayansvaṃ trilokyām* /
*manye somaṃ kṣatatanumamāgarbhavṛddhyā vivasvañ śuklacchāyāṁ nayasi śanakaiḥ svāṁ suṣumṇāṃśubhāsā* // (43) O Brilliant One! Those who have taken refuge at your feet will not be destroyed, even if the gods devour them. You demonstrate this, I believe, because by showing your white light to the three worlds, you gradually lead the Moon, whose body has been decreased, to the bright light, through the effulgence of your principal ray (*suṣumnā*). 

Kṣemarāja plays with the double meaning of *suṣumnā*: the exoteric is the principal ray of the Sun, the esoteric (*rahasyadṛśā*) is the central vein in the yogic body. In the esoteric sense the gods are the sense organs which attain to their 

*[p. 33]* ^pg37

objects through the moon = *apāna* (inhalation). By the light of the *suṣumnā* which expands the central vein, it becomes effulgent by the light of the energetic power of alternation of opening and closing (*nimeṣa - unmeṣa*). Thus the Sun leads the devotee to the white light which is full of the unity of consciousness (*cidekaghana*). 

*āstāṃ janmaprabhṛti bhavataḥ sevanaṃ taddhi loke vācyāṃ kenāparimitaphalaṃ bhuktimuktiprakāram* /
*jyotirmātraṃ smṛtipathamito jīvitānte’pi bhāsvan nirvāṇāya prabhavasi satāṃ tena te kaḥ samo’nyaḥ* // (44) 

O Sun! Whatever worship is done to you in this world right from birth, its fruit is unlimited of the form of enjoyment and liberation – who can describe it? When the wise (good people) at the end of their life only remember your light, you lead them to liberation (*nirvāṇa*). Therefore, who is your equal? 

Whether the *Stotrakāra* believed in *jīvanmukti* is not clear, but Kṣemarāja certainly uses the occasion to interpret *bhukti* and *mukti* in the non-dualist Śaiva sense. The commonly asked question of whether *bhukti* means only sense enjoyment is also taken up. “Liberation is the manifestation of Śivahood in this very life, which bestows repose in one’s own bliss; and enjoyment is of the nature of being absorbed objects” in the wonder of Consciousness, not attachment to sense (*ciccamatkārāveśarūpā bhuktiḥ na tu viṣayāsaktirūpā*). 

*apratyakṣatridaśabhajanādyatparokṣaṃ phalaṃ tat puṃsāṃ yuktaṃ bhavati hi samaṃ kāraṇenaiva kāryam* /
*pratyakṣastvaṃ sakalajagatāṃ yatsamakṣaṃ phalaṃ me yuṣmadbhakteḥ samucitamatastattu yāce yathā tvām* // (45) 

By worshipping the thirty invisible Gods, men get a hidden (invisible) reward, as is fitting, for the effect (of any action) is proportionate to the cause. Whatever proper and direct fruit I will get from devotion to you, who are visible to the whole world, I indeed pray for that as I pray for (obtaining) you. 

*[p. 34]* ^pg38

Kṣemarāja again puts his interpretation at the level of Consciousness: “But you, Sun of Consciousness, are visible to all, being self-luminous… (hence) by my devotion to you the appropriate fruit is ever-present union with you.” (*nityoditatvatsamāveśātmā*). So he prays for the fruit of *nirvyutthānasamāveśa*, i.e. absorption into the Divine Sun of Consciousness without break or falling back into the ordinary state. 

*ye cārogyaṃ diśati bhagavānsevito’pyevamāhus-te tattvajñā jagati subhagā bhoga-yoga-pradhānāḥ* /
*bhuktermukterapi ca jagatāṃ yacca pūrṇaṃ sukhānāṃ tasyānyo’rkādamṛtavapuṣaḥ ko hi nāmāstu dātā* // (46) 

The people who say that by worshipping God he bestows freedom from disease, they are wise in this world, they are fortunate and devoted to *bhoga* and *yoga*. For who else is in this world but the Sun whose body is immortal, and who is a giver of *bhukti* and *mukti* and of fullness of happiness? 

It is generally accepted that the praise of and prayer to the Sun-God bestows health, and especially in the case of Sāmba who, according to legend, was cured from leprosy by the worship of Sūrya. Kṣemarāja paraphrases: “This is proper, because who else can be a giver of health consisting of fullness of happiness, as a consequence of *bhoga* and *yoga,* but the Sun of Consciousness, being an intensity of the light of supreme bliss, and who has an immortal body?” 

*hitvā hitvā gurucapalatāmapyanekānnijārthān-yairekārthīkṛtamiva bhavatsevanaṃ matpriyārtham* /
*teṣāmicchāmyupakṛtimahaṃ svendriyāṇāṃ priyāṇām ādau tasmānmama dinapate dehi tebhyaḥ prasādam* // (47) 

O Lord of the day! Having abandoned their respective objects and strong fickleness for my benefit, my sense organs have made me one-pointed in your worship. So I want to do a favour to my beneficial sense organs. Therefore, I pray that you bestow grace on them before me. 

*[p. 35]* ^pg39

Here the usual role of the sense organs as distracting agents is reversed, and they have become helpful “in your service, by being joined (disciplined) in a state of inwardness” *(bhavatsevanamantarmukhībhāvayuktyā*). And he prays: “Bestow grace on them by removing them from the body of *māyā* and reveal to them their true nature of being the cycle of the rays of the Self.” (*nijamarīcicakrātmatāṃ prakaṭaya*) This refers to the Krama system of understanding the senses as rays of the one light of Consciousness, whose Lord is Cakre*ś*vara. But Kṣemarāja does not elaborate on this interpretation. 

*kiṃ tannāmoccarati vacanaṃ yasya noccārakatvaṃ kiṃ tadvācyaṃ sakalavacasāṃ viśvamūrte na yattvam* /
*tasmāduktaṃ yadapi tadapi tvannutau bhaktiyogā-dasmābhistadbhavatu bhagavaṃstvatprasādena dhanyam* // (48) O You of universal form! Is there any word whose speaker you are not? Is there any object of any words that you are not? 

Therefore, whatsoever we utter for your praise out of devotion and union (with you), may that be a blessing to us, O Lord, by your grace! 

Since the meaning in the verse is clear, Kṣemarāja\`s commentary is extremely brief and does not add much. 

*yā panthānaṃ diśati śiśirādyuttaraṃ devayānaṃ yā vā kṛṣṇaṃ pitṛpathamatho dakṣiṇaṁ prāvṛḍādyam /* *tābhyāmanyā viṣuvadabhijinmadhyamā kṛtyaśunyā dhanyā kācitprakṛtipuruṣāvantarā me’stu vṛttiḥ* // (49) 

Of the paths, there is the northern course associated with the cold season, the way of the gods (*devayāna*), and the southern course of the Fathers (*pitṛpakṣa*), the dark one associated with the seasons that start with the rains. Apart from these two there is another one, the ventral (course) of the equinoxes *viṣuvat* and *abhijit*, devoid of activity. May this state between *prakṛti* and *puruṣa* be auspicious to me! 

*[p. 36]* ^pg40

Kṣemarāja again interprets the cosmic time consisting of the two halves of the year, the northern (*uttarāyaṇa*) and southern (*dakṣiṇāyana*) course in terms of the yogic breaths. The prayer for attaining a middle state between *prakṛti* and *puruṣa* is identified with the two breaths (the left and right courses). As the two equinoxes, *viṣuvat* and *abhijit*, are the cosmic times of the “between”, they are given esoteric meaning: 

“*Viṣuvat* which is the great pervasion conquers (*abhijayati*) due to a pacification (or enduring) of duality, and it is such due to its overcoming all, it is *abhijit*. It is void of activity of good or evil nature because its essence is a resting in its own bliss (*svānandaviśrāntisāratvāt*….).” 

The two courses (of the Sun) correspond to *prāṇa* and *apāna*, and what is different of them (their centre) bestows *jīvanmukti*, the state of *unmanā* (transmental). 

*sthitvā kiñcinmana iva pibansetubandhasya madhye prāpyopeyaṃ dhruvapadamatho vyaktamuddālya tālu* /
*satyādūrdhvaṃ kimapi paramaṃ vyoma somāgniśūnyaṃ gaccheyaṃ tvāṃ surapitṛgatī cāntarā brahmabhūtaḥ* // (50) Having established my mind in the centre of the bridge, having attained the desired goal of the permanent state, and opening the (*cakra* of the) palate, may I reach you in the middle path between *devayāna* and *pitṛyāna,* beyond *satya*, void of Soma and Agni, the extra-ordinary supreme space, having become Brahman. 

Towards the end, the *Stotrakāra* himself reveals the esoteric goal of this path which agrees with Kṣemarāja’s earlier interpretations. This goal is to attain the centre – here expressed as in between the way of the gods and of the fathers. “Having become Brahman means having attained the uninterrupted state. ‘May I attain’ means, 'may I become the Sun of Consciousness, the Supreme Reality.' (*paratattvātmacidarkarūpaḥ syām*). “The Void of Soma and Agni, of the form of unsurpassable space of Consciousness', means the state when the inhalation and 

*[p. 37]* ^pg41

exhalation have ceased ... The bridge (*setubandha*) is the centre which mediates between the two shores, *prāṇa* and *apāna*, etc.” 

Here the highest state is again the overcoming of opposites, and by that the *yogī* or *jīvanmukta* becomes a bridge – comparable to the image of the boat mentioned in verse 34. 

*sarvātmatvaṃ savituriti yo vāṅmanaḥkāyabuddhyā rāgadveṣopaśamasamatāyogamevārurukṣuḥ* /
*dharmādharmagrasanaraśanāmuktaye yuktiyuktāṃ sa śrīsāmbaḥ stutimiti raveḥ supraśāntāṃ cakāra* // (51) 

Śrī Sāmba has composed this fitting hymn of praise giving complete peace, describing the universal nature (*sarvātmatvam*) of Savitṛ, with speech, mind and body, desiring to ascend the *yoga* of equality (*samatā*) by quieting passion and hatred, for attaining liberation from the bond of good and evil (*dharma-adharma*). 

At the end, the *Stotrakāra* states the ideal aimed at by worshipping Sūrya: it is the state of equality or harmony (*samatā*).[^24] Only at this point Kṣemarāja remembers the author, Śrī Sāmba, son of Kṛṣṇa. He comments on the hymn: “What kind of praise? One joined with *yoga,* consisting of repose in the supreme essential nature” (*parasvarūpa-viśrāntyātmanā yogena yuktam*). He defines *yoga* in the Āgamic tradition as *yogamekatvamicchanti vastuno’nyena vastunā*, “they call *yoga* the oneness by uniting one thing with another”, i.e. the individual with Śiva. And *yoga* is further defined as “the ending of all defilements and of attachment and aversion, which is of the nature of equality or harmony (*samatā*).” This definition agrees with the Trika conception. 

Finally, the aim of the recitation and meditation of the hymn is to lead people to the state of *jīvanmukti*.

The last two verses (52-53) contain the *phalaśruti,* the result of reciting this Stotra. In a touching way, the last verse prays that the hymn may remove the suffering of the devotees and lead them to perfection, like a mother. 

*bhaktiśraddhādyakhilataruṇīvallabhenedamuktaṃ śrīsāmbena prakaṭagahanaṃ stotramadhyātmagarbham* /
*yaḥ sāvitraṃ paṭhati niyataṃ svātmavatsarvalokān paśyan so’nte vrajati śukavanmaṇḍalaṃ caṇḍaraśmeḥ* // (52) Whoever recites this Hymn to the Sun continuously, which has been uttered by Śrīsāmba, who is dear to the maiden devotion and faith, and which is both, clear and profound, containing within it the spiritual essence, he will perceive the whole world (all people) as his own Self, and at the end, like Śuka, he will attain the orb of the Sun of glorious rays. 

“Containing the spiritual essence within” is paraphrased by “encompassing the secret (*kroḍīkṛtarahasyam*), which Sāmba has manifested externally. The Stotra reveals what is related to the Sun of Consciousness, and reciting it in continuity means contemplating its meaning. “Perceiving all the people as one’s own self” implies contemplating on the whole world as non-different from Consciousness (*cidabhedātmaviśvaṃ stotraṃ bhāvayan*). 

*iti paramarahasyaslokapañcāsadeṣā tapananavanapuṇyā sāgamabrahmacarcā* /
*haratu duritamasmadvarṇitākarṇitā vo diśatu ca śubhasiddhiṃ mātṛvadbhaktibhājām* // (53) 

Thus this Hymn consisting of fifty verses and containing the supreme secret, which is auspicious as the praise of the Sun, and which describes Brahman according to the sacred tradition (*āgama*), which I have composed and to which you listen, like a mother may it remove all suffering and may it show the way of purity and perfection to the devoted.

(*viśvopaniṣadrūpa-* the 

nature 

of 

the 

universal mystical *māheśvaraśāstrasatattvagarbhā*). Kṣemarāja concludes with a prayer for auspicious perfection (*śubhasiddhi*) which should be given to the devout like a mother, bestowing both, *bhoga* and *mokṣa*. 

In conclusion it can be said that the commentary by Kṣemarāja and his Śaiva interpretation of a Saura Stotra is not forced onto the original; rather it is based on important elements connecting the two traditions. First of all, as André Padoux already remarked, elements of the Sun cult had already been absorbed in the Śaiva tradition, as can be seen in the names *Mārtaṇḍa Bhairava* and *Mārtaṇḍa Kālī*, in parts of ritual, and in the symbolic use of the sun (along with moon and fire) in *yoga*, in the context of the interiorization of time, etc. But apart from these connections, it was more the content of the Stotra which must have attracted the commentator. One element is the theology of light, of the Sun of Consciousness, so central to the entire philosophy of *prakāśa-vimarśa*. The second element is the philosophy of the Word, of the three, respectively four stages of *vāc*, connecting the Vedic with the Tantric conceptions of the *praṇava, śabda, dhvani*, etc. Thirdly, the most interior connection is the yogic experience of entering in the centre between the breaths, and thereby overcoming all pairs of dichotomies. It is by this mystical process that the final state, *samatā*, is reached. 

The names Sūrya or Śiva have less importance; they are taken as indicators of the Ultimate Reality, whose universal symbol is the Sun.
