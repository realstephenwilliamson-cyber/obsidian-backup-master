## Chapter 5

## Addressed by the Word

*The Hermeneutics of Revelation*

Ricoeur’s account of biblical revelation is one of the most debated aspects of his hermeneutics. While he spends a great deal of time writing about biblical texts, he insists that he does so as a philosopher. He is neither a theologian nor a pastor, but a philosopher seeking to think the surplus of meaning given in the biblical texts, in the hope and wager that thinking biblically will illuminate our understanding of what it means to be human in the world. This chapter will examine his hermeneutics of revelation. We will begin with his critical appropriation of the theology of the Word, as represented by such theologians as Barth, Bultmann, and the New Hermeneutic of Fuchs and Ebeling. While Ricoeur follows this theology with philosophical reflection on the priority of the word,<sup>1</sup> he also wants to challenge its conception of the event-status of revelation, as well as its definition of revelation as interpersonal, I-Thou encounter. 

Ricoeur’s critique of the theology of the Word owes much to his detour through structuralism, which leads him to develop a theory of discourse as the unity of event and structure. The influence of structuralism also precipitates a shift away from the I-Thou personalism of his early thought toward a focus on the textuality of revelation. Our discussion of these points will raise three critical questions for Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of revelation. First, how does his account of biblical revelation differ from the general revelatory function of poetics? Second, how does the Bible differ from other existentially significant texts? And third, what is the role of divine action and speech in Ricoeur’s model of biblical hermeneutics?

### The Theology of the Word

As we have seen, Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion is concerned with letting the believing soul speak. That is his phenomenological goal. This 

*[p. 98]* ^pg115

phenomenology is hermeneutical because it is not a direct description of consciousness but an indirect reflection on the language in which belief is articulated. This means reflection must listen to the originary expressions of religious belief—that is, the more immediate representations and designations of God in songs, prayers, and texts that are part of the everyday lived experience of the believer and the community. Ricoeur therefore prefers to focus on exegesis rather than the higher-level conceptual thinking of theology, which is often already in dialogue with philosophical speculation (CI 482, TB xv). Ricoeur wants to get back to what is distinctive about the different literary genres in the Bible, that is, the “modes of thought other than those based on Greek, Cartesian, Kantian, Hegelian, etc. philosophy.” Ricoeur observes that the religious texts of India, as well as Buddhist metaphysics, have their own distinctive modes of thought, and he wants to show that “thinking biblically” also involves a unique mode of thought (TB xvi). 

Ricoeur is correct that theology often launches into speculative heights beyond that of the everyday believer. The average person in the pews has never heard of Hans Urs von Balthasar or Eberhard Jüngel. It would, however, be a mistake for the hermeneutics of religion to ignore theology, since it too is part of the hermeneutical circle in which the believing soul speaks. Theology feeds back into the concrete experience of belief. The influence of Luther and Calvin on the Lutheran and Reformed traditions are good examples of this. So is the Augustinian doctrine of original sin. As we saw in chapter 2, Ricoeur classifies original sin as a high-level speculative concept. For Augustine, though, this concept is also tied to lived experience—his narrative in *Confessions* attests to this fact. This concept has also played out in the subsequent traditions of Western Christianity, so that it now seems self-evident to many readers that Genesis 3 describes the “Fall of Man” and original sin. These speculative concepts, as Ricoeur calls them, pervade Western culture—not only in theology and church doctrine, but also in poetry, music, literature, music, movies. It is significant that Ricoeur opens his essay on original sin with a quotation from *La Rochelle Confession of Faith*, since confessions and catechisms are part of the means by which theology circles back and articulates the lived experience of religious belief. Consequently, the doctrine of original sin is not restricted to second-order discourse, but has become a basic interpretive category in which many Western Christians understand themselves and their lived experience. Theology is part of this hermeneutical circle.

That said, Ricoeur does engage with some of the biggest names of twentieth-century German Protestant theology, such as Barth, Bultmann, Bonhoeffer, Ebeling, Moltmann, and Jüngel. What he takes from this theological reading is not so much reflection on Christian dogmatics, but instead more specific insights regarding the nature of religious language (IA 25). 

*[p. 99]* ^pg116

For example, Ricoeur’s hermeneutics reflects Barth’s account of revelation as a transcendent Word given prior to the reflective act of the subject. The relation between the self and the text is not one of a subject interrogating an object, but rather a subject being put in question by *die Sache*—the subject matter of the text.<sup>2</sup> For Barth this reversal entails a radical break with modern liberal theology and its dependence on historical-critical method, which studies the Bible as an object of scientific inquiry. Barth’s point is that this sort of explanatory inquiry, while useful within its proper scope, tends to approach the text as a human document expressing the immanent workings of human spirit (i.e., history, culture, religion, and ethics), an approach that closes off the most important dimension of the text: that it is God’s Word speaking in and through the human word. Barth thus presents a dialectical concept of revelation—dialectical because it highlights the radical difference between God and human, transcendence and immanence, eternity and time. Divine revelation is an event, an act of God’s self-disclosure in the Word. Whether Ricoeur follows Barth on this point is something we will consider below.

Rudolf Bultmann was another key influence for Ricoeur. Bultmann was Barth’s ally in the struggle to overcome the subject-object framework of Cartesian epistemology, which treats the text as an objective thing and thus cannot account for the way in which the text *addresses* us as revelation. Bultmann, however, thought that Barth failed to recognize the degree to which revelation—the *Sache* of the biblical text—was couched in language and concepts that were inadequate to the real significance of the text. This inadequacy is due to the limits of the mythical cosmology of the biblical authors, which assumed the veracity of miracles, demons, a vertical cosmological hierarchy, and so on. Hence Bultmann’s call to demythologize the text by removing the authentic kernel of meaning—the *kerygma*, or proclamation of the gospel—from the mythical husk surrounding it. 

Ricoeur sees this demythologization as a necessary hermeneutical task, since the modern Western self has a dual condition. Within ourselves we feel the struggle between the believer and the atheist. When we read the Word, we hear both the “adult critic” and the “naïve child” (PPR 222). As Charles Taylor puts it, to live in our secular age is to feel the “cross pressure” of being between belief and unbelief.<sup>3</sup> Like Taylor, Ricoeur is concerned about the conditions of belief. Every culture has what Ricoeur calls an “available believable” (*croyable disponible*), akin to what Peter Berger calls “plausibility structures.”<sup>4</sup> We therefore need an internal critique to complement the external critique of Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud, to shatter the “false scandal” of ancient mythology, cosmology, and metaphysics and “make apparent the true scandal”—namely, the folly of the incarnation and the cross, which scandalize the understanding of all people in all times (CI 389, PPR 219–22). For Bultmann, the demythologized meaning of the text is an anthropological, 

*[p. 100]* ^pg117

*existential* address to the reader regarding the possibility of authentic existence in faith. Demythologization is not demythicization, a blunt rejection of myth, but rather a “decoding” of the existential significance of the myth so it can continue to address us.<sup>5</sup>

Ricoeur is not uncritical in his reading of Bultmann, but he holds that Bultmann understood the hermeneutical circle better than Barth did.<sup>6</sup> While Barth recognizes the Augustinian principle that we must believe in order to understand, Bultmann shows more clearly that it is equally necessary to understand in order to believe. Ricoeur is also very interested in the concept of language at work in Bultmann and his followers. Bultmann’s programme of demythologization is based on a certain understanding of language as the objectification of understanding (*Verstehen*). On Bultmann’s view, the symbolic language of myths tends to obscure the original existential understanding the myths express, which is why it is necessary to critique them to get back to the self-understanding of the author and the events recounted in the biblical text. This is where Bultmann’s theory of language loses its critical edge. Ricoeur writes: “There is no reflection in Bultmann on language in general but only on ‘objectification,’” and as a result “Bultmann seems to believe that a language which is no longer ‘objectifying’ is innocent. But in what sense is it still a language, and what does it signify?” (CI 395–96). Bultmann uses Heidegger’s analytic of Dasein to guide his existential interpretation, but he does not follow Heidegger down the longer path of thinking, which would lead into a deeper questioning of Being, and by consequence, language (CI 399–400).

Ricoeur’s concern was first raised by Bultmann’s students Gerhard Ebeling and Ernst Fuchs, who go further down the Heideggerian path to develop what became known as the “New Hermeneutic.” One of their main goals is to broaden the scope of hermeneutics to include the linguisticality of human experience (RM 320). Whereas Bultmann views hermeneutics as a move away from language back to a self-understanding that is prior to language, Ebeling and Fuchs argue that language is not the objectification of a prior understanding but the very condition of understanding itself.<sup>7</sup> As Ebeling argues:

*The primary phenomenon in the realm of understanding is not understanding* OF *language, but understanding* THROUGH *language.* The word is not really the object of understanding, and thus the thing that poses the problem of understanding, the solution of which requires exposition and therefore also hermeneutic as the theory of understanding. Rather, the word is what opens up and mediates understanding, i.e., brings something to understanding.<sup>8</sup> 

For Ebeling and Fuchs alike, the word is not an object but an event in which understanding comes into being. “For where a word happens, understanding 

*[p. 101]* ^pg118

is made possible.”<sup>9</sup> Ebeling thus uses the term “word-event” (*Wortgesche-hen*), while Fuchs makes much the same point with his term “language event” (*Sprachereignis*). Hans-Georg Gadamer writes that for Fuchs, “the word has primacy over against the text, for it is a linguistic event. \[Fuchs\] obviously means to say that the relation between word and thought is not that the word expressing it belatedly catches up to the thought. The word rather is like a flash of lightning—it strikes” (TM 528).

Like Bultmann, Ebeling and Fuchs are indebted to Heidegger in developing this account of language and understanding. Heidegger was in turn indebted to his reading of Luther on the *verbum efficax*—the effective word that brings into being. This is the speech act by which God creates the world, calling creation into being out of nothing. It is also the speech act by which God redeems the world, justifying sinful humanity, calling the new being of faith into existence out of the nothingness of sin. According to the New Hermeneutic, the word-event brings about a new reality, a transformed self-understanding. This is a transformation of my being-in-the-world. As Fuchs writes, the word transforms my “concrete situation” in everyday life by means of a linguistic donation. That is, the New Testament intervenes and “helps us find our language again. *The New Testament is itself a textbook in hermeneutic*. It teaches the hermeneutic of faith—in brief, the language of faith—and it encourages us to try out this language ourselves, so that we may become familiar with—God.”<sup>10</sup>

### Event, Structure, and Text

Ricoeur follows the theology of the Word insofar as his hermeneutics shows how the word is ontologically transformative, creating a new self-understanding and a new way of being-in-the-world. He also offers a critical corrective by insisting that while the word is an *event* that calls for personal appropriation, it is also *meaning*. The mistake—potentially “ruinous”—of these theologians is to oppose existential understanding to objectivity. While this distinction is helpful for overcoming a narrow scientific or positivistic reduction of being to objective things, it is insufficiently critical in its examination of the word-event and the faith it constitutes. Bultmann and his successors perpetuate the Romantic opposition between *verstehen* and *erklären*.<sup>11</sup> Ricoeur insists that objective, explanatory inquiry still plays a vital role regarding the meaning of the word-event:

If salvation is a word-event, the communication of this word-event does not take place without an interpretation of the whole symbolic network that makes 

*[p. 102]* ^pg119

up the biblical inheritance, an interpretation in which the self is both interpreter and interpreted. (FS 274)

Interpreting the “symbolic network” of the biblical inheritance requires a critical analysis of discourse to allow the meaning of the text to unfold. Ricoeur’s objection is that existential interpretation moves too quickly to the historical moment of decision without attending sufficiently to the way that meaning occurs in the objective structures of the text. 

Ricoeur reaches this conclusion following a long detour through structuralism, which was a major expansion of his hermeneutics. Structuralism distinguishes between two senses of language: *langue* is the whole system of internal rules and the ensemble of signs that make up a language; *parôle*—that is, *word*—designates the actual events of people speaking—the acts in which *langue* is realized as messages in real time. Structuralists give *langue* total priority over *parôle* because it contains all the possibilities of meaning. The individual speaker is bound to the structures of *langue*, and all events of *parôle* are just combinations of the structural possibilities of that language. Structuralism is a very reductive, objectifying discipline, but it highlights a truth that is vital for hermeneutics: that the meaning of word-events depends on this structural aspect of language. 

Ricoeur applies this point in his “Preface to Bultmann.” While the moment of existential decision may come like a bolt of lightning, this lightning strike nevertheless shows up to readers who tarry in the fields of meaning. If hermeneutics rushes ahead to the subjective event of decision, it misses the objective moment of meaning, that is, the semantic structures and contours of the text (CI 397–98). Ricoeur’s point is that both moments—event and meaning—are mutually dependent. This is where Ricoeur finds the objectifying, explanatory tools of disciplines like structuralism and biblical criticism helpful: They can draw out and clarify the structures of meaning that are fixed in the text. To explain more is to understand better.

Ricoeur’s appeal to structuralism is not uncritical, though, since he poses some significant objections to its objectifying methods. First, structuralism tends to reduce subjectivity to an epiphenomenon of language. The speaker is not the active origin of meaning but a passive instrument of *langue* (H 8). Like psychoanalysis, this challenge is a necessary detour for reflexive philosophy and its exalted concept of subjectivity, but Ricoeur is no more ready to reduce subjectivity to the impersonal workings of language than to being or power. Instead, he insists in good phenomenological fashion on attending to how language works in everyday life. Following J.L. Austin’s speech act theory, Ricoeur argues that philosophy of language must start from the basic fact that “languages do not speak, people do” (IT 13). This conviction will return as the most basic capacity in Ricoeur’s account of human capability: *I can* 

*[p. 103]* ^pg120

*speak* (OA 40–55, CR 93–96). If speakers did not instantiate the synchronic structures of language in concrete, diachronic speech acts, there would be no *langue*. These events are therefore ontologically prior to linguistic systems, which in themselves only have a “virtual existence.” Apart from concrete events of speaking, language as a system would not exist. When I speak, I perform an act that is a unique, contingent, historical event—a dialogue including both the event of speaking and the event of hearing (IT 9, 16).

Note, however, that this event “is not merely transitory and vanishing.” “It may be identified and reidentified as the same so that we may say it again or in other words. We may even say it in another language or translate it from one language into another. Through all these transformations it preserves an identity of its own which can be called the propositional content, the ‘said as such’” (IT 9). Ricoeur thus distinguishes the event of saying from the meaning of the said. Both are essential to Ricoeur’s concept of *discourse*, which is a dialectical unity of event and meaning, word and structure, the saying and the said: “If all discourse is realized as an event, all discourse is understood as meaning. What we wish to understand is not the fleeting event but rather the meaning that endures.”<sup>12</sup> 

One way meaning endures is in the form of *works*, such as stories, poems, and essays. Works are compositions consisting of sequences longer than sentences (TA 80). Discourse occurs at the level of the sentence, but, typically, sentences with enduring meaning belong to the larger wholes that are literary works. These works are often composed orally and vary widely in terms of magnitude and importance: from relatively minor oral narratives like why I missed the train to more momentous stories like how I met my wife, to works with much wider and enduring cultural significance like folk tales, epic poems, plays, and novels. In each case the work is an oral composition in which the meaning of discourse endures.

Ricoeur’s second objection to structuralism is that people not only speak, but they also speak *about the world*. This observation is vital because structuralism holds that language does not refer to the world but rather “constitutes a world of its own, within which each item only refers to other items of the same system, thanks to the interplay of oppositions and differences constitutive of the system” (IT 6). Ricoeur argues that linguistic systems are not ends in themselves but rather the means to understanding the world. We speak about the world to each other, and we understand what we hear. These linguistic events of speaking and hearing are essential to discourse. Taken together, these insights form another fundamental thesis of Ricoeur’s philosophy: Discourse is *someone saying something to someone about something*. 

This definition of discourse will have important consequences for Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of revelation, especially insofar as we consider revelation in 

*[p. 104]* ^pg121

terms of divine speech—a divine someone saying something to someone about something. The complication is that the divine Word is typically mediated through the written text. How does this mediation work, so that the biblical text is also the Word of God? We will return to this question below.

### Distanciation, Appropriation, and Authorial Intent

In speech there is usually a close fit between meaning and intent. We can understand the speaker’s intent with reference to our shared context as well as cues like vocal tone and body language, and if we don’t understand, we can clarify by asking questions and getting a prompt reply.

This understanding becomes more complicated with the transition from speech to writing. Writing is a highly beneficial innovation of human culture, since it allows us to preserve transient events of discourse in the enduring medium of texts. Most precisely, writing does not preserve the event itself but the *meaning* of the event—not the saying but the *said* (IT 26–27). Writing detaches meaning from the event, giving the text a “semantic autonomy” (IT 25). This is hermeneutically important because the meaning of the text is no longer bound to the original speech event, nor to the intention of the speaker. The text takes on a life of its own. “What the text means now matters more than what the author meant when he wrote it” (IT 30). The text gains an objective meaning, in the sense that it stands over and against the subjectivity of both the author’s intention and the reader’s interpretation (RM 319).

Ricoeur calls this estrangement *distanciation*—that is, the distance between subjective consciousness and meaning. Philologists in the eighteenth century identified this problem with regard to cultural distance: How is a present engagement with ancient culture possible? How can we recover the meaning of ancient texts and artifacts, given the vast cultural distance that separates us from the ancient world? Ricoeur extends this discussion by showing that distanciation occurs with all writing, thereby raising the hermeneutical question of appropriation—the recovery, retrieval, literally *making one’s own*—of what was previously estranged or foreign so that one experiences a “participatory belonging” to the meaning of the text.<sup>13</sup> For Ricoeur, “the ultimate aim of all hermeneutics” is existential appropriation of the meaning of the text (IT 91).

Romantic hermeneutics argued that appropriation means discerning the original author’s intent and achieving a kind of spiritual union with the mind of the author (IT 22, 72–73). For example, in the introduction to Johann Gottfried Herder’s *The Spirit of Hebrew Poetry,* published in English in 1833, the translator writes approvingly of Herder’s interpretive 

*[p. 105]* ^pg122

approach to ancient Hebrew texts. By his estimation, Herder did something comparable to what Lessing and Winkelmann did in their readings of ancient Greek culture. Each of these thinkers were able

to divest themselves of the conceptions, and modes of thought, which are peculiar to their own country and institutions, and of the peculiar spirit of their own age; by the force of imagination to place themselves in the condition of those ancient patriarchs and prophets, whose thoughts and feelings they seek to apprehend; to see the world as they saw it, to feel as they felt, to imbibe and to express their spirit in its truth and simplicity.<sup>14</sup>

For this approach, the goal of hermeneutics is to take on the experience of the author for oneself.

The most famous advocate of this position is Friedrich Schleiermacher. As Gadamer puts it, Schleiermacher contributes a model of psychological interpretation that “is ultimately a divinatory process, a placing of oneself within the whole framework of the author, an apprehension of the ‘inner origin’ of the composition of a work, a re-creation of the creative act” (TM 187).<sup>15</sup> This was the principle behind Schleiermacher’s call to understand the author better than he understood himself.

This divination of authorial intent has been strongly criticized by structuralism and post-structuralism, as well as Gadamer and Ricoeur, who argue that it is impossible to overcome this historical distanciation directly. Nevertheless, interpretation is an attempt to render distanciation productive through the work of appropriation and application (in Gadamer’s terms, *Aneignung* and *Anwendung*). Appropriation recovers the meaning of the text—*die Sache*, the matter of the text, or what Ricoeur calls “the world of the work” (TA 87). This is the world proposed or projected by the text. The world of the work is not the meaning *behind* the text, whether the author’s intent, its historical context, the conditions of its production, or the original readers’ expectations and feelings. Nor is it the meaning *within* the structures of the text. Instead, “to interpret is to explicate the type of being-in-the-world unfolded *in front of* the text” (TA 86). The text refers beyond itself to tell us something about the world as possibility.

Ricoeur’s idea of “the world in front of the text” depends on his distinction between first and second-order reference. While texts like instruction manuals, phone books, and grocery lists typically refer quite directly to the world, works of poetry and fiction suspend direct ostensive reference. Literature “abolishes,” even “destroys,” the mundane world, and with it “the referential function of ordinary discourse.” This opens a *second-order* reference that refers to the *world* in the sense of Husserl’s *Lebenswelt*, or Heidegger’s notion of being-in-the-world as the totality of my involvements, 

*[p. 106]* ^pg123

my care for my possibilities of being (TA 85–86). For Ricoeur, “the world is the ensemble of references opened up by every kind of text, descriptive or poetic, that I have read, understood, and loved” (IT 37). The text refers to a possible world, proposing or projecting a world I could inhabit—a world of possible meanings and actions. Through the text, imagination creates a “free play of possibilities” in which we can “try new ideas, new values, new ways of being-in-the-world.” Imagination effects a metamorphosis, redescribing reality according to new and unforeseen possibilities.<sup>16</sup>

Ricoeur’s hermeneutics does not, however, unleash interpretive subjectivism, since the text poses a radical challenge to the autonomous, self-positing ego: “It is not the reader who primarily projects himself. The reader rather is enlarged in his capacity of self-projection by receiving a new mode of being from the text itself.” This means that appropriation involves an essential moment of *dispossession*, so that the ego is dethroned from its exalted status (IT 94). The text mediates a movement from the ego (*moi*) to the self (*soi*) (IT 95).

### Biblical Interpretation: Revelation, Poetic Reference, and the Plurality of Genres

Ricoeur’s model of the text is important for the hermeneutics of revelation because both concern the power of *poiesis*. Ricoeur describes literature—particularly works like poems and fiction—as having a kind of second-order reference. In contrast to first-order reference, “the world of the ‘literary’ text is a projected world, one that is poetically distanced from everyday reality” (TA 96). Just as this second-order reference is operative in literary texts, so it is operative in religious texts. Most of Ricoeur’s research in this area focuses on the Jewish and Christian traditions, so he concentrates on the way biblical texts propose a world of new, unforeseen possibilities: “a new world, a new covenant, the kingdom of God, a new birth” (PTH 26). For Ricoeur, the biblical text is an instance of second-order reference *par excellence* (PTH 27). 

Like poetry and fiction, the biblical text is productive, poetic discourse that uses second-order reference to disclose new existential possibilities. For Ricoeur, all poetic texts are revelatory insofar as they bring about a new self-understanding. Recall, however, that for Ricoeur this self-understanding is not a merely cognitive or intellectual event (FS 234). The transformation of understanding is a transformation of oneself and one’s being in the world. To be sure, this transformation does register at the level of the intellect, but it also registers at the deeper affective and volitional levels of the self, all through the work of the imagination. This is a point Ricoeur shares with Nabert: The text addresses the *conatus* and restores the power of primary 

*[p. 107]* ^pg124

affirmation. As we saw in chapter 4, this point is central to Ricoeur’s account of how religion liberates freedom and restores capability.

Revelation is therefore a function of poetics. It is an uncovering, a disclosure of the hidden (FS 223). The referential function of poetic discourse has the power to project a new world, a new being, and so Ricoeur speaks of a “nonreligious, nontheistic, nonbiblical” sense of the word “revelation” (H 138, FS 222). This “a-religious” idea of revelation is “coextensive with the poetic function.” His aim is not, however, to subordinate divine revelation to human poetics but to clarify the nature of biblical revelation (H 138–40). In what sense, then, are biblical texts revelation? Ricoeur writes:

If the Bible can be said to be revealed, this is to be said of the “thing” it says, of the new being it unfolds. I would then venture to say that the Bible is revealed to the extent that the new being that is in question is itself *revealing* with respect to the world, to all of reality, including my existence and my history. (TA 96, cf. FS 44)

It is worth noting the conditional “if” that begins the sentence, as well as the claim that the Bible is revealed “to the extent” that its texts effect this poetic, revelatory function. This suggests that the biblical texts are not revelation in the sense of God’s self-disclosure, but in the sense of the world they reveal in front of the text. Thus Ricoeur also writes that “if the word ‘revelation’ means something, its meaning is to be sought on the side of the issue the texts tell us about, as an aspect of the biblical world” (FS 221).

At one point Ricoeur actually states his preference to avoid the term “revelation,” preferring instead to “speak of a situation in which one refers to a constituting imaginary through the resources of religious language” in its various genres (CC 148). Ricoeur also objects that the term “is too often reduced to inspiration,” in the sense of a divine “insufflation” in which God breathes out the text, or in which the word is “whispered in the ear” of the human author by the divine author (H 114, FS 44). Ricoeur complains that this “psychologizing” of revelation is based too narrowly on one particular genre of scripture—namely, prophetic discourse, in which the prophet speaks or writes on behalf of another. For example, the book of Micah begins: “The word of the Lord that came to Micah of Moresheth” ([[Micah 1#^1-1|Micah 1:1]]), and throughout the text we see an overlapping of what Micah says (“And I said”—3:1) with what the Lord says (3:5, 4:6, 5:10, 6:1), so that Micah speaks the word of the Lord in the first person. Prophetic speech is the model for the idea of revelation as a kind of double authorship: “Revelation is the speech of another behind the speech of the prophet” (H 114).

Prophetic discourse is often taken as the paradigm of revelation because the prophet pronounces God’s Word to his people. Ricoeur’s complaint is 

*[p. 108]* ^pg125

that prophecy is one among several genres in the Bible, such as narrative, law, wisdom, hymns, parables, proverbs, and eschatological sayings.<sup>17</sup> Consequently, it would be better to speak of “revelation” analogically rather than univocally to reflect this discursive plurality (H 113). 

In biblical narrative, for instance, the revelation does not consist primarily in the narration but in the events narrated. These are events that transcend the “ordinary course of history” and thereby “mark an epoch” and “engender a history.” Narrative reveals God as an agent in history, which is a decidedly Hebrew concept in contrast to the Greek idea of God as an idea or principle (H 116–17). Narrative also expands the concept of revelation with its historical realism, in contrast to the “idealism of the word event” (H 118). In other words, the revelation is not just a decisive moment in the self-understanding of the subject, but refers to historical events. One danger of the theology of the Word is that the existential address is reduced to a point-like event with no historical thickness or extension.<sup>18</sup>

Another biblical genre is prescriptive discourse, which “constitutes the ethical dimension of revelation.” This is not merely a heteronomous imposition of a moral demand or external divine law. Rather, it spells out the law and requirements of Israel as God’s covenantal people, whom he redeemed out of Egypt (H 121). The law summons the will and makes a claim on it, but the law is given in the living context of YHWH’s covenant with Israel (H 122). This insight will guide Ricoeur’s attempt to chart a middle course between autonomy and heteronomy toward a concept of theonomy, which we will discuss in chapter 6.

A fourth biblical genre is wisdom discourse, sometimes called *sapiential* literature. Unlike the prophet, who claims that “his speech is the speech of another,” the sage appeals to a prior wisdom in which he and all wise people participate. This is why “more than one sage in the biblical tradition” came from outside of Israel (H 123). These writings have traditionally been held as inspired because they reflect an intimacy with divine Wisdom that is also an intimacy with God. We might be tempted to read these texts as a Hebrew equivalent of the Greco-Roman art of living or a philosophical guide to happiness, but the genres differ insofar as the authors of Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Songs do not pretend to rational self-sufficiency like the pagan philosopher (H 125). In contrast to the pagan models of the good life, Hebrew wisdom literature reflects a life of faithfulness and humble obedience to God. For the biblical sage, wisdom begins with the fear of the Lord ([[Proverbs 09#^9-10|Prov. 9:10]]).

Ricoeur also discusses *hymnic* discourse, found most notably in the Psalms and their speech acts of praise, supplication, and thanksgiving—not to mention the imprecatory Psalms, which Ricoeur does not mention. Hymnic 

*[p. 109]* ^pg126

discourse is revelatory in the way it “forms our feeling in the process of expressing it. And revelation is this very formation of our feelings that transcends their everyday, ordinary modalities” (H 127).

Much of Ricoeur’s work on biblical hermeneutics focuses on the Hebrew Scriptures. His studies with Andre Lacocque in *Thinking Biblically* all focus on passages from what Lacocque calls the “First Testament.” This is due in no small part to this being Lacocque’s area of specialty, but it also reflects Ricoeur’s own occupation with the Hebrew Scriptures. Ricoeur’s interest in the New Testament lies particularly in the genres of proverbs and parabolic discourse, which are revelatory in their own distinctive way. Ricoeur’s early essay “Listening to the Parables of Jesus” shows the influence of the theology of the Word, with Ricoeur speaking of the parable as an event disclosing the new and calling for reversal and decision through a conversion of imagination, vision, and action. Writing of the parable of the mustard seed in Matthew 13, Ricoeur comments that the “natural growth of the seed and the unnatural size of the growth speak of something which happens to us, overwhelms us, beyond our control and our grasp, beyond our willing and our planning. Once more the Event comes as a gift” (PPR 241). Ricoeur will later offer more of a semiotic reading of parables in essays like “The Bible and Imagination” (FS 149–66), but he retains an interest in the paradoxical logic of the parables. What he finds there is a logic of *superabundance*, which “governs the extravagant gift of many of Jesus’ parables, as is clearly seen in those called hundredfold; a grain of wheat that produces thirty-, sixty-, one hundredfold; a grain of mustard that becomes a tree where the birds of the sky come to make their nests” (FS 300). Above and beyond the economy of exchange, which operates according to a logic of reciprocity and equivalence, there is a new economy governed by the paradoxical logic of the gift.

This logic of superabundance becomes a central category in Ricoeur’s thinking, particularly in his writings on ethics. It governs the command to love our enemies, and likewise the difficult task of forgiveness, or pardon.<sup>19</sup> For Ricoeur, the logic of superabundance reveals a love that exists in tension with the demands of justice, which require equivalence and proportion. Love is a kind of supraethical command, and no political regime or order of justice could turn it into an abstract rule because of its excessive character. Love is more demanding than justice,<sup>20</sup> so its logic serves to destabilize these systems by revealing that which transcends them.<sup>21</sup>

Ricoeur also finds the logic of superabundance in Paul’s epistle to the Romans, but it is noteworthy that while he draws this and other insights from his namesake, the apostle Paul, he does not consider how revelation functions in the New Testament epistles. Aside from a few brief mentions,<sup>22</sup> Ricoeur does not discuss the distinctive revelatory function of this genre—a 

*[p. 110]* ^pg127

bit surprising given how much of the New Testament consists of epistles.<sup>23</sup> Since Ricoeur’s hermeneutics is so concerned with the transformation of the self, it would be fruitful to examine the epistolary genre in the Hellenistic world, since letter writing was a significant practice of ethical formation, and much scholarship has been done on the way New Testament authors adapted this genre from its Hellenistic context.

Ricoeur’s hermeneutic of revelation might also benefit from attending to the opening of the New Testament book of Revelation, which combines the genres of prophecy and apocalyptic with epistle, as it is written by John “to the seven churches that are in Asia” ([[Revelation 01#^1-4|Rev. 1:4]]). What is particularly noteworthy, however, is how the text specifies a “chain of revelation” in which the Word is given:

The revelation (*apokalypsis*) of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants what must soon take place; he made it known by sending his angel to his servant John, who testified to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ, even to all that he saw. Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of the prophecy, and blessed are those who hear and who keep what is written in it; for the time is near. ([[Revelation 01#^1-1|Rev. 1:1–3]])

As Richard Bauckham observes, the chain moves God → Jesus Christ → angel → John → the servants of God who will read the Word in the ecclesial community in the context of worship.<sup>24</sup> Particularly striking in this passage is the role of the angel in this chain of revelation, since this poses a different question for Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion: In contrast to “psychologizing” views of the Holy Spirit, what might Ricoeur make of this claim of revelation attributed to an angel as a messenger of God?

In any case, for Ricoeur what these genres have in common is their revelatory power. In terms of Ricoeur’s later philosophy of religion, these texts are revelatory because they have the power to regenerate the self, restoring and refiguring our fundamental capacities as human beings. A narrative like the exodus of Israel from Egypt, for instance, provides a schematism or paradigm of new ways of being. These paradigms connect the “singularity” of the self with “communicability,” proving “a model of reading history, or reading my own life.” “The exodus story has been used in this capacity to generate an indefinite number of exemplifications.” This is why the Exodus narrative exercised such a strong influence on movements like abolitionism and civil rights, and likewise in liberation theology, which has been deeply informed by Ricoeur. By addressing our imagination and our desire to be, these texts generate “a capacity of following.”<sup>25</sup> In sum, the biblical texts regenerate our capability and summon us to follow and inhabit the new world disclosed in front of the text.

### The Bible: A Text among Texts?

For Ricoeur, biblical texts are revelatory because of their poetic power to disclose new possibilities of being in the world. For the believer and the theologian alike, however, this leaves questions about the uniqueness of the Bible. Is it just another text among texts? Granted, it is unique because of its diverse genres, but it might still appear that biblical revelation is just a species of a more general poetics of revelation, and that biblical hermeneutics is likewise just a special application of general philosophical hermeneutics. We might also wonder why religion has a distinctive status in Ricoeur’s general theory of poetic transformation. We can experience aesthetic and ethical transformation from reading other great texts, so what does religion offer that can’t be found elsewhere? How is the regenerating power of biblical texts different from that of other poetic texts? 

For Ricoeur, philosophical hermeneutics reads religious texts as poetic texts that redescribe life. At the same time, religious texts are also distinct among other poetic texts (FS 43). Ricoeur tries to maintain the uniqueness of the biblical texts, and likewise to show that theological hermeneutics is unique and not merely a regional application of general hermeneutics. Yet he struggles to articulate this distinction in terms of a decisive qualitative difference.

According to Ricoeur, the first point of uniqueness is that the Bible does not only refer to human reality, but has God as its referent. The biblical text *names* God and narrates God’s acts in history. This naming takes place in the diverse biblical genres: “God is named in diverse ways in narration that recounts the divine acts, prophecy that speaks in the divine name, prescription that designates God as the source of the imperative, wisdom that seeks God as the meaning of meaning, and the hymn that invokes God in the second person” (FS 227). This “naming” of God is a work of imagination, but it should not be taken solely as an imaginative projection of human possibilities, *à la* Feuerbach. For Ricoeur, imagination involves both creation and discovery. To imagine is not to conjure fantasy *ex nihilo*, but to become responsive to that which is given. The text addresses “our utmost possibilities and deciphers them in a situation which cannot be projected because we are already thrown into it. In theological language this means ‘the kingdom of God is coming’; that is, it appeals to our utmost possibilities, beginning with the very meaning of this kingdom, which does not come from us.”<sup>26</sup> 

Furthermore, for Ricoeur, the kingdom of God is not just another name for authentic being, just as the name God is not simply “the religious name of being.” “In this sense the word *God* does not function as a philosophical concept, not even that of being, whether the latter is taken in its medieval or even in its Heideggerian sense” (TA 97–98). God is named as the one who 

*[p. 112]* ^pg129

delivered Israel from their bondage in Egypt, who brings judgment on idolatrous Israel, and promises restoration after this judgment. The diverse biblical genres name God in these different ways, and the name *God* coordinates these different genres while also serving as their “vanishing point” or the “index of incompletion,” insofar as God is beyond and irreducible to the text.<sup>27</sup> What is lacking in Ricoeur’s account, however, is a sense in which the name of God is not only a gathering point in the text but also God’s self-disclosure to human beings. It is not just that the text names God, but that God names himself in and through the text. We will return to this point below.

According to Ricoeur, the Bible’s naming of God is intensified by the naming of Christ in the New Testament, since the cross and resurrection “give the word *God* a *density* that the word *being* does not contain.” What is this density? On Ricoeur’s account, it is “the power to incarnate all the religious significations in one basic symbol: the symbol of a sacrificial love, of a love stronger than death” (TA 98). Ricoeur’s appeal to symbolism here is surprising, since the person of Jesus would seem to call for the category of *testimony* rather than symbolism, which we discussed in chapter 4. For Ricoeur, testimony refers to a historical concreteness and contingency that symbolism does not, which is why it overturns rational autonomy in a way that symbolism does not. The same is true for the cross and resurrection. They certainly can and do function as symbols, but the category of testimony is more appropriate because they are unique historical events on which the Christian proclamation is founded. Ricoeur often refers to these events as central to Christian faith and hope, but he does tend to interpret the cross symbolically as a symbol of self-giving love, just as he tends to interpret the resurrection more symbolically than historically. We will return to this point as well.

In sum, it is still not clear that naming God, as the unifying rhetorical center of the text, makes the Bible anything more than a literary book with a religious flavor. For some readers this may be enough. It is certainly possible to be edified by the Bible in a literary way, reading it as one would read Homer or Dante. That said, Ricoeur also insists that the Bible is unique in Western culture because it founds Jewish and Christian religious communities. The writings of the poets and philosophers, on the other hand, belong to everyone and there is no sect, school, or church that is founded upon them.<sup>28</sup> One might object that the ancient philosophical schools had the texts of their founders,<sup>29</sup> but Ricoeur maintains that the reception and transmission of the biblical texts is different because they are authoritative for the belief and practice of these communities. Thus arises the question of the canon, which is a central issue for biblical hermeneutics. The canon is the rule of authority over the community, and it is bound up in the question of which texts are in and which are out. This authority is too often presented as an arbitrary fiat of clerical power, so Ricoeur takes care to distinguish between “authoritative” 

*[p. 113]* ^pg130

and “authoritarian.” The community recognizes textual authority as it collects, assembles, and selects particular texts: “The act of closing the canon is the recognition of the founding power of these texts specifically.”<sup>30</sup> This recognition often occurs through traditions of reading and interpretation, as well as the judgment that some texts are superior to others. Finally, textual authority is also constituted as the community becomes the locus of mediation in the conflict of interpretations between different traditions of reading and interpretation. The community is instituted as an interpretive authority, and the individual reader is instituted as recipient of the tradition passed on by the community.<sup>31</sup> The structures of the ecclesiastical community thereby develop alongside the authority of the textual canon, with a kind of hermeneutical circularity between the text that founds the identity of the community and the community that reads and interprets these texts (TB xvi). This authority involves the same interweaving of alterity and ipseity that is characteristic of Ricoeur’s thought. These texts exercise authority “over communities that place themselves under the rule—the canon—of these texts” (SMS 207).

The Bible is therefore unique because it constitutes the identity of the church. This is particularly true of preaching, which moves from the written text back to the spoken word (FS 71). Preaching delineates the essential difference between the biblical text and other texts, whether they are secular or belong to other religious traditions. One preaches on some texts and not others: “The community would be completely changed if you chose a modern poet to do a sermon, or if you took the Bhagavad Gita into the church.” A church could do this, but it would mark a crisis of identity in the community “because its own identity relies on the identity of the text” (FS 70).

### The Genius and the Apostle

All that Ricoeur has observed is accurate and good as far as it goes, but does it go far enough in identifying what is ontologically unique about the biblical texts? The ecclesial community takes the authority of the text to be divine in some sense. This may seem to go beyond philosophy into theology, but if the philosopher aims to let the believing soul speak, it is entirely appropriate to hear what the believing self confesses regarding the authority of the text. The philosopher need not make the confession himself or herself. Thus it is possible for the philosopher to take note of the Islamic account of the Qur’an as a perfect copy of the heavenly Qur’an, dictated by the angel Gabriel, without deciding whether this is true or not. For the Muslim this is a crucial conviction, and thus for a phenomenological hermeneutics of religion it is only appropriate to register this conviction as such.

*[p. 114]* ^pg131

By the same token, we might consider the question of divine authority in the biblical text. One of the most salient philosophical points in this regard is Kierkegaard’s distinction between the genius and the apostle. These are two qualitatively different kinds of authors. The genius writes works that commend themselves by their poetic power. The apostle, by contrast, writes works that do not commend themselves because of their poetic or aesthetic merits, which may be negligible. The apostle is not constituted by any immanent poetic merits but is constituted by a transcendent call. This call does not arise from any immanent human capability or “potential possibility.” It is not the *energeia* of an immanent religious *dynamis*, nor the imaginative expression of religious genius. Reflection cannot locate the originary act by which one becomes an apostle.<sup>32</sup> The call to be an apostle is not an immanent act but “a paradoxical fact,” which “stands paradoxically outside his personal identity as the specific person he is.”<sup>33</sup> Thus whereas the poet is a creature of immanence speaking out of imaginative genius, the apostle is a creature of transcendence and speaks not on his own behalf but with divine authority. This also means that every subsequent proclamation of the apostolic Word is a novel event; it is “just as new, just as paradoxical: no immanence can assimilate it.”<sup>34</sup> Barth opens his Romans commentary by quoting this passage from Kierkegaard, and likewise describes the apostolic call as a paradox.<sup>35</sup> In the spirit of Kierkegaard, then, we might ask whether the Bible is written as a work of religious genius, or as a work with divine authority. Does Ricoeur sufficiently account for this qualitative difference in his description of the biblical texts?

Based on what we have seen in Ricoeur so far, one might conclude that the authority of Scripture derives from the sociality of the church community rather than a divine origin. Does this authority derive from God? Ricoeur does address this idea in “The Paradox of Authority,” where he recounts the way the institutional church accounts for its authority by appealing to Scripture, which was “assimilated once and for all to the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, God himself being taken as their author” (RJ 96). This idealized account of authority was also “intertwined with the institutional authority of the church,” and this authority was based on a model of political authority inherited from ancient Rome (RJ 97). It is significant that in this passage Ricoeur does not consider the apostolic tradition and the early Christian rule of faith—significant, first, because they are so important for the development of the biblical canon, and second, because Ricoeur’s real concern in this passage is to dissociate himself from two ideas of authority that he finds aberrant.

The first aberration is when authority becomes authoritar*ian*—always a danger when an individual or community claims to speak with divine authority. Ricoeur is deeply Protestant in his suspicion of institutional authority, and is critical even in his early essays of clericalism that seeks to commandeer and possess the divine Word. 

*[p. 115]* ^pg132

The second aberration is a kind of enthusiastic appeal to the Holy Spirit. He is quite dismissive of simplistic views of inspiration as the Holy Spirit “whispering in the ears” of the biblical authors, and he makes similarly dismissive comments regarding the Holy Spirit’s role in illuminating the text for readers. He expresses serious reservations about the claim that “we have a personal fusion with the Holy Spirit.” Ricoeur concedes that the Holy Spirit may be overlooked in the Western theological tradition, but he is wary of “all the dangers of the charismatic, of the personal fusion with the Holy Spirit of people who say: ‘I carry the Holy Spirit with me, it is what inspires me,’ etc.”<sup>36</sup> Instead, Ricoeur proposes that hermeneutics should focus on the text, since that is the site where the Word registers. 

Ricoeur is also more focused on the role of the believing community as the locus of reading and interpretation, rather than looking to the purportedly illuminated interpretation of individual readers. As he writes in a 1967 lecture on the “Tasks of the Ecclesial Community in the Modern World”: “The subject of faith is not ‘I’ but ‘we.’”<sup>37</sup>

Ricoeur is right to highlight the role of the believing community in the formation of the canon. He is also right to be wary of oversimplified conceptions of divine inspiration, though his suggestion of the word being “whispered in the writers’ ears” verges on caricature. Still, there is a key question that remains, after all the qualifications and clarifications we have observed. It is a key question for the believing community, for the theologian, and for any philosophical hermeneutics that seeks to hear, in imagination and sympathy, what the believing self confesses: In what sense is the Bible the Word of God?

### Becoming the Word of God

The question of the Bible’s status as the Word of God might seem like the sort of theological issue Ricoeur would avoid, but he does not. This question is probably unavoidable for Ricoeur, who draws so heavily on the theology of the Word, which is all about the revelation of the divine Word in the written human word. Yet, as we have seen, Ricoeur also establishes a critical distance from the theology of the Word. One of the most significant aspects of this shift is the move away from framing revelation in terms of I-Thou personalism to a more impersonal focus on the meaning of the text.

Interpersonal encounter was a prominent theme in Ricoeur’s early account of Transcendence. There Ricoeur adopts a personalist approach to describe how the I only becomes itself in the encounter with the *Thou,* the second-person You*.* This is important for Ricoeur’s account of the relation with the other human, as well as divine transcendence. One of his criticisms of Jaspers is that his conception of Transcendence is too *im*personal—in contrast to 

*[p. 116]* ^pg133

Marcel, for whom Transcendence is the personal God: “*God is the supreme Thou*,” and the philosophical problem of faith “is finally one of understanding how God can be the *Thou* who never converts into *him*”—that is, the third-person *about whom* one speaks. For Marcel, the key to avoiding this disengaged mode of speech is prayer, “the invocation *par excellence.*”<sup>38</sup> Both Marcel and Jaspers want to avoid reducing Transcendence to a static, objective thing, but where Jaspers’ strategy is to keep Transcendence vague and undefined, Marcel points to prayer. Like Marcel, the early Ricoeur sees this personalistic approach as the key to maintaining divine transcendence. In an essay on Jaspers from the late 1950s, Ricoeur writes: “Who talks *to* God as Thou can talk *of* God as Transcendence, so far as that is fitting for man” (RJPR 640).

Ricoeur also found this personalist language in the theology of the Word, but as his linguistic turn continued, Ricoeur grew increasingly wary, or perhaps weary, of this personalist framework. In his view this framework is not self-critical enough in describing revelation as an I-Thou encounter, using analogical language like “person,” “friend,” and “father” to describe God. Ricoeur did not reject these terms, but he wanted to use them with a critical awareness of God’s “wholly otherness,” and by paying closer attention to the way these symbols are developed in the biblical text. He also wanted to subject them to the hermeneutics of suspicion (CI 395–96).<sup>39</sup> This shift toward the text was a consequence of Ricoeur’s work on structuralism. The transcendent Word may be given in a radically novel event, but in being revealed it has become “a fact of culture” (PPR 220). The Word comes to us in the text. The event is mediated by structures of language and texts, and is therefore available to methods of textual analysis. Ricoeur wants to maintain both event and structure in his concept of discourse, but his structuralist detour displaces his earlier description of the I-Thou encounter with God to focus on the meaning-event projected by the text. 

Recall Ricoeur’s definition of discourse as “someone saying something to someone about something.” This relation is fairly straightforward in speech but it is complicated by writing, which creates an immediate distanciation between the author and the written text. The text takes on a life of its own, distinct from the author’s intent. Nevertheless, Ricoeur holds that the text addresses the reader or hearer. The text has meaningful content (*something*) and reference (*about something*) and a reader or hearer (a *someone* to whom), but what about the *someone* who does the saying? In what sense does God speak or address us through the Word? 

This question has been discussed at length by Nicholas Wolterstorff in his book *Divine Discourse*, which is quite critical of Ricoeur.<sup>40</sup> The book prompted some debate among Ricoeur scholars, which I will not rehearse here. Instead, I would like to shift the focus slightly by framing my question 

*[p. 117]* ^pg134

in other terms that Ricoeur employs. Ricoeur describes the Bible as *becoming* the Word of God. What does this mean? For Barth, this means a personal encounter with God, who is living, active, and in some way present in the hermeneutical event. My question is whether Ricoeur identifies any place in biblical hermeneutics for personal encounter with God?

\*

Here it is instructive to compare Ricoeur with Barth—not because Barth’s account of biblical revelation is the gold standard for Christian theology, but because Barth was such a formative influence for Ricoeur. By looking at them together we consider how much Ricoeur departs from Barth. There is still scholarly debate about how Barthian Ricoeur is.<sup>41</sup> It is true that Ricoeur offers a philosophical approximation of Barthian themes, but this is more of a formal approximation than an appropriation of the content of Barth’s theology.

Ricoeur makes a very Barthian point insofar as he does not want to identify the Word of God with the biblical text itself, so that it would be reified in that object. For Barth, talk about the “Word of God” is analogical, with three related senses. The first sense of the Word is the divine Logos, the second person of the Trinity. The Word is thus first and foremost the incarnation of Jesus, the Logos, in human flesh. This revelation is a free decision of God, and as such it is not “tied to the Bible,” but rather “the Bible is tied to the Word of God.”<sup>42</sup> The second sense of the Word, then, is the Bible, but the biblical text is revelation only in relation to the first sense of the Word. The third sense of the Word is the proclamation of the Word.<sup>43</sup> What unites the three senses of this analogy of the Word is the person of Jesus. What makes the biblical text the Word of God is its witness to Jesus Christ.<sup>44</sup>

In his early work Ricoeur does frame revelation in these personal terms, writing of “the Truth which *is* and is *shown* as Person” (HT 177). Ricoeur also follows Barth by refusing to treat the text as a fixed repository of revelation. He admits serious reservations regarding the idea of a “sacred text,” since it risks reifying the revelatory power of the text. At one point Ricoeur even states a preference for speaking of the text as *revealing* rather than *revelation*, since revealing is an ongoing dynamic process rather than a static deposit of information (FS 72). What matters is the event of meaning, not the markings on the page. This means we need a “constantly renewed interpretation” of biblical events like the exodus from Egypt and the resurrection of Jesus. “These are the events of deliverance that open and disclose the utmost possibilities of my own freedom and thus *become for me* the word of God” (FS 47).

Where Ricoeur differs from Barth, however, is in what this *becoming* means. For Barth, God is the active subject of revelation, the agent of the event 

*[p. 118]* ^pg135

in which the Bible becomes the Word of God. It is the Word of God because God is present in this event through the agency of the Holy Spirit. Is this the case with Ricoeur? For Ricoeur, this becoming is the work of the imagination addressed by the poetic, revelatory power of the biblical text. But is it also a work of divine action? On its own, the human word has no capacity for the divine Word. Only through this divine act does it become capable of bearing the divine. We find this point in Jüngel’s *God as the Mystery of the World*, a text Ricoeur is fond of citing. Jüngel writes of the “language event” in which “God relates himself to the world and to man and thus is expressed in language,” but this “is not a capacity of language itself; it is not its own possibility, but rather an alien possibility which is opened up to language and required of it. It is not possible for language as such to correspond to God.”<sup>45</sup> What then does Ricoeur have to say about this divine action?

Ricoeur is quite difficult to pin down on this question. In one notable episode, Ricoeur was faced with a direct question about God’s active role in the hermeneutical process. In a discussion of Werner Jeanrond’s essay “Hermeneutics and Revelation,” a participant posed the question to Ricoeur directly: “Do you see in the hermeneutical process, in the activity of interpreting (looking on this question as a believer rather than a philosopher) the action of God in the action of interpretation as an individual or a community approaches a text? Is God active in that interpretation? Or is it purely reflective, educative, hermeneutic activity? Is hermeneutics a religious activity?” Ricoeur’s response is promising enough at the start: “The emphasis put on texts, of course, has primacy in the history of hermeneutics but it does not exhaust the concept of interpretation.” So far so good. He continues: “As Jeanrond has said, there is also the emotional aspect, the active, the practical side, and the development of a political theology, all these aspects have to be taken into account.”<sup>46</sup> Then Ricoeur switches to the difference between Hebrew and Greek modes of thought.

This was an ideal opportunity for Ricoeur to clarify his stance—as “a believer rather than a philosopher”—yet he dodges the question. Ricoeur recognizes that the biblical texts recount narratives of divine action, but that is different from God acting here and now. For Ricoeur, God acts in the historical narratives recounted in the text, but it is unclear whether God acts through the text in our present interpretation. Something similar is true regarding God’s speech. While God certainly performs all kinds of speech acts *in* the text, Ricoeur is less clear about whether God speaks *through* the text to address us here and now.<sup>47</sup>

Ricoeur admits that his emphasis on the written text was in part a reaction to Protestant theology’s “emphasis on speech,” and that his own view is that there is more of a dialectical relation between speech and writing, and likewise the voice and text.<sup>48</sup> Ricoeur articulates this dialectical relation in his 

*[p. 119]* ^pg136

essay “On the Entanglement of Speech and Writing in Biblical Discourse.”<sup>49</sup> He develops the idea of a “written voice,”<sup>50</sup> akin to his discussion of narrative voice in *Time and Narrative* 2. Narrative voice is “the silent speech that presents the world of the text to the reader. Like the voice that spoke to Augustine at the hour of his conversion, it says, *Tolle! Lege!* Take and Read!” (TN2 99). This narrative voice is not, *nota bene*, to be identified with the voice of the author. It is the distinctive style and tone that has a quasi-vocal presence in the written text. This narrative voice is what “makes the work speak and address itself to a reader” (TN2 102). Just as writing in some sense infiltrates speech, then, so the spoken word “infiltrates” in its own way the written word.<sup>51</sup> There is an absence in the spoken word and a presence in writing.

This idea of the written voice is an important insight in the phenomenology of reading, but it does not quite answer the question of the divine Word in the human word. Ricoeur does, however, suggest it can illuminate the relation between the Word of God and Scripture. There is a vertical gap between the divine Word and the human words that make up the biblical text, which can be read like any other written text. Likewise, the reciprocal relation between the spoken word and the written word can also illuminate the circular relation of the divine Word and Scripture. The Word of God is what founds Scripture, but at the same time Scripture is what manifests the Word.<sup>52</sup> The biblical authors thus use ordinary human words and writings to “let the Word of the Other speak.”<sup>53</sup> The Word of God is present in the text. There is a transcendent Word “gathered in the Scriptures.”<sup>54</sup> Most of Ricoeur’s work focuses on the human aspect of hermeneutics, which is understandable for philosophical hermeneutics. This is also highly instructive for theological hermeneutics, since Ricoeur has a much more nuanced understanding of the “range of human linguistic activity” than Barth does.<sup>55</sup> What Ricoeur provides in this late essay is a clearer assertion that the Word of God is also present in these human texts.

Does this mean that Ricoeur still allows for a concept of revelation as an I-Thou, interpersonal encounter with God? For Barth, revelation is the presence of God: “God himself is the content of His revelation.”<sup>56</sup> We need to be careful, however, not to suggest that the Bible is somehow another incarnation of the divine Word. This would confuse Barth’s first and second senses in the analogy of the Word. The Bible is not Jesus, but a witness to him that also participates in him.<sup>57</sup> Thus, instead of being the personal presence of God, the biblical text is a kind of impersonal, *verbal* presence—a “presence-in-absence.”<sup>58</sup>

In some sense Ricoeur sees the biblical text as living and active, since it pronounces a word of address. But what makes the text living and active? On Barth’s view, the Holy Spirit is not only the active agent who illuminates the meaning of the text, but also the agent who makes Christ present 

*[p. 120]* ^pg137

in the exposition and proclamation of the Word. The Holy Spirit does not magically deliver the reader from the hermeneutical task, but the Spirit’s involvement does mean that the interpreter is at the service of God’s active Word.<sup>59</sup> For Ricoeur, the transformative power of the text lies primarily in the imagination and conatus in response to the address of the text. For example, he writes that just as there is a progressive recognition of Jesus as the Christ within the Gospel texts, so the Gospel enables this recognition in the reader: “The Gospel is not a simple account of the life, teaching, work, death, and resurrection of Jesus, but the communicating of an act of confession, a communication by means of which the reader in turn is rendered *capable* of performing the same recognition that occurs inside the text” (FS 162). How does the text render us capable? Through its power to address the imagination and initiate movement within the conatus. This is a profound insight and an unparalleled phenomenological, hermeneutical account of how texts transform us, but Ricoeur stops short of discussing how the Holy Spirit makes it possible for us to recognize Christ, even though this is a biblical idea and not a peculiarity of a particular doctrinal tradition ([[1 Corinthians 12#^12-3|I Corinthians 12:3]]).

That said, Ricoeur makes another surprisingly direct statement in “The Entanglement of Speech and Writing in Biblical Discourse.” He closes the essay by observing that the “unsaid” of the essay is “to know the Spirit.” The proper sequence of ideas is not *Word, Scripture, Religion*, but rather *Word, Scripture, Spirit*. The Spirit encompasses the mutually constituting relation of Word and Scripture, and likewise the relation between Scripture and the confessing community. The Spirit is the larger circle that contains these other hermeneutical circles. Faith therefore means “believing that the ‘interior testimony of the Holy Spirit’” is at work in the inspiration attributed to Scripture by confessing communities, and also in the hearing and interpretation of Scripture in these communities. Both are “the work of one and the same Spirit.”<sup>60</sup>

If the Holy Spirit is the unsaid of the entire essay, these remarks still leave a lot unsaid. Is the Spirit the third person of the Trinity, or a more impersonal principle of discernment, Hegel-style? That is how it appears in *The Symbolism of Evil*, where Ricoeur describes the Holy Spirit more as a principle of discernment than a person. If this does not satisfy the requirements of a robustly Trinitarian theory of interpretation, these remarks at least do not foreclose this possibility. Instead, they leave open the space for a full-fledged theological hermeneutics.

We might also wonder whether this notion of the written voice signals a return to Ricoeur’s earlier personalism, and revelation as an I-Thou encounter. Not quite. Ricoeur does, however, make room for something like an I-Thou encounter with his later model of the call and response as the essential structure of religious experience. That is the topic of our next chapter.
