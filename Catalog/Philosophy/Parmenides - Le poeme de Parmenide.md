## About

The one known work of Parmenides of Elea: a didactic poem in dactylic hexameters, of which about 160 lines survive. A goddess shows a young man the way of truth about what is, and the deceptive opinions of mortals.

- **Author:** Parmenides came from Elea, a Greek colony in southern Italy. Reckoning from Plato's *Parmenides*, the SEP puts his birth about 515 BC and his activity in the early to mid-fifth century.
- **Date and title:** Wikipedia gives the early fifth century BC, suggesting 490–475 BC, though it notes that Guthrie thought the date impossible to fix. Diogenes Laertius says Parmenides wrote only this one work; the SEP thinks the title *On Nature* (Peri physeōs) probably not his.
- **Survival:** the original was perhaps about 800 lines (Wikipedia's opening gives 800 to 3,000). About 160 survive in quotations by later writers. Sextus Empiricus preserves 30 of the proem's 32 lines, and Simplicius (6th century AD) quotes fragment 8.1–52, so the main argument is almost complete. Diels reckoned the remains cover about nine-tenths of the Way of Truth and one-tenth of the Way of Opinion.
- **Structure:** a proem (fr. 1), a chariot journey to a goddess past gates kept by Justice (Dike); the Way of Truth, arguing that what is is ungenerated, imperishable, whole, unmoved and complete (fr. 8); and the Way of Opinion, a cosmology of light and night that the goddess marks as deceptive.
- **Numbering:** Hermann Diels, *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* (1903), revised by Walther Kranz (1934). Fragments are cited as DK 28 B with a number; Diels counted 19.
- **Interpretation:** the SEP sets out open questions: whether Parmenides was a strict monist, whether his arguments are logical or metaphysical, and what standing the cosmology has.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it one of the foundational texts of early Western metaphysics, and lists Plato and Aristotle among those who quote it; it also notes that Plotinus cites fragment 3. The SEP adds that Parmenides recognized that the moon shines by the sun's reflected light (frs. 14–15).
