### 2.1–4: Exhortation to attention

*[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-1|Text]], p. 464* ^2-1

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-1|1]]:** *What we have heard*, traditions about Jesus received from teachers or preachers. ^2-1-2

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-2|2]]:** *Message* (Gk “logos,” lit., “word), here referring to the Torah. *Declared through angels*, angels delivered the Torah on God’s behalf. See [[Acts 07#^7-53|Acts 7.53]]; [[Galatians 03#^3-19|Gal 3.19]]. There is a tradition that angels were present at Sinai when God gave the Torah, but outside the NT they do not play the role of proclaimers or mediators (cf. [[Torah 54 - V'Zot HaBerachah#^33-2|Deut 33.2]]; Josephus, *Ant.* 15.5.3). In the Tanakh and throughout Jewish thought, God gives the Torah directly to Moses. *Transgression . . . received a just penalty* refers to the Sinaitic covenant (e.g., [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-19|Ex 20.22–23.33]]) that specifies penalties for sins. ^2-2

> [!note] Perfection through Suffering *(p. 464)*
> Pervasive in Hebrews, the language of perfection refers to more advanced teachings (6.1), the condition of those who follow Jesus (10.1,14; 11.40; 12.23), what the Law lacks but what Jesus achieves (7.11,19; 9.9), and what Jesus achieves for himself through his suffering (2.10; 5.9). The term itself, “teleios,” connotes maturity, fulfillment, moral perfection, wholeness, and holiness. In Israelite and Jewish cultic contexts, the Hebrew equivalent for “perfection” (*tammim*) refers to the unblemished state of sacrificial animals and the condition of the priest who makes the sacrifice (see e.g., [[Torah 31 - Emor#^21-16|Lev 21.16–21]]; [[Torah 31 - Emor#^22-20|22.20–25]]). Many exhortations to be perfect before God appear in the Tanakh ([[Torah 04 - Vayera#^20-5|Gen 20.5]]; [[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^18-13|Deut 18.13]]; [[Judges 09#^9-16|Judg 9.16]],[[Judges 09#^9-19|19]]). Philo, like the author of Hebrews, favors perfection terminology, and uses it to name the goal of the virtuous individual (*Leg. all.* 1.94; 3.74; *Names* 24) as well as the state of the person who achieves a vision of the divine (*Leg. all.* 3.74). Finally, according to Hebrews, Jesus is perfected through his suffering. That is not to say that Jesus starts out as imperfect. In Hebrews, Jesus has divine origins, he is neither lacking anything nor in need of correction (see 7.26). Rather, in Greek perfection always connotes a developmental process—to move toward perfection is to move toward completion or accomplishment of a goal. The language of “perfection through suffering” in reference to Jesus has in view the claim that Jesus has completed his divine mission to effect permanent expiation and thus enable emergence of the world to come (Heb *olam haba*, see e.g., *m. Pe*ʾ*ah* 1.1; *m. B. Metz*. 2.11). This peripheral idea of suffering as divine discipline in the Tanakh became mainstream in Hellenistic Judaism. In [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 04#^4-13|Wis 4.13]], the righteous man who dies before reaching old age is said to have been “perfected,” even though his life was brief; in [[LXX 4 Maccabees 07#^7-15|4 Macc 7.15]], Eleazer’s martyrdom is said to have rendered him “perfect.” In the latter case, the language of perfection describes a pious man’s suffering to death. Rabbinic literature (perhaps under the influence of the Hadrianic persecutions of 132–135 CE) also develops the idea of redemptive suffering, where it is called *yisurin shel ahavah*, “chastisements of love”; *Mek.* “Bakhodesh” 10; *b. Ber.* [[Berakhot 5#5a|5a]]; *b. Sanh.* [[Sanhedrin 101#101a|101a]]; cf. [[Hebrews 05#^5-8|Heb 5.8]].

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-3|3]]:** *Escape*, evade the coming judgment (12.25; see also [[1 Thessalonians 05#^5-3|1 Thess 5.3]]). *Lord*, Jesus. *By those who heard him*, evidence for dating Hebrews at least a generation after Jesus. ^2-3

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-4|4]]:** *Signs and wonders*, used of miracles performed by Moses and Aaron ([[Torah 14 - Vaera#^7-3|Ex 7.3]]; [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^4-34|Deut 4.34]]; [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^6-22|6.22]]), referring here to miracles performed by Jesus and the apostles ([[Acts 02#^2-43|Acts 2.43]]; [[Acts 15#^15-12|15.12]]). *Gifts of the Holy Spirit*, unspecified, but perhaps including ecstatic experiences like speaking in tongues ([[1 Corinthians 12#^12-4|1 Cor 12.4–11]]), or increased wisdom, discernment, and understanding ([[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 07#^7-22|Wis 7.22–8.1]]). ^2-4

### 2.5–9: Jesus exalted

*[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-5|Text]], p. 465* ^2-5

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-5|5]]:** *Coming world*, not the present one, but a future world where Jesus reigns. This coming world is not far off, however. Cf. v 8. Unlike the rabbinic idea of “olam haba” (Heb “the world to come”) or Rom 8, where the world-to-come is this world reborn, the coming world in Hebrews is envisaged a spiritual, eternal space, while this world is destroyed (12.22,26–27). [[Psalms 110#^110-1|Ps 110.1]], quoted in [[Hebrews 01#^1-13|Heb 1.13]], functions as a summary statement of this reign. *To angels*, the Greek is ambiguous and could be rendered “for the sake of angels.” In continuity with the argument of the previous verses, the meaning is that neither this world nor the next was created for the benefit of angels. That is why human beings share the inheritance of the next world with Jesus, who lived in this world as a human being. (See 1.14; 2.11–12.) ^2-5-2

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-6|6–7]]:** *Someone has testified . . .* , author of [[Psalms 008#^8-5|Ps 8.5–8]]. *Human beings . . . them* (Gk “anthropos . . . auton”), the terms, singular in Heb (“enosh . . . ben adam”) and Gk are understood to be about Jesus, here seen as the “son of man” (Gk “huis anthropou”). The NRSV’s attempt at gender inclusion masks the Christological connection. *Made them . . . crowned them*, again the Greek is in the singular. *A little while*, in the LXX but not in the MT; in the Hebrew of [[Psalms 008#^8-6|Ps 8.5]], “little” (“meʿat”) refers to status of humanity vis-à-vis “the gods” (Heb “Elohim”), not time. Hebrews interprets the phrase temporally as referring to the time of Jesus’ earthly life. ^2-6

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-8|8]]:** *Them . . . their . . . them*, the Greek is again in the singular. ^2-8

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-9|9]]:** *Made lower*, took on human form (see [[Philippians 02#^2-6|Phil 2.6]]). *Because of the suffering*, see “Perfection through Suffering,” p. 464. *Taste death for everyone*, Jesus not only fully shares the experience of being human, his death constitutes a sacrifice of atonement that benefits his followers. Cleansed of sin they are able to live eternally in the divine presence (10.14,19). Hebrew of [[Psalms 008#^8-5|Psalm 8.4-6]] both *man* and *son of man* refer to all humankind d Or *them only a little lower* e Other ancient authorities add *and set them over the works of your hands* f Or *who was made a little lower* g Other ancient authorities read *apart from God* ^2-9

### 2.10–18: Jesus’ humanity

*[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-10|Text]], p. 466* ^2-10

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-10|10]]:** *Pioneer*, Jesus. *Perfect*, see “Perfection through Suffering,” p. 464. See also 5.8; 7.28; 10.14; 12.2. *Children to glory*, the Gk is ambiguous as to whether it is God or Jesus who brings “many children to glory.” The NRSV translators assume that *for whom and through whom all things exist* refers to God, but Hebrews has portrayed Jesus as a kind of co-creator to whom everything will eventually be in subjection (1.1,10; 2.8–9). ^2-10-2

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-11|11]]:** *Sanctifies*, makes holy. *One Father* (lit., “are all of One”; “father” is not in the Gk), the ambiguous phrase could refer to God as “father” (so the NRSV), to Adam, or to Abraham (cf. 11.12). On God as the father of humanity, see [[Malachi 2#^2-10|Mal 2.10]]. ^2-11

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-11|11]]:** *Not ashamed*, meaning “proud.” This manner of speaking is a Greek rhetorical device known as “litotes,” an exaggerated understatement. ^2-11-2

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-12|12]]:** [[Psalms 022#^22-23|Ps 22.22]]. The Psalm is used to describe Jesus’ suffering and death in [[Matthew 27#^27-46|Mt 27.46]]; [[Mark 15#^15-34|Mk 15.34]]. In Hebrews, the citation highlights Jesus’ role as mediator between God and humans. The Psalm originally speaks in the voice of a supplicant. Jewish sources do not read this Psalm as predicting a Messiah. *Congregation***,** Gk “ekklesia” (usually translated “church” in the NRSV). ^2-12

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-13|13]]:** [[Isaiah 08#^8-17|Isa 8.17–18]]. Isaiah speaks in the first person, whereas Hebrews understands the speaker as Jesus. ^2-13

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-14|14]]:** For the idea that the *devil* brought *death* into the world, see [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 02#^2-24|Wis 2.24]]; although the idea appears in rabbinic literature, it is often minimized or refuted, perhaps due to anti-Christian polemic; see *t. Sot.* 4.17; *b*. *B. Bat.* 16a. ^2-14

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-16|16]]:** *Help angels*, angels are not the objects of God’s care and affection; God’s human children are. *Descendants of Abraham*, followers of Jesus, Jew and Gentile both, are heirs of the Abrahamic promises (see ch 11). ^2-16

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-17|17]]:** *In every respect*, for Hebrews, Jesus is both fully human and fully divine (see 4.14–15; [[1 Corinthians 15#^15-20|1 Cor 15.20–28]]). This thesis became Christian doctrine in 325 at the Council of Nicea and was definitively formulated at the Council of Chalcedon in 451 (see “Christology,” p. 754). Jesus as *high priest* is unique to Hebrews (see 4.14–5.10; 7.1–8.7; 9.11–14). For Jewish anticipations of a messianic high priest, see 1QS 9.11; *T. Levi* 18**.**1–14. Paradoxically, Jesus is both perfect priest and perfect sacrifice. *Atonement,* the author accepts the traditional view that atonement requires sacrifices, and the Yom Kippur sacrifices can be offered only by the high priest (Lev 16). See “Sacrifice and the Temple,” p. 658. ^2-17

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 02#^2-18|18]]:** *Tested*, see [[Torah 16 - Beshalach#^15-25|Ex 15.25]]; [[Torah 46 - Eikev#^8-2|Deut 8.2]],[[Torah 46 - Eikev#^8-16|16]]. Suffering was frequently interpreted as a test from God. The idea is found in biblical (e.g., [[Proverbs 03#^3-11|Prov 3.11–12]]); Second Temple (e.g., [[LXX Sirach 02#^2-1|Sir 2.1–9]]; [[LXX 2 Maccabees 06#^6-12|2 Macc 6.12–16]]); and rabbinic texts (e.g., *b. Ber*. 8.a); Abraham was “tested” at the Akedah ([[Torah 04 - Vayera#^22-1|Gen 22.1]]) and the “tests of Abraham” are developed in early Jewish and rabbinic literature (see 11.17–19n.). *Able to help*, God is cast in the role of judge and disciplinarian, while Jesus is cast in the role of advocate and intercessor; this is consistent with the portrait of God as Father, who is awesome, majestic, and far removed from the human sphere, and Jesus as Son, an elder sibling who shared human experience and a high priest who intercedes on behalf of others (cf. 10.19–31; 12.3–8). ^2-18
