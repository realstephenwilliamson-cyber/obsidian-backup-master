# Mark 8 (JANT notes)

[[JANT Mark 07|← Mark 7]] · [[Jewish Annotated New Testament|JANT index]] · [[JANT Mark 09|Mark 9 →]]

Text: [[NRSVue Mark 08|NRSVue]] · [[Mark 08|BSB and SBLGNT]] · Introduction: [[JANT Mark - Introduction]]

### 8.1–10: Feeding of four thousand

*[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-1|Text]], p. 85* · ([[Matthew 15#^15-32|Mt 15.32–39]]). This second feeding miracle takes place in a predominantly Gentile area (see 6.30–44n.). There were two instances of feeding in the wilderness during the Exodus (Ex 16; Num 12). ^8-1

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-5|5]]:** *Seven*, may symbolize completeness, as it is in the days of creation (Gen 1) or the fullness of praise (“seven times a day,” [[Psalms 119#^119-164|Ps 119.164]]). ^8-5

### 8.11–13: Pharisees request a sign

*[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-11|Text]], p. 86* · ([[Matthew 12#^12-38|Mt 12.38–39]]; [[Matthew 16#^16-1|16.1–4]]; [[Luke 11#^11-16|Lk 11.16]],[[Luke 11#^11-29|29]]). ^8-11

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-11|11]]:** *Sign* (Gk “sēmeion*,*” Heb “ ʾot”), the prophet’s action that verifies that he has been sent from God, was commonly used for the end-time prophets Josephus describes (*Ant.* 20.167–70). Signs are a common motif in the Hebrew Bible, and so the Pharisees’ request might seem reasonable. Mark characterizes Jesus’ miracles as “acts of power” (Gk “dunameis”) rather than signs; see 13.22, and contrast [[John 02#^2-11|Jn 2.11]]; [[John 04#^4-54|4.54]]. In the Hebrew Bible, signs are sometimes ignored (e.g., [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^4-8|Ex 4.8]],[[Torah 13 - Shemot#^4-9|9]]); God provides them as indications of promise (Judg 6, where Gideon receives three signs; [[Isaiah 07#^7-11|Isa 7.11]]**–**14, where the king demurs yet receives a sign anyway). ^8-11-2

### 8.14–21: Leaven (yeast) of the Pharisees and Herod

*[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-14|Text]], p. 86* · ([[Matthew 16#^16-5|Mt 16.5–12]]; [[Luke 12#^12-1|Lk 12.1]]). *Pharisees . . . Herod*, see introduction. *Yeast*, or leaven (Heb “ḥamets”), dough made of wheat, barley, spelt, rye, or oats, mixed with water and allowed to stand until soured. It is not eaten on Passover ([[Torah 15 - Bo#^12-39|Ex 12.39]]); anyone who consumes it then is considered cut off ([[Torah 15 - Bo#^12-19|Ex 12.19]]). Yeast, along with honey, is not allowed as part of the grain offering ([[Torah 24 - Vayikra#^2-11|Lev 2.11]]). Although viewed negatively here, it is not “impure.” ^8-14

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-18|18]]:** *Eyes . . . see . . . ears . . . hear*, proverbial for failure to heed God’s word ([[Isaiah 06#^6-9|Isa 6.9–10]]; [[Jeremiah 05#^5-21|Jer 5.21]]) but also characteristic of idols ([[Psalms 115#^115-3|Ps 115.3]]**–**8). ^8-18

### 8.22–10.52: Second major section: Passion predictions and social teachings

*[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-22|Text]], p. 86* · This section is framed by miracles of giving sight to the blind. At the center are three nearly identical predictions of Jesus’ suffering and death, associated with the concept of “way” (8.27–33; 9.30–37; 10.32–34), and social teachings. The literary device of framing a passage is called “inclusio(n),” and absent chapter breaks and other visual indicators indicated where a unit in an ancient narrative was completed. By using this device, Mark emphasizes true understanding, as well as misunderstandings, of Jesus’ messianic role. ^8-22

### 8.22–26: Healing of a blind man

*[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-22|Text]], p. 86* · ([[John 09#^9-1|Jn 9.1–7]]). ^8-22-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-22|22]]:** *Bethsaida*, at the upper end of the Sea of Galilee, in the territory of (Herod) Philip, one of Herod the Great’s sons, who ruled 4 BCE–34 CE. Philip rebuilt the city and renamed it “Bethsaida Julias” after Julia, one of Augustus’s daughters. Matthew and Luke omit this episode, perhaps because it depicts Jesus’ inability to heal immediately. This two-stage healing may anticipate the two-stage reception of Jesus’ messianic identity: the disciples lack clear vision (they understand Jesus is the “messiah,” but they don’t know what the term means) and only grasp the full reality after Jesus’ death (and after the close of Mark’s Gospel; see 16.8n.). ^8-22-3

### 8.27–33: Peter’s confession and Jesus’ first Passion prediction

*[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-27|Text]], p. 87* · ([[Matthew 16#^16-13|Mt 16.13–23]]; [[Luke 09#^9-18|Lk 9.18–22]]). Mark subordinates the expectation of a Son of Man coming in power to the Son of Man suffering and crucified. Although up to now Jesus has been opposed by the Pharisees, only one occasion (3.6) suggested their murderous intent. From this point on it is *the elders, the chief priests, and the scribes* who will reject Jesus and deliver him to the Roman governor to *be killed.* Pharisees do not appear in the Passion narrative, perhaps because they were viewed as a group in competition with the followers of Jesus but not as the group that colluded in his death (only in 3.6 do the Pharisees conspire \[with the Herodians\] to destroy him). ^8-27

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-27|27]]:** *Caesarea Philippi*, originally called Panion after the god Pan, to whom there was an important cult site, was refounded under Herod’s son Philip, who renamed it in honor of the emperor and himself. A major temple, probably dedicated to the emperor, has been discovered nearby. Perhaps because it was situated near a spring, a source of the Jordan River, and below a sacred mountain (Mount Hermon), the location became a sacred site for different gods. The ancient Israelite shrine of Dan was near here, and *1 En.* 13.7 describes the setting as the site of the revelations to Enoch. Not only Peter’s confession but also the Transfiguration (9.2–10) is located in this area, important to Jews and non-Jews alike. The mountain of the Transfiguration is not named, but the proximity in the text would suggest to an ancient audience that it was Mount Hermon. After the Transfiguration and the healing in 9.14–29, Jesus and his disciples go into Galilee and arrive at Capernaum (9.35), on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee and not far from the border with Philip’s tetrarchy. ^8-27-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-28|28–29]]:** The disciples say that Jesus has been associated with prophetic figures (6.15), but Peter proclaims him to be *the Messiah* (see 1.1n.; [[Matthew 16#^16-16|Mt 16.16–22]] offers a fuller confession). ^8-28

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-30|30–32]]:** The messianic secret is most explicit here; Jesus speaks *quite openly* about what would happen to him, but only to his disciples. ^8-30

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-31|31]]:** *Must undergo* expresses the determinism of God’s plan, similar to apocalyptic texts like Daniel, *4 Ezra* \[2 Esd\], and *1 Enoch*. ^8-31

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-32|32–33]]:** See [[Zechariah 03#^3-2|Zech 3.2]] where, in the presence of the high priest Joshua—Jesus in Greek!—God rebukes Satan with similar language. Peter cannot imagine a messiah who suffers and dies, and Jesus’ sharp response defines this passage as a central, defining moment in the Gospel. Jewish tradition often considered the suffering of good people ([[Proverbs 03#^3-12|Prov 3.12]], “the LORD reproves the one he loves,” and *b. Sanh.* [[Sanhedrin 101#101a|101a]]-b), but here the issue is the role of the Messiah. Other texts understood the suffering of the Messiah by referring to [[Isaiah 52#^52-13|Isa 52.13–53.12]] ([[Matthew 08#^8-17|Mt 8.17]]; [[1 Peter 02#^2-24|1 Pet 2.24–25]]), but Mark does not cite this passage and only hints at it in 10.45; 14.12–25. *sake of the gospel* c Other ancient authorities read *and of mine* ^8-32

### 8.34–9.1: On discipleship

*[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-34|Text]], p. 88* · ([[Matthew 16#^16-24|Mt 16.24–28]]; [[Luke 09#^9-23|Lk 9.23–27]]; cf. [[Matthew 10#^10-32|Mt 10.32–33]]; [[Luke 12#^12-4|Lk 12.4–9]]). ^8-34

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-34|8.34–38]]:** Mark emphasizes the high stakes, necessity of decision, the potential for persecution, and the salvific rewards of being part of the elect community. ^8-34-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-35|35]]:** *Save . . . lose . . . lose . . . save*, self-preservation cannot be the highest value. ^8-35

**[[NRSVue Mark 08#^8-38|38]]:** *This adulterous and sinful generation*, like those who murmured against Moses ([[Torah 53 - Ha'Azinu#^32-20|Deut 32.20]]). Adultery is sometimes a representative sin for the sin of idolatry (e.g., [[Jeremiah 03#^3-1|Jer 3.1–5]]), or for the sin of being unfaithful to God (Hos 1). The *Son of Man* is the figure who carries out divine judgment. **9.1**: *Some standing here*, depending on how readers understood this promise, it can refer to the crucifixion or the beginning of the eschatological age (perhaps seen as the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE). See also 13.30–32. ^8-38
