## About

A set of 77 aphorisms on non-dual Śaiva yoga, attributed to Vasugupta of Kashmir (9th century). They underlie the Kashmiri Śaiva traditions of Spanda and Trika and are read through commentaries, chiefly Kṣemarāja's *Vimarśinī* and Bhāskara's *Vārttika*.

- **Origin:** tradition holds that the sūtras were revealed rather than composed; the IEP and Britannica both report revelation to Vasugupta. Wikipedia gives two versions: a Siddha taught them to him in a dream, or Śiva in a dream sent him to a rock on which they were inscribed. Vasugupta is said to have lived near Mahādeva mountain, behind the present Shalimar Gardens near Srinagar.
- **Structure:** three sections named for the three means (upāya) to liberation, ordered from highest to lowest: śāmbhavopāya, through Śiva, in a thought-free state reached by the will; śāktopāya, through Śakti, by contemplating I-consciousness; and āṇavopāya, through the limited individual, by attending to intellect, breath, body, senses or objects.
- **Commentaries:** Kṣemarāja's *Śivasūtravimarśinī* is the best known; Wikipedia dates it to the 10th century, while the IEP dates Kṣemarāja, Abhinavagupta's disciple, to c. 1000–1050. Wikipedia places Bhāskara's *Vārttika* in the 11th century.
- **Related text:** the *Spanda Kārikās*. Britannica credits them to Vasugupta (8th–9th century); the IEP says they were systematized by either Vasugupta or his disciple Kallaṭa.
- **Translations:** Wikipedia lists English translations of the *Vimarśinī* by Jaideva Singh and Swami Lakshman Joo, and Mark Dyczkowski's English translation of Bhāskara's *Vārttika*. The IEP's bibliography calls Singh's volume an accessible translation and introduction to one of the core texts of monistic Kashmiri Śaivism.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls the aphorisms the foundation of the non-dual Spanda tradition. Britannica's list of the school's works runs from them and the *Spanda Kārikās* through Utpala (c. 900) to Abhinavagupta's 10th-century *Tantrāloka*.
