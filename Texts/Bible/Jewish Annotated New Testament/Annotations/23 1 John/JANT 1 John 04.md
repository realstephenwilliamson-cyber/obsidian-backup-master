### 4.1–5.12: Identifying children of God

*[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-1|Text]], p. 523* · **4.1–2**: *Test the spirits*, the author warns his community to be vigilant against *false prophets* who propagate false teaching (see [[Torah 47 - Re'eh#^13-2|Deut 13.1–5]]; [[Jeremiah 14#^14-13|Jer 14.13–16]]); the “test” is the confession of *Jesus Christ . . . in the flesh* (i.e., not an illusion of being human). This statement implies that the opponents, presumably those recently separated, deny that Jesus came in the flesh. [[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^18-20|Deut 18.20–22]] provides two criteria for distinguishing true from false prophets: (1) true prophets speak on behalf of the God of Israel alone (i.e., not on behalf of other gods), and (2) true prophecy will come to pass. These criteria are not fail-safe; for example, if false prophets are recognized by the failure of their prophecy at some future time, how is one to decide in the present? The rabbis likewise were concerned about the distinction between true and false prophets; they warned, for example, that false prophets would entice Jews to transgress Mosaic Law by promising great rewards (e.g., *Pesiq. Rav Kah*. 24.15). ^4-1

**[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-5|5]]:** *From the world*, the sphere of opposition to God; see 2.15n. ^4-5

**[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-6|6]]:** *Spirit of truth . . . error*, the Holy Spirit contrasted with the spirit of the world or the devil (3.10). The DSS refer to two competing spirits: the “spirit of truth,” is associated with light, truth, and righteousness; the “spirit of deceit” or “Belial” (lit., “of no worth”) is associated with darkness, sin, and evil (1QS 3.17–21; 4.7–25). ^4-6

**[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-7|7–12]]:** *Let us love one another*, see 3.11. The love that community members have for (Gk *dissolves Jesus*) each other is participation in God’s love, shown when *God sent his only Son . . . to be the atoning sacrifice* (see 2.2n.). ^4-7

> [!note] Atonement *(p. 524)*
> Both [[1 John 02#^2-2|1 Jn 2.2]] and 4.10 identify Jesus as “the atoning sacrifice for our sins.” The concept of atonement (from the Hebrew root *k-p-r*, lit., “to purge,” “decontaminate”) is central to the Priestly literature in the Tanakh, where it refers to the ritual purification of the Temple or its parts. In order for God to live in his sacred dwelling place among his people, the Temple needed to be free of contamination ([[Torah 28 - Metzora#^15-31|Lev 15.31]]; see “Sacrifice and the Temple,” p. 658). According to Leviticus, particular parts of the Tabernacle, that book’s predecessor to the Temple, accumulated both ritual impurities, for example when a ritually impure individual (e.g., a person who came in contact with a corpse and did not follow the prescriptions of Num 19) came near it, or from immoral acts. The act of ritual purification was achieved through the application of the blood of the sacrificial animal to particular parts of the Tabernacle, depending on the seriousness of the offense (contrast [[Torah 24 - Vayikra#^4-13|Lev 4.13–18]] with [[Torah 24 - Vayikra#^4-27|Lev 4.27–31]]). The biblical Day of Atonement (*Yom ha-Kippurim*, [[Torah 31 - Emor#^23-26|Lev 23.26–32]]) mandated the annual purification of any accumulated residual impurities in the Temple (Lev 16) as well as impurities among the Israelite people, who were to cease from working for that day and to fast ([[Torah 29 - Achrei Mot#^16-29|Lev 16.29–34]]).
>
> It is only in postbiblical Jewish literature that repentance—that is, engaging in self-reflection and experiencing feelings of regret about past deeds—becomes the key element associated with atonement. The Tanakh, however, especially the book of Deuteronomy, does recognize the importance of repentance, which is an individual or communal action that is not temple-based (see, e.g., [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^4-30|Deut 4.30]]). By the rabbinic period, the Priestly conception of atonement and Deuteronomy’s conception of repentance were combined and both were strongly associated with the Day of Atonement. Indeed, the overarching rabbinic view was that without repentance—a concept the rabbis called *teshuvah* (Heb lit., “returning”) —atonement was not possible: “Sin offering and guilt offering and death and the Day of Atonement, all of them together do not expiate sin without repentance” (*t. Yoma* 5.9). After the destruction of the Temple, when sacrifices could no longer be offered, the rabbis decreed, “Prayer, repentance, and charity avert the evil decree” (*y*. *Ta*ʾ*an*. 2.1,65b). The verses in John, from the pre-rabbinic period, partake in the Priestly notion in Lev 16, where a sacrifice—in the case of 1 John, of a person, not a goat—effects atonement on the community as a whole.

**[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-12|12]]:** *No one has ever seen God*, [[John 01#^1-18|Jn 1.18]]; the Bible is ambivalent about whether humans may see God and live; some individuals see God ([[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^33-11|Ex 33.11]]; [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^34-5|34.5–6]]; [[Torah 54 - V'Zot HaBerachah#^34-10|Deut 34.10]]; [[Isaiah 06#^6-1|Isa 6.1]]; Ezek 1) while other texts state that it is fatal to see God (e.g., [[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^18-16|Deut 18.16]]; [[Judges 13#^13-22|Judg 13.22–23]]). ^4-12

**[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-13|13]]:** *Spirit*, here capitalized to indicate that translators read it as being God’s Spirit. ^4-13

**[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-14|14]]:** *Savior of the world*, see [[John 04#^4-42|Jn 4.42]]; the Son came to save all, not just a particular group. ^4-14

**[[NRSVue 1 John 04#^4-17|17]]:** *Day of judgment*, this expression is not found in the Tanakh, though see “the Day of the Lord” (e.g., [[Isaiah 13#^13-6|Isa 13.6–13]]; [[Joel 1#^1-15|Joel 1.15]]; 2.1; [[Joel 3#^3-4|3.4]]; 4.14; Am 5.18–20; Ob 15; [[Zephaniah 1#^1-17|Zeph 1.17–18]]; [[Malachi 3#^3-23|Mal 3.23]]), a time, strongly associated with darkness and doom, when the wicked are punished and justice achieved. Within Jewish tradition, the “day of judgment” appears in Hellenistic and early Roman texts ([[LXX Judith 16#^16-17|Jdt 16.17]]; *Jub*. 5.10–14; *T. Levi* 3.2–3; see [[Daniel 07#^7-9|Dan 7.9–27]]); rabbinic literature (e.g., *m. Rosh Ha-Shanah* 1.1–2) portrays Rosh Ha-Shanna (lit., “head of the year”) as a “day of judgment” that marks the first day of the ten “days of awe”—a period of self-reflection and repentance that culminate in Yom Kippur. ^4-17
