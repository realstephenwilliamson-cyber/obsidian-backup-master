## Chapter 4: Female Gaṇeśa or independent deity?: Tracing the background of the elephant-faced goddess in mediaeval Śaiva Tantric traditions

*Chiara Policardi*

### 4.1 Introduction 1

A crucial question that has been asked, and will inevitably be asked again, is whether the partly animal form of some Hindu deities is indicative of their non-- Brahmanical or ‘tribal’ or ‘non-Aryan’ origins.<sup>2</sup> This is a complex issue, which requires an in-depth examination of each case on its own terms, and which may never find a conclusive solution.

According to Dehejia (1986: 1–2), local and tribal traditions represent a not irrelevant component in the formation of the Tantric *yoginī* cult. Theoretically, it is presumable that concepts and images of feminine deities initially flourishing mainly in indigenous polities are not abandoned when these tribes try to elevate their status as Brahmanized kingdoms or are embedded into states organized along Brahmanical lines. As Sarkar (2017: 146) observes about goddess cults,

The religious cults that developed in the vast majority of mediaeval Indian courts to enhance the divinity of the king all derived their key symbols (manifested in legends and rituals) from the ancient goddesses of the realm held to protect the royal lineage from its inception.

Indeed, Indic kings frequently adopt divinities—and according to Sarkar 2017 especially female divinities—venerated by the local population as part of a wider strategy of integration of indigenous communities whose numerical or military strength could contribute to the growth of the state. As is well-known, these processes of adaptation and integration of non-Brahmanical groups and customs, effectively called ‘Sanskritization’ and ‘inklusivismus’ by scholars,<sup>3</sup> have constantly informed the entire history of Hinduism, intertwining the political and the religious spheres.

The characteristics of *yoginī*s—and especially the composite animal-human nature that frequently connotes them<sup>4</sup>—point to a complex cultural background, in which non-Brahmanical or non-elite elements have probably played a significant role. However, the extant textual and material data does not make it possible to discern the extent and the specific lines of this contribution, which thus remains 

*[p. 79]* ^pg92

in some measure nebulous.<sup>5</sup> Nonetheless, it is beyond doubt that a tradition of representation of composite, animal-human deities formed the bedrock on which the conception and image of *yoginī* unfolded. As Hatley (2012) has shown, as a deity typology, the *yoginī*s demonstrate remarkable continuity with precedent, non-Tantric figures. Among the possible historical prototypes of therianthropic *yoginī*s, three classes of figures emerge as the most important: *gaṇa*s, *yakṣiṇī*s, and *mātṛ*s (Policardi 2020: 69–96).

The ensuing discussion highlights the main issues raised by the presence of an elephant-faced female figure in several *yoginī-*related texts and *yoginī* temples, tracing her background to the earliest attested textual occurrence and figurative representations. Known as Vināyakī, Gaṇeśanī, Gaṇeśvarī, Gajānanā and with numerous other epithets, this elephantine goddess has been given little scholarly attention in her own right, after the few studies published by Indian scholars in the 1970s (Mundkur 1975, Agrawala 1978, Sharma 1979). Interestingly, she is not known by a consistent name, and her iconographic attributes are not clearly defined by textual and figurative traditions. To a certain degree, this has afforded her fluctuating features across time and space, among which the elephant face is the invariable and most salient trait, the one that provides her with a distinctive identity.

A *communis opinio* found in secondary literature uncritically interprets this goddess as Gaṇeśa’s female form or *śakti*, or his consort—an assumption that is valid only from the Gupta period onward and only in some cases, as will be discussed. Indeed, very early on, this elephant-faced goddess appears as an independent, albeit minor, divinity in her own right. The earliest material attestations date from the first centuries BCE–CE and come from Rairh (Rajasthan) and Mathura. Particularly significant appear to be the connections with the goddess Jyeṣṭhā or Alakṣmī, who, in some texts, is described as elephant-faced and riding a donkey, but in extant figurative representations is usually depicted as a human-faced old woman. She traditionally represents misfortune and disease, but in the early stages of her cult she was presumably conceived as an ambivalent goddess, expressing the subtle balance between auspicious and inauspicious values.

The autonomous character of the elephant-faced goddess, detached from the orbit of Gaṇeśa, seems to emerge particularly in the Tantric context. After the 8th century, she sometimes features as one of the Eight Mothers and is often included in groups of *yoginī*s. An elephantine *yoginī* occurs, under various names, in both Vidyāpīṭha (7th–8th century) and Kaula (post-10th century) scriptures—in particular, in the *Siddhayogeśvarīmatatantra* (Törzsök 1999, forthcoming)*, Brahmayāmalatantra* (Hatley 2007, 2018) and *Ṣaṭsāhasrasaṃhitā*. The *yoginī*s*’* pantheons of these textual traditions find a degree of correspondence in different extant *yoginī* temples. Indeed, an elephant-faced female sculpture is enshrined in the 64 *yoginī* temples at Hirapur (second half of the 9th century) and Ranipur-Jharial (early 10th century) in Odisha and in the 81 *yoginī* temple at Bheraghat in Madhya Pradesh (last decades of the 10th century). Moreover, various sculptural collections of *yoginī*s include such a figure as well.

*[p. 80]* ^pg93

What meanings and implications lie behind the elephantine characterization of this *yoginī* ? What connection, if any, does this goddess have with Gaṇeśa? What is the significance of her vehicle, which is clearly a donkey at Hirapur while an elephant-headed male figure at Bheraghat? Is it possible to trace the genesis of this goddess in marginal, local, non-Tantric traditions? By addressing these questions, this contribution aims at shedding light on the significance and role of the elephant-faced female figure in the thought-world of mediaeval Śaiva Tantric traditions. Adducing illustrative rather than exhaustive evidence, it will investigate the goddess’s background and development by focusing on her elephant features.

Ultimately, the present chapter is an initial attempt to provide a deeper insight into the interrelation between local and Tantric traditions as reflected in the specific case of a deity that combines animal and feminine features. The idea is that mapping and piecing together some of the diachronic and diatopic variants of the elephant-faced goddess may contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the historical process whereby indigenous or local divine figures are incorporated into Tantric pantheons. Given the complexity of the issues at stake, I will put forward some tentative answers, while some questions will have to be left open.

### 4.2 Variations on a single elephantine theme

The first point to consider is whether the various elephant-faced figures in texts and images reflect not one, but different deities that, in diverse times and places, are depicted with an elephant face, but have no other connection between them. While this may be true, the very fact that an elephant-faced figure is almost invariably enshrined in circles of *yoginī*s, as attested by textual and material sources, does not seem meaningless and appears as a sufficient indication, I believe, to make the hypothesis of a single elephant-faced goddess, without a monolithic personality, worth investigating. As a matter of fact, the various representations (see Policardi forthcoming) appear to reflect a locally inflected religious vision of the elephant-faced goddess.

### 4.3 On Gajalakṣmī and on Jyeṣṭhā-Alakṣmī(-Vināyakī?)

As is well-known, Śrī or Lakṣmī is traditionally considered the goddess of wealth, abundance, and prosperity, and the *śakti* and consort of the solar god Viṣṇu. However, such an association with Viṣṇu seems to have developed from the Gupta era onward; as Jaiswal (1981: 99–100) remarks, ‘\[I\]n the early centuries preceding and following the Christian era Śrī-Lakṣmī appears to have been worshipped in her own right, quite unallied with any other sectarian cult and, as such, was worshipped by the people of all sects alike’. The classical iconography, which art historians have designated as Gajalakṣmī or Abhiṣekalakṣmī, shows the goddess seated on a lotus while two (sometimes four) elephants pour water over her from their upraised trunks or from vessels held in their trunks. This auspicious motif is found in early artistic records from both Buddhist and Hindu domains (Jaiswal 1981: 95; Singh 

*[p. 81]* ^pg94

1983: 24–76). As Wessels-Mevissen (2012: 15) observes, ‘\[T\]here is no specific mythological background, nor can the elephants be regarded as the goddess’ vehicles’. Nonetheless, her association with these animals is of utmost significance, expressing Śrī-Lakṣmī’s connection with water and thus with values such as good health, plenty, and fecundity. Purāṇic narrations of the myth of cosmic churning of the ocean often describe the goddess Lakṣmī emerging from the sea of milk bathed by elephants, but this clearly appears to be a previously existent typical motif assimilated by the Brahmanical lore. The antiquity of this iconographic formula, along with other elements, led some scholars (Jaiswal 1981: 95–96; Singh 1983) to suppose non-Aryan roots for this goddess.<sup>6</sup>

The pre-Aryan hypothesis has been sustained with more arguments for the goddess who is considered as Lakṣmī’s elder sister, Jyeṣṭhā, later known as Alakṣmī precisely to underline the fact that she represents the antonym, the absence of Lakṣmī. Traditionally, Jyeṣṭhā is described in the texts and represented in iconography with features that characterize her as an ‘old’ corpulent woman, with drooping lips, large pendulous breasts, and a sagging belly. She carries a banner depicting a crow or sometimes a crow is depicted alongside her.<sup>7</sup> Material records portraying Jyeṣṭhā, which can still often be found in the outskirts of villages in South India, attest a wide diffusion of her cult in the 7th and 8th centuries. In contemporary India her image is rarely a cult object, but, when recognized, it inspires fear (Leslie 1992: 114).

While she is thus commonly imagined and depicted with a human face, in various phases of her cult she was envisaged as elephant-faced. This is attested by  the earliest known textual occurrence of an elephant-faced goddess—namely, the *Baudhāyanagṛhyaśeṣasūtra* (BGŚS), which along with the *Baudhā* *yanagṛhyaparibhāṣasūtra* constitutes an important appendix (*pariśiṣṭa*) to the *Baudhāyanagṛhyasūtra* (BGS). In the corpus of Vedic ancillary literature, this is one of the longest texts concerning domestic ritual. Its dating is a complex matter, as the BGS and its appendices are a layered composition, which incorporated new material over time.<sup>8</sup> While in previous studies they have been dated between the 6th and the 3rd centuries BCE (Leslie 1992: 113; cf. Agrawala 1978: 5), Lubin (2016a: 321) proposes that various chapters were probably added well into the middle of the first millennium CE. This means that the *pariśiṣṭa*s have probably reached their current form in the dynamic historical context of the Gupta era. Such a milieu, as is well-- known, saw the codification of cultural models that would be decisive for the subsequent centuries.

The BGŚS describes *pūjā* rituals for various Hindu deities, including goddesses like Durgā, Śrī, and Jyeṣṭhā (III, 9), the latter followed by the *kalpa* for the god Vināyaka (III, 10). The text prescribes that, during the ritual of veneration, the goddess Jyeṣṭhā is to be praised with several epithets,<sup>9</sup> including *hastimukhā*, ‘elephant-faced’, which assumes particular interest. Also significant are the appellatives *kuṃbhī*, *nikuṃbhī*, and *prakuṃbhī* since these derive from the term *kumbha*, which, among its various meanings, also defines the forehead of an elephant. The goddess is also called by the name Śrī and defined by the two terms *vighnapārṣadā* and *vighnapārṣadī*, which refer to her 

*[p. 82]* ^pg95

divine action over *vighna*s or obstacles. The description of the ritual dedicated to the god Vināyaka immediately follows that of the goddess Jyeṣṭhā. Vināyaka is similarly defined as *hastimukha* and *vighnapārṣada* (III, 10, 6), and towards the end of the *adhyāya*, he is called Gaṇeśvara (III, 10, 9).

Clearly, the contiguity of their *kalpa*s in the text and the identity of some of their key epithets indicate a close connection between the two deities. This, however, does not necessarily imply that Jyeṣṭhā should be regarded as simply the female form of Vināyaka; indeed, here she is not (yet) called Vināyakī. We should take into consideration the possibility of an autonomous existence of a goddess whose aspect and functions were similar to those of Vināyaka-Gaṇeśa, perhaps precisely because of the significance attributed to the elephant. It is presumable that, after an independent genesis and formative phase, the two deities were in some way inevitably associated in a few subsequent cults, but, as we shall see, possibly in not all the cases.

The BGS and in particular its appendix BGŚS are among the most interesting texts for studying the new ritualistic elements that were assimilated into the domestic forms of worship in the period of transition from Brahmanism into Hinduism. According to Lubin (2016a, 322),

Many sections of the BGŚS \[…\] are evidently post-Vedic. These sections give us a dynamic view of efforts by Vaidikas to broaden the scope of ‘orthodox’ practice by assimilating the cult of images into the framework built around the fire cult and the Vedic liturgical texts.

This begs the question as to whether the elephant-faced goddess Jyeṣṭhā can be considered one of the non-Vedic figures whose cult is assimilated into the Hindu mainstream during the formation of the *Gṛhyapariśiṣṭa*s.

Various factors have been considered by scholars as indicative of Jyeṣṭhā’s provenience from marginal, non-Aryan religious strands. These include her unappealing physical features, which in Dhal’s view (1978: 160), ‘definitely indicate that the goddess has assimilated many non-Aryan aboriginal divinities with her’. According to some scholars (e.g. Lal 1980: 169) the descriptor *hastimukhā* in BGŚS can be interpreted as ‘long nose’, thus metaphorically referring to Jyeṣṭhā’s ugliness. However, a different compound, i.e. *gajānanā*, is used in a much later text, the *Suprabhedāgama* (see infra), and it seems unlikely that here too it should be read as metaphorically describing a pendulous human nose. Furthermore, her name, meaning ‘older’, is a relative term, which brings Bhattacarji (1970: 90) to ask ‘older than whom? The obvious answer is older than the Aryan Lakṣmī who is beneficent and of pleasing appearance’.

More compellingly, the antiquity of this divine figure might be suggested by some versions of the myth of churning of the ocean, in which Jyeṣṭhā-Alakṣmī resurfaces, making her appearance before Lakṣmī. In the *Padmapurāṇa* (6.260), when she emerges from the great sea immediately after the powerful poison that threatens to destroy the universe, the gods unambiguously associate her with inauspicious places, situations, and items. Interestingly, the text affirms that ‘wherever skulls, long hair, ashes, bones, chaff or charcoal (that is, the signs of 

*[p. 83]* ^pg96

unorthodox mendicant) are to be found, there will always be a place for her’ (Leslie 1992: 121). In the *Liṅgapurāṇa* (*Uttarabhāga*, Chapter 6), she is likewise associated with sinners and wicked people, with the abodes of those engaged in despising Viṣṇu or other Brahmanical deities, and with ‘that place where there are persons engaged in heretic activities, people excluded from the sacred rites laid down in the Vedas and Smṛtis’ (trans. Shastri 1951: 623). In brief, she represents everyone and everything who stands outside the fence of the Brahmanical religion. In these versions of the myth, the priority of Jyeṣṭhā’s birth from the ocean may indicate that she belongs to an earlier religious strand, and this might be corroborated by her association with the sphere of unorthodox customs.

Furthermore, as Lal (1980: 160) remarks, Jyeṣṭhā’s precedence over Lakṣmī may mythologically express the idea that the forces of chaos and disorder precede those of the order. As a matter of fact, the interdependence of the forces represented by Lakṣmī and Alakṣmī was recognized and expressed already in Vedic times. The *Atharvaveda* (7.115.1–4) envisions two aspects of Śrī-Lakṣmī, one benign and auspicious called Puṇyā and the other malignant and inauspicious known as Pāpī Lakṣmī. Some mantras of the *Atharvaveda* consider the latter as the deity who can drive off the evil actions of Nirṛti, the dire goddess of death and destruction (Dhal 1978: 130–131). In later developments, Pāpī Lakṣmī will be identified with Nirṛti herself. In the *Śrīsūkta*, a hymn in praise of Lakṣmī belonging to the appended hymns of the *Ṛgveda*, Lakṣmī is asked to ward off her elder sister. Alakṣmī (called also Jyeṣṭhā) is indeed said to bring hunger, thirst, poverty, and misery (Dhal 1978: 135–136; Kinsley 1997: 178).

Going another step further and venturing into a highly speculative domain, we can hypothesize that both of the goddesses known as Jyeṣṭhā-Alakṣmī and Lakṣmī were indigenous deities, later assimilated by the Brahmanical tradition. Here, Lakṣmī enjoyed a brilliant career, while a marginal place was reserved for her inauspicious sister. Both of these goddesses were originally related to the symbolism of the elephant but later developed opposite valences. As Leslie (1992, 113) observes, ‘While vaguely negative implications undermine the ideal presented by Śrī, equally vague but oddly positive undercurrents undermine the threat posed by Jyeṣṭhā’. In other words, these sister-goddesses of good and bad fortune can be seen as different sides of one personality.

Their interdependence finds an expression in a myth narrated in the *Nāgarakhaṇḍa* (Chapters 81 and 85) of the ‘late’ *Skandapurāṇa*, according to which Lakṣmī became elephant-faced because of a Brahman’s curse. But the goddess performs great penance so that Brahmā, after a year, pleased by her austerity, changes her back, proclaiming that she will be known by the name Mahālakṣmī and that ‘\[a\] man who devoutly worships you as elephant-faced, shall become a king on the earth and Lord of elephants’ (85.13, trans. Tagare 1960). This narration might be read as a transposition into the mythical language of the awareness of the deep connection between the elephant-faced goddess Jyeṣṭhā-Alakṣmī and the goddess Lakṣmī, typically portrayed with elephants sprinkling her with water. In fact, here they are somehow considered as two aspects of the same goddess called Mahālakṣmī.

*[p. 84]* ^pg97

In the attempt to disentangle the intricate (and under certain respect tenuous) connections between Jyeṣṭhā-Alakṣmī and Vināyakī, a key element is represented by the ass *vāhana*, even though it is attested in few textual and iconographic sources. In Indian culture, donkeys are endowed with a peculiar symbolism: they are considered impure, demonic, ominous, and disease carriers and are thus associated, in Vedic and post-Vedic literature, with inauspicious goddesses (Ferrari 2015: 67–81). Other than Jyeṣṭhā, ass-riding goddesses include Nirṛti, the aforementioned Vedic goddess of death; Kālarātrī, a Tantric goddess representing the terrific aspect of Kālī and Bhairavī; Cāmuṇḍā; and Śītalā, usually considered as the ambiguous ‘smallpox goddess’, but actually, in devotional contexts, venerated as the ‘Cold Lady’ who ‘has the power to vanquish heat (i.e. disease, disorder, injustice, etc.)’ (*ibid*.: 171).<sup>10</sup> According to Ferrari (*ibid*.: 80),

In her most distinguishable aspect, she \[Śītalā\] is the controller of disorder, a performance visually rendered by the action of riding the ass, a wild, unpredictable, thirsty animal associated with inauspiciousness, impurity, excess as well as dangerous and scarring ailments. But Śītalā is not just a controller. She is a destroyer and a remover of impurities \[…\]. When Tiwari says that goddesses like Dhūmāvatī, Jyeṣṭhā, Kālarātrī and Mahāmārī ‘have collectively contributed to the formulation of Śītalā \[…\]’ (Tiwari 1996: 455–6), he is eventually right.

It is possible that, much like the early mediaeval formulation of Śītalā, the elephant-faced goddess Jyeṣṭhā was associated with the ass precisely because of her function as controller over *vighna*s, or obstacles, a function she shared with the male deity Vināyaka. The ass, a wild animal connected with unpredictability, chaos, and excess, symbolized her power over adverse occurrences. In the early phase it was an ambivalent power, capable of both creating and destroying or removing the obstacles: at the risk of somewhat simplifying the complexity at stake, we could hypothesize that the elephant face expressed the positive function of averting evil, while the donkey *vāhana* evoked the adversities, which were, however, brought under control by the goddess.

Subsequently, presumably from the early Gupta era onwards, the benign role of dispelling obstacles and thus promoting success and prosperity became *par excel* *lence* the sphere of influence of Vināyaka/Gaṇeśa, while Jyeṣṭhā was merely considered the goddess causing misfortune and disease. Due to natural (religious) selection, so to speak, Jyeṣṭhā’s vehicle and symbol became the crow, while, presumably, the elephant-faced goddess was automatically interpreted as Gaṇeśa’s female form.

However, significantly, the elephant-faced goddess is associated with the donkey in a text that is much later than the BGŚS: the *Suprabhedāgama*, a scripture of the mature Śaiva Siddhānta. Probably composed (or altered) after the 12th century,<sup>11</sup> the Kriyāpāda of this text contains seven and a half verses devoted to the *sthāpana* of Jyeṣṭhā, i.e. to the installation of the image of the deity (Brunner 1967: 58). The goddess is here described as *gajānana* (45.2), ‘elephant-faced’, 

*[p. 85]* ^pg98

and as *khararūḍhā* (45.3), ‘riding a donkey’.<sup>12</sup> The fact that a female deity by the name of Jyeṣṭhā is depicted in a South Indian Āgama with *both* the elements at play in the present discussion—namely, the elephant face and the donkey *vāhana*—is particularly valuable.

Iconographic evidence of such a combination is found in a *yoginī* sculpture enshrined in a temple at Hirapur, near Bhuvaneshvar in Odisha. Dated to the second half of the 9th century, this shrine is the smallest of the extant *yoginī* temples and comprises exactly 64 *yoginī*s. The elephant-faced goddess numbered as 38 (Figure 4.1) is standing elegantly in a slight *tribhaṅga* pose. She is pot-bellied and has four arms, but all her hands are unfortunately broken. Richly bejewelled, she is depicted with a towering *jaṭāmukuṭa* over her elephantine head. Her feet rest directly on her *vāhana*, in a posture that is unparalleled in other Vināyakī representations. The mount has been identified as a mouse by Agrawala (1978: 26) and Sharma (1979: 34), but it is undoubtedly a donkey, as Dehejia (1986: 92) and other scholars have recognized: indeed, even if the characteristic erect mane is missing, the animal presents a tufted tail and what appear to be large hooves. Hirapur *yoginī* might be one of the strongest indications that a legacy of the original autonomous existence of (Jyeṣṭhā-)Vināyakī probably survived in some places and time periods and was then incorporated in the Tantric tradition of the *yoginī*s.

### 4.4 Possible phases of the religious-historical development of the elephant-faced goddess

To address the religious-historical development of the elephant-faced goddess it may be useful to identify four phases, although this categorization, to a great extent, implies a simplification. This tentative outline aids us in illuminating the intricacies of this relatively minor individual goddess and her continuity through religious shifts up to the 11th–12th centuries. In particular, it will make it easier to assess how her religious-historical trajectory appears to reflect interrelation dynamics between local and Tantric traditions.

#### 4.4.1 Jyeṣṭhā, elephant-faced, ass-riding goddess

In the first centuries BCE–CE a goddess, called Jyeṣṭhā, was represented with an elephant face and presumably a donkey *vāhana*. Her function was to preside over obstacles (*vighnapārṣadā* and *vighnapārṣadī* in BGŚS). The earliest known textual elaboration is found in the BGŚS, a Vedic ancillary text, while, on the figurative level, she is portrayed on a terracotta plaque recovered from Rairh, in Rajasthan (N° GM-MAG-RJ-841746, Sharma 1979: Fig. 1). Notwithstanding the small dimension (height 8 cm) and the very weathered condition, this relief is noteworthy since it is the earliest known figurative depiction of an elephant-faced deity (interestingly, the earliest attestation of such a deity portrays a female and not a male figure).

It is possible that the elephant-faced female figure had been an indigenous goddess before she made her first Sanskritic appearance in the BGŚS and thus in Vedic literature. While there is no descriptive source from that period that would allow us to put flesh on this speculation, according to both Einoo (1996) and Lubin (2016c), during the formation of the latest *Gṛhyasūtra*s and the *Gṛhyapariśiṣṭa*s, new modes of worship, and in particular new types of offerings, were incorporated in the domestic forms of *yajña*. These innovations came from ritual and cultural environments that were largely marginal within the Vedic milieu, and maybe even ethnically different. As Lubin (2016c: 144–145) puts it, \[L\]ike many others, I regard such offerings as an innovation in the Vedic religion, and indeed a borrowing from a substrate or neighboring culture with which the Vedic priesthood and its traditional clientele were in contact—a culture the identity of which will likely remain forever uncertain \[…\]. At no point is there direct acknowledgment that either the deity or the ceremony is non-Vedic, but an indirect acknowledgment of this may be perceived here and there.

*[p. 87]* ^pg100

#### 4.4.2 One elephant-faced mātṛ among numerous mātṛs

Subsequently, the elephant-faced goddess was interpreted as one of the numerous *mātṛ*s. As is well-known, in what has been defined popular (*laukika*) religious practice, so as to differentiate it from Brahmanical worship, the *mātṛ*s play an important role. The identities and cults of these Mother goddesses are, however, initially not affiliated with a single religious tradition, as suggested by the material records portraying them that have come to light in cultic complexes of various sectarian orientations (Joshi 1986: 15; Shin 2011). In the early phases of their cult, the multitudinous Mothers are deities intimately connected to birth and childhood, fecundity and life, sickness and death. Characterized by an ambivalent nature, they are helpful if propitiated, baneful if angered.

The elephant-faced goddess was generically considered as one of these *mātṛ*s, and, to some extent, shared the destiny of other divine figures such as Ṣaṣṭhī and Hārītī, who, included in the rank of Mothers, maintained an individual characterization and are identified by a name (White 2003: 40–43; Hatley 2021: 97). This phase is attested to by a Kuṣāṇa relief from Mathura (N° 33-2331, Sharma 1979: Fig. 2; Panikkar 1997: Pl. 6) portraying a row of five animal-and bird-headed *mātṛ*s, including an elephant-faced figure. On a textual level, the process is reflected by some Purāṇic passages, where a goddess by the name of Vināyakī or an elephant-faced Mother is included in lists of warrior goddesses, such as in passages from the *Matsyapurāṇa* (179.18; cf. Agrawala 1970: 23),<sup>13</sup> *Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa* (I.226.16), and the early *Skandapurāṇa* (64.20–26).<sup>14</sup>

#### 4.4.3 Sometimes included in the group of the Eight Mothers

As perhaps Kuṣāṇa-age materials already foreshadow, and abundant evidence from the Gupta period onward widely attests to, the *mātṛ*s subsequently shift from their early association with Skanda and Kubera towards a more decidedly Śaiva affiliation, and by the 5th century CE, a specific heptad of Mothers emerges. These seven goddesses (*saptamātṛ*s) are conceived and represented as female counterparts of the major Brahmanical male gods, assuming the name, emblems, and mount of the corresponding god. The group usually comprises Brāhmī, Māheśvarī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Vārāhī (or Yāmī), and Aindrī, each name having several variants; the seventh figure, the ‘leader of the Mothers’ (*mātṛnāyikā*), is Cāmuṇḍā, the skeletal and fierce goddess, who has no male deity counterpart.<sup>15</sup> In this form, the *mātṛ*s become the focus of a widespread temple cult closely associated with Śiva. In several cases, their number is brought to eight, with the most typical addition being the goddess Mahālakṣmī.<sup>16</sup>

Clearly, the formation of this group of Seven or Eight Mothers reflects a carefully constructed Brahmanical theology whereby the non-elite cult of innumerable, anonymous Mother goddesses is remodelled and redirected (Shin 2011; Hatley 2021: 96–98). Vināyakī is sometimes associated with this group of deities, such as in a comment by Utpala on a passage of the *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* by Varāhamihira (58.56), 

*[p. 88]* ^pg101

maybe in the *Devīpurāṇa* (87.33, Śarmā 1976 edition) and in the *Maheśvarakhaṇḍa* of the late *Skandapurāṇa* (I.62.60).

Among the material records, I should like to mention a panel recovered at Rikhiyan, in Uttar Pradesh (10th century; Figure 4.2). In this site once existed a *yoginī* temple, which enshrined long rectangular slabs representing *yoginī*s. In this panel, the elephant-faced goddess is flanked on her right by a lion-faced figure, maybe Nārasiṃhī, and on her left by a boar-faced goddess, presumably Vārāhī. Given her position on the slab, here Vināyakī seems to be considered as one of the Seven or Eight Mothers, which were in turn comprised in the group of maybe 64 *yoginī*s of the temple.

In this light, a mural found in Buddhist Central Asia, probably dating from the 8th century, assumes particular interest. At Dandan Oiliq, in the Khotan Oasis (Xinjiang), some Buddhist temples have recently been discovered (Lo Muzio 2017). In the shrine named CD4, a large fragment of a mural painting depicts the god Skanda along with other deities, mainly female. Among the latter, there is a possibly elephant-faced goddess accompanied by an animal which is clearly not a mouse and resembles the *vāhana* of the Hirapur *yoginī*: it could be a donkey (Lo Muzio 2017: 75–76; Figure 4.3). This painting would not only attest to the affiliation of the goddess to the group of the Seven Mothers far away from the heart of Indian culture, but it would also be another example of the combination of the auspicious symbolism of the elephant with the inauspicious one of the donkey in a single female iconography.

During the course of the 8th century, while the *mātṛ*s were gradually receding into the background as an independent group of deities and losing importance in cultic contexts, the cult of *yoginī*s started to assume definite contours,

Several Tantric traditions consider the Seven or Eight Mothers as matriarchs of *yoginī* families (*kula*, *gotra*); this group of deities is embedded in most *yoginī* temples. Various Purāṇic texts consider the 64 *yoginī*s as emanating from the *aṣṭamātṛ*s (see, e.g., *Agnipurāṇa* Chapter 146) and lists of *yoginī* names include the *mātṛ*s.<sup>17</sup> As a matter of fact, among the conceptual and figurative models that historically contribute to the formation of *yoginī* figures, the *mātṛ*s can be considered as the most immediate precursors, merging into this class of Tantric goddesses around the 7th century.

#### 4.4.4 Included in groups of yoginīs

In the subsequent phase of her religious-historical development, the elephant-- faced goddess was almost invariably allotted a place in circles of *yoginī*s. This is attested by several sculptures. Among the most significant ones, there is the Hirapur *yoginī* n° 38 and the Rikhiyan *yoginī*, both mentioned earlier in the chapter.

*[p. 90]* ^pg103

Another elephant-faced *yoginī* is housed in one of the perimetral cells of the largest and most imposing *yoginī* temple, situated on the top of a hill at Bheraghat, near Jabalpur, in Madhya Pradesh (last decades of the 10th century). The goddess, richly carved in elaborate details, is surrounded by various attendant figures, among which one of the most interesting is an elephant-headed male figure, sitting below the *yoginī* ’s throne with his left hand supporting her left folded leg (Figure 4.4). Agrawala (1978, 28) remarks that ‘\[t\]his Gaṇapati portrayal in a subordinate role below the goddess is rather curious and a unique feature of the present sculpture’. But it is far from being certain that this male attendant is to be read as Gaṇeśa. The inscribed label on the pedestal identifies the *yoginī* as Śrī Aiṅgiṇī, a name not found elsewhere in reference to the elephant-faced goddess (Agrawala 1978: 29).

Interestingly, an elephant-faced *yoginī* is found also on Mount Harsha in northeast Rajasthan, within a temple complex dedicated to Śiva, the tutelary deity (*kuladeva*) of the Śākambharī Cāhamānas rulers (9th–10th centuries; Figure 4.5). Only the torso of the sculpture is partially preserved and since it is now embedded in a wall, it is difficult to imagine whether the goddess was standing or sitting (Cecil 2020: 131–132; see also Lanius 1971). She presents two prominent tusks and carries an empty bowl in her hand, probably a wine cup, which make it difficult to interpret the figure as the female form of Gaṇeśa. On the other hand, among the *yoginī* images recovered in the area of Hinglajgadh in Madhya Pradesh (middle of the 10th century), an elephant-faced female figure is accompanied by an animal that is likely a bandicoot, of particularly conspicuous size (Figure 4.6). As is known, the mouse or bandicoot *vāhana* identifies the god Gaṇeśa at least from the 9th century onward.

Remarkably, each temple of *yoginī*s enshrines a group of figures whose composition differs from the others: the types of figures—graceful, terrifying, and animal-faced—, and their positioning along the circumference are quite different in each temple. On the basis of this and a few other elements, Dehejia (1986: 5, 93–94) argues that every *yoginī* temple, albeit connected to a shared common base of beliefs, reflects a different, localized, and independent tradition.

The elephant-faced goddess might be an eloquent tessera in this motley mosaic. If we can delineate the background of similar figures, we might be able to understand the complex, layered belief system underlying Tantric pantheons. Among the different typologies of *yoginī*s, the therianthropic one emerges somewhat as the most powerful and the most related to a magic dimension: the older tradition of representing animal-headed deities lends itself to a reutilization in a new, often Tantric, context, where its original meaning is reinterpreted. In the Tantric phenomenon, local, minor beings or semi-deities are elevated to potent deities, capable of transforming the adepts (Policardi 2020).

The donkey *vāhana* of the Hirapur *yoginī* might not simply stem from the genuine inventiveness of the artists but possibly reflects the fact that both those who conceived the sculpted image and possibly the sculptors themselves were aware of a connection with Jyeṣṭhā, or better with the ambivalent goddess who presided over inauspiciousness, ill luck, ruin or destruction whatever her name

The elephantine goddess is also a recurrent presence in Tantric texts related to *yoginī*s. To mention just a few significant examples, one of the earliest Tantras dealing with *yoginī*s, the *Siddhayogeśvarīmata*, most likely composed around the 7th century, offers a list of 24 *yoginī* names in Chapter 20 (58–60), in the context of the description of an elaborated Khecarīcakra. Among these, two names hint at elephant features—namely, Gajānanā and Gajakarṇī (57b, 58b). *Yoginī*s connoted by animal faces feature again in Chapter 25:<sup>18</sup> ‘They have extraordinary faces such as bear, tiger, elephant, demon, horse, boar and other faces. Seeing them, one should not rejoice, nor should be angry’. Interestingly, the *Siddhayogeśvarīmata* puts on stage groups of *yoginī*s featuring animal-- human figures depicted as decidedly theriocephalic, just like the *yoginī*s in sculpture.

Along the same lines, the more or less coeval *Brahmayāmala* includes in its core pantheon six *yoginī*s who are defined by interestingly expressive names: Kroṣṭukī (‘Jackal Woman’), Vijayā (‘Victoria’), Gajakarṇā (‘Elephant-Ears’), Mahāmukhī (‘Big-Mouth’), Cakravegā (‘Wheel-Speed’), and Mahānāsā 

*[p. 93]* ^pg106

(‘Big-Snout’). Three kinds of figures are suggested: Kroṣṭukī and Gajakarṇā clearly indicate animal appearances, Vijayā and Cakravegā evoke martial and auspicious features, while Mahāmukhī and Mahānāsā point to physical disproportion (Hatley 2014: 207). The presence of a female figure characterized by elephantine traits in the central pantheon of this scripture appears to be of some relevance.

A probably elephant-faced *yoginī* features also in a later scripture belonging to the western Kaula tradition of the Paścimāmnāya, the *Ṣaṭsāhasrasaṃhitā* (ca. 12th century). The unpublished 15th chapter of this text contains a detailed description of the iconography and worship (*pūjā*) of 64 *yoginī*s.<sup>19</sup> The goddess called Vāmanī has a mouse as her mount (*mahākhuyānā*) and a *laḍḍuka* among her attributes (15.153),<sup>20</sup> which let us presume that she is considered the female form of Gaṇeśa and thus that she has an elephant face.

In the same chapter, after having named and described the 64 *yoginī*s (15.100– 165), Bhairava, the narrator, states that if one visualizes these deities or draws them on tissue (*athavā tu paṭe likhya*, 173c) all one’s desires will be fulfilled and 

*[p. 94]* ^pg107

all the *siddhi*s acquired. He adds that this *pūjā* will allow a king to obtain a kingdom (*rājyabhraṣṭo ālabhed rājyaṃ*, 171ab) or the desired victory ( *jayārthī jayam āpnoti*, 171c). After the victory over the enemy, a king should erect a shrine on the battleground dedicated to Śikhāsvacchandabhairava and the 64 *yoginī*s (15.166–178).

Significantly, thus, this passage prescribes not only a visualization of the 64 *yoginī*s but also a figurative representation of them (although there is no mention of any three-dimensional images) and connects the *yoginī* cult with the royal power. Especially in the light of these two circumstances—the hint at concrete images of *yoginī*s and the involvement of the royals—it seems not coincidental that the *Ṣaṭsāhasrasaṃhitā* is coeval with the construction of the major *yoginī* temples.

While the schema of four development phases outlined earlier is useful for framing the intricacy of the issue, it should be seen as a highly simplified illustration of what are gradual and not necessarily quite so linear interplays. The various phases do not present clearly fixed boundaries between each other, and although there are some mutual distinctions, the four zones constitute a continuum in which there are nuances and overlaps along the way.

Clearly, the elephant-faced goddess shared some personality traits with Vināyaka, notably her physical appearance and her function as controller of obstacles, intended as the capacity of both creating and removing the adversities. However, it is possible that originally she was not considered as his female form. With Gaṇeśa’s rise to prominence and subsequent immense popularity, and the parallel decline in the worship of Jyeṣṭhā around the 10th century, the god came to play a not marginal role in Vināyakī’s religious-- historical development. It was as late as the 5th century that Gaṇeśa acquired a prominent position in the Hindu religious landscape, and from that moment onward, in some times and in some places, the elephant-faced goddess was to some extent automatically interpreted—and thus portrayed—as the god’s female form. The unambiguous hint of such an association with Gaṇeśa is the mouse or bandicoot *vāhana*. Such a mount is attested in the passage from the *Ṣaṭsāhasrasaṃhitā* and in the *yoginī* sculpture from Hinglajgadh. However, in several cases, there do not seem to be any parallels with Gaṇeśa’s features—namely, in the terracotta from Rairh, in the Mathura relief, or in the *yoginī* sculptures at Hirapur (Figure 4.1), Bheraghat (Figure 4.4), and Harsha (Figure 4.5).

### 4.5 (Provisional) conclusions

This chapter has attempted to reconstruct the different religious-historical stages in the formation of the figure of an elephant-faced goddess usually called Vināyakī. It has suggested that her genesis may have taken place in a ritual and cultural context that was largely marginal within the Vedic tradition. In other words, it is possible that this goddess, in the early period known as Jyeṣṭhā-- Alakṣmī, has non-elite roots.

*[p. 95]* ^pg108

Written and visual representations are too scarce to allow a reconstruction in terms of precise chronological and geographical coordinates of the process which caused a female, elephant-faced personage to recede into the background, while the analogous male deity rose to prominence. Nonetheless, it is possible to hypothesize that the elephant-faced goddess was originally an independent figure that underwent several shifts, from being one of the numerous Mothers of the Kuṣāṇa period to being associated with the Eight Mothers in some post-Gupta occurrences, eventually coming to be considered as the female form of Gaṇeśa with the rise in popularity of the latter god. Hence, when around the 7th century the cult of *yoginī*s was acquiring relevance, she was merged into this class of Tantric goddesses. It is in the Tantric domain that, apparently, her originally ambivalent nature re-emerges, as expressed by the combination of the auspicious symbolism of the elephant face and of the inauspicious figure of the donkey mount in the *yoginī* sculpture at the Hirapur temple. Such a bipolarity, i.e. the interfusion of auspicious and inauspicious values, is one of the distinctive traits of the Tantric *yoginī* figures.

Clearly, we cannot exclude the possibility that other representations of an elephantine goddess accompanied by a donkey *vāhana* may somehow lie in uncharted temples or museums or in unsuspected texts, awaiting discovery and identification. The four phases of development outlined, along with every line of inquiry presented, are a fitting topic for deeper analysis, which I hope to discuss in future contributions.
