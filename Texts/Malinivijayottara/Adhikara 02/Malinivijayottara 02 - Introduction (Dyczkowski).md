## Chapter Two of the Mālinīvijayottara Tantra: The Seven Perceivers, the Penetrations and the Six Paths

The contents of chapter two of the Mālinīvijayottara are as follows:

- Concerning Pervasion 1
- The Division of the Principles into Fifteen Divisions 2-12
- The Signs of Rudra's Power 13-16
- The Penetrations of Rudra's Power 17-25ab
- The Penetrations and the Five States of Consciousness of the Seven Perceivers 25cd-48
- The Four Eggs and the Six Paths 49-60

This chapter is dedicated to two basic, closely related topics. One concerns the expanse of reality and its contents. This is viewed both subjectively and objectively. The later is summed up and contained in four Eggs that cover the range of the thirty-five *Tattvas* up to śiva. These are: 1) Pṛthvyaṇḍa – the Egg of Earth that contains just that one *Tattva.* 2) Prakṛtyaṇḍa – the Egg of Nature that extends (the texts would say 'pervades') from Watter *Tattva* to Prakṛti. 3) *Māyāṇḍa* – the Egg of Māyā that covers the Tattvas from Puruṣa to Māyā and 4) śaktyaṇḍa – the Egg of śakti that contains the four pure principles above Māyā from śuddha Vidyā to śakti. Abhinava discusses this subject in the *Paramāthasāra* and in the *Tantrāloka.*[^1] In a more detailed perspective the objective order is comprised in the Six Paths of 1) the Worlds 2) the *Tattvas* 3) the Forces (*kalā*), 4) Mantra 5) Parts of Mantra and 6) Letters. These six Paths mark the stages the soul traverses in the course of initiation and so Abhinavagupta deals with them in great detail in chapters eight, nine and eleven of the *Tantrāloka.* The subjective order of reality is comprised in a hierarchy of seven perceivers that extends along the Path of the *Tattvas* and so, by extension, the other Paths also.

Before going into that in some detail, we should note that this chapter of the *Mālinīvijaya* combines this summary of the essentials of its ontology, subjective and objective, with the fundamental essentials of its soteriology. The two are combined here, no doubt, to underscore that they are essentially interrelated. All that exists, however binding, serves as the way to its liberating transcendence. The Tantra is concerned fundamentally with that transformation. Moreover, despite the rich variety of practices, their levels and symbolic characterizations, they all rest upon a single factor, namely, grace, that is, a descent of Rudra's power. Thus, in this chapter Lord supplies a concise description of the signs that Rudra's power of grace has fallen on his devotee in the course of initiation and the preceding experiences that lead up to it. The first and foremost of these is an overwhelming desire that consumes the seeker to find a Guru (see verses 2/13- 16). Vastly important for Abhinavagupta's exegesis are the definitions that follow of the three basic forms that the penetrations (*samāveśa*) of Rudra's power assumes (2/17-25ab). Possibly following the lead of his *guru* śambhunātha, Abhinavagupta, redefines them as corresponding to three categories of practice (*upāya*), the well-known triad of *śāmbhava, śākta* and *Āṇava.* Liberating knowledge is the foundation, purpose and ultimate goal of all practice and grace. Accordingly, Abhinavagupta dedicates the first chapter of the *Tantrāloka* to a general introduction to practice framed around these three categories and calls 'the varieties of knowledge' (*vijñānabhid*). We may understand from this that the *Mālinīvijayottara* is teaching the same as the triadic manner in which the expanse of the realities taught in the Tantras are experienced directly and lead to ultimate realisation by the discovery of the soul's essential identity with śiva.

*[p. 22]*

Let us turn now to a brief, detailed examination of the hierarchy of perceivers. We may begin by citing four verses in which Abhinava provides an overview of Śiva's Śakti as all the major categories of existing things and their dynamism, ranging from the most gross and immanent to transcendental Śiva. Accordingly, he lists the various categories (*bheda*) of realities that comprise the universe which the teachings expound as forms of śakti. In this way he focuses on the reality comprised in the Trika's expanse of manifest and transcendent reality. In doing this, it appears that he is guided here by the contents of chapter two of the *Mālinīvijayottara* that lists essentially the same things. They are: 1) the Six-fold Path 2) the five functions (*pañcakṛtya*), 3) the four states of consciousness and 4) the hierarchy of seven perceivers. Guided by the brilliant insight of his profound understanding of the teachings, he identifies them all with powers of śakti, thus making explicit the depth of this higher suggested sense. Thus, Abhinavagupta writes: *bahuśaktitvam asyoktam śivasya yad ato mahān/ kalātattvapurārṇāṇupadādibhedavistaraḥ// sṛṣṭisthititirodhānasamhārānugrahādi ca/ turyam ity api devasya bahuśaktitvajṛmbhitam// jāgratsvapnasuṣuptāny atadatītāni yāny api/ tāny apy amuṣya nāthasya svātantryalaharībharaḥ// mahāmantreśamantreśamantrāḥ śivapurogamāḥ/ akalau sakalaś ceti śivasyaiva vibhūtayaḥ//*

*[p. 23]*

The many powers Śiva is said to possess are the great extension of differentiation (*bheda*) (of the Paths of manifestation consisting of) the cosmic forces (*kalā*), metaphysical principles (*tattva*), and world-orders (*pura*)[^2] (along with their respective denotators, namely the Paths of the) letters, Mantras, parts of Mantras (*pada*) and the rest. The unfolding of the Lord's manifold power (also consists of the processes of) creation, persistence, obscuration, withdrawal and grace etc. as well as the Fourth (state).

Moreover, (the states of) waking, dreaming and deep sleep and others beyond them[^3] are all the multitude of the waves of the Lord's (creative) freedom (*svātantrya*). So too (the perceivers) headed by Śiva (called) the Great Lords of Mantra, the Lords of Mantra and Mantras, (along with) the two that are Deconditioned (*ākala*) and the Conditioned (*sakala*) are (all) the glorious powers (*vibhūti*) of Śiva Himself.'[^4]

Here Abhinava has encompassed all the realities within the experience of the seven perceivers. The nature and status of the seven perceivers is discussed in chapter nine of the *Tantrāloka,*[^5] whereas chapter ten is entirely dedicated to the teachings of chapter two of the *Mālinīvijayottara* concerning the seven perceivers and so begins (TĀ 10/3-5) with a summary of MV 2/1-9. Chapter ten is the second half of the exposition of the Path of the Principles (*tattvādhvan*). Accordingly, Abhinava ends this chapter saying: 'thus we have explained the Path of the Principles at length' (TĀ 10/310ab). The first part – chapter nine – deals with the nature of the thirty-six principles, chapter ten with the series of seven perceivers who experience them and related matters. An important subject for Abhinava, he expounds it again lucidly in chapter nine of the *Tantrasāra*. It appears that in the Trika Tantras, this subject is taught only in the *Mālinīvijayottara* (2/1-7ab). Certainly, it is the only surviving Trika text that teaches this (Vasudeva (2004): 196 note 26). Neither Abhinava nor Jayaratha quote any other Trika scripture that refers explicitly to this teaching. However, it is commonly found in various degrees of development in the early Siddhānta Āgamas and fully formed in the *Mālinīvijayottara,* It is to this that we now turn.

There are seven types of perceivers (*pramātṛ*) arranged in a hierarchy along the series of thirty-six principles. In terms of the phenomenology of the Pratyabhijñā, they are understood to view their corresponding section of the series of principles as their objects. The first three are conditioned by the 'Force (of limited agency)' (*kalā*). This is the energy of Māyā which obscures consciousness in such a way that it reduces it to the form of perceiving subjects related to objectivity experienced as separate from themselves. The first and lowest of these categories of perceivers is subject to all the aspects of the Force (of limited agency) – *kalā* – and so is called *Sakala* – 'With Kalā' – which I translate for convenience as Conditioned. These perceivers experience the principles from Earth up to the Individual soul and are subject to all the three forms of Impurity (*mala*), namely, Innate (*āṇava*), Māyā and Karma. The following two perceivers above that are still within the realms of Māyā. The first, the *Pralayākala* – Dissolution Deconditioned, resides in the principles from the Individual Soul to Māyā, that is, the obscuring coverings (*kañcukas*) of the Individual Soul. The one above it is called *Vijñānākala* – Consciousness Deconditioned. This type of perceiver resides in the intermediate state between Māyā and the Pure Principles in which the remaining four levels and types of perceivers reside.

*[p. 24]*

The Dissolution Deconditioned (*Pralayākala*) perceivers are divested of the active energies of Karma but continue to possess the latent ones. They sleep for a period of cosmic destruction (*pralaya*) and so are called accordingly. Their state of deep sleep (*suṣupti*) is of two kinds, namely, with and without some sense of perception of dream objects and the self-awareness that accompanies it. The first is called '*savedyasuṣupti*' – literally 'deep sleep associated with objectivity'. The second is called '*apavedyasuṣupti*' – literally 'deep sleep free of objectivity' (see TĀ 9/138 ff. and 10/132cd ff.). Although still within the phenomenal domain characterized by the distinction between subject and object, no distinct phenomena appear (*apavedya*), although the latent traces of the experience of objectivity of the previous Conditioned (*sakala*) state of subjectivity persist. The Dissolution Deconditioned perceiver 'associated with objectivity' experiences just the bare cognitive awareness characterized by the recollection, when emerging out of deep sleep, that “I slept comfortably”. The other type which experiences deep sleep free of objectivity recollects that “I knew nothing at all”. In the Consciousness Deconditioned (*Vijñānākala*) perceiver, both impurities (*mala*) of Karma and Māyā have come to an end as well as their latent impressions. The impurity pertaining to the individual soul (*āṇavamala*) is still functional but is tending to cease (*vidvamsabhāva*). Even so the presence of this impurity is evident in the state of the Consciousness Deconditioned perceiver who under its influence may be conscious but has no freedom, or may be free but is unconscious of being so. The yogi at this level of subjectivity enters a state of deep contemplative absorption (*samādhi*) but, as if paralysed, he does not have the power to maintain it. When he emerges out of it, he is again free, but he cannot sustain higher states of consciousness.

*[p. 25]*

When the principle of Māyā is destroyed, the Dissolution Deconditioned perceiver associated with objectivity (*savedyapralayākala*) enters into the Consciousness Deconditioned state from which it does not, anyway, differ much. Indeed, it appears that originally these categories of perceivers were considered to be aspects of the Consciousness Deconditioned subjects. Both perceivers are destined to awake at the end of the period of cosmic destruction or its yogic equivalent. The Dissolution Deconditioned who were in objectless deep sleep, may return into the world of transmigration of Māyā, descending into it, according to the latent traces of their past Karma, or rise to a higher level. Thus, for example, if they were devout Vaiṣṇavas they come to reside in their corresponding principle, that is, Unmanifest Nature. Conversely, if they were previously engaged in the practice of Śaiva meditation and other Śaiva spiritual disciplines, they may rise to the higher level of Mantra perceivers beyond Māyā. Thus, according to whether they descend or ascend, they are of two kinds. The Consciousness Deconditioned subject may similarly rise or fall when he awakes, but he rises further and does not fall as far down as the Dissolution Deconditioned perceiver. Thus, he may awake at the higher level beyond Māyā to become a Mantra, Mantreśa (Lord of Mantra) or Mahāmantreśa (Great Lord of Mantra). He may also return to the world of transmigratory existence, but he does so as either as a yogi or as a man of knowledge (*vijñānin*).[^6]

Equivalent states may be achieved by yogic practice. Thus, the Dissolution Deconditioned state is achieved by the yogi when the activity of mind, along with that of the senses and even the breathing, stop in deep contemplative absorption (*samādhi*) 'with the eyes closed' (*nimeṣa*). If the adept (*sādhaka*) is sufficiently pure (that is, free of latent traces of past Karma), he is able to quietly set aside that state, which is an impediment to further development, and enter that of the Consciousness Deconditioned perceiver. Rising beyond that level of subjectivity the next, which is that of the Mantra perceiver, is above Māyā within the Pure Knowledge, the first of the Pure Principles, in which objectivity is experienced as one in various modalities with the perceiver. There fundamental Impurity (*āṇavamala*) survives as a latent trace and that too progressively comes to an end in the higher perceivers, the Mantreśas and Mahāmantreśas, who reside in the higher principles of Īśvara and Sadāśiva, respectively. It finally disappears totally in Śiva.[^7]

*[p. 26]*

Vasudeva (2004: 141ff.) observes that the basic idea of progressing to the state of release by traversing progressively higher orders of reality, that is, 'the conquest of the principles or/planes of existence' (*tattvajaya/ bhūmikājaya*) is commonly taught in Śaiva scriptures. However, in the *Mālinīvijayottara* it is taught in great detail. Moreover, the crucial innovation is the idea that it is possible to attain to the highest state of Śiva not only by traversing along the *tattvādhvan* [the Path of the Principles] to ever higher realities, but that one may stop at any given reality and then ascend obliquely through fifteen levels of subjective experients, telescoping them inwards as one progresses towards the experiential mode of the higher perceiver. Any *tattva* [reality principle] (Sakala souls only have access to the realities from earth up to matter [Prakṛti]) can provide the basis for this practice. When meditative immersion into the pure, abstract object-ness of the *tattva* (e.g., its *svarūpa* or 'own-form') is achieved, the Yogin proceeds not to the next *tattva* of the ontological hierarchy (as would the Saiddhāntika Yogin), but to the awareness of himself being immersed in the object. His own Sakala self thereby becomes the next 'ownform' *svarūpa* that he must contemplate as though it were an external thing. As each higher perspective is gained, the lower apperceptive states are folded in and become inactive and objectivized: the divisions, or the refraction (*bheda*) of the *tattva,* thus collapse in pairs (i.e., one experient and his cognitive power). In such practice, the Yogin in a sense never really transcends the reality upon which the ascent is based… The Yogin thus makes an epistemological value judgement instead of an ontological value judgement, and because of this, he ascends not to the next reality (*tattva*), but to the next experiential level [i.e., perceiver] (*pramātṛbheda*) tied to the same reality.'

The importance of these perceivers was acknowledged from the very beginning of the exposition of post-scriptural non-dualist Śaivism. Somānanda refers to Vidyā, Vidyeśa, Mantra and Mantreśvara perceivers (ŚDṛ 1/31) as well as those who are paśu, Śānta, Kevaleśa, Kevalaśambhu, Pralayakevalin and Vijñānakevalin (ibid. 1/41c-43). They are treated systematically by Utpaladeva in the third section of his *Īśvarapratyabhijñā* concerning the Śaiva revelation (*āgamādhikara*)[^8] just after he has briefly presented the non-dual version of the Tattvas. There he skilfully reworks in non-dualist terms the basically Siddhānta teachings concerning the triple Impurity,[^9] gradation of the principles, their corresponding hierarchy of seven perceivers and related topics. The continuing presence of these teachings in the work of Kṣemarāja[^10] is a further testimony of their importance for Trika Śaivites.

*[p. 27]*

Chapter ten of the *Tantrāloka* is divided into clearly defined sections relating to this matter. These include a discussion about the nature of perceptibility crafted around the refutation of the Mīmāmsa view that perceptibility is a quality of perceivable objects, independent of the perceiver. The perceivers are graded according to their state of consciousness as waking or otherwise. Thus, these states are discussed in this context in the primary sources just as they are by Utpaladeva in brief (in ĪP 3/2/13-17). Here, following the *Mālinīvijayottara,* which is inspired by Kaula teachings, they are treated in more detail by including their subdivisions. The following passage up to TĀ 10/186ab is a detailed exposition and explanation of it.

The teachings concerning these perceivers, generally, commonly presented as an integral part of the Tattva system, is taught, in various forms and degrees of sophistication, in most of the early Siddhānta, especially the *Mṛgendra* and the *Svāyambhuvāgama.* They are also found in independent works and commentaries such as the *Tattvaprakāśa* attributed to Rāja Bhoja, *Tattvasamgrahaḥ*, *Tattvatrayanirṇaya* and *Bhogakārikā* all by *Sadyojyotiḥ*, and the *Ratnatrayaparīkṣā* by *Śrīkaṇṭhasūri.* Conversely, with the important exception of the *Mālinīvijayottara,* we do not find it in the Bhairava Tantras. Accordingly, Jayaratha states that this topic which is 'appropriate for the philosophy of the Siddhānta and the like (that are dualist).'[^11]

Why then does Abhinava begin his exposition declaring that it is a unique secret of the *Trikaśāstra*?[^12] We cannot say it was just a manner of speaking, for he reaffirms this at the beginning of chapter nine of the *Tantrasāra*.[^13] Perhaps this is because in many of the Siddhāntas there are only six perceivers, not seven, because Pralayākala is not mentioned. Or does he mean that it is THE one secret of the Trikaśāstra? In the next line he tells us that the *Mālinīvijayottara* is called the venerable Trikaśāstra. So perhaps he means that it is the one, in the sense of most excellent, disclosure of a most fundamental teaching found in the *Mālinīvijayottara.* Or that this is the sole place in all the Trika scripture that it is taught as the 'secret' of what is taught concerning this in the Siddhānta. Certainly, it is a very important topic for Abhinava, who refers to it at length in his previous Trika works, the *Mālinīvijayavavārtika* and the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa.* Indeed, it is so integral to his Trika Śaivism, that, as we have seen, he already has had occasion to expound it succinctly in his introduction to the *Tantrāloka* (see above, TĀ 1/77-80 (78-81)) and then again in 4/29- 32ab. Jayaratha similarly refers to it on several occasions, for example in TĀv ad 1/187cd-188ab and ad 1/191. The general references to levels of subjectivity, in terms of the forms of objectivity associated with it (whether corporeal or mental etc.) in general, are considerably more. Indeed, it is a fundamental feature of the Trika understanding of the nature of the Self and consciousness.
