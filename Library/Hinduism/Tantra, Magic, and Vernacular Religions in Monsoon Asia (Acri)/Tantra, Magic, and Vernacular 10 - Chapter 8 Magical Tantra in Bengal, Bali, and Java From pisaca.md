## Chapter 8: Magical Tantra in Bengal, Bali, and Java: From piśāca tāntrika s to balian s and dukun s

*June McDaniel*

### 8.1 Introduction

In this chapter, I shall examine how magical Tantra is understood in three regions—namely, West Bengal in India and Bali and Java in Indonesia. The research is based on both text and fieldwork and includes data from interviews with practitioners discussing their rituals and concerns. It examines the processes of magical transformation, the understanding of the supernatural, and the roles of deity, gender, and ritual empowerment. It shows how magical practices can be incorporated into both monotheistic and polytheistic belief systems. In these examples, we see that magical concepts can cross Hindu and Muslim lines more easily than many other forms of ritual. This is because they are less public and escape the critical view of theologians from both traditions. While most studies of sorcery and magic have focused on the Western magical tradition, here we examine some Asian models.

We should note that the term ‘magic’ is disputed, as its meaning has changed over times and cultures. While use of the term is first found in 5th century BCE Greece, derived from the Persian *magus* (member of the priestly caste), its meaning changed as it moved through Roman, Jewish, and Christian literature. Among modern scholars of magic and sorcery, a major debate is whether magic refers to social deviance and is a polemical term to stigmatize and exclude non-elite others or whether it can be used as a positive, self-referential term. The ‘thesis of deviance’ contrasts us vs. them, inside vs. outside, true vs. false. From this perspective, magic is opposed to religious expertise and effectiveness, equated with falsity, *superstitio*, and illegitimate activity. An example of this perspective might be that of St Augustine, for whom magic was *pactum daemonum*, a pact with demons against the one true God.

However, there is also a ‘discourse of inclusion’, in which magic becomes a ritual specialization. Here we see practitioners who refer to themselves and their colleagues as magicians and justify such practices as divination, supernatural protection, healing, gaining wealth and luck, becoming invisible, and evoking love. Magic can be based on impersonal, pantheistic natural forces (as we see in the early modern *magia naturalis* approach), or it can be a practice of apotheosis, intended to transform the person into a god (as in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn). Magic here is wisdom and insight into the universe.

*[p. 155]* ^pg168

Modern theorists of magic have often been hostile towards the term ‘magic’, viewing it as prejudicial, ethnocentric, semantically distorting, and arbitrary. It is a ‘refuse-heap for the elements which are not sufficiently “valuable” to get a place within religion’ and should be ‘given a decent burial’ (Pettersson 1957: 119, cited in Otto 2013: 317). The meaning and uses of the term ‘magic’ have been debated by scholars for the last several decades.<sup>1</sup> For the purposes of this chapter, and in the general context of this volume, magic will refer to power gained from supernatural entities, whether gods (working through such objects as skulls and books) or their emanations. As a practice, it is based on ritual power gained from interaction with supernatural entities.

Magic has been viewed in both positive and negative contexts in India and Indonesia. Indeed, as we see in the Muslim context, magic should be absolutely forbidden—but what if it is based on *sura*s from the Qur’an?

### 8.2 India

West Bengal is a well-known centre for worship of goddesses. Bengali Śāktism, or worship of Śakti (the word *śakti* means both ‘woman’ and ‘power’), has several different strands or styles. There is folk Śāktism, which generally involves worship of local nature goddesses and has as its goals health, fertility, and control of luck. There is devotional Śāktism or Śākta *bhakti*, which emphasizes love of the goddess, generally Kālī. Its major activity is ritual worship ( *pūjā*) and receiving blessed food ( *prasāda*/*prasad*), as well as singing and writing poetry. There is also the yogic and Tantric style of Śāktism, which emphasizes supernatural knowledge and spiritual transcendence, gained primarily through meditation and ritual action. It sometimes involves *siddhi*s or supernatural abilities.

We should note that there are many definitions of Tantra. In popular Western literature about India, Tantra has been sensationalized as idolatry, sexuality, and violent behaviour. It has also been portrayed as meditation using esoteric symbolism leading towards enlightenment. People in the field of Tantric studies have long debates on its origins and nature: one list of Tantric requirements includes extra-Vedic sources; physical disciplines; use of mantras, *mudrā*s, and *maṇḍala*s/*yantra*s; importance of guru and initiation; worship of god and goddess; use of forbidden substances; and secrecy.<sup>2</sup> There are sensationalized definitions, focusing on horror and scandal, and more philosophical definitions, which run the risk of being accused of ‘sanitizing’ the tradition. As I have shown elsewhere, there are several strands or styles of Śākta practice, which include Tantric rituals (McDaniel 1994).

Bengali Tantric magic usually involves the goddess Kālī, as well as her emanation forms and the lower entities who serve her. We can divide Bengali Śākta Tantra into ‘folk’ Śākta Tantra and ‘elite’ Śākta Tantra. Folk Śākta Tantra is generally practiced by village *tāntrika*s who are dedicated to a regional form of Kālī or to lower supernatural entities, such as *bhūta*s and *piśāca*s, or by tribal healers like *ojha*s and *guṇin*s, who have had some training with local *tāntrika*s. It is often focused on abilities like the *kṛṣṇaṣaṭkarma*s or black magical powers (these include 

*[p. 156]* ^pg169

abilities like hypnosis, paralysis, creation of passion and hatred, and control of storms and earthquakes). Mantras are generally in vernacular languages. Clients often ask such *tāntrika*s for destructive acts, and this is how the practitioners make money. Practitioners are professional people for whom magic is a livelihood, and they are not rejected by their communities. Magic is related to the Hindi/Urdu term *jadu*, whose meanings range from sorcery performed by *jadukar*s or wizards, to stage magic, hypnosis, and magic tricks.

Elite Tantra tends to be practiced by Śākta priests, gurus, and practitioners who are popularly considered to have perfected knowledge (Tantric *siddha*s). They are usually literate and know Sanskrit as well as Bengali mantras. Elite Tantra has less focus on magic, and its emphasis is the goddess as universal consciousness, with the goal of supreme knowledge or *brahmajñāna* rather than supernatural powers. It emphasizes philosophy, literature, and the arts. However, the supernatural powers that come along with its practice of Tantric mantras, *mudrā*s, and *maṇḍala*s can be described as magical abilities, for they come from the goddess and other supernatural entities, and they involve the manipulation of *śakti* as supernatural power.

One example of a Bengali folk *tāntrika* involved in magical practice was Pisacha Baba, locally known in Calcutta as a *piśāca tāntrika* or a Tantric practitioner who has power over lower spirits (the *piśāca*s are believed to haunt burning grounds; they have dark skin and red eyes, and they are able to possess people and to cause illness and insanity). Pisacha Baba gained his powers through rituals performed at the burning ground and claimed abilities in exorcism, creating protective amulets, and taking supernatural revenge on family members and neighbours.

I interviewed him at his small storefront, with a front room full of irritable-- looking people waiting to see him. He showed me his back room, which was more interesting, for it was full of grey-brown human skulls and thigh bones. It had a wall poster of a skeleton sitting in meditation on a corpse, with a red fiery mandala at the navel.<sup>3</sup> In order to speak with him, I had to have him sit on my lap and feed him rice with my hands. He was a large, heavy guy, dressed in ragged red robes—it was a challenge. But I had to ritually become his mother, to be safe from his supernatural powers—both he and my local informant agreed on this. The skulls and bones held *śakti*, and he displayed some of the oldest bones which held the greatest power. He said that he dealt with spirits who tormented people—*bhūta*s, *preta*s, and *piśāca*s. He could protect people from them by chanting mantras and using amulets with *yantra* designs on them, and he could exorcize the spirits if they possessed people. He could also send them out for revenge if he thought the situation warranted it. Sometimes he was called into family disputes over inheritance, bad marriages, and people owing each other money. He would try to negotiate these, but if people were recalcitrant, he could send out *piśāca* spirits to influence them. For him, Tantra was a technique for gaining and using supernatural power.<sup>4</sup>

Sometimes we have combinations of styles. In Bolpur in West Bengal, the Kālī priest Tapan Goswami dealt with both folk Tantra and religious devotion (*bhakti*). He learned about Tantra from personal experience and was inspired by the spirit 

*[p. 157]* ^pg170

of his dead grandfather, who was a Śākta *tāntrika*. He too made use of skulls, in his case performing a ritual of feeding skulls to keep lines of communication open with them. Skulls have their preferences—some like puffed rice, others prefer lentils, curries, meat, or wine. With the meals, he also performed *homa* and *yajña* fire rituals. He would bathe the skulls in milk products and honey, and then he would worship them. They would give him *śakti* in return, shown through supernatural powers and physical strength. He said that the best skulls were from young, vigorous people who have died from violence—nobody wanted skulls from old and sick people. When the skulls are pacified, they become protectors, fighting off *bhūta*s and *preta*s who come to distract priests and *sādhu*s. There were 108 skulls under his altar, which, if worshipped properly, could awaken the goddess from sleeping in her statue. He noted that male gods like to be worshipped with stones and *liṅga*s, but goddesses preferred skulls.

He described three levels of *tantra sādhana*. The *tāmasika bhāva* is the one that deals with death and lower spirits. It is the beginning approach, but the goddess only visits briefly during the rituals. *Rājasika bhāva* is better, dealing with accomplishments in the world, and *sāttvika bhāva* is best, for that is devotional. At the tamasic level, the person gains control over the spirits. But with devotion comes the longer-term presence of the goddess, gaining her blessings as well as supernatural abilities. For him, Tantra was a developmental process, reaching through magic to devotion. The goddess had called him, and his life was a response to that summons. He had also been called by the spirit of his dead grandfather, who came to him in dreams and meditations. The spirit stayed in the seat of five skulls ( *pañcamuṇḍī āsana*) where he would meditate during life, and when Tapan Goswami meditated there, his body would become full of bliss and mantras, and he could feel his grandfather moving through him like the wind.

Tantric practices may come from sitting at the feet of a guru, but they may also come from texts. As an example of a folk Tantric text, I would mention the *Ḍākinītantra*. It is a small handbook of folk Tantra, sold at Bengal Śākta spiritual sites or *śāktapīṭha*s, locales sacred to the goddess Kālī and her forms. The book is written in Bengali script, in red letters, though some Sanskrit mantras are included. This Tantra gives magical rituals for enchanting people; changing their luck; making them sleepy; turning white hair black; curing bites from snakes, insects, and large mammals; and dealing with ghosts, spirits, and witches. There are instructions on techniques for performing the eight major black magic rituals, the *kṛṣṇaṣaṭkarma*s, and other Tantric *sādhana*s, including the invocation of supernatural beings (such as *yakṣinī*s*, yoginī*s, *bhūta*s, *preta*s, and *nāgakanyā*s). It has mantras to use for seeing and controlling ghosts, spirits, and witches, and ones which allow the *tāntrika* to change physical form and fly in a spiritual body, as well as instructions for abilities like *vāksiddhi* (with this power, whatever the practitioner says comes true). There is a great focus on Tantric magic, on gaining supernatural power, and little interest in such classically religious goals as union with *brahman* or devotion to deities.

Folk Tantra may sometimes be found in literature. Bengali informants sometimes spoke of the greatest Tantric magician, King Vikramāditya of myth 

*[p. 158]* ^pg171

and legend. Vikramāditya is found as a character (usually hailing from the town of Ujjain, and sometimes from Paṭaliputra) in a variety of texts, including the *Gāthāsaptaśatī* of Hāla Sātavāhana, the *Bṛhatkathā* of Guṇāḍhya, the  *Kathāsaritsāgara* of Somadeva, the *Siṃhāsanadvatriṃśaka*, and the *Vetālapañcaviṃśati* (Pandey 1951: 1). He was described as a passionate worshipper of the goddess Bhadrakālī who possessed many styles of magical powers, and she resolved to help him found his kingdom. The tales of Vikramāditya mix magic and devotion, and Śākta *tāntrika*s loved stories about him. Many Bengali Śākta informants referred to Vikramāditya as a *paramatāntrika*, the ideal or highest type of Tantric practitioner. In his life challenges, he combines the skills of Śākta Tantra with the devotion of Śākta *bhakti*, and there are many stories about his adventures.<sup>5</sup>

Some stories show a competition between kings for self-sacrificial fervour, both seeking boons from the goddess through sacrifice. In these stories, Vikramāditya was a king and a *siddhapuruṣa*, a person who has succeeded in gaining supernatural abilities. He gained these through both devotional love and Tantric practice, including offerings to supernatural beings, such as the band of 64 *yoginī*s. He was described as charitable, brave, and just, learned in the sacred texts (Vedas, Purāṇas, and Tantras). He possessed all forms of magical ability, including the ability to leave one body and enter another at will. But he is a hero, for his magical abilities were used to help others. He is in some ways like the image of King Solomon in early mediaeval European stories and Biblical commentary, blessed with knowledge and wealth, with such extraordinary abilities as understanding the language of the animals and controlling supernatural beings.

Vikramāditya’s stories weave together Tantric images with Indo-European fairy-tale motifs. A *vetāla* (usually translated as ‘ghoul’ or ‘vampire’, but actually a corpse that has been reanimated) becomes his servant and flies him around the world. He gains herbs which can change a person’s gender from male to female and vice versa. He performs the Indian rope trick, gains knowledge of past lives, turns enchanted animals back into humans, and exorcises possessing ghosts and demons. He restores the dead to life (by using Kālī’s magical ash). He gains numerous magical objects and wins beautiful wives in the process.

Descriptions of Bengali Tantric magic can be found in Tantric and yogic texts, as well as popular literature, and generally involve many forms of *siddhi* or supernatural power. These include visualization of *yoginī*s or supernatural women as mates or helpers, healing, exorcism, changes in bodily form, spiritual travel, and secret knowledge. While the focus of folk Tantric magic tends to be pragmatic ends, elite Tantra emphasizes visionary experience, compassionate action, literary knowledge and inspiration, and eventually union with the gods. Folk Tantra is much more widespread in West Bengal than elite Tantra.

### 8.3 Bali

The understandings of Tantra are even more complex in Indonesia, a nation of over 14,000 islands with the largest Muslim population in the world. Islamic 

*[p. 159]* ^pg172

concepts have influenced many areas, including magic, and there are both Hindu and Muslim forms of magic. The island of Bali is primarily Hindu, with folk Tantric magic performed by local practitioners called *balian*s. There is also Hindu elite Tantra, performed by *pedanda siwa*, Brahmin priests of Śaiva affiliation. Their major work is the creation of holy water, though some also have skills in Tantric magic. Then there is Muslim magic, with its own folk and elite aspects, practiced by *dukun*s and some of the religious scholars known as *kyai hikmah*.

The first Sanskrit inscriptions in Bali date from about the 9th century CE, and Hinduism became more widespread under the Majapahit kingdom (1293–ca. 1500 CE). The Majapahit religious culture worshipped Śiva as primary god, though it also included elements of Mahāyāna Buddhist Tantra. Some kings practiced Tantra, and we can see this in the case of the 13th-century King Kṛtanagara, who was an initiate of the rituals of the *Hevajratantra*. He called himself an avatar of both Śiva and Buddha (Kinney et al. 2003). His descendants founded the Majapahit dynasty.

According to Balinese sources, when the Majapahit empire declined due to invasions by Javanese Islamic sultans in the 15th century, writers, artists, priests, and musicians fled from Java to Bali, bringing further Indian influence to the island. Around the middle of the 16th century, a Javanese Śaiva leader, Daṅ Hyaṅ Nirartha, came to Bali and systematized the worship of Śiva along the lines that were prevalent in Majapahit. Later forms of Balinese Hinduism came to emphasize the role of Śiva (pronounced Siwa in Modern Balinese) in the form of Ardhanareśvarī (*sic*, and not Ardhanarīśvara, as is commonly found in India), who shows the perfect harmony of male and female and the balance of all opposites. It is through meditation on Śiva that the *pedanda* creates holy water for purification and blessing (Hooykaas 1973: 27).

Balinese folk Hinduism includes mediumship, spiritual healing, and group possession, and its major religious specialists are various types of *balian*s. The *balian*s fight against local evil spirits, especially the *leyak* or sorceress, and dark magic is sometimes associated with the Tantric *vāmamārga* and magical spells. It also includes the use of daggers (*keris*), which have power or *sakti*, and may be inhabited by ancestors. The dramas and conflicts of folk religions are often expressed by the *dalang* or puppet master, who performs shadow plays for the community and is often viewed as both priest and guru. His plays can reveal how to balance opposing forces. Healing may involve insight into neglect of the *kanda empat* and ways to fix this problem. The *kanda empat* are the ‘four siblings’ or spiritual guardians with which every child is born: the placenta, the amniotic fluid, the umbilical cord, and the fetal membrane. If respected and honoured, they will protect and help the soul; if not, they can be maleficent forces.

Balinese Tantra has been handed down by practitioners and is primarily described in texts preserved in palm-leaf manuscripts called *lontar*. These writings are mainly in Sanskrit and Old Javanese (*kawi*) and include Śaiva texts referred to as *tutur*s and *tattva*s. They contain information on Śaiva doctrine, cosmology, ethics, anatomy, traditional herbs, meditation, yoga, and other subjects. 

*[p. 160]* ^pg173

These manuscripts may also be used by healers who traditionally give advice, medicine, amulets, and instructions for offerings and ceremonies (Hobart 2003: 59–61).

Hindu Tantric magic in Bali is mostly performed by *balian*s and involves the use of mantras, *mudrā*s, and *maṇḍala*s, as well as visualization of subtle bodily centres and *prāṇāyāma* breathing practices. The origins of these practices may appear in visions or be handed down by teachers using *lontar* texts. Sometimes we see a sort of ‘shamanic call’, where gods and ancestors call the *balian* to perform rituals. Intuitive powers known as *ilmu tetenger* may develop, allowing the healer to experience a form of psychic insight during healings (Muninjaya 1982: 35). *Balian*s may then perform ritual practice, such as mantra meditation under a tree or at a small waterfall or at a meeting point of three rivers (*tapa kungkum*). They may also fast on certain days, and from certain foods, and maintain silence. Healing powers can also come spontaneously through possession, which is referred to as *perewangan*. The healer who experiences *perewangan* may enter a possession state to perform healing through trance (Boedhihartono 1982: 26).

There are descriptions of *balian*s in the royal courts of the 19th century (Hobart 1982: 11–14). These early *balian*s were mostly literate Brahmin males. The few historical representations of female *balian*s are generally negative, associated with the Hindu goddess of death, Durgā.<sup>6</sup> These female images were often portrayed as witchlike and frightening, while representations of male *balian*s ranged from helpful to harmful (Vickers 1980). The dark or destructive side of magic in Bali is called *guna-guna*, said to be performed by a person who is *kesaktian*, full of power. This power or *sakti* is neither good nor evil and can be used for many sorts of goals. However, once a *balian* is officially initiated or consecrated, he or she vows to use the power only for good.

There are many varieties of *balian*s. One type is known as the *balian taksu* or *ketakson* trance medium: he or she goes into a trance to communicate with the spirit world and is believed to chase away unwanted influences this way. *Ketakson*s evoke the spirit of a dead person and pass on information to the family about what kinds of offerings are needed for cremations and other ceremonies. They can also channel living people to give guidance or locate missing objects. Most of the female *balian*s are *ketakson*. There will usually be an initial meeting in which the healer will engage a range of techniques to ascertain the state of the client, any imbalances that are present, and possibly past lives of the individual. He or she will study the person’s name, palm, and aura, and then enter a trance state. Families may come with questions about deceased family members—mainly requests for the dead about the performance of upcoming funerary rites, and the identity of reincarnating ancestors when a baby is born. Often, the *balian* will invite a *nāga* spirit to enter and help in the cure. The *balian taksu* works with *niṣkala*—the unseen forces—to take power from nature or spirits. He or she may create medicines from holy water, flowers, and plants, or conduct cleansing rituals. In a survey conducted in 1978 by the Department of Health in Bali, the largest group of Balinese healers were *balian taksu* (Muninjaya 1982: 39).

*[p. 161]* ^pg174

Another type, the *balian usada*, refers to a person who owns or has access to sacred manuscripts, especially those which describe massage techniques and traditional medicines made from plants and animals. Their palm-leaf manuscripts are known as *lontar usada*, derived from the Sanskrit word for healing, *auṣadha.* *Lontar*s are understood to heal in two ways; on the one hand, they contain descriptions of illnesses, how to diagnose them, and how to cure them; on the other hand, the *lontar*s themselves contain magical power, which can be effective in healing.

The *balian usada* may also use intuition, visions, mantras, and prayer. The *balian* may voluntarily decide on this role, or he may receive supernatural knowledge during a severe illness. He may decide to gain further knowledge by studying the *lontar*s and working with recognized healers. These *balian*s also study both white and black magic, which are very similar except for the intention of the practitioner. They may use amulets or mantras to give more power to the herbal medicine, and many of these mantras come from Tantric texts, especially Śaiva texts. Some suggest yogic practices for clients, accompanied by fasting from certain foods.

The status of the *balian usada* within the community is supported by the fact that they usually come from high castes and all are male (Hobart, 2003: 59–60). Even if a woman wanted to become a *balian usada*, it would be very difficult for her since menstruation is deemed as impure and would thus be an obstacle in pursuing the life of a literate healer (which requires access to sacred spaces which must remain ritually pure). Also, if a woman is seen out at night alone or at powerful magical or sacred sites (practices which are often necessary), she may be accused of witchcraft (Howe 1984: 212–217). Other types of *balian* include *balian paica*, healers who possess a power object, such as a stone or an object that is believed to have been materialized during meditation, and the *balian tenung*, who performs divination and faith healing. The *balian tapakan* is a person who obtains power (*sakti* or *ilmu*) through supernatural inspiration. They have no formal training but are eventually recognized by the community as healers and often take later priestly initiation. They may communicate with the souls of the dead and be possessed by them, or they may get in touch with gods and goddesses of death. Many *balian*s take the honorific title Jero.

The *balian* Mangku Teja Kandel said in an interview that Tantra for *balian*s involves both light and dark clasped together, like hands. Light protects and saves, dark creates danger for others, but many of the forms that look terrifying are actually protective and defensive. For him, Tantra is a technique of magic. He wanted to be involved in magical situations from childhood, and he created his own initiation, first becoming a *tāntrika* and then a *balian*. He had read about Tantric *sādhana*s in religious texts, and he went to the local death temple ( *pura dalem*) to perform rituals to the death deities, Durgā and Prajāpati. He offered them a black chicken and its blood at midnight, chanting the *Durgastava* hymn, and wore a Durgā *poleng* (a black and white checked sarong). Durgā appeared to him in a frightening form and gave him mantras and *mudrā*s to perform. He declared his lifelong dedication to her.<sup>7</sup>

*[p. 162]* ^pg175

Some of these practices were revealed during trance states by his ancestors, so he had to gain their permission to practice the rituals, and they agreed to communicate with him at special shrines. This is how he developed many of his abilities. He gained skills in healing, purification, palm readings, foreseeing the names of babies and future spouses, and fortune telling. He eventually took *mewinten diksa* to become a *pemangku* (temple priest, usually from a low caste), which is a socially recognized initiation.

But he said that mostly people come to him for minor magical goals—success in business, to become beautiful, and for love magic. He stated that *balian*s come from an ancient Tantric lineage, structured by Ṛṣi Mārkaṇḍeya and the 11th-century East Javanese King Airlaṅga. This lineage was changing now with Indian influence, adopting new practices like the use of skulls and bones, and adding more focus on magic.<sup>8</sup> His family accepted the practice, as he had many *balian* relatives, and he hopes that his son will become a *balian* after him.

Another *balian* interviewed is Jero Sushum, a *balian tenung* who heals people from black magical spells. She tells her clients what chants, offerings, and sarong colours they should use during rituals to help spirits in the afterlife. Her life as a *balian* began when she spontaneously fell into a trance at a Śiva temple, and a god spoke through her. She had a call to become a *balian*. She learned Tantric mantras and *mudrā*s from the god in trance, which she said helped her to defend herself against black magicians, and later took *ekajati diksa* when a *balian* guru wrote mantras on her tongue, forehead, shoulders, and every joint. The guru used honey as ink, and he applied it with a special leaf. Rituals came to her spontaneously, and they included the use of Balinese mantras, *mudrā*s, and *vajra* symbols. She also learned from a vision how to perform a version of the *sūryasevana* ritual.

She works with the dead—she said that death is like a courtroom, and she can get you a better lawyer due to her abilities at mantra and *banten* offerings.<sup>9</sup> She talks to both long-dead ancestors and recently dead souls, and she can see them wearing white sarongs. They enter and possess her, and she speaks their messages, which often have suggestions about *banten* offerings. She emphasized that becoming a *balian* was not her choice, she had been called and chosen by the god Ida Sang Hyang Sinun. She mentioned that she can see the power of other *balian*s, but she does not spy on them—for doing so, she would need the permission of the god and other *balian*s.<sup>10</sup>

Her husband is also a *balian*, but he is her assistant, and he is instructed by his wife. During the interview, she spoke, while he served snacks to the long line of people waiting for her. She has 11 brothers and sisters, and eight of them are *balian*s or *pemangku* priests; it is commonly believed in Bali that spiritual power runs in bloodlines.

Many Balinese believe that the increase in illness during the rainy season is due to demonic influences, as the rainy season is a time of year when black magic practitioners are believed to transform themselves into evil *leyak*s, sorceresses who bring illness and misfortune to those they encounter. When the *leyak*s transform themselves, the physical body is believed to stay in bed asleep in human 

*[p. 163]* ^pg176

form, while the *leyak*s’ transformed shape will appear in the world as a physical body. *Leyak*s gain their abilities by birth, spell, or amulet, and it is believed that some have secret knowledge of the *lontar* manuscripts, which they spend years studying to use for black magic (Eiseman 1989: 128). The *balian* opposes the *leyak*, who is served by witches and their dark *sakti*.

Witchcraft is associated with the Hindu goddess of destruction and the underworld, Durgā. She is equated with Kālī as a dark goddess, and there are various origin stories for her in Bali. According to one in the *Mūrva Kāla*, when the goddess Umā rejected her husband Śiva’s advances, his sperm fell on the ground, creating the monster Kāla. Because of this rejection, she was transformed into the evil Durgā (Levenda 2011: 71). Witches are thought to be uncivilized, jealous, and greedy, the opposite of all that is normal and decent. Some people equate witches with *leyak*s, others understand them to be humans who serve the *leyak*s. Witches are believed to command local spirits and use *keris* daggers to affect others, controlling them by mantras and mudras.

While folk Śaiva Tantra is widespread, there is also elite Śaiva Tantra in Bali. Śaiva Brahmanical priests ( *pedanda siwa*) are trained in Tantric rituals as a part of their study for initiation and worship. They are taught the use of mantras, *mudrā*s, and *maṇḍala*s by their gurus. Their most important ability is in the ritual of *sūryasevana*, in which the god Śiva/Sūrya is invoked to transform ordinary water into holy water. This ritual is performed each morning by Balinese priests. Holy water is used to purify, cleanse, and prevent evil forces from polluting a body or space. Water has a central place in the Balinese religion, and all water is sacred to some degree in daily life (Hooykaas 1973: 11).

One *pedanda* interviewed was Ida Pedanda Putra Yoga. As he noted, Tantric meditation may involve inner *maṇḍala*s, which are visualized, and outer *maṇḍala*s, which may be drawn, and there are also gods who are ritually placed within the body:

In meditation on Śiva, the body is equated with the universe, and we recognize that gods live in parts of the body. Using visualization and *bīja* mantras, gods come to dwell in every part of the body. We learn these mantras through oral tradition, they are given at initiation (*dīkṣā*) and then there are later teachings by the guru. They are secret, and not written down—mantras are only oral. The bodies of the gods are made of mantras.<sup>11</sup> While some *pedanda*s work with the *kanda empat* figures within the body and communicate with the dead, the major focus of the *pedanda* is helping and healing the community. They are understood as literate priests rather than magicians.

However, some *pedanda*s do show shamanic elements in their life stories, especially the shamanic call. For Ida Pedanda Gde Ratu Gunung, the call came after marriage and hotel work, where he had acted as a hotel *pemangku* (a lower level of priest usually associated with temples) doing blessings and *pūjā*s for the guests. He came from a Brahmin *pedanda* family. But as he approached retirement, he 

*[p. 164]* ^pg177

started feeling faint, ‘half-dead’, and neither doctors nor *balian*s could help him. Finally, he took holy water and had a vision. An ancestor appeared to him and asked if he was willing to undergo purification; he said yes. He felt his body being transported, and he saw his ancestor, who was really Daṅ Hyaṅ Nirartha, the 16th-- century priest who is considered to be the founder of the *pedanda* tradition, as well as the ancestor of all Brahmanical Śaiva priests. Nirartha chanted mantras over him, and he heard them in his mind, and then Nirartha wrote the mantras in sacred letters (*akṣara*) on his hands. He fell into a dream where somebody pulled a hair from his head, and his soul was thrown from his body out onto the sea and returned three times.<sup>12</sup> Then he went in a northeast direction and saw a river flowing into the sea. A beautiful woman with long hair was sitting on the sea, calling him to come closer. There was a strong current in the sea around her, and he was afraid to approach. She asked him to become a *pedanda*, and he said yes. This was the goddess Gaṅgā.<sup>13</sup> Such a call is not required to become a priest, but it shows the deity’s interest in the person. He continued to see that ocean in dreams, sometimes with naked yogins in the waters. He has also had visions of the god Śiva, which left him icy cold, with a lingering vibration in his head.<sup>14</sup>

### 8.4 Java

There is Muslim magic in both Bali and Java (especially with recent Muslim immigration from Java to Bali for work in the tourism industry). A form of Java-- imported folk magical practitioner in Bali is the *dukun*. The term is Indonesian, associated with Javanese Kejawen (‘Javanism’) or Kebatinan syncretistic mystical currents. *Dukun*s may follow shamanic beliefs, Hindu beliefs, and Muslim beliefs; *dukun*s often mix traditions. They are known for their prophetic visions, and their power may be inherited or learned through initiation. They are also known for various magical practices. In the practice of *gendam*, a *dukun* will chant the target’s name to summon and control him; in *naruga*, the *dukun* implants an emotional suggestion in the victim. It may be a benevolent love spell, or a malevolent spell-causing the target to murder someone else. In the practice of *sirep*, the *dukun* causes people to fall into a deep, unshakeable slumber; in *susuk*, the *dukun* implants a metal object in the patient’s body with mantras, to make the patient stronger or more sexually attractive. These magical techniques bear a resemblance to the dark *kṛṣṇaṣaṭkarma*s of Śākta Tantric magic.

Objects with power are referred to as *pusaka*s. Ceremonial masks are often empowered, and *dukun*s may also use amulets and drawings. Drawings can be viewed as an extension of the healer’s powers. There are hundreds of sacred drawings used in Balinese healing practices, the majority are used for protection and may be placed on specific points of the body, on paper, or on some other object. Often the images involve mirroring, in vertical or horizontal symmetry. Drawings are wrapped in paper and tied with a string so that the recipient is not able to examine the drawings, for they will then lose their power (Hobart 2003: 63–72).

*[p. 165]* ^pg178

There are many descriptions of types of Javanese *dukun*s in the anthropological literature, and perhaps one of the best-known lists is in Clifford Geertz’s book *The Religion of Java* (Geertz 1960). These deal with social roles, forms of healing, and magical accomplishments. They include midwives, masseurs, mediums, ritual specialists, diviners, herbalists, and specialists in spells. As Parsudi Suparlan (1991: 3) notes, the most popular definition of *dukun* is a person who heals by using magical power. From the *dukun* perspective, the universe is animated by power that is continually in movement. Parsudi Suparlan (*ibid*.) describes five types of power: *pulung* (glorious power), *wahyu* (victorious or auspicious power), *andaru* (compassionate power), *teluh braja* (evil power), and *guntar* (angry power). These powers can work towards good fortune (life, growth, fertility) or bad fortune (chaos, illness, calamity). Power can be localized in places, and also in persons and invisible beings. More positive types of power are found in prophets, saints (*wali*), and guardian spirits, while more negative forms of power are found in devils (*iblis*) and evil spirits (such as *jinn*, *banaspati* and *gendruwo*). Powers can also be present at certain times and in certain names (*ibid*.: 4).

The soul (*sukma*) regulates the powers in the body, and the person is viewed as a container (*wadah*) of these powers. If the person has too much power for the soul to control, or too little for the soul to defend itself, bad fortune may result. The *dukun* may change the balance of powers within the person, or have the person change his or her name, which will redistribute the power through body and soul (*ibid*.: 7).

The *dukun* can help the person avoid suffering or can induce it. Both involve manipulation of power. The highest stage of knowledge is found when the practitioner can accumulate power and have a stable relationship with invisible beings who can help during magical practice (*ibid*.: 7). Evil spirits can be chased away by the use of mantra and mandala diagrams or by power objects, such as *keris* knives, gems, and carved stones.

A person’s ability to become a *dukun* is generally passed down from their *dukun* ancestors. However, some form of preparation, at least initially, is necessary for *dukun* to receive their spiritual power. This usually consists of long periods of meditation and fasting for days or months. Once the *dukun* has received this spiritual power, he or she needs to learn the skills and knowledge of *dukun* practice. Some *dukun*s learn these skills from another *dukun* or from books on *ilmu Jawa* that are available in bookshops. Others say their skills were taught to them by spirits whom they continue to consult for advice on the diagnosis and treatment of their clients.

Another area that shows this sort of syncretism is the local magical tradition of Joglosemar, in which Balinese Hindu and Javanese Muslim ideas mix with other traditions, such as Buddhism and local animism. It is part of the contemplative or *kebatinan* (from the Arabic word *batin* or inner) traditions and accepts multiple worlds reached through meditation on the body, with various colours of inner light based on the elements, used towards a goal of harmony (*rukun*). It is popularly used for the prediction of auspicious days through astrology, but its darker side is associated with shapeshifting, black magic, and dangerous goddesses. 

*[p. 166]* ^pg179

There is use of mantras and magical drawings to avert evil. It shows the influence of Muslim magic or *ilmu hikmah*, which itself has folk and elite aspects.

Indonesia is primarily Sunni Muslim, an approach known locally as Santri. It follows the Shafi’i legal school. In mainstream Islam, magical practices are forbidden. But underground and in rural areas, syncretistic and magical ideas continue. Sufi symbolic interpretations mix with folk and Tantric ideas to create the core of Kejawen or Kebatinan Islam, which has both inner esoteric and outer aspects. Practitioners gain supernatural power from pilgrimages to saint tombs, royal graves, and statues, and revere certain nature spirits (like Nyai Loro Kidul, queen of the Southern Ocean, considered to be ritually married to the sultan of Yogyakarta).<sup>15</sup>

The more elite form of Muslim magic involves using *sura*s from the Qur’an and Arabic *dhikr*s, and it may be taught and practiced by religious authorities like the *kyai hikmah*. Its more folk types may be practiced by the *pawang* or *dukun santet*, using chants (in Arabic, Javanese, and Sundanese), empowered water, and amulets. We might distinguish between these by saying that elite magic makes use of the Arabic language, chants from the Qur’an, the Hadith, and various *kitab* or holy books, while folk magic can be justified as simply not being against Islamic teachings.

Major forms of magic include developing invulnerability to fire and knives (*debus*), inducing possession (by person or animal), and bringing luck. We may debate if it can be classified as Tantra, as it does use mantras and *mudrā*s, and esoteric meditation, but not *maṇḍala*s (due to the Muslim prohibition of images). However, there are magical squares with Arabic letters, and geometrical forms with letters and numbers, which resemble *maṇḍala*s.

An area associated with magic is the island of Banten in West Java. The practice of *ilmu hikmah* is widespread in Banten, though it is practiced underground, according to an interview with Ayatullah Humaeni, an anthropologist who grew up in Banten and did research there.<sup>16</sup> It is taught in some Muslim schools or *pesantren*, by religious scholars called *kyai hikmah* or *ahli hikmah*. It maintains religious legitimacy by using Arabic language and holy books. Mantras are used to create holy water for blessings, and with this, the *kyai* can give blessings (*karomah* and *barokah*) to followers. This is intended to give them a greater ability to follow Islamic law and the capacity to exorcise evil spirits. Arabic writing in various patterns can be used for supernatural ends. But all Muslim magic must be shown to be derived from God, and all ritual practices need to prove their religious legitimacy. Some magic follows the tradition of *sihir*, an Arabic term meaning divination, fantasy, and trickery.<sup>17</sup> It makes use of spells, magical gestures, magical letters, and empowered gems.

More unusual abilities include *ilmu sambatan*, possession by Muslim spirits, including martial arts masters, and *ilmu hadiran*, possession by a dead *kyai* or religious teacher to learn answers to religious questions.<sup>18</sup> The power of *ilmu kebal* (invulnerability to evil and weapons) is gained by fasting, silence, and chanting words of power. The words are to be chanted over water, which the person then drinks.

*[p. 167]* ^pg180

Muslim folk magic makes use of amulets (*jimat*) and empowered objects. There are mantras in local languages, like Javanese and Sundanese, for bravery and love. There is a special class of *debus* mantras for invulnerability. There are magical knives known as *keris*, which can hold spirits, and animal skins with mantras sewn inside (*wafak*). Harder to find are magical jewels, such as *naga mani* or cobra stone, and elephant stone, which is a hardened drop of elephant sperm. Humaeni discussed how love magic or *pelet* is widespread, and to be *dipelet* or love-mad is a local illness. There are also local folk magicians or *pawang*s who are believed to be able to control animals and weather, and are hired for wedding and circumcision parties, *slametan* feasts, and national events.<sup>19</sup>

There is also use of *taweez*, or magical amulets. The locket has sacred objects concealed within it, including small pieces of sacred fabric and paper with handwritten incantations and spells. No ordinary ink was used for writing these magic formulas because writing for a holy purpose like this requires the use of magical oils as a substitute for normal ink. The outside surface of the amulet, however, has Arabic calligraphy on it written in waterproof ink. On the back of it, the *taweez* symbols of mainstream Islam are depicted, such as two crossed swords pointing upwards representing the double ‘Zulfiqar’ sword of ‘Āli, the cousin and son-in-- law of the Islamic prophet Muḥammad.

The area of Banyuwangi in East Java (which acts as a port between Bali and Java) is also famous for magic, especially black magic. This generally takes on two major forms. The first is *sihir*, black magic used to kill another person. This generally comes in the form of *busung*, where the victim’s stomach will grow grotesquely in size. It is believed various items such as knives, nails, broken glass, even small pans or animals can be found inside the stomach. *Busung* victims rarely escape death. The second type of black magic in Banyuwangi is *rapuh*, sorcery designed to make the victim suffer throughout their lifetime. Symptoms include sudden blindness or deafness, paralysis or uncontrollable shaking and trembling. Such problems are generally thought to be caused by a type of folk practitioner, the black magician or *dukun santet*, who uses his powers for black magic. A code of ethics among Banyuwangi *dukun santet* forbids them from using their magic against people in the same village. If this occurs, then the accused *dukun* must undertake an oath of innocence in the local mosque. A *dukun* found guilty by fellow villagers was usually exiled from the village and sometimes his home and possessions were burned. If the *dukun* uses his powers for personal gain or malicious intent, they will be lost or weakened. In the past, *dukuns* participated in communal activities, blessing the villages during harvests, wars, epidemics, and sports competitions. However, today we have the increasing institutionalization of Santri Islam, and *dukun*s are generally contacted only for individual problems (Ayatullah Humaeni 2012).

On the elite side, there are certain religious schools ( *pesantren*) which not only teach Islamic text and interpretation but also teach courses in ‘magical sciences’ or ‘mystical knowledge’ (*ilmu hikmah* or *al-hikmah*)*. Ilmu sambatan*, 

*[p. 168]* ^pg181

*ilmu kontak*, *ilmu hadiran*, *ilmu kebal*, and *hizb* are several other varieties of magical practice taught.<sup>20</sup> *Debus* teachers perform a variety of magical rituals intended to create invulnerability to evil and harm, with Islamic invocations in Arabic alongside Javanese and Sundanese chants. Teachers may give magical abilities to students at these schools, with an offering of money (*mateng*) or by means of fasting (*mentah*). There are fasts like the Ramadan fast, for three, seven, or 40 days and fasting on rice and water. There are also fasts which involve silence or chanting. At the school, the teacher (*kyai*) determines which students are worthy of magical abilities. He will take a student’s right hand, recite a *mantera*, and slash the student’s hand with a long knife. The student will then gain such abilities as healing, love, authority, compassion, protection, and generally being able to gain wishes (Ayatullah Humaeni 2012: 79–80).

The rituals of *debus* or invulnerability generally open with the Qur’anic sura al-Fatiha, followed by prayers for protection to Muḥammad, Abd al-Kadir Jailani, and others, then the use of coconut oil and incense. The participants drink water empowered by chants and prayers. This is followed by demonstrations of martial arts, and then the *debus* itself—with people stabbing knives into their stomachs, breaking coconuts with their teeth, eating glass or porcelain, climbing stairs made of machetes (*ibid*: 84). Such acts show the person’s religious dedication.

Sometimes magic has been used towards political ends. During the 1926 communist revolt in Banten, several *kyai*s who became the leaders of the revolt were recorded as performing rituals for invulnerability (*ilmu kekebalan*), involving fasting and prayer, and they gave amulets and holy water to their followers (Williams 1982: 372). Even today, the *kyais* can rise to be informal leaders in the villages of Indonesia if they are understood to have supernatural powers.

### 8.5 Discussion

There is no single definition of magic in the traditions discussed here, but the definition of ‘ritually manipulating supernatural power for instrumental ends’ fits these cases reasonably well. They simply have different sources of power.

In the Hindu traditions, magical power is called *śakti* or *sakti* (depending on regional pronunciation), the energy which supports the universe. In Muslim tradition, magical power comes from God’s divine blessings, *barakah* and *karamah*. Magic is religiously justified when its power is linked to the god or gods, but it becomes forbidden when there is no divinity to anchor it. Both traditions make use of this type of religious power.

In folk Hindu Tantra in India, we have positive healing aspects and a devotional relationship with the goddess Kālī that often comes as a result of a special call or dream command. Then there is the dark magical path which may be called *vāmācāra* <sup>21</sup> or *vaśīkaraṇa*, and occasionally there is reference to *Shaitan indrajāla*. Black magic focuses on dark or evil beings—*bhūta*s, *preta*s, *ḍākinī*s/*dayini*s, *vetāla*s*—*who are controlled by mantras. This practice may be 

*[p. 169]* ^pg182

contrasted with the more positive uses of Tantric practice, which involve rituals to bring closeness to the goddess and spiritual wisdom of natural and supernatural worlds.

In folk Hindu Tantra in Bali, we have *balian*s who are *kesaktian*, full of *sakti*, and have specialized abilities—knowledge of healing, of ritual texts, of how to work with power objects. They can heal and counsel people. There are also black magic practitioners, people who practice *guna-guna*, invoking the death goddess Durgā to become witches and *leyak*s. These may be defined as Tantric through their practices—mantras, *mudrā*s, *maṇḍala*s, internal purification, visualizations, etc.

In Sunni Islam, magic is forbidden. However, there is elite or ‘orthodox’ Muslim magic, in which blessings from Allah include knowledge of how to interpret the Qur’an, bravery, protection, and resistance to evil, gained by use of Arabic texts and *dhikr*s. In the folk Muslim magic of Java, we see Allah’s blessings used towards powers like invulnerability to weapons, control over weather, creating desire in women, and having power over spirits and animals. While we do not see the use of the term ‘Tantric’, we have the practices of Tantra: mantras, *mudrā*s, *maṇḍala*s (as charts of letters and numbers), ritual purification, manipulation of the elements, belief in supernatural power, etc.

We see in these various cases that there is no single understanding of magical power. These do follow the popular general definition, the supernatural manipulation of beings or forces for instrumental ends. Magic may be understood as positive or negative, constructive or destructive, depending on how it is used. It can find a place in monotheistic religions, as well as be used in polytheistic and animistic ones. Such magic can be used for both blessings and curses. Tantric magic is a place where non-standard forms of Hinduism and Islam can meet. It can be handed down in families but often is a result of a special call by a god or spirit.

Magic is the great situation of moral ambivalence. Rather than a strictly dualistic view of the world as composed of good and evil, the view of Tantric magic tends to be a monistic worldview in which malevolence and benevolence are seen as intrinsic parts of the whole, and subject to manipulation. Thus, magical practices require justifications.

Such justification is more difficult in Islam, for there is a commentarial history on what is allowed and what is forbidden. The Qur’an notes that the origin of magic is demonic, and states, ‘\[T\]he devils disbelieved, teaching men magic’ (2:102). All of the accepted Islamic jurists have declared the learning and practicing of magic to be prohibited. Imam Ali says, ‘One who learns magic, less or more, has become an infidel and his association with Allah is completely severed’.<sup>22</sup>

We also see at the end of the Qur’an, *sura* 113 called Daybreak, ‘In the name of Allah the Beneficent, the Merciful, Say I seek refuge in the in the Lord of daybreak, From the evil of that which he created; From the evil of the darkness when it is intense, and from the evil of malignant witchcraft…’<sup>23</sup> This should be recited three times after the dawn prayer and three times after the sunset prayer. This keeps the problem of magic in mind. There is an exception: if it is for the 

*[p. 170]* ^pg183

purpose of nullifying the sorcery of other sorcerers. ‘At times, on the basis of general obligation (*wajib kifa’i*) some individuals must necessarily learn it so that they can nullify the sorcery of a false claimant (to prophethood), should he desire to mislead the people by means of sorcery, and thus lay bare his lies and false claims’. <sup>24</sup>

One exception can allow others. Some practitioners argue that magic may not be supported by mainstream Islam, but it is understood by practitioners not to violate Islamic values. Others claim that if it is performed out of dedication to Allah, then anything is possible. An interesting variant of this argument comes about in relation to some of the violent Muslim gangs found in Indonesia. The sociologist Yon Machmudi noted in an interview that many of these people had murdered and performed other destructive acts in the past, but the leaders of violent groups like the FPI (Front Pembela Islam or ‘Islamic Defenders Front’) often tell their members that their sins of selfish violence in the past can be forgiven by Allah—if they do the same violent acts to support Allah.<sup>25</sup>

Hindu Tantric folk magic requires less justification, as there are no sets of rules for people to follow. We do sometimes see ethical requirements in some Tantras and Balinese texts, but they are not consistent. It depends on their views of the nature of gods and goddesses. In Hinduism, the deities are not moral exemplars. They are beings far above human understanding, and human beings cannot ever fully understand their will. However, there are actions that human beings may perform to influence them.

In Bengali Hinduism, the devotional influence allows a wide range of behaviours for those dedicated to the goddess. As we see in the stories of King Vikramāditya, Kālī has no problem with magical behaviour if the person is a favourite of the goddess. Indeed, many Tantras discuss how magical, sacrificial, and devotional acts can make one a favourite of the *yoginī*s, the attendants of Kālī, who will bless the practitioner with yet more supernatural abilities. Kālī is like nature: she will create and destroy according to her own wishes. However, dedicated ritual practice and devotion can sway her wishes.

Balinese Tantra is more concerned with universal harmony and balance. If actions will lead to that balance, they can be justified as a part of the greater picture. Both elite and folk practitioners may be called to meditative and magical practices by visions of gods and ancestors, and such direct revelations can justify whatever rituals are shown. Being *kesaktian* means that the person has supernatural power, and if he or she is initiated, it will be used only for good. But without initiation, it is up to the individual to determine the appropriateness of the action. In popular thought, the lack of initiation may imply misuse of supernatural power.

While Bali is well-known for its public trance states, associated with dramas like the Calon Arang, a very different form of trance continues out of sight. Both folk and elite Tantric practitioners have been called to supernatural adventures and have returned to work within their communities. Those who practice magic can be very helpful, as long as it is the right type of magic. While some teachers 

*[p. 171]* ^pg184

have adapted Tantra and magical practices to modern innovations like wellness spas and the concerns of foreign devotees, it will be an interesting area of study to learn how these traditions survive in the face of political and social pressure.
