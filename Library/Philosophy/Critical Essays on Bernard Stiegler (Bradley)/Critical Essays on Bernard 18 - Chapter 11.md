## Chapter 11

*DÉRIVE* OR JOURNEY OF KNOWLEDGE 

IN THE KOREAN SMART CITY? 

*South Korea can be described as a model case for most political, economic,* *social and cultural issues of the last fifty to seventy years. A country with a four-thousand-year history, including 45 years of colonial history, a compressed modernity with rapid industrialization intertwined with a convoluted, often violent democratization process, and now a global player in technology and culture.* (Das & Pratihar, 2021, p. 236) 

Writing from an ecosophical, auto-ethnographical and critical post-media perspective (Bradley, 2022a; Bradley et al., 2023), my interest concerns the production, contribution and loss of knowledge in the so-called smart city. I am expanding, applying, reevaluating and self-criticizing my own project on the therapeutic object (Bradley, 2022b) which I designated as the *objet re-petit-ive abc* (through Lacan, Winnicott and Guattari) to assess how digital pedagogical objects (educational technologies as transitional objects) can co-function therapeutically inside (Genosko, 2022) and outside institutional settings (in transitional spaces). Drawing on auto-ethnographic experience, I am trying to understand the different approaches between the *dérive* as promoted, on the one hand, by the Situationists and Guy Debord in the 1950s and 1960s and more recently by British writer Will Self in his work on psychogeography (2007), and, on the other, the idea of the ‘journey of knowledge’, a notion mentioned by the late French philosopher Bernard Stiegler. This as we shall see concerns a fundamental question of the curation of negentropic forms of knowledge (Bradley, 2022c). The issue is how to think of the *dérive* precisely as a differing and deferring mnemonic technique producing tertiary retentions (Stiegler, 2020a, p. 92) – that is exosomatic, namely, as external memories tied to or invested in the geography of the immediate milieu. With a nod to ecological and ecosophical considerations, I am trying to think about the mnemonic 

*[p. 213]* ^pg228

technique as a way of producing critical reflections on the immediate urban landscape, the catastrophe of climate change, and as a possible way to bring to mind the question of what the landscape is and how it is being transformed into a technical milieu (Rose, 2017). In this sense, my concern is with the possibility of a Stieglerian and/or Guattarian *détournement* (philosophical hijacking) of the smart city. How can we introduce a new form of philosophical idiocy in the smart city? What exactly constitutes the *détournement* of the smart city? 

Auto-ethnographic vignette I 

I have an experience of psychogeography at once absurd, surreal, a little embarrassing, but also profoundly and importantly *idiotic*. This event took place when two colleagues and I went to Seoul a few years ago. The event was held on Friday night and I rushed from Tokyo to Seoul after finishing teaching in the afternoon. I got to Korea through the manipulation of GPS, whether that was navigating myself on the trains and buses in Tokyo, the pilot using satellite GPS to navigate the skies, or myself in some Barthes-like bubble of confusion of Hangol signs running around in Seoul later that evening trying to find the venue for the workshop. I got there eventually, but very much disoriented in the phantasmagorical *dérive* of my psychogeographical experience – flicking between digital globes, voices in my head, icons, maps, languages – Japanese, Korean, Chinese, English – Radiohead songs, and so on. The next day, we, who are normally working and presenting on our sojourns, have the luxury of the whole day off. As we are to meet a friend later in the evening, we are luxuriating in aimless, wandering, errant time. We are playing the role of the ‘transcendental idiot’. 4T We leave the hotel and wander the busy streets of the Korean capital working up an appetite before lunch. First of all, we take a walk and find an outdoor event near the hotel, the Korean version of ož (mochitsuki) or rice pounding. My Indian colleague joins in and pounds the rice for a minute or two and then devours the free meal afterwards. He is already deep into Korean culture. Now what should we do? After some larking about on the exercise swings under the strict dictates of my Japanese colleague, I take out my phone, search the map app, and find a wonderful-looking park with statues of famous people nearby. I figure, why not go 

*[p. 214]* ^pg229

there? I'm in charge. I am at the helm. I've been here many times before. I am at the helm – go forward, turn left, right, and so on. We walk for 30 min but find we are back where we started. And the park is not there, of course. I insist it is. But I'm wondering why it’s not there; maybe the string of Japanese words I put into the map is somehow wrong. Or maybe my connection is down. We are wandering about hither and thither and again I copy the string of Japanese characters and input the park’s name in the map app and it’s there. Phew! A park with statues of famous people sounds very interesting. We are now walking in a different direction. And then we walk for 15 minutes more. But I'm thinking perhaps there is some problem. I seek counsel. My Japanese colleague discovers my GPS is dependent on a Chinese vector. I double check and my friend points out that Changchun World Sculpture Park is in China, some 500 miles away. Chinese icons are now popping up on my map. I yearn for my Barthes-like bubble to hide away from the mocking of my friends. On reflection, I think this is a good, albeit idiotic example of psychogeography. I think we used a foreign map to negotiate a foreign, unfamiliar space. We went to Korea, but navigated Seoul using a Chinese map, coded in Japanese, Korean, Chinese and English. Absurd and surreal! A situationist, phantasmagorical *dérive*! My Japanese friend calls it psycho-schizo-geography and helpless drift, I had inadvertently performed a digital *détournement*. 

*Dérive* 

A *dérive*, or drift, a walk without aim or goal, is a term from Guy Debord and the French Situationists in 1950s Paris. Debord hoped it was a way to break the spectacle of late capitalism, to enable urban dwellers to experience the reality of their environment and to, in effect, remake and redefine their phenomenological world. The *dérive* allows one to be drawn by the encounters one comes across in the terrain one traverses. The *dérive* was the new practice of the Parisian *flâneur*, that is the solitary walker who saunters through the metropolis, enjoying the spectacle which unfolds and presents itself. There is a rich literary heritage regarding this found in the works of Charles Baudelaire and later in Walter Benjamin’s critical theory (1999) and there is excellent educational research on its application (Cutcher & Irwin, 2018; Manning & Massumi, 2016). Sadie Plant describes the *dérive* 

*[p. 215]* ^pg230

as follows, noting how it held out the promise of breaking through the paths and passages of a predesigned environment: ‘to *dérive* was to notice the way in which certain areas, streets, or buildings resonate with states of mind, inclinations, and desires, and to seek out reasons for movement other than those for which an environment was designed’ (2002, p. 59). To *dérive* is consistent with diversion, a subversion or hijacking, which is to say, to understand that location is always a question of the unexpected, of upsetting and reversing relationships, whether in cities, in the workplace, or in the confrontation with received ideas or dominant images of reality. As the *dérive* is a reversal and transformation of perspective, the psychogeography of the *dérive* is a way to decouple oneself from dominant forms of geography that define contemporary urban life (think about passing through space using Google Maps as your guide, without raising your eyes from the screen). Indeed, the *dérive* is not a question of the hypermediated world but rather more akin to a meditational practice.<sup>48</sup>

Journey of knowledge 

The late French philosopher Bernard Stiegler insists it is necessary to care for youth, to teach young people to take care of themselves, to teach techniques of the self, to teach people to care for the self, that is, to teach them how to bracket off the world, to perform an epoché, in order to create another. For Stiegler, this is to take youth on what he calls a ‘journey of knowledge’, which he pursues through different course software and collaborative applications. In his *Nanjing Lectures* (2020a), Stiegler speaks of the journey of knowledge and hints at the possibility of deviation: 

I try to teach my students not to use Google via just an ‘informational’ approach but instead to practice journeys of knowledge via individuating approaches – and, in so doing, to add to the neganthropic enrichment of the world, to its diversification. 

By individuating myself through a knowledge that, by this very fact, I in turn individuate, I contribute to a diversification that opens up the possibility of a deviation and through it of bifurcations punctuating a difference. (p. 92) 

As we shall see, I am questioning whether the use of technology to mediate the immediate (technical) milieu can be a ‘journey of knowledge’. 

*[p. 216]* ^pg231

My concern is with the digital, transitional object and how to transform it therapeutically and pharmacologically. I am anxious to break our deadly repetitions and this for me would be consistent with the *dérive* and *détournement*. For Stiegler, this demands an understanding of tertiary retentions (memory devices) and how they function in exosomatic (externalized), prosthetic, digital – often toxic – technologies which are transforming modes of inscription and attention in the smart city, or what philosopher Noel Fitzpatrick calls the networked ecologically smart territories (NEST) (see https://www. nestproject.eu). 

Subjective city 

In the 1993 interview Postmodernism and Ethical Abdication with Nicholas Zurbruggm, Félix Guattari says something very prescient about what is happening to our urban abodes, but, alas, his technophilia got the better of him: 

The future which lies ahead is much more likely to be that of Bangladesh, Mexico, or Tokyo, where millions of inhabitants cluster together. In this respect, it is necessary to reinvent the body, to reinvent the mind and to reinvent language. Perhaps the new telematic, informational, and audio-visual technologies can help us to progress in this direction. (Genosko, 1996, p. 155) 

Compare his comments to Vinzenz Hediger who thinks the smart city projects across the planet in terms of ‘digital ghost towns’: ‘The t 21st century megalopolis turns out to be a digital ghost town’ (Das & Pratihar, 2021, p. 238). Indeed, something on the unconscious level has happened to our smart cities which we need to account for. Megacities like Lagos, Karachi, Delhi, Manila and Seoul, says Vinzenz Hediger, challenge ‘modern notions of order and visibility’ (Das & Pratihar, 2021, p. 222) and it is this which we need to think through. In this light, has the reinvention of the body, the mind and language of what Guattari speaks of ever occurred? Has this reinvention ever led to therapeutic practice and a new sensibility regarding the environment? Clearly, I think not. 

1993, the year of Guattari’s article, is also very important for Bernard Stiegler. The timing is very important as it is when the world wide web started to become an everyday technology in homes across the planet. In the 

*[p. 217]* ^pg232

interview Avoid the Apocalypse, Stiegler (2018d) describes the explosion of media which took place after that date: 

What is the Web? It’s an absolutely incredible editorial system, which developed hyper-exponentially, as they say, that is, as soon as, in 1993, the possibility was opened up of creating a website, websites have proliferated. Three months later, there were one million websites, and today there are several billion. 

Yet Guattari’s comments remain prescient as it is forecasted that two-thirds of people in the world will be living in cities by 2050, according to the United Nations (Hoornweg & Pope, 2017). New Delhi, Tokyo, and Seoul will have huge populations as will cities in Mexico, Bangladesh, China, Nigeria and the Philippines. For example, it is forecasted that Delhi will overtake Tokyo as the world’s biggest city by population by the next decade as fewer and fewer people choose to live in the Indian countryside (Welford & Yarbrough, 2021). We also know that 33 megacities are estimated to come, cities with populations of 10 million or more, and by 2030 that figure will increase by 10, with those megacities mostly in developing countries (Razvadauskas, 2019). As a consequence, we must therefore understand the role of the smart city or smart territories not just in advanced industrial nations but across the planet, as by 2050 six billion people will live in urban settings.<sup>49</sup> So here we must take account of the specifics of locality as the reality is quite different in Tokyo, Buenos Aires, Kabul, Delhi and so on. Locality is key if we are to trace the emergence of new forms of subjectivation and understand how desire is part of the emergence of subjectivity. 

Philosophy has to catch up and respond to this reality. The role of the smart city and the understanding of the data economy, Big Data, advanced machine learning and new forms of robotization are of vital importance if environmental and social sustainability is to be maintained and enjoyed. How can Guattari’s three ecologies (2005) of the social, the mental and the environmental be updated to take account of these smart, megacities? What kind of urban spaces can we have that sustain the intelligence of their inhabitants? How can algorithms best serve pedagogical, archaeological, ecological, societal interests? What is to happen to democracy in the megacities of the world and what will this new urbanism be like? 

*[p. 218]* ^pg233

We must understand the smart city then not merely from a technological perspective but as a model for a new political economy and we must appreciate the smart city through a renewed understanding of the concept of locality and ask, as Stiegler does, how we can propose or compose a territory as a ‘laboratory’ for new forms of life and new forms of urban citizenship (Stiegler, 2020c). This is precisely where the philosophies of Guattari and Stiegler connect and combine on the question of reinvention. I am interested in assessing the politics of digital technologies, and the passage from control societies (Deleuze, 1992) to ecological learning territories as this has important ramifications for education. I am concerned with the ‘associated technical milieu’ (Alombert, 2019) as a possible extension of Guattari’s three ecologies, that is, a path toward the fourth ecology of the media (Bradley, 2019; Ueno, 2016). This for me necessitates a schizoanalysis of the smart city. How does control function in the smart city or technical milieu and how can this be *détourned* into therapeutic ecological learning territories? How has the control society of Deleuze been transformed into the automatic society of Stiegler (2018b)? How has the pervasive feeling of disorientation, disruption and shock turned so easily into the common experience of withdrawal? And how can the smart city work to empower its inhabitants to disautomatize 

knowledge and contest generalized forms of proletarianization? Where is the Subjective city? 

Idiocy in the world’s smart cities 

How smart are the smart cities in New Town Kolkata, Amsterdam, Barcelona, Milton Keynes, Masdar City (United Arab Emirates), Neom (Saudi Arabia), Singapore, Songdo, Stockholm, and Toronto? In those cities, we have smartphones, we have smart cities. We are taught to be smart. Everybody is smart in the smart city. We lead smart lives. But what does it mean to be smart, and why is there not more idiocy, more wandering, more idle passing of time, more errant behavior, more wondering, more exploration of affect and sensation in the smart city? How does the transitional object function in transitional spaces (Bradley, 2022b)? To *dérive*, to practice *détournement*, do they mean anything in the smart city? It seems to me that we talk about smartness all the time, but we equally have the proletarianization of knowledge, the loss of skills, the loss of embodied 

*[p. 219]* ^pg234

knowledge or savoir-faire as Stiegler calls it (2016c). Phenomenologically speaking, we do not know what is immediately before us. On the one hand, we have the smart city, smartphones and smart lives but on the other, we have a process of proletarianization and the loss of knowledge. What form of profound stupidity do we suffer in the smart city? And can a form of ‘transcendental idiocy’ be its solution?

Curiously, it seems to me that we promote the smart city at the expense of the mental lives of inhabitants. Now, like in Guattari’s time, there is a mushrooming of new forms of mental pollution at work in the smart cities of the world (Guattari, 2005). Perhaps now it is not so much the inhabitants but travelers who pass through the non-places (non-lieux) of the world, places without history and identity (Augé, 1995), non-places where social life is poor. Are smart cities livable? Are smart cities the new non-places of the world, without history, without memory, without community? And to what extent does the imposition of automated technological systems and standardized ways of life deprive inhabitants of social life? Importantly, what is the impact on the mental life of young people in cities like New Delhi, Tokyo and Seoul? What is the nature of alienation in the smart city? Let us look at Japan for a moment before turning to Seoul to see why for some the Korean capital has replaced Tokyo as the ‘laboratory of the connective neohuman world’ (Berardi, 2015, p. 186).°°

The practice of psychogeography in Japan Recent research in Japan (Mitsuhashi & Gyobu, 2021) helps us to rethink how the detour through urban landscapes might raise critical consciousness of the immediate urban milieu and question the very distinctions of environment and milieu, the natural and the artificial. As the title of their paper suggests — How Did the Young Children Encounter the Japanese Urban Landscape?: A Study on Emergent Pedagogy for Sustainability Transformation — the writers are principally interested in the phenomenological encounter of Japanese young people with the urban landscape and they use concepts from Japanese philosophy (Watsuji Tetsuro Fit PAHS, 1889-1960; see Watsuji, 1935) and indeed French psychoanalysis (Félix Guattari) to ask important questions about sustainability. They question how subjectivities emerge in the embodied

*[p. 220]* ^pg235

minds and imaginations of young people through the very exploration of urban landscapes. Mitsuhashi and Gyobu raise the question of the post-human and the entanglement of the human-nature world and inquire into the nature of agency as new subjectivities emerge. And they apply the assemblage theory (Buchanan, 2020; DeLanda, 2016) and the language of Deleuze and Guattari in the Japanese context to inquire into possible assemblages of human and non-human agency. Their focus is how maneuvering in immediate environments can raise critical questions about sustainability, ethics and ecology, and the looming environmental crisis. This leads them to perform an interesting philosophical experiment, combining Japanese philosophy, the assemblage theory of Deleuze and Guattari and indeed Guattari’s solo work on ecosophy. They follow Guattari in noting the explosion of urban populations and raise questions regarding ways to live and coexist in urban megacities such as Tokyo. They follow his exhortation to understand and explore the new ethico-aesthetic paradigm, a form of chaosmosis, in light of technological transformation (Guattari, 1995). And for them the key question is about the concept of the landscape which derives from Watsuji’s philosophy. They invoke traditional concepts such as satoyama \[illegible\] sato means village and HH- yama means a mountain) and satoumi (ŒE. sato means village and YE- umi means ocean) to explore changing senses and formations of landscape and they appeal to Guattari to understand the crisis in these ‘original existential territories’. 

What we can extract from this interesting examination is the crisis of milieu or locality because the traditional milieu is deterritorialized. The traditional landscape of Japan, the integrity of satoyama and satoumi has become deterritorialized, fragmented and disassembled. To understand the crisis of original existential territory leads them to examine the concept of fūdo (ŒE), that is climate, or what Augustin Berque translates as milieu (Berque, 2004). The concept of milieu so conceived puts into brackets (*epoché*) the concept of nature itself and thus poses the question of whether the environment or the social environment can be said to be natural at all. This for them necessitates a profound rumination on the question of landscape and what subjectivities might emerge in the *changing sense* of landscape. We should note that the concept of landscape connects with the fūdo, the way of the earth. So again, if the milieu has become a technical

*[p. 221]* ^pg236

milieu, this means a crisis of both landscape and the human-nature relationship.

To answer these questions, they turn to the Japanese philosopher Watsuji who contends that fiido is the way human beings find themselves in natural environments. Fido is always relational and it is here that Guattari’s ecosophy and Watsuji’s relational philosophy are ripe for thinking together.

Traditionally conceived, ftido is a question of the relationship between self and natural environment. The question here is: has the perspective of fio disappeared in modern culture? If so, when we think of fido, we should understand the cultural practices that generate milieu and also the arrangement or rearrangement of what milieu is in the contemporary moment. Fudo then is both natural and cultural, collective and individual, subjective and objective, all at the same time. The fundamental question is how to notice the singularity and the human subjectivities generated in this new sense of milieu. Here we can think of the smart city in terms of technical milieu. Can active trajectory (Berque, 2000) be a starting point to reconfigure the landscape? If so, this would be a point where the dérive connects with the ‘hijacking’ of the environment of the smart city and exposes its latent contradictions. This, along with Guattari’s idea of the assemblage and how we produce individual and collective subjectivities, and how we arrange collective assemblages of enunciation. Guattari’s question is a timely one: How does the process of subjectification emerge from heterogeneous assemblages? How can we ensure that subjectivity itself does not lose the taste for difference, the unpredictable, for the singular event? Like Guattari who calls for a reorientation of subjectivity and the ‘mobilization of all the components of the “subjective City”’ (Genosko & Guattari, 2015, p. 113), Stiegler too asks us to imagine the incalculable, the improbable and the unprogrammable in the smart city. The question is therefore about the malleability of the technical milieu and here both Stiegler and Guattari look to a different sense of milieu. It is Guattari’s ecosophy and concept of transversality (Cole & Bradley, 2018) which allows us to both escape the charge of technological determinism (sometimes levelled at Stiegler) and the charge of environmental determinism which is operative in Watsuji’s sense of fudo. This is the site where the interaction between Watsuji and Guattari can be extremely interesting because we see that the landscape can be ‘trajective’ as Berque

*[p. 222]* ^pg237

puts it, ‘ceaselessly regenerating a different milieu upon every immediate experience of a subject’s walking into the landscape, encountering with each detailed component of the landscape assemblage, becoming one part of the landscape assemblage oneself, and critically reflecting the emerging subjectivities toward the landscape’ (Mitsuhashi & Gyobu, 2021, p. 6). Guattari sees that the ecological milieu can be regenerative and can recompose subjectivity precisely because of the machinic focus he gives to the technological milieu as such: 

Beyond the interpersonal relations, there are also the relations with the technological environment. Subjectivity is not only human. It is also machinic. For me, there is no border between the human, society, *tekhnè*, the appropriation of the environment, and the constitution of existential territories (Guattari, 2013, p. 332): 

Either humanity, with the help of the architect and urbanist, will reinvent its urban future, or it will be condemned to perish under the weight of its own immobilization, that today threatens to render it helpless in facing the extraordinary challenges with which history confronts it. (Genosko & Guattari, 2015, p. 115) 

And for Guattari the task is to collaborate *transversely* with philosophers, architects, urban designers, computer engineers to reinvent the future, which in our time means the invention precisely of the smart city. And this is again where Guattari and Stiegler speak to each other, one from the perspective of the Subjective city, the other from a critique of the smart city. 

The practice of psychogeography in Korea 

Across the Sea of Japan/East Sea in South Korea, a recent article in Korea promoted me to rethink my reflections on educational technology. I reflected on my research on the therapeutic, transitional object and concluded I had not appreciated the role of the outside in which the therapeutic object functions. Geographer Kim Minsung’s article (2021) concerns the pedagogical benefits of psychogeography. Kim too raises questions on whether one can *dérive* through and with educational technology. What is psychogeography? For Kim, this concerns our psychological, phenomenological, and corporeal experience of the city, and 

*[p. 223]* ^pg238

how it reveals forgotten, discarded, or marginalized aspects of the urban environment. Kim, who asked 24 pre-service (student) teachers in South Korea to make psychogeographic maps often using mobile apps or photography, is concerned with traversing and drifting through urban space and what creative visualizations we can derive from this dérive practice. Kim remarks, in ways similar to Paul Virilio and Marc Augé, that the space between the start point and end point has become empty and devoid of meaning. If this is so, can there be an authentic dérive when one is always veering off course, caught in collective audio-visual fantasies, as Virilio says, that is, when one is always disoriented in the non-places of the world? How can the dérive function in the smart media milieu that suffers from the ‘desertification of human relations’, as Virilio puts it (Featherstone, 2010)?

As Kim sees psychogeographical practice as a potential remedy for the potential evacuation of meaning, for the loss of the experience of space, what then is this practice of psychogeography in the nonplaces of the world? Psychogeography, Kim claims, is a practice of active urban curation (see also Bradley, 2022c), of creatively reconstructing space in the imagination. For the Situationists this exposes what lies behind the spectacle — that is the spectacle of the inexistent community, the community of those who have nothing in common (Lingis, 1994). Again this is a question of the détournement of the spectacle. Kim claims through the practice of the dérive, we can somehow find the ‘essence of urban space’ (p. 93). And from this, critique is possibilized in the very disruption, détournement and experience of everyday life. The question of the curation of space is important because it suggests the disruption of perception in and through urban trajectory and locomotion. Somehow the dérive can regain the ‘depth of vision’ lost through technology (Virilio, 1997, p. 40). What is of vital importance is to find new meanings in this drifting, wandering, errant behavior and Kim claims that psychogeography has pedagogical potential for effective creative exploration and visualization of urban space (p. 90). What I took from this interesting study of psychogeography in the Korean context was the potential to deploy the dérive as a curative practice in the smart city and indeed to test whether it was possible elsewhere. But Kim’s work raises more questions than it answers. As we shall see, for me, the total hell of the world is to undertake a dérive in a suburban, cybernetic setting like the smart city.

Auto-ethnographic vignette II 

During my time in Seoul, South Korea in October 2022, I spent the month of October wondering about the nature of the cartography of the smart city and I undertook an attempted *dérive* in the smart city of Songdo. But alas, it proved almost impossible to do. My experience of trying to get to the center, if I can call it that, of Songdo, proved truly difficult. From the station I tried to walk the four kilometers to Central Park, which is modelled on New York, but I found out the roads were clogged up and there were no pedestrian sidewalks on the one busy road I took. As a consequence, I had to take the bus. When I got to Central Park, I realized that the park was dominated by hotels and tall buildings, many of which seemed to be owned by foreign capital. They loomed over the park and dominated the surroundings and made wandering and wondering an uncomfortable experience. Everything was familiar and I expected nothing unexpected. This is not unusual I suppose as eyes seldom focus on what is in front of or above them when skyscrapers dominate the horizon, but I found it curious that the architecture surrounding the park with its fashionable and crookedly designed buildings offered up the prospect to my imagination that the buildings were almost ready to fall into the park. Songdo had a truly apocalyptic dystopian feel. I had the feeling that I had better take shelter in case the looming skyscrapers fell upon me. The open sky felt like it was soon to plunge down (Virilio, 1997). My phenomenological experience had an almost apocalyptic feel, like something was going to smash into the spectacle of the smart city at any moment. Passing through Songdo’s ‘disposable architecture, whose material manifestations are banal and constantly mutating’, (Halpern, 2014, p. 6) I found it almost impossible to navigate the city in the manner of a true and open *dérive* and I was left wondering about the perverse rhythm of Songdo and the necessity for a rhythmanalysis (Lefebvre, 2013) of this smart city. It seemed there could be no playful wandering and wayfaring in an ‘elastic and plastic territory, infinitely mobile, networked to the information economy’ (Halpern, 2014, p. 33). Paradoxically then, there is no freedom of passage in ‘flexible mobile territories like Songdo’ (p. 35). I was left wondering about the impossibility of the *dérive* of young people in the urban technopoles of the world. I was left wondering whether the aforementioned research by Kim in Korea (Kim, 

*[p. 225]* ^pg240

2021\) and by Mitsuhashi and Gyobu in Japan (2021) could be reproduced in a smart city like Songdo or in Japan. For me, the imperialism of data science and the algorithm will not do the job of recomposing individual and collective subjectivities and this is why I lean towards Stiegler’s consideration and criticism of the smart city. The imperialism of data science involved in constructing the smart city will not liberate its inhabitants from collapsed forms of social life.<sup>51</sup>

On the cartographical *flâneur* 

What has gone before may appear overly pessimistic, so I shall try and think positively and ask if one can perform the *dérive* when one is led solely by algorithms, when one is given over to information. Of course, all cities are organized, but then equally, only some cities can become disorganized and unmappable and uncontrollable. Again, the question is: can one undertake a *dérive* in the smart city? Wandering and wondering and lingering and being the idiot in the smart city, is that really a possibility? The question returns us to Baudelaire and the *flâneur* and how to think about the arcades in 19th century Paris (Benjamin, 1999, 2006). The *flâneur* is open to new encounters and notices things most people choose not to see or hear (Tester, 1994). One turns left, one turns right, one finds crooks and crannies in the arcades, one finds things unexpected and surprising. But nowadays if the smart city and its open spaces are surveilled, rendered immediate, accessible and visible, then to what extent can one find the unexpected or the surprising?<sup>52</sup> If the smart city is curated by designers and civil servants and the inhabitants are deprived of co-creating aspects of the city, how can one divert and diversify from the preordained engineered landscape one traverses through? 

For technophiles, one way to do this is to mediate the immediate milieu with technological devices. Kim points us to the merits of the *Dérive* app. What is the *Dérive* app? The think-tank Hérétique describes it as follows: ‘*Dérive* app gets you lost in your city and lets you share that experience with others?’ (deriveapp.com). And the creator of *Dérive* app 2.0 describes it as follows: ‘a web-based mobile app that facilitates a randomized meander through an urban environment by prompting the user with tasks that nudge him to experience his direct surroundings unconventionally, heightening the 

*[p. 226]* ^pg241

experience of the city for the user, while essentially playing a game’ (deriveapp.com). But how is attention and indeed memory sustained through the *Dérive* app? Can the *Dérive* app function through hypomnesis, that is, the making-technical of memory, or as a mnemonic device restoring meaning to the history and experience of the smart city? But why should wandering be subject to algorithmic direction? Who is the new *flâneur* in the smart city and can one wander when one is effectively operated by ‘algorithmic governmentality’ (Rouvroy et al., 2013)? How can one wander when everything is set out by others and before one’s eyes and how is the ‘curated *dérive*’ different from the ‘open *dérive*’? How does one comport the body through a *Dérive* app? To answer these questions, I will turn to Stiegler later who offers some sharp criticism of the design of smart cities. 

Disruption 

Disruption is a fact, and it is hardly credible to pretend to avoid it. But the approach envisaged here is to create an ‘alternative disruption’, as a kind of ‘disruption of disruption’. As it is currently used, in the service of business models coming mainly from North America, and sometimes Asia (Japan and South Korea in particular), the current basis of disruption is not sustainable, given that it produces insolvency, incivility and inurbanity. (Stiegler, 2020b, p. 65) 

One criticism of Kim’s paper is that there is no mention of disruptive, toxic digital technologies and the way they interfere with the locomotion of users. Again, what are the disruptive possibilities in open urban localities? Can the act of *dérive* be a function of new urban engineering, where inhabitants ‘become the primary source of territorial intelligence’ as Stiegler argues (2020b, p. 64)? Through the *dérive,* are forms of new territorial intelligence possible? For Stiegler, the question is how to redesign technology from contributory territorial practices themselves. But what does this mean? Here Stiegler is essential reading as he poses many critical questions about the smart city. He asks what and where is the unpredictability of the smart city, if optimization, uniformity, calculability, and homogeneity have become entropic and toxic. In the smart city, for Stiegler, little, it seems, is unforeseeable or incalculable, little is beyond the grasp of the algorithm. The smart city is a ‘living lab’ for an *inexistent* 

*[p. 227]* ^pg242

society (a society of inurbanity and uncivility): 

Everything started to malfunction in the 1970s and today it doesn’t work at all, especially since 1993 when the Web appeared and made the Internet accessible to everyone. Progressively, society has been uberized, or even hacked… This phenomenon can completely explode the social link and when there is no more social link; it is civil war. For 23 years, digitization has spread and we are now beginning to see the consequences. We call it the data economy, the connected society, smart cities, automation or the new robotization. (Stiegler, 2018a, p. 24) 

What is the smart city? 

The smart city can be defined as ‘a techno-utopian quest, whose cultural image always lies just beyond actual developments in informatics for city services’ (McCullough, 2020, p. 26). Others follow this description but from a philosophical perspective which invokes Gilbert Simondon and which sounds close to Stiegler. Ekman writes (2018, p. 2): 

Smart urban planning and ‘smart cities’ might best be approached as future attractors, points of bifurcation in the present, or potential crystalline topoi from which important new city-developments emerge. 

More prosaically, we might define the smart city as an environment where digital technology is used for data collection, network connectivity, and enhanced control. The smart city is a socio-technical milieu designed to organize, collect and analyze data in order to make predictions from that data to better manage and control individual and collective behaviors. In this respect automation is about the control and not the autonomy of inhabitants (Krivý, 2018). Yet critics like Stiegler will say that the word *smart* signifies mere practices of computation and feedback loops, while the word *city* now means little more than a platform to access global infrastructures, data mining and target advertising. Others agree and they return me to the experimental wandering in the smart city of Songdo. Shwayri claims that ‘the planning of Songdo, like the planned modern cities of the twentieth century, has ignored local realities while focusing on creating an exportable model’ (Shwayri, 2013, p. 40). The smart city has become a non-place of nudges, manipulation and enticement, leading to less 

*[p. 228]* ^pg243

and less minding or mindful practice; in other words, less meditating and more mediating. The question is: can we give this behavioral nudging and manipulation a therapeutic and pharmacological sense if the non-place of the smart city has become the automatic society *par excellence* (Stiegler, 2018b). With millions of connected devices from smartphones to wearables, operating through wireless networks, smart environments and smart cities, how can we counter the disruption to not only social relations and political institutions, but also psychic and social individuation? 

A *truly* smart city for Stiegler would be one where the quality of life of its inhabitants and citizens is improved through the promotion of civic and political engagement. As such Stiegler calls for the reinvention of urban knowledge in all its forms, which I take to be an essentially revolutionary task. This would be to counter a growing trend towards inurbanity and uncivility, and the dominance of the psychic drives. Stiegler claims this is where there is a decoupling of the drives from desire and where the drives lead to regression, to the very collapse of desire. And so for Stiegler, through reinvention, through the reticulation of territory, algorithms could function to alert inhabitants to meet and discuss local, political issues. But because at the moment smart or automatic cities are disrupting cities originally founded on citizenship, Stiegler’s question is a timely one: ‘Is it possible to institute a smart citizenship? Under what conditions would such a smart citizenship be possible?’ (Stiegler, 2018b, p. 3). Stiegler contends that as we face a new form of industrial revolution in urban environments like the smart city we must work to cultivate the intelligence of the inhabitants. The truly smart city would be dis-automatizing, that is, it would offer up the function of ‘deliberating’ (2018c, p. 127) – or in my language wondering and wandering and playing the part of the transcendental idiot. The question of deliberation is important because it would ‘generate local forms of knowledge capable of engendering negentropy – to place automatisms at the service of dis-automatization’ (2018c, p. 127). Negentropy then would be a resistance to the entropic determination of information. For me, this aspect of deliberating would be *idiocy*, that is, a counter and paradoxical form of smartness involving drifting, wandering and wondering. This is a question of the right to wander and wonder in the smart city. In this respect, digital contributory technologies should not bypass the decisions of individuals and groups but should allow for more debate and the 

*[p. 229]* ^pg244

consolidation of decisions. For Stiegler, this would challenge and reverse ‘the inurbanity of the automatic city’ (2018c, p. 126). 

Automatic societies bring disruption to social life in the confusion of technical, social and biophysical systems. As such, we have innovation after innovation but without corresponding social and collective individuation. In this respect, for Stiegler, disruption is not sustainable as it produces ‘insolvency, incivility and inurbanity’ (2020b, p. 65). Stiegler cites the experimental Plaine commune in Paris as a counter-movement to this trend. The Plaine Commune was set up in the Greater Paris borough of Seine-Saint-Denis as part of a sort of ‘special trade/economic’ area. The Plaine Commune was envisioned to be a ‘living laboratory’ and ‘contributory learning territory’. With this in mind, Stiegler calls for a radical rethinking of what the smart city is and how it can become more inclusive and contributory. What is the dream of this future digital society? In *Dans la disruption* (2016a), Stiegler calls for another disruption, ‘the great negentropic bifurcation’ (*la grande bifurcation neguentropique*) which would offer us the spectacle of another society. A bifurcation which would disrupt the very disruption of our technologically-mediated world. For Stiegler, it is not enough to allow civil servants to develop, without due consideration, the vision and transformation of the smart city. In other words, we cannot accept the ready-made concepts (*prêt-à-penser*) of what the smart city will be and cannot give decision-making completely over the algorithms or those in power. Simply put and pointing to Korea’s often criticized smart city experiment at Songdo, Stiegler claims that Korea’s smart city project which started in 2001 – supported by foreign investment – struggles to attract urban dwellers because over-automation leaves the inhabitants uncomfortable in this automated environment (much like my experience of Central Park in Songdo): 

\[The civil servants and urban planners\] now realize that it doesn’t work at all, that they have to move on to something else… The players behind these projects are beginning to understand that you cannot imagine and build a smart city without involving the inhabitants very early on and throughout the process, using a radically different method. (Stiegler, 2016a) 

Stiegler argues that Songdo, the largest smart city project, which started in Seoul, has failed. The ‘ready-made model’ of the smart city does not work: 

*[p. 230]* ^pg245

‘\[T\]this ready-made model of the smart city does not work… this is the model that the Koreans promoted, and they now realize that it does not work at all, that they have to move on to something else’ (Stiegler, 2016b). In Stieglerian terms Songdo represents the dystopian nightmare of an algorithmic noosphere or exospheric *Gestell*, equipped with computational technologies of measurement and observation, functioning as a machine to extract data to better calculate and control inhabitants. The smart city is the microcosm of smart capitalism *par excellence*. Halpern argues that Songdo, an imaginary ‘part science fiction, part twentieth-century utopianism’, (2014, p. 31) operates ‘at the synaptic level of its inhabitants, linking the management of life at a global and ecological level to the very modulation of nerves’ (p. 3). Songdo is a futural experiment aimed at ‘re-organizing bodies, down to the synaptic level, and reorienting them into global data clouds or populations with other similarly reorganized nervous systems globally’ (p. 4). Others too agree with this negative assessment of the smart city. Ekman argues: 

As the largely missing cultural and social life in Songdo in South Korea hints, ‘smart’ technical connectivity and a high degree of ‘augmentation’ do not in and of themselves mean the generation of more, or even enough, social or cultural capital. (2018, p. 6) 

Indeed, Ekman adds that smart cities should ‘maintain and develop the quality of the social and individual urban life forms and the experiences of its inhabitants and visitors’ (p. 9). And in *The Neganthropocene* (2018c), Stiegler argues for a new form of government and democracy in the time of algorithmic governance: 

\[W\]e are entering the era of automatic cities – and beyond that of automatic economies, based on the data economy. What place remains in this situation for ‘government’? And if there is such a place, what then would constitute good government? (p. 119) 

Stiegler is challenging us to rethink the becoming mnemotechnical of every aspect of our lives (in the sense that there is an over-reliance on externalized forms of memory which lead to a loss of memory and intelligence in the long run) and as such he urges us to create new forms of therapeutics in the wake of the dominance of smart city technologies. He asks what would 

*[p. 231]* ^pg246

make a smart city ‘truly intelligent’? (p. 119). His question is a good one because it points us to the crisis of ‘digital urbanity’, to the proletarianization of knowledge, the loss of skills in the time of the automatic society, in the time of toxic forms of digital technologies. The problem is the smart city does not automatically produce ‘a new functional intelligence’ (p. 120) that would constitute a new urbanity: 

Let us ask, firstly, what a really intelligent city would be, and even, more generally, what a city has to do with intelligence, and furthermore what intelligence is – this question is indeed necessary, given that we belong to a time when the mean IQ is known to be decreasing, as indeed is life expectancy, and given that it is an epoch in which, more generally, functional stupidity seems to have become inherent to organizations, of which cities are of course cases. (p. 119) 

The truly intelligent, the ‘really smart’ city must ‘seize hold of’ automated processes to undertake ‘territorial design’ (p. 122). Following Simondon, the participatory aspect of this ‘territorial design’ would realign psychic and collective individuation *via* technical individuation (p. 122), according to Stiegler, and this involves a territorial reticulation ‘capable of reconstructing a territorial dynamic that produces a sense of place’ (p. 122). Stiegler defines the ‘truly smart city’ as one which is ‘urban, habitable, desirable and attractive’. This remains a possibility, he says, but ‘only if we reinvent automatic society, and reinvent it in every town and every city, so that every city becomes a laboratory for the production of a truly social automatic society – rather than allowing it to produce a dissociety, an automatic dissociation and disintegration that could only pave the way for a social explosion’ (p. 126). 

A harsh critic 

A young man takes me to task for my perceived Luddite attitude towards smartphone technology, the smart city and the deleterious role of the internet in our lives. He says that the internet and algorithms are not the root cause of all social ills as I seem to imply. He says that the internet and algorithms can work in amazing ways, for example, to spread health and safety information through TikTok, to guide people to healthcare centers and new places to meet. He says this is available to both young and old and 

*[p. 232]* ^pg247

adds this is therapeutics in action. He says this can happen in the smart city and it is not enough just to blame the internet or social media, the impersonal nature of algorithms, the imperialism of data science, or the designers of the smart city for the ills and crises of societies across the planet. He bates me: Why not be nudged, manipulated or enticed to meet and belong? He says that there are ways to think differently, to embody thinking and to promote non-hierarchical structures. He tells me that even gaming and gamification have their merits and I should not be so dismissive of their potential pedagogical impact. 

He carries on in his lambast. My attitude towards young people simply doesn’t take into account the thoughts and feelings of young people themselves (I take great issue with this and tell him he is being rather rude and ungenerous). I tell him about Stiegler’s attempt, some say failed attempt, to promote a contributory economy, the contributory income, and contributory knowledge at the Plain Commune in St Denis in North Paris. I tell him that Stiegler offers the concept of the ‘journey of knowledge’, that is, a way to resist giving young people completely over to the internet, to algorithms and social media. I tell him that Stiegler sees the role of the teacher as essential in the transmission of knowledge. I tell him that this position of Stiegler is at odds with Michel Serres who appears quite happy in *Thumbelina* (2014) to have young people be completely given over to information. My harsh critic tells me my attitude towards technology doesn’t take into account the diversity of online culture. I simply misunderstand this new reality. He says that global and social issues and the problems of smart cities are not purely technological problems and again, rather rudely, says that there is a generation gap at work in my thinking. He says that my suggestion that inhabitants in the smart society are overburdened, exhausted and suffering from new forms of mental illness and loneliness is ahistorical. He suggests societies have always been like this. He fires back at me: What is the pedagogical response to it? Provide some real concrete solutions. Exasperated, I say this is now my task going forward. 

The pedagogical effects of schizo-cartographic exploration It is not a matter of dreaming of a return to the walled medieval cities, but to move, on the contrary, in the direction of a supplementary deterritorialization, 

*[p. 233]* ^pg248

shifting the city toward new universes of value, to confer on it as a fundamental goal a production of a non-segregated and resingularized subjectivity, that is to say, ultimately, liberated from the hegemony of a capitalistic valorization solely based on profit. (Genosko & Guattari, 2015, p. 102) 

I follow my harsh critic’s advice and ask how should we confront the (im)possibility of the revolution of desire in the smart city? How might we use Pokémon Go (Bradley, 2019), live streaming (Bradley, 2017), the *Dérive* app or automated cars to take us on a ‘journey of knowledge’ in the smart city? How can we use them to promote education and health initiatives? As my concern is with the differing and diverging use of technologies, I ask: how might we navigate the smart city in unpredictable ways, using apps like Pokémon Go, the Dérive app, to take young people on a ‘journey of knowledge’? Perhaps this might be in effect a revolutionary kind of nudging of desire (Bown, 2015, 2022). This might mean the digitization of natural desires led by algorithms but for educational purposes. The fundamental question is about the capturing of desire, the harnessing of attention and the creation of non-toxic addictions. 

On further reflection, I take the charges from my harsh critic on board but I hold steadfast to my views. We should continue to think about psychogeography, about wander lines (*lignes d‘erre*) (Deligny, 2016) and the *dérive*. We should hold fast to the ideas of Paul Klee, that is, of taking a line for a walk, of thinking active lines moving at liberty, somehow going on a stroll without destination and through this we should inquire into how to take students on a journey of knowledge. The optimist in me says we should trace these lines of exploration and wandering, not as an archaeologist or historian but rather as a geographer or psychogeographer. Why? Because we must survey the present, to draw kinetic territories and moving maps. Not to diagnose or prescribe but to explore and speculate about the components of repetitions which can potentially pass or cross a deadly repetitive refrain. This is to draw lines of flight, to chart the emergence of new existential possibilities and to explore the potential opening of new existential horizons. But the pessimist in me also says that in the smart city there is a lingering question about whether one can effectively undertake the *dérive*. That is one of the problems I have with both Mitsuhashi and Gyobu’s landscape experiment in Japan and Kim’s 

*[p. 234]* ^pg249

psychogeographical project in Korea. Moreover, how can gamification, neuroscience, and the use of unanticipated rewards, that is combining technology with learning goals, *truly activate desire*? For Stiegler, the answer would be a kind of therapeutics of pharmacological practice, a way to develop new forms of subjectivity. For Stiegler, we need new kinds of addictions, not addictions to technology as such, but addictions to and curiosity with the local environment, that is, to learning, to wandering and wondering, to thinking as such, to social life as such. But again, what are the dangers of the outsourcing of attention or the outsourcing of desire in the smart city? It seems to me that we are in a grave state at present and we have to find ways to stimulate young people to find modes of life other than the virtual realm. At our most desperate, maybe it has to be through the mediated organization of reality, the repurposing of technology, but using it pharmacologically, indeed, *in extremis,* outsourcing a portion of our desire to technology, because ultimately our concern is about capturing the attention of youth. 

As we are slowly emerging out of the coronavirus pandemic, the task is to find ways for young people to explore the world outside their homes and their bedrooms. One way might be for students to explore their local milieu, their local place and space through their bodies, through photography and video technology and to map or diagram it. One other way to do this might be to think about psychogeography, through the activity of *dérive* or drifting. Here my intention has been to tie together some thoughts on critical postmedia, ecosophy, desire, and the unconscious with an understanding of embodied phenomenological drifting. My lingering problem is with the *open dérive* and its (im)possibility vis-[illegible]-vis digital technologies. It seems difficult to imagine how it is possible to guide users, to allow users to wander freely and to connect with their local milieu, within the confines of the smart city. With that caveat added, through drifting and *dérive*, through spatial exploration and visualization, I think there is much to learn from psychogeography. But I insist that this might also be done through forms of mnemotechnics, through recording video screens, snapping photographs or recording sounds, or using GPS to record affective responses to phatic, socially constructed mnemonic technologies and the environment in which they are immersed. 

*[p. 235]* ^pg250

I think it is fruitful to explore the passage through often unknown invisible urban spaces and to grasp what affects are manifest in due course. In this play of dislocation-relocation, technology can be used therapeutically, pharmacologically and curatively in the language of Stiegler. And again here Stiegler and Guattari find common ground as ecosophy promotes the logic of the flux as a creative wayfaring. What would constitute a true *détournement* of the smart city? For me, what is of interest in any possible *détournement* is the ‘rerouting, hijacking’ or the disruption of perception through an errant wayfaring of the urban trajectory. And what is important is the disorientation of users and the affective response to their local environment, their phenomenological experience of the smart city. At our most affirmative we can say that the smart city can become a territory of hyper-learning, a territory independent of actual persons where knowledge is housed not in only in vast databases and archives but in the materiality of the local milieu as such (Stiegler, 2014, p. 26). But we should understand this on the condition that it is an ecological relational space. As such we must develop a critique of automatic reason, and the stupidity of digitalization. A new urban intelligence, a new transcendental idiocy is necessary to contest the endemic stupidity which pervades the smart city. In the smart city, and while both Guattari’s ethico-aesthetic political philosophy and Stiegler’s desperate search for the unexpected beyond the horizon of expectation<sup>53</sup> seem difficult to imagine and realize given the ubiquity of algorithmic control and algorithmic governance, the construction of new resingularized subjectivities is precisely what is at stake in our crushingly desperate and disrupted times. We must simply reimagine the Subjective city beyond the crushing organization and control of the smart city.<sup>54</sup>
