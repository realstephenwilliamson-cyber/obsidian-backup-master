## About

Theodor W. Adorno's (1903–1969) critique of Western philosophy's habit of making concepts identical with their objects. It turns Hegel's dialectic into a "negative" one that refuses a final synthesis.

- **1966:** published as *Negative Dialektik* by Suhrkamp, Frankfurt am Main.
- **English:** translated by E. B. Ashton (New York: Seabury Press, 1973); Wikipedia cites a 1990 Routledge edition and links an updated translation with commentary by Dennis Redmond.
- **Structure (SEP):** a long Introduction on "philosophical experience"; Part One, set against Heidegger's fundamental ontology; Part Two, reworking categories inherited from German idealism; Part Three, nearly half the book, three "models": freedom (Kant), world spirit and natural history (Hegel), and meditations on metaphysics.
- **Main ideas:** thought imposes identity on its objects and suppresses what is particular in them; Adorno calls dialectics the consistent consciousness of nonidentity. He argues for the priority of the object, arranges concepts in "constellations," and writes that the need to let suffering speak is a condition of all truth. The final model turns to Auschwitz, which Wikipedia says Adorno treats as a failure of Enlightenment rationality; there he states a new categorical imperative, imposed by Hitler, that Auschwitz not repeat itself.
- **Place in his work:** the SEP calls it his magnum opus and the fullest statement of his metaphysics and epistemology. His book on Hegel (1963) preceded it; *Aesthetic Theory* followed posthumously in 1970.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia names its influence on Holocaust historians (Zygmunt Bauman, Yehuda Bauer, Saul Friedländer) and on writers such as Joel Kovel, John Holloway and John Zerzan.
