We leave aside Quine's talk of "sensory receptors" and the physiological processes. We begin with what, independently of physics and physiology, one needs to begin with: "a prior tendency on the child's part to weigh qualitivative differences unequally" 14 so that some differences *must* count for more than others even at the prelinguistic level, if the child is to learn at all. This similarity is "perceptual similarity," which has, "for all its subjectivity, a degree of objective validity" 15 inasmuch as these similarity standards are intersubjective. (Quine's *explanation* of how this intersubjectivity is achieved is evolutionary - biological; as is reflected by his talk of "receptors" and the like, but we need not concern ourselves with this *explanation.* Our concern is rather with the description.) The other basic concepts (each with a strong descriptive "backing," and each "detachable" from physiology and the like) that Quine introduces are "trace," "salience," and "pleasure." (Each has a behavioral manifestation, and behind each one may *posit* a hypothetical mechanism, which again we pass by.) Traces are what relate a present perceptual datum to past ones: the trace of a perceptual experience preserves information adequate to show perceptual similarity between this experience and later ones. <sup>16</sup>Salience is the correlate of noticing: the perceptual field is "a field of gradations" rather than "just one or several clean-cut stimuli." 17 "Trace" and "salience" are connected inasmuch as present salience is affected by past experience. All this has a perfectly descriptive sense, even if, in Quine's view, "This is all meant to be, in the end, a matter of physiological mechanisms, manifested in behavior.' .18 Perceptual experiences are pleasant or unpleasant in varying degrees, and the trace of an experience preserves its pleasure or discomfort. There is a drive in the subject to increase the similarity with past pleasant ones. 

Given these basic concepts of perceptual similarity with a degree of intersubjectivity, trace, salience, and pleasure, Quine can move on to the learning of observation sentences such as "Red" and "Mama." Pointing heightens salience. The mechanisms of assent and dissent ("yes" and "no") are learned with the help of perceptual similarity and the pleasure principle. But learning single-word sentences such as "Red" and "Mama" is not yet learning to *objectify.* We have already mentioned that, for Quine, merely to acknowledge the presence of red

For the infant, "Mama," "red," and "water" are of the same type; they are not terms for things, but each stands for" a history of sporadic encounter, a scattered portion of what goes on." 19 Their use is "preindividuative," they are all mass terms at this point. It is with individuating terms that the process of objectification begins. Learning to objectify, then, is, *at first,* learning to individuate, and to pick up the scheme of enduring and recurrent physical objects. At the purely linguistic level this involves learning to use the plural form and also such forms as "that apple," "an apple," "some apples," "another apple," "those apples," as well as the "is" of identity as in, "This is the same apple as the one seen yesterday." When "Mama" is separated from "apple" and "water," "Mama" becomes the singular term *par excellence.* Mama is spatio-temporally visibly continuous (though interrupted by casual and partial eclipses); her identity is a matter of shape. "Red," "water" and "sugar" stand for discontinuous occurrences. Their shapes are amorphous; Mama is a body. 

It is not surprising then that, since objectivity relies so heavily on individuation and the individual term *par excellence* is "Mama," "Bodies are the charter members of our ontology, let the subsequent elections and expulsions proceed as they may. ,,20 They are the objects *par excellence.* Physical science then replaces this notion of body by "the more inclusive, more recondite, and more precise notion of *physical object.* ,,21 

Perhaps the most important step in this process of objectification - to the final goal of quantification - is learning the distinction between singular and general terms, between "Mama" and "dog." Learning to use "dog" is learning to recognise a second-order similarity, i.e., similarity of similarities. (Each dog is a body and so exhibits first-order similarity based on continuity of shape, displacement, etc.) Furthermore, learning "dog" is not learning mere presence, it is to learn its individuative force, in other words, what to count as one dog and what as another. Like "red" and "water," there are simultaneous scattered occurrences to which "dog" applies; like "Mama," each dog is one gestalt, a body. More complex observation terms are attributive compounds, such as "yellow paper," or such compounds as "Mama-in-the-garden," each of which may be construed also as an observation sentence ("Yellow paper is there"; "Mama is in the garden").

The child is now on the road to learning predication: occasion sentences by learning (through query, assent, and dissent) the circumstances in which to assent to or dissent from the sentences; standing and eternal sentences by that and a transfer of conditioning from the non-verbal to the associated verbal stimulus in terms of traces and salience. From specific sentences he learns by generalisation the mode of composition. But at this stage the similarity he senses between "The dog is an animal" and "Show is white" is "a tendency in the sound of the first term to dispose him to assent to the second. ,,22 

Truth functions are next in line. Negation of occasion sentences is learned through query and dissent; negation of standing sentences, through negative conditioning. Conjunction is learned by learning the circumstances in which it commands assent and the circumstances in which it commands dissent, and similarly for alternation. One of Quine's interesting ideas in this connection is to "found" the truth functions on a more primitive relationship, which he calls the "verdict function.,,23 Verdict logic is three-valued; the three verdicts are assent, dissent, and abstention. Verdict functions can be learned by induction from observation of behavior. They are prior to our two-valued logic. Compared to them, truth values are higher-order theoretical achievements. 

With this apparatus at his disposal the language learner is not very far from being able to refer in the strictly Quinean sense. He needs to learn the relative clause (by which general terms may be constructed from sentences by substitutional transformation, as in, "I bought Fido from a man that found him" --+ "Fido is such that I bought him from a man that found him," in general: I *Fa* l --+ I *a* is a thing *x* such that *Fx* l ), the *such that* construction, and its variable (which is the most primitive form of the variable, a regimentation of the relative pronoun). When the relative clause is conjoined to the categorical ("Everything *x* such that *Fx* is G," etc.), our language learner has learned to refer; the variable has become objectual. 

I think that in this account reference matures in three stages: first, with the separation of "Mama" from "red" and "water," i.e., with the appearance of individuative terms; second, with the universal categorical, "A dog is an animal" (Quine calls this the earliest phase of reference worthy of the name), and, finally, in the relative clause inserted into the universal categorical whereby the variable "such that" turns objectual. Several features of this account need to be noted at this stage: First, for Quine, bodies are objects *par excellence.* They are the sorts of objects which are spatio-temporally and visibily continuous and which retain an identity of shape in spite of deformation and displacement. When he speaks of man being "a body-minded animal, among body-minded animals," apparently he is talking about all human beings and not merely those who share "our" object-positing pattern. Second, using an idealistic jargon but of a scientistic kind. Quine speaks of *positing* objects. Further, to posit one object rather than another (to choose "rabbit" as the referent of "gavagai" rather than something else) is to impose our "provincial" pattern, rather than settling what the fact of the matter is, since ultimately there is no fact here. In this, I sense, as in an earlier context, a missing link in the argument: it may be that where we would posit rabbit, the native would posit rabbithood or whatever else. But this "inscrutability" of reference or "indeterminacy of radical translation" does not imply that the "object-positing" pattern itself is parochial. *Our* object positing, i.e., *what* we posit, may be parochial, and yet, in an important sense, to posit some object or other may be *an* essential feature of man's relationship to his world. 

This leads me back *again* to the alleged "linkage" of fully developed objective reference to the quantifier. Earlier I pointed out what seems to be its difference from the Kantian thesis about objective reference and insisted on its compatibility with both the Brentano and the Kantian positions. I now want to return to that theme in order further to explore the peculiar character of Quine's thesis. One way of doing that is to ask what it is about the quantificational apparatus that captures objective reference at its best, as contrasted with the earlier stages, viz., the individuative term (or single-word sentence) and the universal categorical. 

I find in Quine's writings several plausible answers to such a query: 1. The mere word "red," when uttered even in the presence of red, may be fulfilling one of several different functions: it may be naming a colour, a patch, a body, or an episode, or it may be an occasion sentence. There is no way of settling what the word refers to. 

2\. "Red" may be functioning as a general term true of many objects (as in, "This is red"), or it may be a singular terms naming a single abstract entity (as in, "Red is a colour"). Ostensively, the abstract singular terms and the concrete general are indistinguishable.

3\. It is meaningless to ask absolutely what the word refers to; one can only ask this relative to a background language. It is not clear why these difficulties would not arise in the case of "(3x) (x is red.)" The inscrutability of reference and the indeterminacy of translation vitiate as much the distinction between singular and general as they do the apparatus of quantification. Both are, on Quine's view, parochial, in fact objective reference itself is parochial. 

These three then are not satisfying as answers to the question what it is about quantification that captures objective reference at its best as contrasted with individuating terms and universal categoricals. 

Another line of answer is suggested by Gilbert Harman. <sup>25</sup>"Mama," "Fido," "Red," according to Harman, as uttered by the child, refer not to objects but to occasions. So also with sentences such as, "This is a dog," where "a dog" is a predicate of certain occasions but not yet of objects. The same is true even of identity statements such as, "This is the same dog as this": here, for Harman, "there is no reason to suppose that the 'this's refer to dogs rather than to presentations or occasions." Harman wants to say that the truth conditions for these sentences can be purely phenomenalistic. With the quantified sentence it is otherwise. I am not convinced that this account for the difference would do. If "occasions" are presentations, then neither "Fido" nor "This is a dog," refer to presentations. Neither the language-learning child nor the lay adult not using the quantificational form refers to presentations. The former may be referring to Mama as a discontinuous series of occurrences and using "Mama" as a mass term. But each such occurrence is not a presentation. Besides, we are talking about a stage where "Mama" has been separated from mass terms, where the language learner has learned to use singular terms. Even if the single word sentences admit of phenomenalistic truth conditions, the sentence, "This is a dog," does not. The "this" refers not to a presentation but to what is presented; it serves to identify for the auditor what the speaker is talking about. Even if Quine is right that this identificatory function lapses under regimentation, the reference, independently of that regimentation, is already objective, not phenomenalistic. 

I will mention one more unhelpful answer before suggesting what appears to me to be the real gain with the introduction of the quantificational construction. According to it, in the transparent sense of "reference" which Quine accepts, reference must be to an existent. Add to this Quine'S thesis that the existential quantifier encapsulates the most

general meaning of "existence." It is then only natural that the same existential quantifier will encapsulate the function of objective reference. This answer is certainly consistent with what Quine has said but does not help us understand why it is that the prequantificational stages of language learning do not confer on the learner the ability to refer in the strict sense to something that exists. Even if this answer is unhelpful, it nevertheless suggests the direction in which we should look. One may distinguish between referring to an entity (that exists) and referring to an entity *as* an existent. The transparent sense does not attain full maturity, unless reference is made to an entity *qua* existing, in other words, unless the fact that the entity being referred to exists is not merely "presupposed," not merely "implied," but explicitly "shown" in the very form of the referential expression. Perhaps "Red," suitably interpreted, does refer to an existent colour. "This is a dog," if true, does refer to a dog. But none of these expressions contains a part which shows that the reference exists. "There is an *x,* such that" does precisely this. It fully explicates the sense of transparent reference. Reference is here not only to what is but to it *qua* existing. In the jargon of idealistic metaphysics objective reference is here fully self-conscious. 

Two other related considerations follow from what has just been said. In the first place, by paraphrasing the name as a predicate and making the variable of quantification the vehicle of reference, the object referred to is *detached* from its name. What is is not a dog or red but something, an *x,* which is such that it is a dog or red. This is only to be expected: referential or objectual quantification needs nameless objects, or at least that the name and the named be separable. Even if the object has a name, it *could have been* nameless. The use of the variable makes this explicit. Second, the referential function of the quantifier also brings out a connection between Quine'S thesis that objective reference best takes place through the apparatus of quantification and his later thesis about the inscrutability of reference. The first made its appearance much earlier than, and independently of, the latter. Now we begin to see that the latter was implicit in'the former. If the reference is not to a rabbit but to an x such that it is a rabbit, it also becomes evident that many alternate descriptions may be true of this x. Of this x itself we can say very little, if anything at all. It is not a rabbit, for "rabbit" as a name has been paraphrased into the predicate "is a rabbit." The *x* admits of different true predications. Add to this the thesis about reference to a background language, and we have got rid of the last vestige of the fact of the matter. Quine's theses cohere well enough.

IV. PIAGET 

In this section I propose to draw attention to only those thoughts of Piaget which bear on the child's construction of "object." The important point is that, for Piaget, acquisition of speech needs an already constituted substructure. If speech were a system of conditioning and conditioned reflexes, an infant could acquire speech as early as the end of the first month, when the first conditioned reflexes already exist. "A long practice of pure action is needed to construct the substructure of later speech. ,,[^26] A notable feature, therefore, of Piaget's theory is the important role assigned to what is called the sensori motor stage, during which the child acquires its most rudimentary sense of "objectivity" but *independently of language.* What is for us the outer world is, to begin with, for the infant, a series of moving pictures that appear and disappear, without substantiality, permanence and location. [^27]There is no one objective space but "a series of spaces differing one from another and all centered on the body proper. ,,[^28] The visual, tactual, and auditory spaces, all centred on the body, lack coordination. They are called by Piaget "ego-centric spaces," not only not coordinated but also not including the body itself as an element in a container. [^29]During the first six to eight months of an infant's life the world provides only sensory pictures, open to perpetual destruction and rematerialisation. The only identity objects have for him concerns a certain permanence. "The aim of sensori motor intelligence is practical success, not truth. ,,[^30] 

Central to understanding Piaget at this point is his notion of *schema.* Though to begin with schemata are characterised by the behavior sequences to which they refer, it would be wrong to identify a schema with its associated behavior sequence. Rather, a schema is a cognitive structure, generated by the assimilatory functioning. Objects are assimilated *to* something. That to which an object is assimilated is a schema of action. Returning now to Quine, I should say at the outset that it would be wrong to accuse Quine of being insensitive to the many other aspects of the situation of language learning than those portrayed by him. It is obvious that he is concerned to emphasise only one important aspect of it. If Piaget's thesis is valid, then this much of mechanism is too simple for language learning. v. HUSSERL 

Husserl's *Experience and Judgment* contains investigations into the genealogy of logic, and since Husserl shares the view that objectivity in the strong sense is constituted by formal-logical operations, these investigations may be regarded as being about the genesis of objectivity.[^40] The genesis which Husserl portrays is less clearly psychological than Quine'S; its relation to the child's acquisition of language and construction of reality is difficult to ascertain. It is closer to a "rational reconstruction" of the formal logical concepts. Husserl would characterise the genesis as "transcendental phenomenological," meaning thereby that the entire account presupposes the *epoche* by which all reference to the *constituted* world of things and persons is suspended, so that the rational reconstruction has to fall back upon the evidences within the meditating ego's own life of consciousness. Whatever the plausibility or validity of such a genetic enquiry, the present encounter with Quine requires from us that we as much "put within brackets" Husserl's transcendental-phenomenological point of view as we already have the behaviorism of Quine.

Predicative judgment brings about a new level of objectification, the correlative of which is an active synthesis of the elements (S and *p)* which earlier - at the level of receptive explication - were apprehended in a mode of passive synthesis of coincidence. Whereas *p* originally emerged as an enrichment of sense of S, a new cognitive interest returns to S, identifies it as being "again" there as S, and actively brings about the transition to the new sense *p* accruing to it. As a result of this activity and the cognitive interest motivating it, the object substrate of receptive explication becomes the subject, and the determination *p* becomes its predicate. In receptive explication, the S is clarified, explicated as being p, but the "being-determined-as" is not apprehended. In predicative judgment, this being-determined-as is a component of the constituted objectivity. New forms are thematised: the subject form, the predicate form, and their unity in the proposition form. I 

There are two conflicting motives in Husserlian phenomenology, one of which leads, in my view, to a more genuinely transcendental philosophy. According to one of its original programmes, phenomenology was to be a *descriptive science oj essences* and essential structures of various regions of phenomena and also of the empty region of object in general. The concern with *meanings,* as contradistinguished from essences, is equally original; it pervades the *Prolegomena* and the first three of the Logical Investigations and, of course, the first volume of the *Ideas.* But the specifically phenomenological enterprise of *clarification oj meanings* - and this is the second of the two motives - slowly moves to the forefront, for a while overshadowed by the essentialism of the beginning, but later on freed from it and reasserting its primacy as the philosophical activity *par excellence.* The concern with essences affiliates phenomenology to the classical rationalistic tradition, while the concern with meanings brings it closer to the empiricistic tradition. If in the former enterprise phenomenology appears to be an essentialism of Aristotelian sort, in the latter, Hume remains its acknowledged precursor. One of my contentions, which I will try to substantiate in the following pages, is that the peculiarly Husserlian sort of transcendental phenomenology develops out of the second concern rather than from the first. This rather broad claim needs to be qualified in various ways, which I will be doing as we proceed. For the present, I may perhaps add

II 

If philosophy is to be a first science, radical in its attempt to go back to the first principles of all knowledge and in that sense free from presuppositions, it seems at the outset as though it has to be an eidetic discipline. Only an eidetic discipline could be the foundation of the empirical sciences. But unlike eidetic disciplines such as pure logic and mathematics, philosophy, as the foundational science, has to be morphological and not nomological, descriptive and not deductive. Such a descriptive science of essences should be, if it is to be grounded in evidence, based on intuition in the sense that propositions belonging to it would have to be backed up by intuitive evidence, by such evidence as is appropriate in each case. How and in what sense such intuitive evidence is to be achieved was one of Husserl's principal methodological concerns. In brief, such evidence had to be gotten by the use of appropriate examples, imaginative variation, exercise of free phantasy, and a synthesis of identification culminating in a judgment of the form, "It is so and cannot be otherwise." The purpose of imaginative variation through free phantasy is to isolate what is contingent from what is necessary. All these substantive as well as methodological theses are full of problems. Assuming that there are essences which one can intuitively apprehend, why should such apprehension of essences be any more presuppositionless than perceptual apprehension of empirical particulars and facts? Is it not rather the case that whereas perceptual apprehension of empirical particulars and facts does *not* presuppose either the actuality or the awareness of essences, the apprehension of essences does presuppose awareness of empirical facts - imaginative, if not perceptual - as its starting point? 1 Why then the claim to presuppositionlessness on behalf of the supposed eidetic science? Without stopping to discuss these and other related issues, let me briefly formulate two likely Husserlian responses: 

First, there is a *sort of* contingency about empirical facts and empirical sciences, which demands that we found their possibility in some sort of necessity. 

Secondly, neither empirical facts nor empirical sciences are intelligible by themselves; the principles of their intelligibility lie outside of themselves, so that it is precisely the task of eidetic phenomenology to recover and formulate those principles which are none other than essences and essential structures.

There is a certain justification for the notion of essential necessity. But whether this necessity is such that it can be the basis for a presuppositionless philosophy is quite another question. In order to appreciate this last point, let us take a quick look at the concept of "essential necessity." In the first place, we have to bear in mind that the idea of "empirical contingency" and the idea of "essential necessity" are correlatives and are parasitical on each other. They define each other. At the same time, we also need to recall that there is no one concept of necessity; there are in fact many, of which "essential necessity" is one. I do think that it is a valid concept, consistent and having application. It cannot be subsumed under the concept of "logical necessity." Let me take what is perhaps the most well-known instance of essential necessity in phenomenological literature, that consciousness is always *of* something. One may want to deny its essential necessity either by showing that it simply is not true, or by showing that its truth is analytic, a consequence of how we define "consciousness." If you define "consciousness" as that which refers to an object (in a certain specifiable manner and sense), logically necessary truths may be deduced from such a definition (e.g., that nothing which does not so refer to an object could be a state of consciousness). Now, assuming that the proposition' 'Consciousness is always of an object" is true, we cannot regard it as a case of logical truth. It is either an empirical truth, or a definition and so neither true nor false, or a necessary truth and not being a logical truth, essentially necessary. For Brentano and Husserl, this proposition is not an empirical generalisation but a conceptual truth, it defines the mental but is not a nominal but a real definition. Let us now turn to the claim of essences and essential structures to be the principles of intelligibility. If the first response of the Husserlian was to found the contingency of empirical facts and sciences upon the necessity of essences and eidetic disciplines and so in the long run on phenomenology as a science of essences, the second response is to trace the intelligibility of the empirical sciences back to eidetic principles to be formulated and validated by phenomenology. III 

The foregoing section gave not an exposition or an exegesis of Husserl, but a series of reflections on *any,* especially Husserlian, essentialism. These reflections have brought us to a point from where we can see the limitations of any essentialism. We are thus led from the concept of essence to that of *meaning.* Phenomenology is now no longer a morphological *science* of essences, but a radical attempt to clarify meanings, to trace higher order meaning-formations to more basic layers of meanings and to the appropriate acts constituting such formations. Whereas the pursuit of essences is guided by the ideal of apodictic truth, the activity of clarification of meanings is guided by the goal of returning to "originary" experience. This is the sort of philosophical enterprise that more and more brought the concepts of life-world and transcendental subjectivity to the forefront in Husserl's thought. My comments on this strand of Husserlian thinking shall consist, not in a connected exposition, but as before in a series of disconnected reflections intended to throw some light here and there. 

First, what does Husserlian phenomenology mean by clarification of meanings? What sort of philosophical enterprise is it? It seems that there are two components in it: first, the fixation of a concept by the method of imaginative variation and, secondly, a return to the "originary experience" in which the given meanings are constituted. The first both delimits a concept by looking for the limits of its possible application and helps the transition from an empty symbolic understanding to an intuitive, filled understanding. What I am suggesting is that the famed Husserlian method of eidetic variation may be interpreted as a method of fixation and intuitive illustration of concepts rather than as a method for discovering essences. On this interpretation, it is possible to avoid the charge of circularity that is often brought against the method. We do not pass from utter ignorance to a new discovery. We rather move from a nascent and empty understanding to an explicit, 

*[p. 203]* ^pg230

methodologically determined and intuitively filled understanding. But even this illustrative understanding is not enough. Unlike an essence, a meaning does not exist *per se.* It is not an entity, an item in the world. It is not even a real component of the mental life of the person who intends it. Husserl characterises a meaning as an ideality and also as the intentional correlate of an intentional act. Clarification of meaning therefore requires us to return to those acts in which and in which alone such a meaning could be intended. 

The transformation of "essences" to meanings, or rather the transition from the one to the other, is a move that is analogous to Quine's "semantic ascent." This is how Quine characterises his "semantic ascent": 

It is the shift from talk of miles to talk of "mile." It is what leads from the material mode to the formal mode ... it is the shift from talking in certain terms to talking about them. II 

First order object language is onto logically oriented, it is about the world. By "semantic ascent" we use a meta-language to talk about our object language. NaIve ontological commitments are then seen in their true nature, i.e., as *commitments.* In Husserl's language, the ascent (let us call it "phenomenological ascent") is from ontological affirmations - via reduction and *epoche* - to the recognition that what were ontological predicates are in fact "meanings," and then from meanings to the acts in which those meanings are constituted. In the pre-phenomenologicallanguage, we speak of a thing as being material or spiritual, a tool or a work of art. The phenomenological ascent transforms these predicates to meanings or noemata and then seeks to clarify them by returning to the acts in which they are intuitively verified. 

The philosophers, who follow the "later" Wittgenstein want to clarify meanings by focusing on how we use the appropriate words, i.e., by locating the appropriate words within relevant language games. On such a view, to lay bare how a word is used is not by any means, on the part of users of the language, to introspect into their own minds, but it is to observe a language game as a publicly observable social phenomenon. Hence Austin's distinction between beliefs of ordinary men and what ordinary language means. Once the relevant language game has been exhibited, the implicit rules made explicit, the permissible and the not permissible moves distinguished, we would have done all that needs

Although Husserl would not have agreed that the nature of linguistic activity is correctly captured by the metaphor of playing a game, he nevertheless does assign, along with the Wittgensteinians, a priority to speech - to the speech act, the *ausdruckende Akte* - over language. The problem of clarifying meaning, also for him, consists - at least in its initial phase - in a return to the speech act. But speech act is not merely a rule-governed behavior of a certain sort. It is an intentional act. Speaking is a mode of consciousness. Not only is speaking an act of consciousness, it has a universality that does not belong to any other sort of act. This is what is implied in Husserl's thesis that the noema or *Sinn* of *any* act is expressible. He recognises that the *Sinn* of a nonlinguistic act (say, of an act of perceiving) is not identical with the meaning of the sentence which expresses that nonlinguistic act. Speech is not primordial; it presupposes nonlinguistic acts, but it has a universality (in the sense of making such mapping possible) which is not shared by any other act type. It is the need of this return to the consciousness of speaking in all it modalities - ranging from empty intending to intuitively filled affirmation - that distinguishes Husserlian concern with meanings from the Wittgensteinian. But the return to the living act of speaking brings us face to face with the dialectic between sense and presence, the empty and the filled, the ideal meaning and its concrete fulfilment in experience. Speech act transcends itself.

This difference may be traced, among others, to a fundamental difference in motivation. Hegel's reflections are intended to bring out the *truth* of the reflected upon, so that in his *Phenomenology* he presents us with a series of forms or shapes of consciousness arranged as "lower" and "higher" in a hierarchy in which every succeeding member is the truth of the preceding one and which appears to have a closure in absolute knowledge. I shall now briefly recapitulate what I have done. I have distinguished (within the context of Husserlian thought) between "essence" and "meaning." For a philosophy committed to radical reflection, discovery of essences cannot be the resting place. I have raised various questions as to why it cannot, the chief of which concerns the claim of essences to be in themselves "intelligible objects." Their *facticity* has been used to challenge that claim. At the same time, two different facets of Husserl's concept of essence emerge before us: one of which we may call the "essentialist" conception of essence, the other, by contrast, a non-essentialist conception. The former, in line with a long metaphysical tradition, opposes "essence" to "accident." Within the total being of an individual, it seeks to isolate a set of properties that constitute its essence, as contradistinguished from its complementary set which consists in accidents. The individual qua individual becomes, in accordance with this conception, an accident. Individual being is accidental being, phenomenology therefore disregards individual beings in favour of essences. Such an "essentialistic" conception of essence cannot survive, I am afraid, either the Quinean or the Hegelian criticism. But at the same time, almost unnoticed, a very different conception, a "non-essentialist" conception of essence emerges: any "what" of the individual, any constituent of its *Sosein,* may be regarded, with a suitable change of attitude, as an (empirical) essence, and then, with a further appropriate change of attitude, as an *eidos.* Such a procedure permits Husserl to say that an individual has its *own* essence, that the total content of an individual being constitutes an independent essence which he calls a "concretum." Such a conception departs radically from the rationalistic tradition, escapes the Quinean and the Hegelian criticisms, but has also to give up the claim that essences are *per se* "intelligible

*[p. 208]* ^pg235

objects" - thereby leading radical philosophising to its concern with meanings. 

So far as Husserl himself is concerned, all these strands of thought almost coexist even in the late writings. One can at most speak of shifts in emphasis. Phenomenology never ceased for him to be an eidetic enquiry. But if my present thesis is correct, it becomes more and more clarification of sense, within the ov~rall programme of which the eidetic programme comes to occupy a subordinate place. 

Let us now see how these distinctions affect the conception of transcendental subjectivity, so central to Husserl's thinking. Did Husserl regard transcendental SUbjectivity as an *eidos?* Or, did he regard it as a domain of concrete experience? There seem to be indications that he did oscillate between these two conceptions. The latter conception underlies his talk of the "life of transcendental experience." Two conceptions of essence were noted earlier. Corresponding to the essentialist conception of essence, transcendental subjectivity may be regarded as the essence of empirical consciousness, as contradistinguished from its accidental features. In this sense, then, transcendental consciousness and empirical consciousness are distinct and different; the latter alone is individualised, the former a pure *eidos* with no individuating traits within itself. However, such a conception does not accord with the empiricist *motif* of Husserlian thought, with its thesis of the "wonderful parallelism" between the empirical and the transcendental,16 and the claim that with the *epoche* nothing is lost but everything reappears with "a change of signature." In accordance with these, we may say that the transcendental subjectivity is still the essence of empirical consciousness, but essence in a non-essentialistic sense - the sense in which every "what" of an individual may be "transformed" into an essence. In this sense, then, transcendental consciousness and empirical consciousness are not two different domains, the latter an instance of the former; but the two are the same. Transcendental consciousness *is* empirical consciousness, freed from its mundaneity, i.e., aware of its own function as self-interpreting as well as meaning-giving, therefore as constituting both itself and its world. 

It is remarkable that when Husserl did introduce the concept of the life-world, in the *Crisis,* he assigns to it the role of being the *Sinnesjundament,* the foundation of *meaning,* of the exact sciences - but did not regard it as containing the *truth* of those sciences.

Husserl wanted his phenomenology to be regarded as a transcendental philosophy. Since in this paper I have been trying to isolate what seems to me to be a more promising philosophical strain from his essentialism, it is now in order to indicate, how, from my perspective, such an enterprise fits into the general framework of transcendental thinking. One of the motives of transcendental thinking has been to philosophise from the beginnings and to free one's thinking in that process from all unacknowledged presuppositions and naIvety. Such thinking should not only provide foundation for other bodies of knowledge, empirical or *a priori,* but should be able to account for its own possibility as well. In the second place, transcendental philosophy, since Kant, has sought to combine the Kantian concern with the conditions of the possibility of empirical as well as *a priori* knowledge with the pre-Kantian idea of universal ontology. As recent researches have brought to light, Kant was fully aware of the close connection between his own use of "transcendental" and the scholastic use of it. 17 In the third place, while searching for the presuppositions of all knowledge, transcendental thinking inevitably sought to avoid either of the two methodologies available to earlier philosophies: empirical-inductive generalisation and logical deduction, for transcendental philosophy aims at grounding both factual sciences and deductive sciences. 18 

It is in the light of these that we may briefly consider the claim of transcendental phenomenology to be a *transcendental* philosophy. 

(a) An essentialism such as Husserl's may very well be construed as containing a transcendental turn inasmuch as essences and essential structures may be regarded as being the *a priori* conditions of the possibility of the factual *world* and correlatively of the empirical *sciences.* But an essentialism, in fact any ontology, has to be unaware of its own presuppositions. It cannot explicate, within the naIvety of its framework, what it is *to posit* an object - in this case, an essence or an essential structure. It has to take its object-domain, the domain of essences, as "pre-given," but cannot ask as to the *sense* of that pre-givenness.

THE DESTINY OF TRANSCENDENTAL PHILOSOPHY\* 

I 

Transcendental philosophies are not all of the same sort. They share a common philosophical motif in so far as they are "transcendental," but otherwise they differ a great deal among themselves as much as ideal-isms or empiricisms do. This common motif is the search for *afoundation* for knowledge, thinking and experience. But not every foundationalism is transcendental philosophy. For example, many of the logical empiricists who held the view that the edifice of knowledge rests on basic, protocol sentences are far from being transcendental philosophers. It would seem that only certain kinds of foundational thinking deserve to be called transcendental. Foundationalism, as such, cannot constitute transcendental thinking. Perhaps we should say that the foundation sought after by the transcendental philosophies should be *a priori.* A formalistic philosophy which first sets up an *a priori* uninterpreted system and, then, assigns to it an interpretation comprehensive enough to include large features of experience, would still not be transcendental, for such an uninterpreted system would not be a *foundation;* it would rather be a formal-conceptual framework which illuminates, at most, certain formal structures of the world. But one may also seek to provide an *a priori* metaphysical foundation for experience.
