**[[NRSVue Acts 22#^22-1|22.1–21]]:** **Paul’s speech.** Paul rehearses the events related to his becoming a believer in Jesus (see 9.1–31; 26.9–18). ^22-1

**[[NRSVue Acts 22#^22-3|3]]:** *Tarsus*, see 9.11. *Gamaliel*, see 5.34. *Ancestral law,* not only the laws of Torah, but other customary practices ([[LXX 3 Maccabees 01#^1-23|3 Macc 1.23]]; [[LXX 4 Maccabees 04#^4-23|4 Macc 4.23]]; [[LXX 4 Maccabees 05#^5-33|5.33]]; [[LXX 4 Maccabees 16#^16-16|16.16]]; Josephus, *Ant.* 10.11; 11.231; 17.149; *Life* 191). The description of Paul as a strict observer of the ancestral law conforms to the overall depiction of him as a law-observant Jew and is similar to Josephus’s depiction of the Pharisees (*J.W.* 2.162; *Ant.* 17.41; *Life* 191). ^22-3

> [!note] Paul and the Jews *(p. 269)*
> Paul first appears alongside other Jews as an enemy of the church and as consenting to the death of Stephen (8.1). A transformation occurs in ch 9 when a vision of the resurrected Jesus convinces him to become a believer and ultimately one of the most important evangelists, particularly among Gentiles. Jews, and particularly priests and other leaders, appear in this section largely as stock characters who function as the hostile opponents to Paul. In Pisidian Antioch, Jews drive Paul out of the district (13.50); in Iconium, Jews seek to stone him (14.5); in Thessalonica, the jealousy of the Jews leads them to set the city in an uproar (17.1–9); in Corinth, Jews attack Paul and bring him before Gallio the proconsul (18.1–13); in Achaia, he dis- putes with Jews in public (18.28); in Greece, he was apparently the target of a plot (20.3); in Jerusalem, some Jews from Asia Minor accuse Paul of teaching against the Temple and the Law (21.27–30) and charge him with bringing disorder and speaking treason against the emperor (17.6–7), causing people to worship contrary to the Law and the Temple (18.13; 21.28; 24.6), and being an agitator (24.5). Acts repeatedly shows how these accusations are unfounded and often inspired by jealousy and antagonism toward the preaching about Jesus. The stories of Jews attempting to prosecute and even kill Paul resemble the actions Luke depicts Jews as taking against Jesus in the Gospel. The highly stylized narrative should caution against any attempt to read the depiction of Jews as historically reliable.

**[[NRSVue Acts 22#^22-22|22]]:** *Not be allowed to live*, the Jerusalem crowd calls for Paul’s death, just as another one had called for the death of Jesus ([[Luke 23#^23-21|Lk 23.21–23]]). ^22-22

**[[NRSVue Acts 22#^22-25|25]]:** *Roman citizen*, Paul’s status protects him against torture (see 16.37). ^22-25

**[[NRSVue Acts 22#^22-28|28]]:** *Born a citizen*, see 16.37; 21.39. Paul is more “Roman” than the Roman tribune who had to buy his citizenship. Acts does not say how Paul’s family acquired citizenship. ^22-28
