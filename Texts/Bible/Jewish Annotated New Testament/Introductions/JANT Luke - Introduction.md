## Name, Authorship, and Sources

The Gospel According to Luke and the book of Acts are traditionally ascribed to Luke, the physician who accompanied Paul ([[Colossians 04#^4-14|Col 4.14]]; [[2 Timothy 04#^4-11|2 Tim 4.11]]; [[Philemon 01#^1-24|Philem 1.24]]). The author, henceforth “Luke,” was not an eyewitness (1.2) to Jesus’ activities; the prologue (1.1–4) indicates not only an appeal to earlier sources but also the attempt to correct them (1.1–4). Luke’s sources likely included some form of Mark’s Gospel; possibly a written text today called Q (from the German word for “source,” *Quelle*), reconstructed from materials common to the Gospels of Luke and Matthew and consisting mostly of material such as the beatitudes ([[Matthew 05#^5-3|Mt 5.3–11]] || [[Luke 06#^6-20|Lk 6.20–23]]) and the “Our Father” ([[Matthew 06#^6-9|Mt 6.9–13]] || [[Luke 11#^11-2|Lk 11.2–4]]); independent material called L for Luke’s special source (e.g., Lk 1–2); and the author’s own redactional comments. Luke also shares some material with John, such as stories about Jesus’ friends Mary and Martha ([[Luke 10#^10-38|Lk 10.38–42]] || Jn 11–12) and the notice that Satan possessed Judas Iscariot ([[Luke 22#^22-3|Lk 22.3]] || [[John 13#^13-2|Jn 13.2]]).

Both the Gospel and Acts are addressed to Theophilus (Gk “friend of God”), who may either be an actual patron or an ideal reader. This ideal reader is likely a Gentile, for Luke defines Jewish practices (e.g., 22.1), sometimes erroneously (e.g., 2.22), and the Gospel lacks the Aramaic terms found in its canonical counterparts. The ideal reader is also likely a Christian, and as such is familiar with the Scriptures of Israel in their Greek form (the Septuagint), which the Gospel frequently quotes and to which it makes numerous allusions. The Gospel was likely written toward the end of the first century, following the first revolt against Rome (66–70 CE), when the Church had become increasingly Gentile, when the focus on Jerusalem that Paul maintains had been replaced by the Gentile mission, and when the third generation of Jesus’ followers — the generation after the “eyewitnesses and servants” (1.2) — were coming to terms with both the delay of Jesus’ return and the fact that most Jews, although possessing more or less the same Scriptures as the Gentile church, neither saw those Scriptures as predicting Jesus’ messianic role nor accepted his follower’s claims of his messianic status.

## Style, Contents, and Structure

Luke’s style ranges from the elegant Greek of the prologue (1.1–4 comprise a single Greek sentence) to evocations of the Septuagint. By casting the nativity stories of John the Baptist and Jesus (1.5–2.52) in the cadences of Israel’s Scriptures, the Gospel suggests continuity between ancient Israel and the Christian story. The narrative follows Mark’s plot (although the material in [[Mark 06#^6-45|Mk 6.45–8.26]]; 9.41–10.12 is excluded) and includes an additional “travel” account or “itinerary” (9.51–18.14) inserted between [[Mark 09#^9-41|Mk 9.41]] and 10.12. Here Luke recounts some of the most beloved of Jesus’ teachings, including the parable of the good Samaritan (10.29–37) and the parable of the prodigal son (15.11–32). Distinct as well are Luke’s nativity stories, with their focus on John the Baptist and the virgin Mary, and the resurrection and ascension accounts.

## Interpretation and Reading Guide

The Gospel displays particular interest in people outside the elite, variously identified as women, children, the sick, the poor, tax collectors and sinners, and Gentiles. Problems emerge, however, when this interest is seen as offering an alternative to Jesus’ Jewish context. When Jesus speaks with and about people in all these categories, he remains embedded within Judaism rather than removed from it. The Gospel also provides details on these various social roles. For example, Luke depicts (Jewish) women with freedom of travel (1.39; 8.2–3; 23.27,55–56), access to their own funds (7.37; 8.3; 15.8; 21.2), serving as patrons (8.1–3), owning homes (10.38; see also [[Acts 12#^12-12|Acts 12.12]]); and attending synagogues (13.10–17) and the Temple (2.22,36–37,41–50). Children, of utmost value in Jewish culture, have parents and caregivers who so love them that they seek Jesus’ healing and blessing (e.g., 18.15). The sick, who should not be confused with the ritually impure or a broader category of “sinners,” appear embedded in caring familial and social networks. Most people in antiquity were poor, and the Jewish system, starting with the Tanakh, mandated communal responsibility for their support. To regard Jesus, appropriately, as caring for women, children, the sick, and the poor, embeds him within Judaism rather than separating him from it.

*[p. 108]* ^pg139

The Lukan Jesus also has a particular interest in associating with “tax collectors and sinners.” Rather than seen as suspect because of laxity in halakhic observance, sinners and tax collectors are people who have separated themselves from community welfare (for a modern analogy, sinners would be drug pushers, pimps, insider traders, and arms dealers). Finally, Luke depicts Gentiles as welcome within the Jewish community (7.1–11).

The Gospel’s presentation of Jews and Judaism is complex. On the one hand, especially in the first two chapters, John the Baptist and Jesus are born into a vibrant Jewish environment of faithful Jews. The Gospel opens with the priest Zechariah, John’s father, receiving divine revelation in the Jerusalem Temple; Joseph and Mary visit the Temple to dedicate their son and to celebrate the Passover. Luke even describes the circumcisions of both John and Jesus.

On the other hand, Luke depicts the synagogue as a place of violence (4.28–29), details Israel’s continual failures while highlighting the fidelity of Gentiles and Samaritans (e.g., 7.9; 17.16–18), and presents scathing caricatures of Pharisees (see “Pharisees in Luke,” p. 122) and chief priests. The harsh rhetoric resembles that of the biblical prophets and the Dead Sea Scrolls, but that comparison is limited, since Jesus’ criticisms against fellow Jews are now embedded in a text directed primarily to Gentiles. Indeed, some scholars suggest that the first two chapters are additions, created in the early second century and designed not to foreground the positive practices of Judaism, but to counter the arguments of Marcion (ca. 85–160), a Christian teacher who promoted the idea that the God of the Old Testament was not the one revealed by Jesus (the stereotype of the “Old Testament God of wrath” vs. the “New Testament God of love” is a recrudescence of the Marcionite heresy). Whereas the nativity materials could be removed from Luke’s Gospel, and the text coherently could begin with 3.1, no manuscript evidence suggests that the first two chapters are additions, and the themes in the nativity accounts (critique of Roman authority, continuity with Jewish history, etc.) continue in the rest of the Gospel.

Luke’s view of Jews and Judaism is also complicated by the relationship of the Gospel to Acts. This second volume of Luke’s composition begins as does the Gospel, with Jesus’ followers in the Temple and as being well regarded by their Jewish neighbors. Yet Acts accuses “the entire house of Israel” (2.36) of crucifying Jesus and having “killed the Author of life” (3.14–15); the text ends not in Jerusalem but in Rome, with Paul announcing that the Jews will “never understand” (28.26), but that the Gentiles “will listen” (28.28). One plausible reading of the combined volumes is that Luke regards Jesus as the fulfillment of God’s plan and regards his followers as the true heirs of Moses and the Prophets; at the same time, Luke depicts non-Christian Jews as generally hypocritical, intolerant, and violent. Readers should attend to the tension between Luke’s depiction of Jesus and his early followers as observant Jews, and the presentation of those Jews who did not become Christian as having been, in Luke’s view, unfaithful to their own tradition. Similarly, readers should note the tension between Luke’s claims to empirical proof for Jesus’ messianic status (e.g., eyewitness testimony to miracles and to his resurrection, his fulfillment of Scripture) and Luke’s suggestion that belief comes only through divine revelation (24.31).
