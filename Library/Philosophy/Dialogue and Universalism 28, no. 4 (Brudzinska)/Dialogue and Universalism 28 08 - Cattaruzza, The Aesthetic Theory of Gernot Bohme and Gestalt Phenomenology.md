THE AESTHETIC THEORY OF GERNOT BÜHME 

AND GESTALT PHENOMENOLOGY 

*ABSTRACT* 

Gernot Böhme’s original proposal regarding an aesthetic as a philosophic theory of perceptual knowledge could, in our opinion, be usefully compared with certain aspects, historical-theoretical and methodological, of Gestalt psychology. From an historical point of view there is the attention commonly paid to the work of the 18th-century philosopher, Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten, considered as an important precursor of the study of sensitive knowledge, while the subsequent basic themes of the perceptual-cognitive approach, of the expressive qualities, of the distinction “physical reality /actual reality,” of the physiognomic problem, to cite but a few, recall nuclear questions, although perhaps not traditionally included in the “major canon”—to use a terminological proposal of Rocco Ronchi’s—of philosophy and specifically of experimental phenomenology. 

 Keywords: Gernot Böhme, Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten, sensitive knowledge, presence, atmosphere, physiognomic qualities, one’s own body. 

We plan to follow the original, stimulating itinerary proposed by Gernot Böhme concerning the bases of his general theory of perception propounded over a period of more than twenty years in the form of lectures, essays, conferences and various monographs. We intend here to make reference to the richer and more reasoned version, according to the author himself, published in 2001 with the title *Aisthetik. Vorlesungen über Ästhetik als allgemeine Wahrnehmungslehre*. In 2010 an Italian translation appeared with the engaging but enigmatic title *Atmosphere, Extasy, Staging*, and with the subtitle “Aesthetics as a General Theory of Perception.” We indicate the main stages of this interesting philosophical-scientific program by dwelling chiefly upon certain key-points of the route marked out so stimulatingly by the author. 

In doing so we will evoke parallel reflections and ideas of authors both inspired by gestalt and phenomenological experimentation, and involved in 

*[p. 168]* ^pg170

exploring perceptual processes. Belonging to different times and places more than united by a homogenous doctrinal formulation, they exhibit a “family air” both in their style of research and in their creative investigation of phenomena, of effects, of “ways of appearing.” In this sense the group includes the Goethe of the *Farbenlehre,* the Stumpf of the *Tonpsyichologie*, and others up to Max Wertheimer, Heinz Werner, Vittorio Benussi and the Italian perceptological (“percettologico”) school. And it is precisely the independence of the phenomenal that reveals the typical epistemological *leitmotiv* of the afore-mentioned approach. 

 For his part, Böhme begins with the perceptual meaning of the Greek word *aisthesis*, which enables him to connect confidently to the Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten\`s *Aesthetica* (1750). It is here, in Baumgarten’s project of a theory perceptively founded in the ambit of feeling and in opposition to rational knowledge, that Böhme recognises the most authentic inspiration of his own approach. This last strives to reconnect to that central nucleus of Baumgarten’s work which in his opinion, after all, was never fully taken up again and developed. Moreover, the suggestions put forward by various admirers and successors of Baumgarten, who link the theory of sensitive knowledge first and foremost to a theory of the work of art, set a limit, according to Böhme, to the range of action and the size of the thematic core originally proposed by Baumgarten. In this way, in fact, the focus is reduced in areas of inquiry equally pertinent to the sensitive-cognitive mode—like that of nature and applied art, as for instance design or kitsch and, in general, the aesthetic-expressive aspects of daily life. In this sense we select certain significant statements of Baumgarten’s, starting with that relating to beauty intended as the perfection of sensitive knowledge, with the added qualification that it is such inasmuch as it is observable in a broad sense. 

 It is not by chance that the perceptologist philosopher Paolo Bozzi, who recently repeatedly promoted the “virtuous association” of perception and the “art” of observing, considers Baumgarten the “guiding spirit” of experimental phenomenology: 

“The truly unique examples of sensibly manageable perception from botha scientific and a literary point of view live only in the moment of observation \[...\] The clean eye of one who allows himself to be absorbed by what he is observing \[...\] Every time we listen to music we do that! I suppose that for very many painters it is the same—the landscape.”[^1] Böhme, for his part, in placing sensitive knowledge at the centre of his lessons—in the text we are examining—cannot avoid making reference to works of art in terms of “testimony.” From this point of view, his inquiry re-enters

a type of “instrumental” knowledge that partners and enlarges the Aristotelian *Organon*. He mentions, in fact, that for the Stagirite the “arts” (*technai*) constitute the “knowledge” that guides the *poiein*, namely the production of something. This Aristotelian distinction also applies to an instrumental theory of language, as, for instance, that stated by the psychologist and philosopher Karl Ludwig Bühler in his *Sprachtheorie*. In particular the third “principle” of his linguistic inquiry distinguishes the “activity of speaking” from the “linguistic work,” recalling the Aristotelian categories of *praxis* and *poesis*. These are found as much in the linguistic field as in that of the age of development: “Eminent linguistic works, like other human creations—he notes—for example the Ninth Symphony or the Brooklyn bridge or the Walchensee power plant, are rich in interest for their inimitable characteristics and particular qualities.” Moreover, as he adds, “in a work one can study traits typical of its creator and of his creative work, and more besides.”[^2] Thus, the scholar of the age of development recognises, for example, as already a “linguistic work” the child’s first expression that refers to an event that has made an impression on him. On the other hand, the activity of speaking is no less meaningful and basic. In fact, according to Bühler, each activity has its own area, of which he had already spoken in a work published in the *Kant Studien* where he recognised in *need* and *occasion* the two sources of the activity. The statement of the two factors, however, was already known to Aristotle and had been plastically expressed in the Goethian physiognomy studies. 

Böhme, in turn, intends to dedicate his aesthetic research to three special fields in question—fields that lead us to finding new problems and requiring unconventional answers. Essentially what counts is the beginning—that is, setting out from the concrete problems of the field of aesthetic activity. Here there come into play the spheres of nature, of design and of art, which have to deal with the conditions of things, needs and developments more recent and unpublished: from the problem of modern art’s communication to its consumers—because of a hiatus arisen between the works themselves and their public—to the subject of socio-political criticism of the pathologies inherent in the so-called “aestheticization of the real.”[^3] It is a phenomenon already apparent in the grandiose political stagings (parades, huge rallies, etc.) of the past, and not only then, but which acquires, in the “aesthetic economy” of late capitalism, and with the unprecedented role of the *new media*, contours that are no less disquieting, Therefore it is legitimate, we may observe, to place alongside “use value” and

Among the new professions that feed this demand are designs and advertising, but also sectors of architecture, of landscape design and, in general, of all the means of mass communication. As regards the aesthetics of nature specifically, one cannot disregard the most recent environmental issues, with the challenging problems of readjusting and redesigning a ravaged nature, of renewing the relationship between environmental quality and human perceptive affectivity.[^4] But this is precisely the decisive point of the question: is the nature that is perceptively cultivated different from the one that is scientifically investigated? Or, in other terms, what has technological instrumental exactness to do with experience based on sensitivity? Böhme refers again to the original approach of Baumgarten and the Goethian naturalistic tradition. Bozzi, for his part, as author of a *naive physics*, refers paradoxically to the father of modern science, Galileo Galilei, to demonstrate the unavoidability of perceptual experience even in a context of measuring: “Galileo measures with an inexact pulse the exact frequency of pendulums, and once he knows that the pendulums are exact he recommends his colleagues use the pendulum to measure the pulse!”[^5] No less interesting is what results from the comparison with Aristotelian physics, according to which the fall of bodies manifests itself with uniform motion, being the speed directly proportional to the weight of the body. Galileo, however, by constructing inclined planes with a square section instead of semicircular, corrected the Aristotelian hypothesis*,* perceptually corrected *only*, by adopting a suitable instrument of observation: the sound of equidistant bells that were struck by iron balls in their fall in ever shorter times. This demonstrated what normally is not visible, namely the accelerated motion of the fall of the bodies. In this way Galileo, according to Bozzi, had introduced into science a more refined and productive phenomenological method. 

 Naturally in Böhme there comes abundantly into play the subjective-emotional-affective component through the mention of the notion of the “living body,” according to a phenomenological line of attack which, from Edmund Husserl and from Maurice Merleau-Ponty, arrives at the “new phenomenology” of Hermann Schmitz.[^6] And this is notably made manifest also in the Böhmian treatment of new objectives and therefore of a new aesthetic that modern art in particular (from the figurative to music and to literature) requires. In this sense one could still ascribe a meaning—albeit, notes Böhme, by subtraction—to Malevic’s Black Square, for the slight assonance, for example, with the Russian iconological tradition. But the “strained interpretation” is no longer admissible with Josef Albers’ *Homage to the square*. The alternative could then be that of focussing upon the immediate presence of chromatic reality in its actuality. 

Referring to that same Albers and to the distinction between *actual fact* and *factual fact* he advanced in relation to his own pictorial work, Böhme develops it in terms of the contrast between “physical reality” and “actual reality.” The first, the *factual fact*, is the product with its objective properties, the chromatic material included; the second, the actual reality, the *actual fact*, is the tonal and emotional effect which that image expresses and which, referring to the terminology of Ludwig Klages, Böhme defines as the “actual reality of the image.” 

 In 1913, in his pioneering work *Die Gestaltwahrnehmungen,* Bühler begins by citing one who was in turn an artist, architect and art theoretician, namely Adolf Hildebrand and his highly regarded work *Das Problem der Form in der bildenden Kunst*. In particular, he considered to be extremely important the Hildebrandian distinction between *Daseinsformen* (*forms of existence)* and *Wir-kungsformen* (*effect forms*). The first represent the physical-geometrical reality, while the second consider the effect and the phenomenal significance that they emanate. In Bühler’s opinion these considerations were fundamental in order to decline the ways of appearing, but also basic for the subsequent reflections displayed in his *Ausdruckstheorie* (1933), where, among other things, the principles of the theoretics of Klages are debated. They turned out equally useful for the Bühler project of a *Gemäldeoptik* to be developed in the ambit of the *Far-benerscheinungen* (1922) according to a terminology adopted by Katz. 

 As regards music—according to Böhme—the lack of a formal classical regime heightens the experience of the presence of sound and therefore of the voice, as much instrumental as human. In this line of development even the noises that go towards enriching the sphere of the acoustically reproducible, by which the new musical styles are inspired, play an important role. To these important observations can once more be aligned the Bühlerian reflections on the acoustic fact of vocalisation expressed in para 13 of his *Sprachtheorie* (1934) dedicated to onomatopoeic language. In vocal sounds there exists an extraordinary wealth of timbre colours. Thus every instrument has its own voice thanks to the specific combination of sounds that derive from a basic sound and from harmonic sounds. So the sound of the violin will have the “voice” of the violin, the sound of the trumpet the “voice” of the trumpet, etc. But unsurpassable for wealth of registers is the human voice, comparable in part to an organ keyboard or to instruments different from those monotonal ones like the saxophone. And it is not by chance that we recall virtuosos of the voice like the American “vocalists,” who turn like an instrument to the larynx and to the chest to produce

*[p. 172]* ^pg174

intensely expressive emotional results. No less important is the area of noises, which in characteristic manner impregnate even the sonority of the instruments (the flute whispers, the harp picks, the piano pounds, etc.). But more—the human voice whistles, snaps, etc. with an unrivalled range of tones. The major part of sounds and noises is the natural one, of the street, of the nearby door, which constitutes an expressive presence richly endowed. 

 In literature, too, Böhme recognises the works of the avantgarde, starting with the Dada movement, not placeable within the confines of a traditional hermeneutics, or at least ascribable to lived experiences or social factors. The literary text would then reveal itself as an “intertextual mesh” where traditional meaning evaporates. But it is above all related to the subject of the *Aisthetik* as a doctrine of *perception* that Böhme undertakes to draw attention to its foundations and method. In fact, depending on the window chosen: neurophysiology, psychology, analytical-linguistics, etc., perception can be variously thematised. The great philosophical systems of the past (those, for example, of Immanuel Kant or Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel) have constituted the general frame of reference for a strategic positioning of aesthetics in terms of the systems themselves, according to an intellectual and non-sensitive evaluation. Aesthetics instead, like *Aisthetik,* is the prerogative of its own physicality, already qualified as such by Ernst Mach in his *Analyse der Empfindungen* (1886) where he exactly distinguishes *Leib* from *Körper*, and then taken up by Husserl and Merleau-Ponty, not without problems and open questions. Consequently, according to Böhme, perception must be defined *per sé*, as specific, unavoidable knowledge and not assimilable into mere physical reality. 

 Bozzi, too, intends to circumscribe perception’s most pertinent range of action when he states: 

“it is the world’s objects and the experience of them that constitute the things of perception. I can imagine various theories of perception (for instance, by Hume, Bergson, Helmholtz, Husserl \[…\] and psychological theories of perception: behaviourist, new look, etc.) but then I uncover facts, perhaps in the laboratory, perhaps in the ecological world, and these are the facts to tell me how things are. They are the facts of perception and their intrinsic logic to bring theories down, supplying material for a new theory.”<sup>7</sup> Böhme intends to concentrate on certain paradigmatic examples, capable of representing an adequate starting point for the perceptual theory of the “New aesthetics” where, however, he focuses particularly on the perceiver-perceived relationship. So the apparently simple example of the sentence “I see a tree” can change colour, in its possible visualisation, in a scene where the subject that perceives the tree finds himself under its foliage and so feels its presence. rally Böhme, beginning his close examination with a statement on perception instead of from perception itself, is aware of possible snares lying in wait: for that reason he stresses the need to proceed “from the linguistic expression to what is there expressed.” 

This is a concern that has bothered the scholars of specifically visual perception, one which Gaetano Kanizsa, for instance, resolves by directly investigating visual phenomena already known or to be discovered (spontaneous or creatively produced), like the famous triangle that bears his name. This is about irregular surfaces or stereoscopic visual objects or stroboscopic movements. There is even the effort to visually study non-vision, as in the case of phenomena of occlusion and, in particular, phenomena of partial occlusion which give rise to modes of appearance of the “completely a-modal,” with all the effects (curvatures, distortions, contractions) which it creates, together with the emotions and feelings (of surprise, curiosity, uncertainty) associated with them. The phrase “completely a-modal” was not by chance proposed by the originally Husserlian training psychologist Albert Michotte, who with his collaborators in Louvain studied, in phenomenal-experimental terms, such physical notions as causality. 

 But already Vittorio Benussi was concerned—for instance, in his last work, 1925, *La suggestione e l’ipnosi come mezzi di analisi psichica reale—*to distinguish two types of object (in the wake of the Meinongian School), those perceptively present and those mental ones, perceptively absent. These latter, however, could be, so to speak, reinserted if past, or preinserted if future, into the perceptive present through the linguistic instrument which, for this reason, is a “conductor of reality.” Therefore, Benussi observes, the perceptively absent event, whether an unknown danger or a Lucretian thought, is plugged into “the circuit of interdependence that constitutes the real environment in which our present is resolved.” On the other hand, even in the absence of conceptual signs, but in the presence of autonomous emotional and sentimental traces, there remains the possibility of transforming into the perceptive present an internal aspiration. So the state of mental cancellation, the absence of thoughts and images which, for example, marks out the ecstatic state of mystical concentration, can correspond to the phenomenon described by St. Teresa of Avila as “quiet prayer.” This happens because, according to the Triestine perceptologist, a mental presence can be transformed into a perceptive presence, internal or external. So in the “quiet prayer” the mental presence, shown or hidden, of a particular state of lucidity, first experienced as aspiration, can be transformed into the corresponding internal perceptive presence, assuming the character of reality. 

 Naturally Böhme has for a long time been aware of the existence of theories of the five senses, namely sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch (this last currently broadened to include thermal sensitivity, the sense of pain and that of balance): and all can be traced back to their respective reference organs. But the neurophysiological correlation is not the object of Böhme’s investigation—he prefers to proceed phenomenologically. Therefore, returning to the example of 

*[p. 174]* ^pg176

the tree and the experience of its presence, he claims the role that its *shadow* plays,[^8] insofar as, by entering there, he senses the living-body presence of the tree. In this enveloping perception an acoustic element can also play a role. Two questions clearly follow: the relevance of the proprioceptive experience and the fundamentally intersensory character of the perceptive presence. The first, the object of perceptological studies that frequently cross the field of expressive qualities, is also a recurring theme in examples put forward by Wittgenstein in his observations on the philosophy of psychology. The second opens up the fascinating territory of sensory intermodality and synaesthesia, the latter, however, not analysed in its single sensory components, but rather taken as a totality, like an integrated whole, from which to then progressively retrieve the individual sensory phenomena. It was no coincidence, in the chapter dedicated to synaesthesia, that Böhme refers back, like Merleau-Ponty, to the theoretical-experimental works of Werner, which get closer to the thematization of intersensoriality through the enhancement of the expressive qualities which he defines as “Physiognomic,” revitalising a tradition of ancient studies, and cultivated by authors such as Johann Wolfgang von Goethe and Ludwig Klages, to mention two of the most representative. For his part, the Viennese scholar develops the synaesthetic value of the physiognomic qualities in a unitary perceptive context which precedes the specific sensory differentiations.[^9] But in Böhme’s example of feeling the presence of the tree by entering its shade it is the “atmospheric” effect the event produces that counts. Taking inspiration from the terminological proposals of the *New Phenomenology* by Schmitz, our author’s theme is, as a basic paradigm that of “atmospheres” which, passing through physiognomy, staging and ecstasy, is found in the perceptive present button. However, the ”atmosphere” and its “character” are a subject that has also been treated from a phenomenological point of view by Gestalt scholars, and in particular by Kurt Lewin in his pioneering article “Kriegslandschaft” (1917). 

 But let us take a step backwards and return to the tree and its shade. The latter does not seem part of the material world, but at the same time is perceptible and connected to it. Above all, if it is a shade in motion this provokes curiosity, but also submission from childhood on. Mach remembers in the *Erkenntnis und Irrtum* (1905) the perplexity of the child when, as he moves, he is followed by his shadow, from which he tries to detach himself by climbing up the sharp-edged steps of a ladder. Now, it is precisely this character of “atmosphere” linked to the shadow that neither disappears nor contracts *hic et nunc*: perceivable exactly when you enter it, it manifests a dispositional character that is emotional-affective, “quasi-objective.” And this is also confirmed by an experience of discrepancy, as for example when one experiences a joyous spring day in

If, when one speaks of “atmosphere,” one relates to oneself with a bodily feeling, the phenomenon of the “atmospheric” takes on a more “thing-ness” character, as in the case of autumn, of wind, of cold, of a voice. On the other hand, also the effect of emotionally engrossing “atmospheres” can be “met.” In fact, the perceiving subject “meets them as something other than himself.” 

 This objective dimension, but still qualitatively characterised and therefore profoundly expressive, is a recurring theme, albeit rather difficult, of the gestalt aesthetic reflections, starting from the same Christian von Ehrenfels in the famous article he dedicated to gestalt qualities in 1890. But even authors oriented towards further enhancing the physiological basis from the theoretical point of view, like Wolfgang Köhler, emphasize the expressively threatening effect of the rumble of thunder. In his opinion meteorological weather is imbued with psychological characteristics. In the same way one can speak of gloomy landscapes or happy days, where one perceives above all the emotion implicit in the landscape or the serenity of a spring day.[^10] But it is mainly Wolfgang Metzger who emphasizes the role of the expressive way that the things and events we encounter appear to us, even if what appears turns out to be surprising, illogical or unexpected.[^11] 

 Böhme recalls Schmitz’s distinction between “quasi-objective atmospheres” and the “atmospheric” as “semi-thing” in whose wake follow the clearly marked stages of physiognomy, staging and scene, ecstasy in the literal sense of *outside oneself*. By way of these sequences and setting out from the atmospheric feeling one reaches the thing-ness perception verbally expressed by the sentence “I see a tree.” It must be stressed that in the analysis of the *Aisthetik* the perceptive presence is not obtainable by abstracting from the ordinary thing-ness perception, since the original base, the *primum*, is constituted by the atmospheric presence which follows the ordinary perception of things. Wertheimer, instead, at the beginning of a famous article of 1923, seems to take the opposite route,[^12] since he sets out from the thing-ness perception– “If I look out of the window I see, let’s say, a house, some trees, the sky …” However, even for him this description is not obtained by abstracting from a sum of shades of colour or gathered like an abstractly calculable assemblage. Naturally the succeeding stages aim at different objectives: in particular, for Wertheimer, in particular, the identification of the principles appointed to the organising of the perceptual field; for Böhme the signalling of spaces atmospherically matching everyday life, scanned in “scenes” and “ecstasy,” even if oriented towards things. Both lines of research can nevertheless converge in the one defined and developed by the psychologist and philosopher of the Triestine School Bozzi, and by his pupils
