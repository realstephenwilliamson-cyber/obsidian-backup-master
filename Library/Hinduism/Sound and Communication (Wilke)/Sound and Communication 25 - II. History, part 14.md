What was still useful material to Patañjali for cracking intellectual problems, and in the Sarasvatī-Upaniṣad was a magic mystical spell or prayer formula invoking the protection of the goddess, was transformed into a method of interpreting the world by the Kashmiri Tantrists inspired by Bhartrhari. For them the mysterious aura of the verse on the four languages, the first three of which are no longer in the world, and the fourth is spoken by humans (the most widespread of the Vedic language riddles), is the framework within which they develop their otherwise strictly systematic theory of the four stages of language: language has not only audible, public aspects (vaikhari), but is also subject to thinking as inner speech (madhyamā) and to intuition (paśyanti). Even the consciousness itself (parā). Participation in the nimbus of the Veda via the puzzle verse means that the philosophical teaching that language arises in the area of tension between discursive and non-discursive consciousness becomes an esoteric teaching with ritual and spiritual relevance. And there’s more: the whole is interpreted theistically to indicate a form of the Great Goddess, namely Parā Vāc, the “Cosmic Language.” This results in numerous theological cross-references. The Cosmic Language is at the same time the wife of Śiva who, with him, executes the creation of the world, and she is also the kundalini, the coiled snake — a symbol of cosmic energy and creative power — in the human body, to name but a few of her guises. At the same time, however, despite the more or less mythical trimmings, the whole always remains philosophy with an existentialist background. The self-reflection, self-expression and self-creation of God in the form of the world, as described in Abhinavagupta’s esoteric exegesis of the alphabet, is also constantly occurring in the human consciousness. Here the creative, autopoietic activity of the supreme God becomes a very concrete psychological reality. It becomes delightful bliss, wonder and aesthetic reverie in the experiences of dissolution imparted by art and religion. It is fairly certain that Abhinavagupta has had a huge (but often invisible) influence on music theory and practice and contributed to inspiring the universal mythical Nāda-Brahman concept. What is interesting for the Nāda-Brahman discussion is that Abhinavagupta sees the real musician’s life in a “real symbolic,” iconic-indexical relationship to Parā Vāc. Nothing else is as close to the immediate experience of the integral world consciousness (samvid) as the immersion that can be triggered by music. Nevertheless, Abhinavagupta still avoids equating one with the other. It is the spiritual, deeply delighting experience of the absorption and going beyond oneself, the complete identification with the object, and a feeling of being no longer of this world okottara), and at the same time immersing oneself in the encompassing greater Whole that characterizes Abhinavagupta’s rasa aesthetics. One

The feeling of being not in this world that is experienced when hearing music is at the same time the entry into the wholeness of the world. Tertium comparationis for Abhinavagupta is the spontaneity of experience of sound phenomena. According to him it is precisely this spontaneous lysis that enables one to have a de-individualized world experience of non-dual wholeness. Ultimate liberation does indeed occur on a still more existential plane than mere enjoyment of art, but otherwise both have the same effects. 

If one takes Abhinavagupta’s thought processes as a starting point, it is only a short step to the sound contemplation (nāda-upāsana) of the musicologists. Underpinned by brilliant philosophical argumentation we also find such contemplation later in Bhāskararāya, the “Banarsi-wallah” and Maharashtra Brahman with a Kashmiri background, who is known as the most significant theorist of the Śrīvidyā. This seventeenth-century thinker identifies the fourth stage, that is Parā Vāc or the highest form of the hidden language, with the Nāda, here mantra sound, and thus also with something very specifically sensory. He uses the example of a string of pearls to illustrate how very different sound is from other worldly phenomena. Sound is at once substance (i.e. the pearls in the comparison) and structure (the string). To sense sound in such a concrete way (in the body and beyond the body) means to experience a wholeness - which in Bhāskararāya’s interpretation also means achieving an immediate insight into the reality of the universe. If one hears every sound simultaneously present in the fifteen-syllabled Śrividyā

Unlike the Advaita-Vedānta, this realization is, one should note, not just a gaining of knowledge (74714), i.e. not a purely cognitive phenomenon, but a feeling (vedana), that is an experiencing that includes body, sense and emotions. This experiential dimension had already had great influence on Abhinavagupta’s samvid concept. 

As in Abhinavagupta’s aesthetics theory and Tantra, Bhāskararāya’ s Nāda Yoga is peppered with philosophy, theology, ritual, contemplation and psychology. His instructions show how strongly mantra practice, sensory perception and rationalized lysis can flow into one another. The Tantric sound experience he describes has, similar to Abhinavagupta’ s aesthetic absorption and de-limiting paradigm, and the musicologists’ Nāda-Brahman, total affinity with a somatized, sensorized Brahman that can be experienced emotionally.

What Berendt failed to see was the close connection between language and music in the indigenous context. The central terms Vāc and Brahman remained closed to him. What Berendt missed is therefore precisely the decisive factor of the indigenous Nāda-Brahman: its development out of expressive language and primordial phonemes, the fusion of sound and consciousness, and the linguistic and mantra-ontological foundation of the Indian theory of music. One should remember that even in Matañga, music was seen as being derived by linguistic sounds. That the central factor ~ Brahman — became attached to the Nāda in India only relatively late, and that the lysis aspect was introduced into the Nāda only with the Brahman (and “bliss,” nanda, thought to be inherent in it), are facts of which Berendt, the jazz historian and New Age proponent, knows just as little as of the Tantric, sensorized monism and the characterizing role of devotionalism (above all in Southern Indian music), which strengthened the lysis aspect even more. 

Just as decisive in the formation of the Nāda-Brahman, even if it came in an almost invisible form, was, however, the ancient Indian idea of the goddess Vāc, the “goddess Voice” as the queen of the universe that as an old, cultural legacy so to speak, determines its deep grammar (and presumably would have delighted Berendt if he had been familiar with the Vāc). The Vāc was specifically the voice and, unlike the non-dual experience of the 2015248 and Śaṁkaras, this allowed the Brahman to be experienced and embodied in a more tangible way, to unite the inner and outer worlds, and to conceive of nanda (supreme bliss) as sensory lysis. Against the background of the Vāc it was basically easy to imagine the human body as the seat of the cosmic Nāda and to propound the divinity of song. In typical form, Śārṁgdeva does not turn directly to the Vāc idea (nor to Sarasvatī, who is mentioned only briefly as a music-lover), but instead indirectly to the transformed, philosophically reflected Vāc, by building directly on the linguist and language philosopher Bhartrhari and (in a more hidden fashion) on the metaphysician and aesthetic theoretician Abhinavagupta, both of whom had set the academic standards for pursuing metaphysics within a framework of science. 

In the dominance of the voice the Vāc remained omnipresent right into everyday culture. Typically, for musicologists song was most important when music was considered. In Śārṁgdeva the instruments and instrumental music do not appear until the end of the treatise (chap. 6). On the other hand, the idea of the divinity of song already appeared in his initial (cryptic) verse, which also secretly hinted at the Nāda-Brahman. Apparently Śārṁgadeva was aware of the audacity of the statement (song as an alloform of God). He referred to the Sāma- Veda

In music practice the human voice was the role model for instrumental music, instrument building and the symbolism associated with the instruments. In this context it must be remembered that the human voice itself was regarded as the “divine lute” (daivīi-vīnā), “human lute” (mānṇusi-vīṇā) or “body lute” (Śāāriri-viṇā), by analogy with the actual instrument, the “wooden lute” (dāravíī-vīṇā) that should imitate the voice. This metonymic idea of the “body lute” goes right back to later Vedic times, as discussed in our Veda chapter (2.4.4.3), and is definitely present in the “fifth Veda,” the Nātyasāstra,” the fundamental text for Indian aesthetics and the first work on music. And so, the Vāc also remained subjectively present in the divine body lute. Enriched by the Tantric and Yogic cultures of knowledge, the later music theoreticians praised the human body as the medium of the primary manifestation of the Nāda and located both the audible and the inaudible Nāda in the body. The Nāda points quite clearly to Tantric and Yogic collective memory, first and foremost to the cosmological primordial Nāda of the orthodox Śaiva-Āgamas, but doubtless also to the Yogic production of inner sounds through contemplation of mantras and resonance.

##### 6.3.3 The Yogic Tantric Legacy and its Recoding

Prem Lata Sharma supposes that the prime role of the voice in Indian musicology is based on the fact that the “inner space in the human body” was perceived to be the field of Yogic experience and the realization of the unity of the inner and outer space had also been the actual goal of India’s music culture.” She quotes as evidence a passage from Mataṁñga’s 70440657 that clearly assumes a body-mind continuum for the production of sound in song and borrows terminology from Yogic-Tantric mantra practice: 

“By the will of the ātman \[the human soul\], the वप \[wind\] (that is) moving upward from the base of the earth (22607 navel) (and) is held on the ‘wall’ of the nādīs \[Yogic nervous currents\] and in the space \[cerebral region in the body\], is known as svara, the delightful ऽपात् . 

Thus, according to Sharma, what belongs in voice or sound production are mental and somatic factors: a) the will to produce sounds; b) the activation of the mind; c) the activation of the inner “fires” or the ki-

Without doubt there were interactions between the Yogic world and that of music. For example the standard repertoire of the wonderful auditory experiences of the Yogis (heavenly music and inner sounds) included musical instruments (especially those that were also used in religion). On the other hand, the musicians adopted Tantric Yogic knowledge and terms in their vocabulary and applied them as patterns for explaining the physical processes in song and at the same time as a background for interpreting the cosmological content of the Nāda. The Yogic image of the body and the Yogic Nāda practices fused inner and outer worlds, gross and subtle body, corporeality and interiority, and the micro and the macrocosmos, so that all these were references to one another. Precisely the concentration on sound was seen as the best medium for experiencing this interconnectedness. For example the Yogins had recognized that in the body one can sense vibrations and even hear recognizable sounds like those of the external world — from thunder to the humming of bees, if one plugs one’s ears or engages in mantra recitation for a longer period® and continues to feel the final sounds of the seed syllables as subtle vibrations. The body itself was filled with sounds — just like the universe. Like the ether, sound was something that pervaded both the interior and the exterior. Against the background of this type of world-view, it was plausible that the music-loving doctor and polymath Śārṁgadeva should draw the conclusion that the Nāda was inherent in the Brahman, which as absolute being is the origin of the world and pervades it. What applies to the Nāda also applies to song, the music of the “body lute.”

##### 6.3.4 Devotional Nāda-Yoga in South Indian Classical Music

Devotional Nāda-Yoga in South Indian Classical Music 

Music and the devotional orientation gave rise, in contrast to the classical forms of Yoga, to a strong emotionalization and personalization. The religious aspect exists in both North and South Indian music, which since the 16h century has been found in theorized form as hindustāni and karnātaka styles.” However, religiousness is only a priority in South Indian music, which is presumably due to the fact that Southern India was much less affected by Muslim occupation than the North was. 

In India Rāga music was always a very regional phenomenon and was therefore originally also called Deśi music (literally “regional music”). The first theoretical discussion can be found in the second Christian millennium (from the 12th century onward). In this context there was a bifurcation in which two different systems crystallized out, one for the North and one for the South. But clearly this is not apparent until the 16th century. 

In the North Indian (Hindustani) music, most of the texts are of a secular nature, principally in the form of love-related topics, with the exceptions of Bhajan and Kīrtanam. Prominent forms include Dhrupad (a strict vocal composition known since Antiquity), Khyāl (an extension of the Dhrupad form with virtuoso developments), Thumrī (esoteric love songs adopting pronounced folk elements while adding ornament to them), the Bhajans (hymns of praise oriented on purely religious contexts) and Kīrtanas (the musical “narration” of stories of the gods). Above all Tulsīdās (1532-1623), Sūrdās and Mīrā Bai (likewise 16th century) were powerful in religious compositions. But thanks to the Krṣṇa eroticism, it was easy for religious and secular topics to merge. 

By contrast, South Indian (Carnatic) music was almost without exception explicitly religious. Kriti/krtí compositions (classical devotional songs) are typical of this genre. The three musicians Tyāgarāja, Muttu-svāmi Dīkṣitar and Śyāma Śāstri are regarded as the zenith of Carnatic classical music, and Tyāgarāja (a Smārta Brahman from Tan-javur, Tamil Nadu) is thought to be the greatest genius of the three.

##### 6.3.6 From the Liminal to the Sublime and Popular

Even in India, music was not always as divine and uncontentious as it became in classical times. The Nāda-Brahman bliss has a liminal early history and is virtually inconceivable without the theistic taming of music. Lysis and fusion have indeed been a religious and cultural ideal since Upaniṣadic times, but lysis in music and an emotional response to music were not considered desirable in ancient India. Lysis and religion are not always necessarily compatible, and this applies especially in music. It often seems to have less of a disciplinary effect on the listener than of releasing him from his inhibitions. Total dissolution and loss of control are absolutely contrary to the ritual and moral aspect of religions. In ancient India the resulting reservation concerning music, which

In the Brāhmaṇas, the formative texts of the Brahmins, music, dance and theater were therefore by no means well regarded, and even perceived of as an “army of death.” For along time the Gandharvas, the heavenly musicians, were seen as the archetypal hedonists indulging in wine and free love (gandharva-vivāha = “Gandharva wedding”). For this they were not only loved, but also feared. However, religion could not manage completely without the Dionysian aspect. One means of control and rationalization was to count all the material that was sung or recited, and so reduce it to a certain level that should not be exceeded — i.e. the “counting” was exhausted not only in the meter regulating the language material, but also in the quantity of text. When the ritual was over, one was not allowed to continue. This was quite unlike the later endless mantras of the Rām-Bhāktas and Hare-Krishnas, for instance. An

Total drunkenness was regarded (like lysis in music) as a kind of divine obsession. Singing in groups and singing the name and deeds of God (kīrtana) loudly became the religious centerpiece of whole movements, and in many institutionalized bhakti traditions such activities play a vast role. These institutionalized traditions range from one of the oldest — the Vārkarī-Sampradāya (from the 12th/13th cent.) and their annual pilgrimage to Pandharpur (Maharasthra), which for many devotees represents the personal center of their lives — to one of the most recent, the Hare-Krishna devotees (ISKCON), who in this regard follow completely “traditional” patterns (11 their case inspired by the ecstatic Bengali Krsṣṇa-bhakti of Caitanya

of the 16th century). Group singing helps to focus the spirit, absorb feelings and channel them in a devotional way. In the case of Krsṇa devotees it is also associated with dance. If one devotes oneself to music, communal singing with its repeating patterns and its accelerating and decelerating rhythms is capable of generating trance-like states and musical ecstasy. If the cantor whom the others follow is one’s own guru, it is especially easy for music, deity and guru to fuse (and in this moment the world melts away, too) within a great oceanic oneness. The immersion in the “Brahman bliss” of music will be emotionally colored by the love for the teacher. The universally popular bhakti chanting made the Nāda-Brahman into an integral part of popular religion, without the concept itself needing to be known at all. But the concept itself also became part of popular culture. There are religious songs not only to deities in both classical and popular styles, but also to the Nāda-Brahman. Nowadays hardly anyone is familiar with its complex theology and this is not even necessary. The absorbing music is the essential factor, the immediate participation in a sacred cosmos, an inner feeling of the presence of God and a “supermundane” joy that takes hold of all the senses.

6.3.7 

Confluence of Traditions in the Nāda-Brahman and Transreligiosity 

The history of the Nāda-Brahman has not yet been researched. Examination of Śārṁgadeva represents just the beginning. The following period should be given a much closer look than we can undertake in this book. So the theses developed in the following should be seen as hypotheses that require more investigation. 

The philosophical origins of the Nāda-Brahman outlined above, like the devotional turn, are core parts of a field of discourse in which the Nāda-Brahman developed as a cosmic sound pervading the entire world. The key aspect is that it did so not only as a strong, cognitive, intellectually received concept and aesthetic symbol, but also as a mythical substance that could be experienced in an existential sensory manner, especially in music. Brahman, Vāc and Nāda had already had cosmic relevance in the previous history, but their fusion in the Nāda-Brahman, reified in music, and their enrichment through devotion brought about something new that one could call somatic affective participation in the cosmos, or sensory experience of the unity of being postulated by the philosophers. The theistic version was devotional fusion with God, sensory perception of divine delight and physical spiritual participation in the deity, the primordial source of life. The philosophical and theological streams, whose confluence allowed both interpretations (unitary vision of the cosmos and loving participation in God), were generally closely associated with practice - mostly mantra practice — whether it was Vedic, Yogic, Tantric or devotional. 

Overall, a great many streams flowed into the Nāda-Brahman and the understanding of music, especially monistic tendencies, devotionalism and general Indian performance culture and a focus on the voice and sound. In the case of the “inventor” of Nāda-Brahman, Śārṁgadeva, it 

was principally a blending of an Upaniṣadic idea of Brahman, Śivaitic Nāda sound cosmology, Bhartrhari’s Śabda-Brahman, the ancient idea of Vāc (i.e. of the voice as the seat of divinity) and Yoga (used more metaphorically for listening to music). The musical Nāda-Brahman created a new holism that could be experienced through the senses and was capable of many and varied connections because the

*[p. 888]* ^pg912

Nāda-Brahman latently encompassed many things, or else could be interpreted in relation to them: the Advaita-Vedāntic non-dual unity of everything, theism and devotionalism, mantra practice and (ritual) rememberance of divine names, indigenous teaching of aesthetics, OM mysticism, esoteric Yoga traditions, ancient linguistic and numerical speculations, the “otherworldliness of sounds,” and esoteric alphabet mysticism. This resulted in a very complex open-ended unity with a diverse range of potential interpretations and foci, so that music became the medium of deliverance and an instrument for furthering knowledge of cosmic oneness, as well as a potent devotional means of transport to God and a training ground in which a highly cultivated sense of emotional atmospheres could be developed. The Nāda-Brahman became filled with personal devotion, with “love for the featureless,” the transcendental “nameless” (47777), with all possible types of holistic sound experience or unity, which one could tune into via sound. 

Yet the history of Nāda-Brahman did not end with music. The blending of Nāda and Brahman in the Nāda-Brahman was not only continued by many musicians, but also became a trend independent of music and grew to such an extent that, when only Nāda was mentioned (e.g. in Yogic literature), one instantly thought of Nāda-Brahman.!'” In the period that followed, the various traditions that Śārṁgadeva united in the Nāda-Brahman, now saturated with the idea of Nāda-Brahman, detached themselves again from the musical discourse. As the Nāda concept was already familiar in the Yoga tradition, and Yogic ideas of Nāda flowed into the (later) Upaniṣads and joined with Brahman and with OM meditation, it was easy for the Nāda-Brahman to become associated primarily with the Yogic Nāda and the world of inner sounds and be “heard” automatically even when (only) Nāda was mentioned. In more general terms, the Nāda-Brahman was able, following the traditional elements it combined, to generate different models of interpretation: musical, Yogic, Tantric, mantric, Vedāntic, metaphysical, artistically 

aesthetic, 

devotional, mystic, linguistically esoteric and phonically esoteric, all the way to modern esoteric models in which natural science and religion fuse. 2\) A further strong feature in the formation of Nāda-Brahman as well as the history of its reception was the Advaita-Vedānta. The experience of unity and abundance that it offered dissolved the Nāda in the voiceless and soundless silence of pure presence and object-less awareness and this affected the Nāda-Brahman with all the connotations peculiar to the Advaita-Vedānta tradition. Śārṁgadeva’s commentators supported this Vedānta aspect, but they were not able to popularize their de-mythologizing abstraction of the cosmic sound. The sensory and substantial Nāda-Brahman in music and devotion was more powerful than the metaphorical Nāda-Brahman of the Vedānta, which was robbed of its voice and its body. The shift reflects processes of negotiation of emancipatory and participatory world-orientations and alternatives of meaning: language versus sound, semantics versus expressivity, rational abstraction versus body-mind-holism, conceptual thought versus feeling, and silence versus vibration. The question of which was closer to the (non-dual) consciousness was answered in different ways in the various discourse worlds of Sanskrit Hinduism. Yet the dichotomies were rarely played out as rigorously as they are categorized here. They rather tended to manifest themselves in a range of mixed forms. This is also true of the understanding of music in which the Vedānta, as invisible ground-plan, remained very powerful. Ronald Kurt is probably correct to trace the “reference to the purifying power of the Raga and the self-less (“I-less”) being one with God” of musicians socialized in India to the Brahman teaching and a monistic world-view." However, in Indian religious history there are varying answers to how non-duality and cosmic wholeness can be experienced, and the Vedānta was not the only version of monism to flow into music practice. The musical Nāda generated a different quality of experience of “ego-lessness” than did Śaṁñkara’s tat tvam asi, which can be better understood in terms of aesthetics and devotionalism than in terms of pure Vedānta.
