PREFACE 

These essays span a period of fourteen years. The earliest was written in 1960, the latest in 1983. They all represent various attempts to understand the motives and the central concepts of Husserl's transcendental phenomenology, and to locate the latter in the background of other varieties of transcendental philosophy. Implicitly, they also contain a defense of transcendental philosophy, and make attempts to respond to the more familiar criticisms against it. It is hoped that they will contribute to a better understanding not only of Husserl's transcendental phenomenology but also of transcendental philosophy in general. 

The ordering of the essays is not chronological. They are rather divided thematically into three groups. The first group of six essays is concerned with relating Husserlian phenomenology to more contemporary analytic concerns: in fact, the opening essay on Husserl and Frege establishes a certain continuity of concern with my last published book with that title. Of these, Essay 2 was written for an American Philosophical Association, Eastern Division symposium in which the other symposiast was John Searle. The discussion in that symposium concentrated chiefly on the relation between intentionality and causality - which led me to write Essay 6, later read as the Gurwitsch Memorial Lecture at the Society for Phenomenology and Existential Philosophy meetings in 1982 at Penn State. Essay 3 had its origin in an attempt to respond to Jaakko Hintikka's novel interpretation of the Husserlian Noema. For this group of essays, I have greatly profited from discussion with Searle and Hintikka. 

The six essays belonging to the second group, earlier in chronological order of composition, are concerned with understanding the central concept of transcendental subjectivity. This is done by relating it, in Husserl's own thought, to the concepts of "essence," "meaning," and 

*[p. x]* ^pg9

"life-world" (Essays 8 and 10), by contrasting it with the Heideggerian idea of "existence" (Essay 11); by relating it to the more familiar notions of "mind" and "body," and the Cartesianism-behaviorism problematic (Essay 9); by raising questions about its modal properties such as contingency and apodicticity, as also its alleged *Evidenz* (Essay 7); and finally, by comparing the idea of genetic constitution of objectivity and Logic wit!1 similar ideas in Quine and Piaget (Essay 12). 

Given this in large measures new and more powerful formulation of the idea of transcendental subjectivity, the three essays in the third group more directly take up the question, what form transcendental philosophy can and should take today, if it is to meet the contemporary challenges. Essay 13 argues that in Husserl's own thinking, a genuine transcendental philosophy can be separated off from the essentialism (which Husserl believed in) and need not be restricted to a given body of scientific knowledge as its explanandum. The task is to give a theory of constitution of meanings, rather than a regressive account of the possibility of a body of truth-claims. Essay 15, presented first in a symposium with Gadamer, responds to the powerful hermeneutic critique of transcendental philosophy, which, first anticipated in the Hegelian critique of Kant, resurfaces with great power in Heidegger's critique of Husserl. Essay 14 sums up my resulting conception of what a transcendental philosophy can be like today. 

Central to these essays are: the Husserlian conception of *noema* as the sense of intentional acts; the distinction between essence and meaning; a certain understanding of the thesis of the "parallelism" of the empirical and the transcendental; a conception of transcendental subjectivity which appropriates into its texture linguistically, corporeality and historicity without limiting it to them; and a freeing of transcendental philosophy from any given privileged representation of the world. 

Although I share a great deal of common concern with the linguistic and semantic interpreters of Husserl, I am of the view that the. linguistic-semantic interpretation and the transcendental-philosophical understanding need each other. Although some of the essays included in this volume, especially those in the second group had already prepared the way, it was the invitation by the University of Calcutta to give the K.C. Bhattacharya Memorial Lectures for 1979 which first led me to write down my thoughts on the future of transcendental philosophy. A Visiting Fellowship at the Visva-Bharati University in the summer of 1979, provided the facilities to write down the lectures. The text of those lectures, originally intended to be published as a book, lies dispersed in 

*[p. xi]* ^pg10

these essays, especially in those of the first and third groups of essays. In putting this volume together, I am under a debt of obligation to the Registrar of the University of Calcutta and the Electors of the K.C. Bhattacharya Memorial Lectureship, for electing me to that honor and thereby giving me an opportunity not merely to share my thoughts with colleagues in Calcutta, but also to renew an association which, speaking for myself, is nothing less than being a spiritual tie. The late Philosopher K.C. Bhattacharya's son, Kalidas Bhattacharya, my former teacher in Calcutta and later Vice-Chancellor at Visva Bharati, chaired the last of these lecture sessions. He has inspired me as well as a generation of Indian philosophers to undertake creative thinking. This volume is dedicated to him as a token of deep gratitude.

INTRODUCTORY ESSAY: 

ON THE POSSIBILITY OF TRANSCENDENTAL PHILOSOPHY 

The best way to demonstrate the possibility of something is to show its actuality, for actuality implies possibility. At least since Kant, transcendental philosophies have been on the scene. However, such simple demonstration of the possibility of transcendental philosophy has not been effective and is not likely to be so - so strong is the presumption that transcendental philosophy just could not be possible, or, if it *was* possible earlier, it is not possible *now.* This last claim introduces a new point: namely, that certain types of thinking are just not possible today, either because they have been proved to be unworkable, invalid, false, misguided, or because the historical conditions of their possibility are no more there. I do not know of any such *proof* of the invalidity of transcendental philosophy. There is also no reason to begin by conceding the historicism that underlies the alternate claim that today such foundationalist thinking is not *possible* (or, is it meant "not desirable"?). 

In this introductory essay I will consider some ways of showing that transcendental philosophy is possible. I will ask about its logical possibility, psychological possibility, subjective possibility and phenomenological possibility. Under the question of "logical possibility," I will take into account an argument by Donald Davidson which is taken to prove, by a sort of transcendental argument, the impossibility of transcendental philosophy. I By the question of "psychological possibility" I mean the question about motivation: how is it possible for one, within the naIve, natural attitude, to be motivated to take a "transcendental turn"? Granted that logical impossibility is not demonstrated and that one can be motivated to take the turn, how can one have the proper

Finally, is transcendental philosophy a matter of the right sort of *argument,* or is it possible, by the proper sort of *reflection,* to bring to intuitive evidence an entire domain of transcendental experience: this is what I want to discuss under the question of "phenomenological possibility." 

I. LOGICAL POSSIBILITY 

I f the transcendental philosopher wants to establish the necessary validity of our conceptual scheme (as against the sceptic), that enterprise presupposes that there are other alternative conceptual schemes, or at least that other conceptual schemes are conceivable. Now Davidson, as is well known, has argued that the very idea of alternate conceptual schemes is unintelligible. If Davidson's argument is valid, then he has succeeded in proving that the very idea of an alternate conceptual scheme makes no sense. If the idea of conceptual schemes alternate to ours makes no sense, it does not make any more sense to speak of a common conceptual scheme shared by all mankind. As a consequence, the very distinction between conceptual scheme and uninterpreted reality or data, must also be given up. To give up that distinction is to give up all transcendental arguments, and so all foundationalist transcendental philosophies. Consequently, Davidson may be looked upon as having given a transcendental argument to prove the pointlessness of all transcendental arguments. Let us see how that fateful demonstration proceeds. 

Davidson distinguishes between two sorts of views (about many possible "worlds"). Some mean by "other possible worlds," what we would describe "by redistributing truth values over sentences in various systematic ways," which basically uses our present language and a fixed system of concepts. This is not what Davidson is out to criticise. He is concerned rather with those who envisage the possibility of describing one and the same world from "radically different" points of view (or, "conceptualise" one and the same "content" by "radically different" conceptual schemes). The contrast between these two views is thus succinctly put by Davidson: "Strawson's many imagined worlds are seen (or heard) - anyway described - from the same point of view; Kuhn's one world is seen from different points of view.,,2 Davidson claims to prove that the second metaphor is unintelligible. 

2\. Ibid., p. 9.

Underlying Davidson's critique are the following assumptions. It is assumed, first, that to speak of alternate conceptual schemes is to speak of *radically different* schemes in the sense that they must have to be mutually untranslatable; secondly, that a conceptual scheme must be "expressible" in a language (this one, in fact, is implied by the first); that a theory of meaning is reducible to a theory of truth (and that the latter theory is encapsulated in Taski's convention T). Given these three assumptions the putative alternate conceptual schemes, being mutually untranslatable and, in particular, being untranslatable to my (our) home language, would make no sense to me (us). From the third assumption it follows that the idea of an alternate conceptual scheme (in this radical sense), which could be true even if untranslatable, has to be rejected, for the notion of truth cannot be divorced from that of translation, if the convention T embodies our best intuitions about "truth." Now, how does this critique of the idea of "alternate conceptual scheme" work against the idea of transcendental philosophy? This is how it works: if the very idea of alternate conceptual scheme makes no sense, the idea of conceptual scheme itself has to go. With it has to go the distinction between scheme and content. It would then be uncalled for, to legitimise the application of a conceptual scheme to given contents. The *quaestio juris* of the Kantian philosophy cannot even be meaningfully raised. We have here a fourth assumption, in addition to the three already mentioned, namely, that the task of a transcendental philosophy is to legitimise a conceptual framework. 

In order to be able to show that Davidson's argument does not succeed in demonstrating the impossibility of transcendental philosophy in general, I will question some, if not all, of these assumptions. I will not question the second assumption. Although a conceptual scheme need not have to be a language, I will grant that it can be expressed in a language. I reject the third assumption, namely, that Taski's convention T expresses our best intuitions about truth, and that a theory of meaning is reducible to a theory of truth (of the Tarskian or any other sort). The idea of "alternate conceptual scheme" which Davidson criticises is a red herring. There are no such untranslatable languages, nor is the idea of transcendental philosophy committed to this strong notion. It is not committed to such a strong notion, for in the first place, all transcendental thinking is not bound to deal with the question of legitimisation; secondly, even when it does raise *quaestio juris,* as in Kant's case, it is not to justify one scheme *as against* other alternative schemes; and thirdly, a transcendental philosophy is possible which does not undertake to legitimise any particular conceptual scheme. All these points can be made even in connection with the Kantian

*[p. xvi]* ^pg14

project, [^3]not to speak of other sorts of transcendental philosophies. 

To the issue as between (i) conceiving of many possible worlds from the same point of view and (ii) seeing the same world from many different points of view, I will return later in this essay in section VII. 

For the present, I will only insist on the need for admitting a minimal form-content distinction, even if not the full-fledged Kantian distinction. The "content," then, like the Husserlian *Hyle,* may be conceived as a "boundary-condition" for noematic discourse,[^4] as the notion of the bare given that, without supporting any infallible epistemiC claims, is satisfied with recognising the passivity and receptivity of sensory experience. A more generalised version of this minimal concept (which is formulated in terms of sensory experience) would be one according to which within every level ·of discourse there would be appropriate distinction between the given and the scheme, but *no one that runs through all the levels of discourse,* a version that may have to be worked out along the lines of the Hegelian phenomenology.

II I. TRANSCENDENTAL REFLECTION: [^7]PHENOMENOLOGICAL 

POSSIBILITY OF TRANSCENDENTAL PHILOSOPHY 

If transcendental philosophy as to uncover the way or ways consciousness or subjectivity constitutes objectivity, it in any case must involve consciousness's coming to reflect on its own operations. This explicit and methodical turning back of consciousness on itself is precisely what I mean here by "reflection." Reflection, in this sense, has to be distinguished, on the one hand, from that pre-reflective translucency or reflexivity which characterises all our conscious life, its basic *Bei-sichsein,* and on the other hand, from what passes by the name of "introspection" in older forms of empirical psychology. It is by appropriate reflection that the modes of object-constitution by subjectivity can be made available as phenomenological data rather than as hypothetical, theoretical posits. 

I will not attempt, in this introductory essay, to sketch a *theory* of refexion, what I will give is rather a typology of it. Reflexion may be either empirical or transcendental. Transcendental reflection should also be distinguished from merely logical reflection. As Kant puts it, logical reflection is merely *comparative.* Given two or more objective representations at hand, logical reflection "compares" them, i.e. looks into whether relations such as inclusion or exclusion, compatibility or incompatibility obtain amongst them. Transcendental reflection would be concerned with the conditions of the possibility of such objective "comparison" of representations. Consequently, whereas logical reflection is a purely "analytic" procedure, transcendental reflection asks, how could given representations relate to whatever is their object or objects. 

Now transcendental reflection, as reflection upon consciousness in its object-constituting role, may be either reflection on the noetic acts or reflection on their noematic contents. Thus it may be either noetic reflection or noematic reflection. Phenomenological reflection, i.e. the reflection that is practised in transcendental phenomenology, begins with noematic reflection, and after delineating the structure of a noema moves on to reflecting upon the acts whose noema it happens to be. Although each of these steps *may be* regarded as a step in an *argument* (such as: a noema must be the noema of an act; an act must be someone's performance), a truly phenomenological reflection must be able to fill in these empty argumentative intentions with confirmatory intuitive evidence. 

The noematic reflection on its part may be either phenomenological[^9] or critical. Of this latter sort is the neo-Kantian *geltungstheoretische* reflection, or transcendental-logical reflection.

IV. SUBJECTIVE POSSIBILITY: THE PROBLEM OF ACCESS 

Granted that a transcendental philosophy is logically possible and also that there is a broad-spectrum method that opens up the transcendental domain of subjectivity for our inquiry, one may still want to know: how can we at all come to exercise this method? What I am raising is the problem of access. To say that it is transcendental reflection which establishes the access is precisely to beg the issue. If transcendental reflection is reflection on the transcendental, i.e. the constituting subjectivity, how can I at all reflect on *that* if the only subjectivity I am familiar with is my everyday, empirical, conscious experience? This everyday empirical consciousness, far from being constituting, is a part of the world, causally connected with my body and its natural (and cultural) environment. How can I establish contact with *(my?)* transcendental subjectivity? 

Hegel, more than any other philosopher, realised the force of this problem of access, and wrote his *Phenomenology* of 1807 as a response. The way he posed the question is: how can natural consciousness rise up to the level of philosophical consciousness? To put it in the present perspective, any reflection on consciousness, within the natural attitude, will be an empirical reflection and therefore cannot yield anything oftranscendental significance. Hegel's plan was, as is well known, to lead natural consciousness along a pathway having several well defined stages or "shapes of consciousness" - the journey culminating at a point where philosophy in the strict sense (i.e. Hegel's *Science of Logic)* can begin. The account consists of description and critique, each "shape" first tells its own story and is then subjected to a critique which leads beyond it. In this account, one is still faced with the question: who makes the critique, who examines the shapes of natural consciousness, what criterion is employed in this critique and what is the source of this criterion if not the natural consciousness itself? Are we not, in this account, already presupposing a standpoint that lies beyond the natural? 

*[p. xxi]* ^pg19

And, yet, is it not precisely to such a standpoint that the story is intended to lead us? 

Husserl is the other transcendental philosopher who seriously and incessantly thought about the problem of access, thus giving one the impression as though he was a philosopher of perpetual "beginning." To cut a long story short, again, we need to recall that it is the methods of *epoche* and reduction lO that provided for Husserl the access to the transcendental domain. By bracketing existence so as to be left only with essence, then by bracketing all transcendence so that one is left with the purely immanent experience with its act-noema structure, and finally by suspending the natural world-belief so that what is left over is the domain of consciousness within which the "world" is intended as a noematic structure and the world-belief is recognised *as a belief,* the meditating philosopher finds himself *living* the life of a transcendental ego. 

There are several questions that arise. As in the case of the Hegelian "access," one may ask: is it not the case that here too one is presupposing a conception of the transcendental to begin with? Why, otherwise, should one perform the reductions and the *epoche?* One may also ask, what could possibly motivate this radical "conversion"? A still more radical sceptical doubt would be: suppose someone, appropriately motivated, performs all the reductions, the eidetic, the phenomenological and the transcendental, but whatever changes, transformations or "conversions" occur, take place only in the interiority of that person's inner life as a result of his "voluntary" change of attitude. What light could, then, such an "inner' transformation throw upon the nature of consciousness - not to speak of the nature of the world? Postponing consideration of the question of motivation to the next section, I will briefly respond to the other two questions. 

The first worry is unfounded. For, to talk of an access is also to talk of access to something. The query itself requires that we have a conception of where we want to go, the destination. Unless we have some idea of the destination, it would be pointless to talk of a path. One cannot begin doing transcendental philosophy unless one knows what to look for or, rather, the sort of thing that would satisfy the explanatory needs of that kind of philosophy. 

The last of the three questions raised above suggests that the metaphor of path or access is misleading. That metaphor suggests as though the transcendental is on the other side of the natural, as though we are

To "purify" one's own conscious life by stripping it off naive interpretations, self-understandings and also of ontological commitments, is neither to transport oneself to another land nor to "transform" our everyday consciousness to something else. It is rather to exhibit its true nature, even in naivete, in its role as interpreting both itself and the other, as constituting its, and a common, our, world. The transcendental role lies concealed "anonymously" within the empirical. Transcendental philosophy seeks to uncover this role. 

Vacaspati Misra, in the introduction to his famous commentary *Bhamati* on Samkara's Brahmasutrabha~yam formulates the paradox of beginning thus: "How can one at all begin an inquiry into the nature of *Brahman-Atman* (which is the subject matter of Samkara's treatise)? If one knows the subject matter, one needs no inquiry - for no one undertakes an inquiry into what is known. If one does not know the subject matter, no inquiry is possible, for one would not know what to inquire into." 

Vacaspati's own solution is simple and straightforward. The possibility of inquiry requires that its subject matter be known in general features, but not determined in its specific features. These two conditions, he says, are fulfilled in the case on his hand: we all have a certain familiarity with our selves and so with *Atman,* but there is considerable difference of opinion as to whether the self is the body, or an inner substance, or a universal spirit. Hence the possibility of inquiry. 

Can we say the same in our present context? I am familiar with my own life of consciousness. I am also aware of conferring meaning upon, or interpreting what is given to me. But this familiarity with my consciousness is shrouded by a powerful naturalistic interpretive scheme: belief in the inescapability of the causal order and the understanding of consciousness as a part of nature. The reductions provide the means for seeing that consciousness, in fact, is not mundane. Here the third sceptical doubt is relevant. The reductions do not simply change my inner attitude. Consciousness is such that with a change of attitude towards *it,* the mode of consciousness itself undergoes a transformation. If I think of the real table before me as a merely imagined one, the actual table does not become a merely imagined one. But if I am perceiving a man over there, and "neutralise" the belief that there is a real man over there, the *altered,* neutralised "perceiving-that-there-is-a-man-over-there" both *is* and *is not* the original act of perceiving. It is the same, for it is still my perception and of the same object; it is *not* the same, 

*[p. xxiii]* ^pg21

for it is onto logically non-committed, the positing of its object is internal to the noematic structure but not positing out there in the world. The empirical both *is* and is *not* the transcendental. 

V. THE PROBLEM OF MOTIVATION: PSYCHOLOGICAL POSSIBILITY 

What could motivate a philosopher, a mundane creature immersed in world-belief, to take the transcendental turn? As far as I can see, one or more from amongst several motives may motivate such radical turn, but, to be sure, all these are *mundane* motives. There can be no transcendental motivation for doing transcendental philosophy. From within mundane experience, there are occasions as well as needs - both theoretical and practical - which may motivate philosophising in this manner. Consider Kant: his motives, in looking for the transcendental foundations of knowledge and morality, were as much historical (how to reconcile science with religion, for example) as epistemological (how is scientific knowledge as a body of synthetic yet a priori judgments possible?). Husserl's motive was, to start with, to clarify the basic concepts of logic and mathematics (which are historically accomplished disciplines) by looking for their "origin" in the eidetic structures of the mental life of the thinker, but - later on - to realise the immanent telos in the historically inherited idea of philosophy as the first philosophy. There may be many other possible motivations, which are effective within the na·ivity of world-belief, but which, when they are permitted to work out their goal, bring that very world-belief into the scope of critical reflection. Thus transcendental reflection must be motivated by mundane interests. 

VI. THE SPECTRE OF FOUNDATIONALISM - THE CARTESIAN AND 

THE WITTGENSTEINIAN PARADIGMS 

According to many contemporary philosophers, foundationalism should be shunned. But no one - to my knowledge - has given any satisfactory reason why foundationalism *as such* should be rejected. There is no one sort of foundationalism. If some of them have not worked well, they have to go or would need to be modified. But the idea of seeking for a foundation of experience and knowledge is not thereby - i.e. with the failure of the received theories - proved to be unworkable. Two major factors that have contributed to fostering the antifoundationalist temper are, of course, *historicism,* and its attendant 

*[p. xxiv]* ^pg22

*relativism.* But neither historicism nor relativism logically entails antifoundationalism, they demand a concept of the foundation of experience and knowledge that is rich and flexible enough to be able to accommodate and ground the supposed (historically) changing or alternate "conceptual frameworks." I think it is possible to have such a concept of foundation, but for that purpose it is also necessary to limit the excesses of both historicism and relativism. I will return to this point in the next section. For the present, I want to insist that although transcendental philosophy has to be, by the very nature of its project, foundationalistic, how viable it is depends upon how this foundation is formulated. 

Even if we leave aside for the present the issues about historicism and relativism, there is another source of anxiety about the sort of foundation the transcendental philosophies aim at: they all seem to be thinking within what is often called the "Cartesian paradigm." The putative foundation is sought for within the interiority (and, privacy!) of one's mental life. As opposed to this paradigm, with its attendant problem of privacy, other transcendental philosophers (Apel, Schnadelbach) have opted for the Wittgensteinian paradigm of a publicly observable language-game. But in this choice, one makes a trade-off. If the Cartesian paradigm gets bogged down in the privacy of the inner, the other paradigm, equally one-sidedly, permits recognition only of the public and leaves no room for the private. What we need is a more powerful paradigm. It is here that the phenomenological theory of subjectivity comes in handy. Its inherent intentionality breaks the bonds of interiority, its ideal noemata escape privacy. 

What however is more important for my general purpose in this essay, is to urge that the conception of the mental as the inner, the private, is a mundane concept, tied to the mundane concept of an empirical ego (who owns the mental) and the methodological concept of introspection or empirical reflection. Transcendental subjectivity as the *field* in which all mundane concepts have their origin, cannot itself be described in those mundane terms, or rather if it is described in mundane terms one should not forget the double meaning of the words used: one in the mundane, the other in the transcendental context. 

VII. "NO PRIVILEGED REPRESENTATION OF THE WORLD" 

\- THE WORLD AND WORLDS 

It is true that many of the transcendental philosophies have sought to support some one privileged representation of the world. Thus Kant 

*[p. xxv]* ^pg23

lent philosophical support to the world of Newtonian physics and Euclidean geometry. We now can tell, and it is commonplace to urge against Kant, that since the Newtonian physics has been "superseded," (if not falsified) by other systems, the Kantian project is historically dated. In fact, one suspects that any transcendental project is bound to come to grief in this manner. Human knowledge being a growing process, and its large historical changes not being commensurable and cumulative, no permanent matrix is possible, to use Rorty's words, which can *a priori* circumscribe the range of human knowledge and imagination. And yet, transcendental philosophy wants precisely to determine such a matrix. 

But Kant not only gave a theory of science (of his time), but he also gave a theory of constitution of *objectivity in general.* He gives an account of what are the *a priori* conditions of the possibility of objective experience in general. Thus he gives a theory of perception as well. Now it may again be true that his conception of objectivity, particularly his account of the *a priori* conditions of objective experience, is tied to his theory of physics, and one comprehensive theory is made to serve both the purposes. This makes Kant's overall theory elegant, but also weak. We need a general theory of objectivity that would be neutral as against competing physical theories. The theory of perception has to be detached from theory of physics, since perception is prior to physics. 

As soon as we do that, it becomes clear that a transcendental philosophy need not be tied to a privileged representation of the world in terms of anyone historically accomplished theory. There are indeed several options at this point. 

One may, while still doing transcendental philosophy, restrict oneself to perception and uncover the intentionalities that go to constitute perceptual objectivity. Or, one may justly recognise that theoretical thinking, by virtue of higher order intentionalities, accomplishes a new level of objectivity (let us call it the Kantian), and then proceed to uncover the transcendental foundation of *theory in general,* of the essence of theory, or of the theory-form (to use Husserl's word). One is still at a level that is neutral as against the historical growth of knowledge. 

Once one looks beyond these a-historical and so "neutral" contexts, the spectre of historicism and relativism looms large. If we cannot speak of *the* world, not even of *the* life-world, is then a sort of relativity of worlds the last truth of things? In fact, the very idea of truth is threatened by being relativised to a world. The idea of truth as distinguished from truth-in-a-world (recall an analogous concept "true-in-L") would then seem to have been rendered empty. Does not such a relativism undercut the very possibility of transcendental philosophy? 

*[p. xxvi]* ^pg24

If the essays in this volume have a point, it is this. It is true that in the past the transcendental philosophies have had their starting points in a monistic conception of truth and so of *the* world. That monism has no future. The idea of pluralism of worlds has come to stay. A relativism that follows from the recognition of this pluralism is one of the desiderata of modern culture. The transcendental philosopher, therefore, cannot start with a preferred representation of the world, or - with a preferred science or even a preferred ontology. He cannot any longer undertake to *justify* anyone world picture or "version," to use Goodman's term, to the exclusion of other versions. If we are to find a way out of relativism, it can only be *after* the phenomenon of relativism has been granted its initial recognition. But where can we go from there? What path lies open for us? 

Here phenomenology provides us with an invaluable guidance. Each world, or rather each version, is to be viewed as a noematic structure. Just as the identity of an object is constituted by the system of noemata through which "one and the same" object is presented, so also in the case under consideration: the one world - not in the sense of the totality of all worlds, but in the sense of that whose versions they all are - may be looked upon as that *regulative* concept which not only orders the various quasi-incommensurable worlds, but also delineates the path that shall lead us out of a hopeless relativism towards communication and understanding. This last point entails that while we need to give modern relativism its due, it is no less important that we clearly see its limitations. 

First of all, it needs to be emphasised that the different worlds, many of whom are seemingly incommensurable, are not all totally disconnected from each other. On the contrary, many of them intersect and overlap, thereby constituting a common, shared domain. Secondly, be they incommensurables or not, once the na"ive ontological claim is bracketed, *a* world becomes *a* world-noema. The various worldnoemata do not any longer have *the same sort of* conflict amongst each other, as the various worlds had. Thirdly, each world-noema may be correlated to - or constituted by - appropriate noetic acts (acts of interpretation, theory-construction, etc.). With this noesis-noema correlation, we gain a fresh ground that is not itself relativistic. The non-relativistic foundation that was initially lost with the demise of a monistic theory of world, is now recovered at the level of noesis-noema correlation. Finally, since it is always possible, in principle, to ascertain, and thereby to relive in its essential features the sorts of interpretive acts that go to constitute a world, howsoever foreign, the correlation-structure with regard to any given world may be recovered as a structure 

*[p. xxvii]* ^pg25

within the sUbjective life of *any* thinking ego. The different worlds, then, find their" origin," or the origin of their senses as worlds, within the subjective life of a transcendental ego. 

Underlying the possibility of any world whatever then - or, of the sense "world" - is the general structure of the transcendentally purified life of consciousness. This is the same as the ultimate, not further objectifiable, *world-horizon* which may be our only hope today in our search for *the* world. But this world-horizon cannot fill in the place vacated by the Absolute of Metaphysics. 11 As Findlay has insisted, any particular world might not have existed and so is contingent, but the horizon as that which makes any world whatsoever possible, the structure of transcendental subjectivity, cannot be meaningfully denied. As an *a priori* structure, this horizon is closed, but it is - again learning from Findlay - open-ended with regard to its contents. 

Transcendental philosophy thus may - indeed must - begin with a pluralism of worlds, but would overcome this pluralism *from within,* rather than by opting arbitrarily for a preferred world-view. 

I am now in a position to speak to the issue so forcefully formulated by Davidson. Are the different worlds all from the same point of view, or are they from different points of view? I do not think anyone would want to deny that there are different points of view from which one and the same thing can be seen, one and the same subject matter can be studied. But what needs to be denied is that there are *radically* different points of view, such that these points of view or the associated' 'conceptual frameworks," or possibly even the perceptual views, are totally incommensurable and mutually untranslatable. I quite agree with Davidson that radical difference in this sense makes no sense. But between the view that there are such incommensurable worlds (or, mutually untranslatable languages, totally unintelligible alternate conceptual frameworks) and the view that all talk of possible non-actual worlds involves nothing more than "redistribution of truth-values" over sentences in our present language "in various systematic ways;" 12 or, for that matter, between the conception of many imagined worlds all described from the same point of view, and the conception of many worlds as but different perspectives on the same content, there is an intermediate position which I want to adopt. For formulating this intermediate position, the idea of "transcendental ego" is precisely what we need. 

We have to distinguish between two levels of discourse: the internal

and the external. At the internal level, there are radically different worlds, conceptual frameworks, languages - such that for the person who *nafvely lives within one,* the others are "bare others," at most "interesting," but "make no sense." Translatability and intelligibility are at most *ideals,* but never meant to work out. Translation, understanding and communication take place within a common, shared world. So far, this is a valid description of our experience of our own (shared) world and of the "bare other," the alien world that "makes no sense." At this level, there is a home language. 

One requires, however, if I am to be able to speak of alternate conceptual schemes, that I must be able to translate the others into my own; or mine into someone else's. This requirement has nothing to do with the primacy of the English language or, for that matter, of any other language. What it requires is that the languages must be mutually translatable. But when I assert this, I am not taking the "internal" standpoint, but rather the' 'external" standpoint of a transcendental ego, for whom any language is as good as any other, before whose gaze all possible worlds are spread out and none is more his own than any other. The transcendental ego's is no standpoint: all possible standpoints are arraigned before his gaze. The transcendental ego has no "home" language. 

Thus, the (empirical) person living in his world, speaking his language, using his conceptual framework, is subject to a point of view of his own, shared by its community - *in different degrees* by his age. But he does not, in his pre-reflective na"ivity, know that he sees the world *from a standpoint.* He lives in, perceives, knows the world, the only world that is there. That, however, he is subject to a standpoint, a perspective, a conceptual framework is brought out by reflection. But to be able to survey all possible points of view, conceptual frameworks, languages objectively - as making sense to each other, therefore as commensurable (and mutually translatable), one needs to take up a stance, which is none other than that of a transcendental ego. 

The thesis of relativity of worlds is an initial response of reflection. But this thesis of relativity has to be limited by the thesis of the common horizon within which these many standpoints are after all possible. The *one* world is not the common *content* to which the different worlds or versions provide or apply different conceptual schemes. The one world is rather the regulative ideal which is *being constituted* through the mutually overlapping, coinciding, conflicting plethora of worldnoemata. The many worlds are then neither gotten by redistribution of truth values for sentences in the home language, nor are they different conceptualisations of one and the same pre-existent world. They are 

*[p. xxix]* ^pg27

noemata *of* one world, but the one world is also *being* constituted through them. 

VIII . TRANSCENDENTAL SUBJECTIVITY AS THE ULTIMATE, 

EXPLANATORY PRINCIPLE 

Since there is no one concept of *explanation,* it may very well appear to some that the so called transcendental subjectivity really explains nothing. It does not explain *why* experience runs the course it does, *why* the world is like as it happens to be, *why* the scientific theorising has taken the course or has had the history that it has, and so on and so forth. However, all that this shows is that if transcendental subjectivity explains all these, then it must be explaining them in a somewhat different *sense.* What precisely is this *sense?* 

First of all, transcendental philosophy does not want to give any explanation of things or facts. It only seeks to account for the possibility of *meanings.* Instead of asking "why is there material nature?" (or, "How did nature come into being?"), it asks: "How are the concepts "matter" and "nature" constituted?" If one were to answer the first sort of question, one would have to tell a story such as to be found in scientific cosmologies or theological doctrines of creation. If one were to answer the second sort of question, one would have to return to the sorts of intentional experiences (and the sorts of ways in which those experiences have to be ordered) in which something could be presented as a "material thing" or as "nature." Consciousness must have a certain structure such that something like a "world" may at all be possible, or that a "theory" as a structure of propositions be possible. The Kantian idea of being the *a priori* condition of possibility still defines the sense of transcendentality. 

Even if transcendental subjectivity is the condition of the possibility of objectivity and world and science, from the structure of that subjectivity it is not possible to deduce what in details the world and our science, i.e., our ontology and our theory would be like. There is no route from consciousness to the world. For philosophy, the route is from given, i.e. already constituted meanings, to their genesis. Transcendental philosophy therefore has no answer to the sorts of why-questions I asked at the beginning of this section. 

There is a certain contingency in history which transcendental philosophy cannot eliminate. It makes us understand the sense of history, or rather the genesis of its sense, without resolving it into a necessary and inevitable progression of categories. 

*[p. xxx]* ^pg28

In order to be able to bring out the peculiar nature of this as an explanatory principle, I will compare the idea of the transcendental subjectivity with three other candidates for the status of "ultimate principle": the God of religion, the Pure Consciousness *(Suddha-Caitanya)* of Advaita Vedanta and the Hegelian Absolute Spirit. (a) The God of a developed theistic religion is the creator of the world, so that all items of the world down to the minutest details are due to him, they all carry the impress of his infinite wisdom and infinite goodness. But still God transcends the world, is beyond it and other than it; and the world could *not* have been *logically deduced* from His nature, for in His infinite creativity, He is still free. (b) The Pure Consciousness of Advaita Vedanta is the underlying Reality of the world, not merely of the actual but of all possible worlds. The world is not the creation of Brahman (= Pure Consciousness), not an emanation out of its being, not to speak of being a logical consequence of its nature (a la Spinoza). The world is an appearance of Brahman. As there can be no appearance without a reality that appears, the world needs Brahman, but the Real does not need to appear in a way that conceals its true nature (A rope need not appear, to an erring person, as a snake, and so on). Without Brahman, there can be no world, but Brahman does not need the world. The dependence is one-sided. Appearance presupposes Reality, but Reality does not need to appear. This does not rule out an immanent, intra-mundane (e.g. scientific) explanation of items in the world, but the world as a whole is an inexplicable phenomenon projected on Brahman by a beginningless (but to end with metaphysical knowledge) Ignorance. (c) The Hegelian Absolute Spirit is the *immanent* reason of the world, both of nature and of history. As immanent reason, it as much needs the world as the world needs it. As a concrete universal, it is not a pure identity that excludes and is indifferent to all differences (as the Pure Consciousness of Vedanta is), but includes them all. It is an identity that, like the identity of an organism, maintains itself in and through its differences. Being the *reason* of the world, the science of it - the only science, strictly speaking - is Logic which lays bare the internal process of development of the *Begriff* or the categorial structure of the Spirit. 

The Pure Consciousness of Vedanta and the God of theism stand at opposite ends of a scale, as explanatory principles considered from the perspective of what is it that they are supposed to explain. The former explains the mere possibility of an (illusory) appearance, not its actuality. The latter explains the actuality of the world. The former makes no claim to explain the details of the world, leaving that to the empirical sciences. The latter precisely wants to do so. The Vedantin mystic may 

*[p. xxxi]* ^pg29

"see," "experience" the *presence* of Brahman in all beings, in a blade of grass or blowing of the wind or a piece of rock - as the theist sees the *hand* of God, His infinite wisdom and goodness. But the Vedantin claims to see *no reason why* that mundane item exists, he claims to experience its truth; the theist sees the *reason* of its being, namely God's will. The Hegelian Absolute Spirit, being *the* concrete Universal and the immanent reason of both nature and history, is taken to *explain all* things whatsoever, but as Hegel's response to Herr Krug's challenge makes it clear, what things there are to be accounted for depends upon the theory under consideration so that the sundry sticks and stones which constitute our everyday world are taken up and transformed and transmuted in the final view of things. As a result the explanandum and the explanans in a way are made to suit each other. There is nothing, then, that falls outside of the life of the Absolute Spirit. The sticks and stones, pens and hammers, common folks - all nevertheless are left out. They do not belong to that ontology which the science of Logic constitutes. 

Of the three, then, it is only the God of theism who is putatively an explanation of every item in the world. None of the other two is - The Pure Consciousness of Vedanta admittedly is not, for from the Absolute point of view the world is *not;* the Hegelian Spirit fails to explain the sundry items of the world for, as in the case of all *theory,* it can explain only after the explanandum has been suitably "transformed and transm uted. " 

The transcendental subjectivity is in one respect closer to the Pure Consciousness of Vedanta, in another to the Absolute spirit of Hegel. It is closer to the Pure Consciousness of Vedanta, for it has a certain neutrality as against various possible worlds, it is not committed to any one world or anyone science, it is concerned with possibilities; it is closer to the Hegelian Absolute Spirit inasmuch as it is not the merely underlying but *uninvolved* basis of all things, but rather the field in which all meanings, but not things, are constituted. (To show that things are constituted as well, one has to show further that things are but tissues of meanings.) 

IX. PHILOSOPHY AND TRUTH 

Is not philosophy after all concerned with the *truth* of things? Transcendental philosophy, not unlike logical positivism, seems to relegate the concern with factual truth (answers to questions such as what the world is like and what is the self) to empirical sciences and 

*[p. xxxii]* ^pg30

reserve for itself only the (second order) concern with meanings. Of course, there is a difference between transcendental philosophy and logical positivism even *in this regard* (There are, to be sure, enormous differences in other respects.) The questions of *factual* import are assigned by transcendental philosophy not to the empirical sciences alone, but to all first order concerns with things: religious, moral, social and political, for example. But is it not the case that by removing itself from a first order concern with the world and by restricting itself to (second and next higher orders, if necessary) meanings, does it not abdicate the true and proper function of philosophy? 

It does indeed look like that. There are however two redeeming features that may reverse the picture. First of all, the meanings with which transcendental philosophy is concerned are not merely linguistic meanings (which some of them are), not merely meanings of subjective intentional acts (which again they all are, to be sure), they are also meanings a/things. Consequently, the study of these meanings does not yield, as with logical positivism, merely facts about how language is used (or consequences of such facts), but the *inmost* truths about the possibility of things being what they are. 

One speaks of dying to live; of giving up immediate self interest to fulfil a higher interest; of disinterestedness itself as a supreme interest. We are facing, in the case of transcendental philosophy, a similar paradox. One turns away from the sundry truths about the world and about items in the world, only to face up to a deeper truth of things at the level of their constitution. At the first order level, search for truth is characterised by conflicts between points of view, between religions, between moral systems, between theories. These conflicts generate strong passions, even in the theorist (where the idea of "theory" contains the idea of a dispassionate viewing.) Transcendental philosophy removes itself from this scene, not in order to escape from the battle field, not to shirk the responsibility of finding the truth, but in order to discover the truth at the only level that is beyond relativity, and is yet compatible with the fact of relativity.

II 

With this historical misunderstanding set aside, I now propose to turn to more systematic issues about overcoming psychologism as they were encountered by Frege and Husserl. Although the two men agreed in their opposition to psychologism as a theory of logic, they held different views about the nature of psychology itself, and, more importantly, about the concept of subjectivity. 

From his rather scanty statements about psychology it appears that Frege thought of psychology as a *descriptive* science of actual mental processes. The laws of psychology are, for him, like laws of nature: they are universals in the mental realm.[^12] Mental processes happen in accordance with these laws. IV 

We now come to realise why psychologism is a tempting theory to fall into, why a logical refutation, such as Husserl's in the *Prolegomena,* does not dispel its attractions once and forever. The situation is not unlike the status of speculative metaphysics in Kant's First Critique. Kant lays bare the "origins" of speculative metaphysics in the structure of reason, and exposes its pretensions to be a science, but by no means claims to be able to eliminate its questionings. On the contrary, if Kant is right, metaphysical questions are unavoidable. To be able to see through their pretensions, is not to get rid of them, but to rehabilitate them, as questions, if not as bodies of cognition, securely in the necessary structure of reason. An analogous situation holds good of psychologism - possibly of other varieties of relativism and anthropomorphism. The transcendental point of view enables one to speak of the "origin" of psychologism, to see its mistakes as an ontological theory. Yet it does not permanently weed out its possibility. One gains an understanding of its possibility, by realising that a "mundanisation' (of which "naturalistic" interpretation is one variety) is a way in which transcendental subjectivity "apperceives" itself, thereby construing itself as a private, inner, domain that is inserted into public, outer, nature. 

Such an understanding of psychologism was not within the range of Frege's thinking. But he rightly saw - as contrasted with Husserl's overly optimistic critique in the *Prolegomena* - that psychologism could not be logically refuted in the strict and rigorous sense that Frege attached to "logical." This by itself could have, but did not, in Frege's case, led to a deeper reflection on the nature of the mental.
