Anonymity and Personhood: Merleau-Ponty’s Account of the Subject of Perception Sara Heinämaa University of Jyväskylä University of Helsinki 

Several commentators have argued that with his concept of anonymity Merleau-Ponty breaks away from classical Husserlian phenomenology that is methodologically tied to the first person perspective. The descriptions that we find in *Phenomenology of Perception* concerning the anonymous life of the body, as distinct from the personal life of the ego, are claimed to signal a movement which eventually leads to the “ontological turn” and to the allegedly fundamental ontological concepts of the flesh and raw being. Thus understood Merleau-Ponty would either abandon Husserl’s transcendental egology and fall back on empirical reflections or else develop some other type of transcendentalism – transcendentalism of the flesh or transcendentalism of raw being – free from the “egocentricity” of classical phenomenology. 

An early commentator Gary Madison, for example, states that the subject of perception, as characterized by Merleau-Ponty, “is a subject without personal identity which lives outside of itself and which loses track of itself in the perceptual spectacle.”<sup>1</sup> 

More recently, Renaud Barbaras has argued that in *Phenomenology of Perception*, Merleau-Ponty draws near to Scheler’s philosophy and develops the view according to which the differentiation into self and other is secondary and based on an undifferentiated stream of experience without any self, thematic or non-thematic. Barbaras explains that Merleau-Ponty “approaches the question of the other, not by starting from a *cogito*, but from a flowing psychic ‘indifference’ which is realized in emotional evidence and which precedes the subjective differences.”[^2] 

Barbaras contributes to a new trend in Merleau-Ponty studies that rejects Husserlian phenomenology as a form of metaphysics of the present and contrasts it either to Heideggerian-Derridean deconstruction or to Bergsonian-Deleuzian vitalism or both. In this line, also Len Lawlor and Alia Al-Saji argue that Merleau-Ponty abandons Husserl’s egoic philosophy of consciousness and moves, already in *Phenomenology of Perception*, in its 

[^2]: Barbaras (1991, p. 468, p. 52; 2004, pp. 34-35).

analyses of temporality and sensibility, towards an ontology of flesh.[^3] This fundamental “ontological move,” it is argued by Lawlor and Al-Saji, is prepared by Merleau-Ponty in *Phenomenology of Perception* by a critique of Husserl’s analysis of time and its supposed privileging of the living present[^4] and by a return to Bergson’s discourse of “past in general” and “pure memory.”[^5] 

Many contemporary commentators see Merleau-Ponty’s discourse on anonymity as a philosophical breakthrough – a break away from Husserl’s framework which is seen as hopelessly subjectivistic and solipsistic. Some judge and reproach it as a disastrous mistake and misunderstanding that leads to a confusion of philosophical and empirical concerns. Both parties agree that Merleau-Ponty’s concepts of anonymous perception and anonymous experience mark a divergence from classical Husserlian phenomenology. 

I will question this view and argue that Merleau-Ponty’s relation to classical phenomenology is not as simple as such readings suggest. By studying crucial sections from *Phenomenology of Perception*, I show that by anonymous perception Merleau-Ponty means neither self-less experience, nor any experience with a general or collective subject fusing together personal selves. Rather than theorizing about a special type or level of egoless experience Merleau-Ponty struggles to find philosophical expression for certain hidden ingredients of concrete sense-perception, ingredients that are implied by the necessary bodily character of the perceiving self. These ingredients include sedimented accomplishments of earlier acts, some of which are not our own acts but acts of others unknown to us and preceding us in time. Thus understood, perception is “prepersonal,” not in the sense of being an egoless stream or a collective accomplishment of several simultaneous subjects but in the sense of having a history and a “prehistory,” as Merleau-Ponty states.[^6] The aim of this article is to clarify what this means.

Thus my paper provides a novel Husserlian account of Merleau-Ponty’s discourse on anonymity and our prepersonal life by arguing that this discourse remains Husserlian in two important senses: (i) it analyses senses in terms of constituting selves and communities of such selves and (ii) it accounts for the formation of experience by the temporal sedimentation of intentional activity. The argument proceeds in four steps. The first section argues against the widely spread notion according to which Merleau-Ponty’s anonymous subject is collective. In the second section, I demonstrate that Merleau-Ponty uses the term “anonymous” primarily to characterize the lived body of a personal subject. In section three, I introduce Merleau-Ponty’s idea of trace and show that for him both the perceived thing and the perceiving body are traces and as such refer to earlier constitutive acts of alien subjects. I then argue that Husserl’s concepts of sedimentation are crucial for our understanding of this idea. Finally, in section four, I argue that Husserl’s theory of depresentation and its two modalities, recollective and empathetic, is crucial for our understanding of Merleau-Ponty’s discourse on anonymity. 

1 A Selfless Subject or a Collective of Subjects? 

I start by questioning readings according to which Merleau-Ponty’s concept of anonymity denies self-awareness (cogito) and postulates an undifferentiated generality or a communal plurality as the primordial subject of perception. According to such readings, perceptual experience would be rendered exact, not by the sentences, “I see” or “You hear,” in first or second person singular, but by the plural “We see,” by the passive statement “It is seen,” or by the 3rd person assertions “Seeing happens” or “They see.” In contrast to this, I argue that Merleau-Ponty maintains that the “I perceive” (cogito) accompanies all our perceptions, that for him the anonymous subject is singular, not general or plural, and that each concrete perception is necessarily twofold, including a personal and an anonymous element.[^7]

First, I want to focus on the question of *self-awareness* and *cogito*. So let us turn to the texts and ask if Merleau-Ponty really abandons all cogito-based approaches and starts from an egoless experience, or ends up in a middle ground between the first-person perspective and the third-person perspective. It seems to me that such explications are overstatements or misunderstandings based on a one-sided emphasis on Merleau-Ponty’s critique of intellectualism and Cartesianism. For we find, in *Phenomenology of Perception* as well as in later work *The Visible and the Invisible*, clear statements to the opposite. In the chapter on “Other selves,” for example, Merleau-Ponty states quite explicitly: 

There is (…) a living truth of solipsism \[*solipsisme vécu*\] and quite insurmountable. (…) although I am outrun \[*dépassé*\] on all sides by my own acts, and submerged into generality, the fact remains that I am the one who lives these acts.[^8] 

At the end of the book, in the chapter on “Freedom” Merleau-Ponty returns to the topic of generality and individuality, and states again: “I grasp myself in perception as *a* perceiving subject \[*un suject percevant*\].”[^9] And again in *The Visible and Invisible*, in the much discussed chapter on “The intertwining – the chiasm,” he explains his approach as follows: 

We are not here proposing any empirical genesis of thought: we are asking precisely what is that central vision that joins the scattered visions, that unique touch that at once governs the whole tactile life of my body, that *I think* that must be able to accompany all our experience.[^10] 

In the light of such textual evidence, it seems clear that Merleau-Ponty neither denies that perception includes self-awareness nor rejects the cogito, *I see* or *I hear*, as a simple falsification or distortion of perceptual experience. On the other hand, it seems equally clear that Merleau-Ponty’s discussion on the anonymity of sense-perception includes more than just the minimal claim that the perceiving self remains unthematized in the perception of things but can be discovered by reflection. He argues, for example, that “through reflection,”

This paragraph, and other similar paragraphs, suggest that Merleau-Ponty’s phenomenological reflections disclose some kind of collective of subjects: in addition to the personal self, there is some other subject (someone) involved in my perception. The interpretative task is to clarify what kind of involvement is at issue and to make sense of it within the frame of phenomenology. 

From this viewpoint, the other dominant reading that we find in commentaries is not surprising: Merleau-Ponty’s concept of anonymity is claimed to denote a plurality of selves or a system of selves. The perceiving subject that is found in reflection would not be a solitary agent nor a selfless stream, but a self that already belongs to a community of other equal selves.[^14] The subject of primordial or primary perception would not be anyone of these singulars but would be their compound or collective. Thus, perceptual experience would be rendered exact not by the singular phrases “I perceive,” “You perceive,” or “Someone perceives” but by the plural “We perceive” or “They perceive.”

Although some sections in *Phenomenology* may seem to support such readings, there is a number of others which do not fit in. In the chapter on “Other selves,” for example, Merleau-Ponty reasons as follows: 

We iron out the I and the Thou in a plural experience, we introduce the impersonal into the centre of subjectivity, we eliminate the individuality of perspectives. But have we not, in this general confusion, lost the alter Ego with the Ego? (…) \[I\]f the perceiving subject is collective, then the other which it perceives is equally so; so when, within this collective consciousness, we try to bring out the *plurality* of consciousnesses, we shall find ourselves back with the difficulties which we thought we had left behind.[^15] 

Here Merleau-Ponty rejects explicitly the notion that the perceiving subject is anonymous in the sense of collective, plural, or general. Such a view would not just do away with the solitary and all-encompassing self; at the same time it would erase the other, the “thou.” From a collective subjectivity it is equally impossible to get to the “I” as to the “You” and to all other variants of the personal: We, They, He, and She. 

It is crucial to notice that the anonymous subject that Merleau-Ponty’s reflections disclose, together with the personal self, is not plural but is an unspecified singular. He states quite explicitly, and repeatedly, that it is someone who sees “in me” or “along with me.” We saw above that he formulates his view repeatedly and systematically in singular and does not use general terms or plural pronouns. What we have then, in addition to the personal ego, is not a collective or a generality, but an individual other.[^16]

The idea that the French term “on” conveys is that the subject who perceives in me is unknown and cannot be identified either with a general or a proper name. Further, it also suggests that this other cannot be reached by the same means that give me the fellow-perceivers who share space and time with me and with whom I converse, communicate, and co-operate.[^18] In other words, this other is not a part of the social and cultural sphere in which I operate and act. 

Thus, there is no multiplicity or generality of subjects but a couple or a pair, me and an unspecified, nameless or un-named singular, someone else with me. Moreover, these two “subjects,” the personal and the anonymous, do *not* take part in perception in equal footing so that we could express the experience by saying that “We perceive” or “They perceive.” It is I who perceives – and for this reason the passive form “It is perceived” is misleading – but my perception is not autarchic: I perceive only *due* to someone else and *according* to someone else, and I detect and identify this other, through reflection, as a functionary of my own experiencing. There is a fundamental asymmetry or lack of reciprocity between these two subjects: my personal acts are established (*etablir*) on the foundation (*fond*) of the anonymous operations of the “one,”[^19] and on the other hand, the anonymous subject as such, i.e. as a subject of operations that contribute to the constitution of the perceptual scenery, receives its sense as a subject from my personal self.[^20]

So I sense, not according to *my* habits or *my* will, but according to the thing. It is as if the qualities of the thing would “demand” these movements from my body and my organs or would “invite” them to move in certain ways.[^34] What Merleau-Ponty then compresses with his concept of anonymity is the idea that the movements of our own body are given to us as unique modifications of a sensibility and motility implicated in the sensible thing. 

As said, all this happens normally without thematic attention. Neither the stimulating and activating surrounding world nor my activated or re-activated sense organs are thematized or represented in my experience. As Merleau-Ponty puts it: “the world arouses my body by a mute presence which awakens my intentions without deploying itself before them.”[^35] However, subsequent reflection can bring these “moments” of sensation and perception to our notice.<sup>36</sup>

At the very end of *Phenomenology of Perception*, in the chapter on “Freedom,” Merleau-Ponty returns to the problem of anonymity. Here he states that our subjectivity is characterized by a “double anonymity.”[^45] What he means is that all our perceptual experience can be said to be anonymous in two different senses. On the one hand, we are absolute, unique and unparalleled, subjects of experience, and on the other hand, we are individual modifications of the forms or styles of perceiving and sensing – seeing, touching, and hearing – that we inherit from our human and animal ancestors. 

The self as the absolute subject of experience is “anonymous” in the sense that it cannot be named, not even called “the self” or “the ego,” for there is nothing in our experience to which to compare it: “Who perceives this red?” Merleau-Ponty asks, and answers: “It is nobody who can be named and placed among other perceiving subjects.”[^46] Here is the experiential source of the philosophical solipsism mentioned at the beginning, the “living truth of solipsism,” as he puts it.[^47] On the other hand, the subject of perception is also anonymous in the sense that the perceiving self seizes its own perceptions as modifications of a general style of perceiving that stems from past and belongs to a “tradition.”[^48] These two modes of anonymity are not alternatives for any philosophy or theory of subjectivity.
