## Conclusion

The nine chapters of this book, studying ten Jewish authors (counting two in Chapter 6), some of them famous, have not demonstrated that a large number of Jews thought about Hinduism, although these ten authors probably represent additional interested Jews and certainly a large number of readers. Hinduism was never central to Jewish consciousness the way Christianity and Islam were, or dietary or charity laws, or the Messiah. Nor did ideas about Hinduism form a single evolving tradition of thought in which later generations extended and reinterpreted the ideas of earlier generations – as one finds, for example, in the many reinterpretations, over time, of the rabbinic legend of Bar Kokhba.<sup>1</sup> Except for Samson Bloch, the Jewish authors who wrote about Hinduism seem unaware of what earlier Jews wrote. Jacob Sapir, for example, read Benjamin of Tudela’s travelogue, but never referred to its reports about the kingdoms of South Asia. This neglect of earlier writings is true even of authors living in the same cultural and geographic setting, as did Judah Ha-Levi, Abraham ibn Ezra, and Benjamin. Rather, what we find is a series of discrete ideas about Hindu religion influenced by each author’s distinct interests and goals, his sources of information, his conceptual worlds, and his social and intellectual challenges. Interest in Hinduism never developed out of fundamental traditional Jewish texts – the Bible and rabbinic literature – but out of individual responses to secular information about the outside world.

We can nevertheless detect in these responses, however disconnected from each other, some common patterns of thought. Although living in different countries and eras and shaped by different intellectual environments, these authors concurred in some general ways regarding reasons for their interest in Hinduism, some basic ideas about Hinduism, and certain methods of comparing religions. All of this might suggest the features of an essential “Jewish approach to Hinduism,” but I will point out problems with such a conclusion.

Behind the Jewish interest in Hinduism

What kind of interest did these Jewish authors take? What were its motivations? The most frequent purpose for discussing Hinduism was to defend 

*[p. 225]* ^pg238

Judaism. Ha-Levi is the first and best example of this. Hindu claims threatened to undermine his central argument for the truth of Judaism, and he fiercely attacked the veracity of Hindus and validity of their beliefs. Matityahu Glazerson also needed to defend Judaism, this time against the attraction that Hinduism held for modern Jewish youth. We also find David d’Beth Hillel attacking Hindu doctrines and worship. He never made his motivation explicit, but when he thanked God “who separated us from these errors and gave us a true law testifying that He alone is the one true God,” I see a fear of mixing with false beliefs and “abomination.” Jacob Sapir was fascinated with temple worship but his descriptions of it reveal a strong revulsion and a need to separate himself from impure idolatry.

Menasseh ben Israel offered an Indian concept, reincarnation, as evidence for his book defending the Jewish doctrine of the soul’s survival after death. The motive for his interest in Hindu ideas was defense of Judaism – not defending it from Hindu ideas but from the secularizing claims of current European thought. Then, however, Menasseh had to discredit any Indian claims to originality by arguing for the Jewish origin of this concept, transmitted to India by Abraham. This was another type of defense, preserving Judaism’s superiority and historical priority.

A second motivation for discussing Hinduism was simply to inform Jewish readers about the outside world. Ibn Ezra is the best example. He recounted the doctrines of Indian sages as a useful perspective on biblical verses without attacking those doctrines, and he valued the opinions of Indian scientists for their contributions to the science he was teaching his students. Similarly, Bloch’s account of Indian religion assumes the value of this information as an aspect of secular knowledge, freeing his readers from the stultifying world of rabbinic study and teaching them human commonalities and mental errors to avoid. A third motivation goes even further: outright admiration for Indian values. We see this in only one Jewish author, Jacob ben El\`azar, who praised Indian moral teachings, concurred with an Indian theology of God’s justice, and commended to his Jewish readers the Indian book teaching these values.

Less clear are the intentions of Benjamin and Immanuel Bonfils. Scholars still debate the underlying message of Benjamin’s *Book of Travels*, especially since its descriptions maintain a silent neutrality. In its reports of the south Asian kingdoms, including some dramatic narrative about Ibrig, the book seems merely to inform and entertain its Jewish readers. This is the second type of motivation. But when Benjamin condemns the sun worship of Qulam as “folly” and Ibrig’s trust in its manipulative priests as “error,” the author is stepping out of this neutrality to declare a doctrinal position. Did the author feel compelled to assure his Jewish readers that he disagreed with the foreign beliefs and practices he was describing? If so, he was defending himself and his storytelling.

Also difficult to ascertain is why Bonfils transformed the Brahmans of the Alexander Romance into Jewish rabbis and prophets. He claimed that 

*[p. 226]* ^pg239

he meant only to entertain his readers, but his translation inserts a theology in which God, often called *‘Adonai*, determines the fate of Alexander, and a portrait of India’s Brahmans that has them articulate serious Jewish beliefs and values. Bonfils wasn’t defending Judaism, therefore, so much as constructing a world in which the Jewish God ruled over the entire earth and Jewish values were articulated by non-Jews.

Ideas about Hinduism: Images and judgments

At the end of each chapter of this book, I pointed out some recurring motifs in the ways that the Jewish writers pictured India and Hindu religion. One notices, first, that nearly every Jewish writer thought India’s beliefs and customs were very old, which led to various conclusions about ancient philosophy or idolatry or cultural customs.

Second, and more important, the absence of divine revelation seemed to most of the Jewish authors a central fact shaping the nature of Indian thought and beliefs. Another way to phrase this is to say that Torah (as revelation and its rabbinic interpretations) was a central source of truth and value for the Jewish writers, and their awareness of Hinduism’s lack of Torah shaped their conceptions of Hinduism. Ben El\`azar stated this absence of revelation explicitly, and it underlay Ha-Levi’s explanation of why pagan religions failed to reach God. I argued that Ibn Ezra explained the errors and insufficiencies of Indian philosophy from its source in mere human (Aristotelian) reasoning. Hillel and Sapir judged everything they saw in India from the perspective of Judaism’s revealed truths and laws, which temple Hinduism violated through what they considered “idolatry.”

But for those Jews who interpret Indian thought through the perspective of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], Indians are not totally devoid of truth and right practice. They possess valid knowledge but in an incomplete form lower than full revelation.

Bloch, in contrast, ignored the question of revelation while claiming that Indians believe in an abstract monotheism and in moral principles similar to those of the Torah. This implies that on the level of abstract principles, Indian thought reached the same basic conclusions as the revealed truths of Judaism. Which reminds us of Ben El\`azar, who portrayed the “Indian” book *Kalilah and Dimnah* as valid moral teachings functioning for Indians in the way the Torah does for Jews. His picture of India’s ethical and theological teachings does not appear clearly inferior to the revealed scripture of Jews.

Thus, most of our authors saw India as an ancient land devoid of the guidance of revelation, or (in accord with [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]) following a lower form of it. But from this shared image, they tended to define the particular features of India in two very different ways, expressed through two main groups of motifs. The first group, represented most fully by Ha-Levi’s view (and Maimonides), saw India as a land of idolatry and sometimes magic. For Ha-Levi, as I argued, India’s idols, together with its talismans and *Book of Nabatean Agriculture*, pointed to a Sabian type of idolatry that employed 

*[p. 227]* ^pg240

statues for veneration of astral bodies in hope of drawing down power from above. Benjamin portrayed the south Asian kingdoms following another form of idolatry. They venerated the sun and fire and sacrificed their children to fire. This was performed upon *bamot*, high places like those used in the idolatry of biblical times. It was tied to magic because in Ibrig, the priests of the temples of this worship used magic to call upon satans to deceive people at the time of someone’s death, and in Qulam, people used magic to construct the sun wheels that alerted them to the dawn. Idolatry was also central to the views of Hillel and Sapir in the 19th century.

Samson Bloch too pictured magic in India, but not in conjunction with idolatry, but simply illusory magic performed by priests trying to control their followers, somewhat like the deceptive priests of Benjamin’s Ibrig. Which exemplifies another recurrent motif, oppressive priests, as found in the writings of Benjamin, Bloch, and Sapir.

The second major group of motifs pictures Indian beliefs as essentially philosophical and monotheistic, and Indian behavior as moral and civilized. More of our Jewish authors thought of India in this way than as idolaters practicing magic. The first of these authors, Ibn Ezra, imagined Indians as philosophers who expounded doctrines typical of Aristotelian thinkers, as scientists who had discovered central principles in mathematics, astronomy, and astrology, and as moral citizens who never commit “theft, murder, oppression, and false witness.” Ben El\`azar similarly saw Indians as moralists who used their reason to discover a God of cosmic justice and the basic laws of moral behavior. Bonfils’ Brahmans are also philosophers and moralists, but with rabbinic features, quoting biblical and rabbinic sayings to expound a strict prophetic morality. Bloch, another thinker in this group, began his account of Indian religion with its abstract philosophy of an ineffable single God, and he conceived of the Vedas’ ethics as essentially identical to that of the Torah. And lastly, Menasseh and Glazerson, viewing Indian thought through the lens of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], recognized sparks of truth in Indian thought, either as the doctrine of rebirth or as the whole imperfect Hindu system, which Glazerson represented as moral and monotheistic.

Of course, this division of Jewish images of India into two groups of motifs is inexact, and some Jewish pictures of India combined aspects of both groups. Menasseh’s Indians received from Abraham knowledge of both magic and philosophical truth. Benjamin’s Indians practiced idolatry and magic while also living moral lives. Bloch construed Indians as both profound philosophers and priests deceiving people through magic.

But the grouping of motifs around either idolatry or philosophy still generally holds, and it leads to the modest observation that our authors judged the philosophical India more favorably than the idolatrous India. They found less difference between philosophical Indians and Jews, and found the differences between their Judaism and India’s image-worship impossible to bridge. That is, Jews could be objective or even admiring when they 

*[p. 228]* ^pg241

saw Hinduism as philosophy and morality, but they disparaged Hinduism as image-worship.

Here a quotation from Eric Sharpe’s *Comparative Religion: A history* holds some relevance. “At this point, the conflict which is already apparent between an ‘open’ and a ‘closed’ view of other religions can perhaps be defined as one of philosophy versus cult.”<sup>2</sup> Sharpe is comparing Greek thinkers with early Christian thinkers. He characterizes the goals of the Greeks as seeking information and truth, in contrast with the early Christian commitment to soteriology and seeking perfection within a cultic framework. He also sees the early Church Fathers mirroring these same two poles – on the one hand, the New Testament and Justin Martyr, Tertullian, and Cyprian, and on the other, the somewhat more accepting views of Clement and Origen.

The Jewish writers can be divided broadly along the same lines: Ha-Levi, Benjamin, Hillel, and Sapir represent the rule of cultic perfection based on exact forms revealed by God, and Ibn Ezra, Ben El\`azar, Bonfils (and Philo), Menasseh, and Bloch represent a more generous appreciation of Indian philosophy and values. As it turns out, this is the same division that is reflected in the two main Jewish images of Hinduism, as either idolatry or philosophy.

My generalizations about Jewish writers, however, suffer from at least two problems. One of them involves the authors’ variant sources of information about Hinduism and shows why it is important to study an author’s sources. I argued that the image that individual Jewish authors formed of Hinduism, as primarily philosophy or idolatry, shaped their judgments of it. But if the more appreciative Jewish authors had thought that Hindus venerated images, would they not have changed their opinions?

Suppose Ibn Ezra had imagined Hinduism as not only philosophy but also the practice of Sabian theurgy? What if Benjamin had learned about Indian science and philosophy, if Menasseh and Glazerson had seriously considered popular temple Hinduism, if Bonfils and Ben El\`azar had translated Arabic-language accounts of Indian theological sects and their various objects of worship, if Hillel and Sapir had encountered a philosophical Advaita monism? We don’t know what ideas they would have formed about Hinduism if their information had been different.

Moreover, my contrasting categories of “image-worship” and “philosophy” blur differences among the Jewish thinkers. I used the term “idolatry” to subsume the ideas of several Jewish authors, but Ha-Levi, Benjamin, and Sapir meant something so different (astral magic vs. fire-worship vs. image veneration) that we can hardly say the three authors were speaking of the same thing. As for Indian philosophy, the authors imagined it too in differing ways. Ibn Ezra’s Indians held Aristotelian ideas, Menasseh’s believed in reincarnation, and Ben Elazar’s taught cosmic laws of morality, while Bloch’s Indian philosophers espoused basic Maimonidean theology.

These problems, not to mention further ones of definition and categories, undermine any larger conclusions about an “essential Jewish approach 

*[p. 229]* ^pg242

to Hinduism.” What is “Jewish,” and what is “Hindu”? Based on which sources of information and which categories of interpretation? We are left with only the simple observation (I repeat) that the Jewish writers in my study, considered as a group, objected to image-worship and leaned toward philosophy and morality.

Patterns in the comparison of religions

Let us examine, finally, the several variations in the ways that these ten Jewish thinkers compared religions. Comparisons assume some sort of common element shared by both entities in the comparison which permits them to be compared, a *tertium comparationis*, “the third (part) of the comparison.” For example, an apple and a mango can be compared through the category of “color” or “sweetness” or “nutrition.” Since most of these Jewish thinkers, however, considered their Judaism an absolute good and a standard by which to judge the world, their bridges between Judaism and Hinduism were usually an extension of what they considered essential in their Judaism. This would be like comparing a mango to an apple based on the “appleness” that each fruit exhibits. For example, Ha-Levi thought all the pagan religions failed to reach God and encounter the divine *Amr* because only Judaism possessed a divine revelation of instructions for how to achieve this goal. The *ter* *tium comparationis* in his comparison of religions was success in reaching God, as expressed in a life of piety centered around selfless love for the revealed personal aspect of God. Thus, what Ha-Levi considered the theological essence of Judaism became his principle of comparison and judging other religions.

Ibn Ezra likewise viewed closeness with God as not only the essence of Judaism but also, as I argued, the proper measure for comparing religions. But I also argued that he would not have thought that Indian sages had failed completely, for he imagined them recognizing God in the form of *Elohim* with its astrological powers and continuous creation. What they lacked, however, was the full knowledge of God provided by divine revelation, which enabled “cleaving to the Name” and guided people toward eternal life.

In the 20th century, Glazerson employed the same *tertium compara* *tionis* of closeness to God. At first, he compared the ability of Hinduism and Judaism to provide happiness to their adherents, and found them equally effective in doing this. But where Judaism differed fundamentally from Hinduism was in bringing its followers past the level of nature, to which Hinduism was limited, to the transcendent level of closeness with God.

That is one type of *tertium comparationis* which our Jewish authors employed, comparing religions through a theological definition of Judaism’s essence. A related type of comparison appears in the writings 

*[p. 230]* ^pg243

of Ben El\`azar, Bonfils, and Bloch, who identified a more philosophical or sociological essence of Judaism for their comparisons. Ben El\`azar praised Indian thought for its morality and its theology of a just God; hence, morality was the *tertium comparationis* between Judaism and Indian thought. Bonfils found much to approve in the ideas of Dindimus, turning his monotheism, moral diatribes, and self-discipline into Jewish form and language; hence, these values were the bridge of commonality. And Bloch compared the principle of reciprocal morality among Jews, Indians, and Chinese, again using morality as his *tertium comparationis*, and pointed to similarities in the institution of priesthood among Indians and in the Bible, thus using a religious institution as a bridge. The bridges of comparison – morality or monotheism or institutional structure – used by these three authors seem to stand outside their interpretations of Judaism, allowing a fairly objective comparison. But their comparisons always began with concepts they considered essential to Judaism as they understood it, which then colored their interpretations of Hinduism.

A second type of comparison appears in Benjamin of Tudela’s *Book of Travels*. I identified an implicit criterion by which the book compared religions, which was the relative degree of “*dat*” that a people’s beliefs and behavior manifested. In the *Book of Travels* this word implies social and religious regulations and revealed doctrine, and nations may reflect *dat* in whole or in part. I argued that the south Asian kingdoms portrayed by the book reflected a large degree of *dat* but failed to manifest full *dat* because of their beliefs, which Benjamin condemned as “folly” and “error.”

At least tacitly, Hillel and Sapir interpreted the religious life they observed in India somewhat similarly, as a failure to embody the *dat* exemplified by Judaism, Islam, and Christianity. But their interpretations centered around idolatry of the kind described in the Bible and rabbinic writings. In two prayers that Hillel wrote, he condemned Hindu worship and images as “error” and “abomination” which “the true law” rejected by proclaiming “the one true God.” So for Hillel there was an exact truth set forth by the Torah, which also defines a wrong way of worship called idolatry, *\`avodah zarah*. He compared foreign beliefs and practices to this Jewish definition of wrong doctrine and worship. Sapir concurred; he declared that Hindus had been told a lie and that they sacrificed to idols of nothingness. This way of comparing religions asks how much a religious tradition embodies the rabbinic definition of *\`avodah zarah*.

One final form of comparison is expressed more through vocabulary than ideas: the use of a Jewish language of religious institutions to depict Indian phenomena. This could be considered a failure of imagination, the inability to describe someone else’s religion in words other than those of one’s own religion; poor information is also to blame. But using language from one’s own religion also means that one is imagining broad similarities. Benjamin calls the Quran “their Torah.” Ben El\`azar uses biblical 

*[p. 231]* ^pg244

phrases of obedience to Torah to explain how Indians follow the “laws (*h. uqot*) and instructions (*torot*)” ([[Isaiah 08#^8-20|Isa. 8:20]]) in *Kelilah and Dimnah* and try to place its tales “between your eyes … lest they depart from your hearts all the days of your life” ([[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^4-9|Deut. 4:9]] and 6:8). Bonfils’ portrait of the Brahmans Judaizes them through language derived from Bible and Talmud, assimilating them with prophets and rabbis. Brahman schools are meeting houses (*kenesiyot*) containing books of laws and teachings (*h. uqot, mishpa* *tim, torot*) that Brahmans study as a source of joy, which their sages and ancestors (*h. akhameinu v’kadmoneinu*) taught them. In the 19th century, Bloch portrayed Indians possessing a concept of holiness, priests of the biblical kind, temples that he calls “houses of prayer” (*batei tefilah*), and interpreters of laws and customs (*mishpatei torot*, *h. uqim*, and *minhagim*), like rabbis. And Sapir uses the words *ner tamid* (eternal light) and *terumah* (heave offering) to describe Hindu ritual, terms originally referring to the Temple in Jerusalem. The bridge of comparison in all these examples is the language of Jewish institutions – Torah, rabbis, synagogues, priesthood, laws, Jerusalem Temple – which the authors equated with elements they perceived in Indian religious life.

A subcategory of such comparison uses the Hebrew word *qadosh*, meaning “holy.” Four of the writers identified perceptions of holiness among Muslims and Hindus. Benjamin thought Muslims viewed their caliph as “their holy king,” and Bloch employed the word *qadosh* for Hindu attitudes toward the Vedas, priests, certain locales, and bulls. Hillel named sites “holy to” Hindus and Muslims, and Sapir wrote about the holiness that Hindus ascribed to bulls and disposing of corpses in the Ganges. Though Bloch and Sapir derided some of these attributions of holiness, and though Hillel and Sapir believed that only Jewish locales and worship possessed true holiness, the use of this important Jewish notion, holiness, to characterize foreign religious perceptions assumes a common human attitude, while using a Jewish term for it, as a bridge of comparison.

Intellectual history, as I have tried to practice it, examines writings in their unique historical settings, to focus on the distinctive nature of each author’s ideas enmeshed in their particular systems of thought and social context. I would like to think that an intellectual history of Jewish ideas about Hinduism coalesces most naturally not into the broad generalizations I have suggested in this Conclusion, but rather into a narrative of specific details and distinct moments of historically rooted thought. The ongoing Jewish interest in Hinduism twines together these various moments. My version of this narrative began with Judah Ha-Levi facing his collapsing political and intellectual worlds, and continued with Abraham ibn Ezra the scientist, and then Benjamin the traveler, and onward this story went, eventually to the three 19th-century writers, each episode beginning anew but entering into the rich and complex history that this book has explored.
