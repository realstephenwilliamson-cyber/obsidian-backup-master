##### 1.5.3 Religious Aesthetics and Acoustic Piety

As already shown, many of the findings in literary reception aesthetics can be transferred directly to the religious texts. These include the unity of the emotionality and intelligibility of the aesthetic message, its auto-reflexivity, the dynamics of the linguistic process, the priority of effect over meaning, and not least the imaginative cooperation of the recipients. 

Yet 

specifically under the perspective of religious aesthetics/aisthetics and religious semiotics, there are problems to be addressed that can only be given insufficient treatment by sociological, literary and linguistic communication theories, or else are not even considered. One of them is that in the religious context lexical understanding is often not top priority. According to established communication theories, in such cases one should not speak of communication at all any more, since non-semantic self-expressivity, formal sound structures, ritual participation in the text and its efficiency are so much to the fore that discursive elements and semantic meaning recede completely into the background. 

Here it appears to be helpful to understand language by analogy with music, or to understand speaking by analogy with music-making (with others), as Sybille Krämer proposes in her article on “performativity as mediality.”! The advantage of this analogy, according to Krämer, would be “bringing the one-sidedness of the speaker-centricity of the speech act theory into the consciousness” and “leaving the domain of a mere sign-theory-based view of language.”!® In her appeal for “a rehabilitation of the surface,” or “a rehabilitation of the voice,” however, Krämer is not thinking of ritual recitations, but more of everyday communication and the “performance character” of performativa, and “ritualized speech acts” in social interaction. She claims the analogy with musical events demonstrates not only the polyphony of every converseation and the latently effective ideal of homophony, but also the creative power of the voice, which is “not only a vehicle and instrument of speech” but also interprets, comments on and undermines speech “with its full, completely unpredictable corporeality.”'”! She states that the voice expresses the “unsayable” that speech does not express, and creates in its “own life” a “surplus” of meaning, because the decisive point here is not what, but how something is said. ###### 1.5.3.1 Modes and Multi-Dimensionality of Acoustic Piety

Acoustic piety includes ritual acts, strictly regulated forms and sensory perception, along with cognitive content, propositional statements and devotional expressivity. Text performances do not always exhibit all of these aspects but always several levels of communication, and even one and the same text can therefore crystallize out in individual reception in different modes of acoustic piety, depending on which aspect is perceived as the dominant one. This will be explained using the example of the Lalitāā-Sahasra-Nāma, the thousand-name litany for the goddess Lalitā. 

The hymn is a token of the literary type of the thousand-name litanies (sahasranāma). Invocations in the form of litanies (with 1000, 108 or fewer names) are among Sanskrit Hinduism’s most common forms of prayer and divine praise. They serve religious communication,” no matter whether they are part of a “public” ritual or a personal prayer.?”® Like all prayers (worldwide) the Lalitā litany invokes and evokes the presence of the divine and at the same time gives this presence a form. As with all Sahasranāmas, the choice and compilation of the names contains a whole theology.” The verse meter (anustubh) is the same as that in the other litanies, and also the semi-musical recitation style is analogous and is subject to the same slight variations of more or less strong musicalization. The readings may be in metric form or more strongly ritualized, with each name in the dative and framed by the syllable OM and a greeting formula, whereby frequently, and in the temple service always, a flower petal is offered to the deity along with each name. The two examples represent ideal types of the most widespread practice forms or modes of acoustic piety. They are not, however, the only modes. A more esoteric form could be, for example, to “place” (nyāsa) the names on different parts of the body to “deify” it, which is however more typical of shorter litanies, mantra formulas and the syllables of a godď’s name or of an invocation formula such as namah Sivāya (“honor to Śiva”). Different literary types may be especially suitable for certain modes, e.g. bhajan singing for emotional religiosity, the constant repetition of a single name of a god for intensive “rememberance” and thought on the god, monosyllabic “seed sounds” for sound contemplation, etc. The various modes and performance practices certainly do have different ritual frames, forms, contents and effects. The subjective intentional orientation must not be overestimated, but it is a very vital factor and differently channelled by different traditions, religious communities and aesthetic styles. The interplay of performance type and intention will produce different forms of cognition, self-perception and sensory awareness. For example it is not irrelevant whether intensive recitation, meditation, remembering and assimilation practices of the god’s name are intended to build up a “body made of sound” (as in Tantric Yoga) or a “body made of emotion” (as in the ecstatic bhakti of Bengali Krṣṇa devotees). The common denominator of totally immersing oneself in the names and letting oneself be completely over-coded by them, is in turn a different form of spiritual, emotional and bodily experience than the acute mental and physical concentration and painstaking care to pronounce them correctly so that they will unfold their full effects. The effects and objectives are, however, not only traditionally expected and ritually induced, but also shaped by intentionality. The primarily soteriological orientation of the practice popular in monastic settings, bhakti lineages and individual lay persons, of calling the god constantly to mind, i.e. the reciting of names as continuously as possible to make the subjective life perfect, complete and divine, has a

Sound is an extremely powerful religious symbol that has remained extraordinarily stable and at the same time has always acquired new interpretations, and led to new rites and even new religions or new religious traditions. The mantra power in the Veda, for instance, originated within the institution sacrifice. The Tantric mantra deities introduced a different mantra conception than the Veda, and changed not only the ritual, but also the entire symbol system. Clearly a case where a symbol was the moving force of innovation, was the Nāda-Brahman, a new coding of the Brahman (in the 13th century) which semiotized the whole world as pervaded by original sound and made it possible to understand one’s listening to music as a form of Yoga. To use Luhmann’s words, sacred sound is part of “system rationality” and for hundreds of years was extremely effective at stabilizing and producing (ever fresh) meaning. Whether one might want to call sacred sound, such as the conception of mantra power, magic or not, can be debated. We will give one clearer example of magic in the form of the Sarasvati-Rahasya-Upanisad. This example is of special interest because it confirms Sørensen’s distinction between magical rite (concrete, goal-directed and context-near) and an abstract theological system (symbolic interpretation), but simultaneously shows that both domains can exist intact side by side in one and the same text. Moreover, abstraction and symbolic interpretation need not be context-distant at all, particularly if a monistic world-view prevails.

It is noteworthy that in contrast to Sørensen, Jakobson saw the great role played by symbols in the “linguistic (and not only linguistic) creativity” of human beings.” He followed Peirce when pointing out the strength of the symbol was due to fact that it has a general meaning and that this meaning in its turn can “only be a symbol.””® This seems to be an important insight also regarding cultural symbol systems. According to Peirce and Jakobson the advantage of symbols is that they allow general, open-ended statements about the future, while icons present only past experience and indices are related to the present. A symbol functions as a general rule. Jakobson explained: “A symbol, for example a word, is therefore itself a ‘general rule’ acquiring its meaning solely through its application to various individual cases, namely the spoken or written replicas, which resemble objects. Sound charged with sacredness and transforming efficacy was in India a kind of “general rule.” It remained a relational constant. However, investigating sound as symbolic form, the matter instantly becomes more complex and goes beyond the range of linguistic symbols. The Veda is a good example. One should remember that Vedic texts represent pars pro toto a symbol and can be seen in the sense of a general rule, since Veda recitation aims for exactly this: the creation of exact spoken replicas of the holy “knowledge” (Veda) and holy “hearing” (Śruti). At the same time, however, Veda recitation is a prime example of the de-emphasis of the conventional, symbolic sign nature of language, because it is not the word’s meaning that is the deciding factor or harbors the “magical” efficacy, but the correctly pronounced sound material and thus instead the iconic and indexical sign values. In Sanskrit Hinduism, in linguistic terms sound is a “perfect sign,” or in literary terms it is an iconic “total representation.” In terms of the cultural philosophy and theory of myth (in the sense of Cassirer and Hübner) one can speak of a “symbolic form” and “numinous substance” in which the material and the immaterial fuse, and precisely this factor plays a major role in what makes “holy hearing” and “sacred sound.” In cognitive theory (in Sørensen’s sense) here sound would be an indexical, essentialistically charged metonymy. ##### 1.5.4 Review and Outlook for the "Sonic Paradigm" and Sound as Symbolic Form

The previous chapters have made it clear why and to what extent we relate sound and communication directly to one another, and where we follow on from sociological, linguistic and literary theories of communication and signs. They also show where we see a necessity to go further in order to examine our Hindu data on a theoretical basis and in a manner sensitive to context. The approach we favor, of the aesthetics and semiotics of religion, appears to be the best one for doing this. At the outset we explained why we consider a performative communication concept to be especially appropriate and illuminated it by means of a complex, multi-dimensional communication model in order to explain acoustic piety and animate sounds. This model has proved itself to be a suitable one for building on and expanding cognitive theories of “conceptual blending.” Sørensen’s cognitive theory of ritual and magic helped in the elaboration of the cognitive mechanisms underlying the common forms of acoustic piety in India. The broad discussion of this theory, however, made it just as clear why we give priority to symbolic forms and a context-sensitive history of ideas, and why we no longer wish to argue in terms of cognitive theory, but primarily in terms of the aesthetics of religion and cultural history. 

As Max Weber showed, ideas themselves are powerful forces that shape cultural perceptions and social actions. It is actually astounding that the cognitive scientist Sørensen, who notes that “meaning is constructed

and manipulated inside mental spaces,”?? ascribes such strong means of over-blending and creativity only to ritual and not also to the culturally specific forms of expression of religious imagination, reflection and standardization, or to that which we have called the “paradigm” in our own blending model. As appropriate to our area of interest, we use this term primarily to refer to the sonic paradigm or the sonic paradigms of Sanskrit Hinduism. Such cultural paradigms seem to us to be central as the basis of the cultural understanding that is necessary, for example, in order to experience sounds as animate and to anticipate ritual efficacy and experience it a real way. Doubtless the sonic paradigm is especially strongly present in ritual forms of practice, but it is also tangible in the narrations and reflexive statements of the sacred literatures, in “directions” for “holy hearing” or “holy speaking,” and is especially marked in sonic cosmologies and theologies. Moreover, the paradigm extends religious communication. It is an important argument in this book that the sonic paradigm pervades different segments of culture. Even certain scientific code systems presenting elaborated non-religious knowledge systems (such as grammar and astronomy) in brief acoustic formulas, map a sonic paradigm. One of the most distinguishing marks of Sanskrit culture has been to express abstractions not only in graphic symbols and writing, but also in acoustic symbols. Obviously the phonocentric life reality and sonic awareness also promoted formalism and abstraction alongside diverse forms of fusion. 

In the discussion of sound as symbolic form, therefore, not only ritual forms play an important role, but so do cognitive systems, various symbolic forms and different spheres of rationality. However, these different spheres of rationality, for instance religion and science, have been differently organized than their European counterparts and their relationship between science and religion suffered less a break even in modern times. We are going to argue that the semi-literal, intermediate zone of Sanskrit culture that combined orality and literacy and never gave up its phonocentric preference (what we call “sonality”) created a “third space habitus.” This culture generated not only pronounced forms of acoustic piety, but also different scientific styles than those found in Europe. Regarding the history of rationality it is not insignificant that even the scientific abstractions of natural sciences have been communicated in sounds and poetic verses. The aesthetic styles of the traditional Indian scientists differ profoundly from scientific practices in the West.
