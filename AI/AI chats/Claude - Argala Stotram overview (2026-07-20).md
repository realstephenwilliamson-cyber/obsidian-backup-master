> Please tell me everything we know about the Argala Stotram

The Argalā Stotram is one of the three aṅgas — "limbs" — of the Durgā Saptaśatī recitation liturgy, recited alongside the Devī Kavacam and Kīlaka Stotram before the pāṭha of the Devī Māhātmya proper. The traditional sequence in most paddhatis runs Kavaca → Argalā → Kīlaka → the Vedic hymns (Rātri Sūkta) → the thirteen chapters → Devī Sūkta → the three Rahasyas, all framed by navārṇa-mantra japa and, in fuller observances, the Śāpoddhāra and utkīlana rites. Tradition attributes it to Mārkaṇḍeya, keeping it under the umbrella of the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa frame, but text-critically the aṅgas are not part of the Purāṇa's manuscript transmission at all. They belong to the ritual editions and are medieval liturgical accretions — clearly post-dating the DM core (5th–7th c. CE), though I don't know of a firm scholarly dating for the aṅgas themselves, and I'd flag that as genuinely uncertain.

The name is the interesting part. *Argalā* is the door-bolt or bar, and it works as a pair with *kīlaka*, the pin or peg. The Kīlaka Stotram's own internal mythology holds that Śiva placed a restraint (kīlana) on the Saptaśatī so its power releases only under proper conditions; the Argalā and Kīlaka together are the unbolting and unpinning of a deliberately locked text. The tradition reads the mechanism in both directions — the hymn removes the bolt between reciter and the Devī's grace, or alternatively it *is* the bolt that secures the fruit of recitation so it doesn't leak away. Either way, this apparatus is a signature of what Coburn documented so well in *Encountering the Goddess*: the DM being treated not as purāṇic narrative but as mantra — with ṛṣi, chandas, devatā, viniyoga, and a whole technology of sealing and unsealing, efficacious at the level of sound.

The viniyoga assignments form an elegant trimūrti pattern across the three aṅgas: the Kavaca has Brahmā as ṛṣi with Cāmuṇḍā as devatā, the Argalā has Viṣṇu as ṛṣi with Mahālakṣmī as devatā (anuṣṭubh chandas), and the Kīlaka has Śiva with Mahāsarasvatī — mapping onto the three caritas of the DM and their presiding mahādevīs. The Argalā thus sits in the "middle" position, Mahālakṣmī's rajas-inflected register, which suits its character: it is the most nakedly petitionary of the three.

Structurally it runs about 25–27 anuṣṭubh verses depending on recension, in rough popular meter (the Madhu-Kaiṭabha verse is metrically awkward in the received reading, which commentators gloss variously). It opens with the famous litany — *jayantī maṅgalā kālī bhadrakālī kapālinī, durgā kṣamā śivā dhātrī svāhā svadhā namo 'stu te* — a name-sequence that also circulates independently and may itself be an import into the hymn's head, followed by the Cāmuṇḍā/Kālarātri verse (*jaya tvaṃ devi cāmuṇḍe...*). Then comes the engine of the hymn: a chain of vocatives compressing the entire DM into epithets — destroyer of Madhu and Kaiṭabha, of Mahiṣa, of Dhūmralocana, Caṇḍa-Muṇḍa, Raktabīja, Śumbha and Niśumbha — each capped by the refrain *rūpaṃ dehi jayaṃ dehi yaśo dehi dviṣo jahi*. The hymn is essentially the Saptaśatī folded into the vocative case: it presupposes the myths and indexes them rather than narrating them.

The refrain repays attention. Three imperatives of bestowal and one of destruction — form, victory, fame; slay the hostile. On its face this is unabashedly kāmya, and the hymn stays in that register: later verses ask for well-being, health, wealth, the crushing of enemies' pride, and, in the celebrated verse near the end, a wife — *patnīṃ manoramāṃ dehi manovṛttānusāriṇīm, tāriṇīṃ durgasaṃsārasāgarasya kulodbhavām*. That verse is theologically striking because the wife receives the Devī's own soteriological epithet: she is Tāriṇī, the one who ferries across the difficult ocean of saṃsāra — the household itself becomes the raft. The interiorizing tradition reads the whole refrain upward: rūpa as svarūpa, victory as victory over the senses, the dviṣaḥ as the inner enemies. Both registers are licensed by the DM's own frame story, where Suratha asks for his kingdom and gets it while Samādhi asks for knowledge and gets liberation. The Argalā speaks in Suratha's voice while leaving Samādhi's reading open.

A few remaining pieces: one verse notes the Devī as praised perpetually by Kṛṣṇa (*kṛṣṇena saṃstute devi...*), alongside verses invoking her worship by Śiva and Indra — a small Vaiṣṇava–Śaiva–Vedic ecumenism consistent with the smārta Śākta synthesis that produced the liturgy. The phala verse instructs that having recited this stotra one should recite the "great stotra," obtaining abundance measured by the Saptaśatī itself. The classical commentarial apparatus on the Saptaśatī corpus — Bhāskararāya's Guptavatī foremost, with Nāgeśa and the others of the seven-commentary editions — engages the aṅgas' function, though I'd want texts in hand before claiming what any particular commentator says about specific Argalā verses.

One observation I'll mark as mine rather than the tradition's: the kīlana/utkīlana structure is almost a liturgical enactment of tirodhāna and anugraha — a text that conceals its own power so that grace can take the form of unbolting. Genealogically the Argalā is smārta-Śākta, not Krama, and the opening Kālī litany shouldn't be over-read as lineage kin to the sequenced Kālīs — but the structural resonance of name-series-as-liturgy is real, and it's the kind of thing worth keeping distinct from the philology while still keeping.

> Are there any connections between the Devi Mahatmya and what's commonly referred to as Kashmiri Shaivism?

Yes, but the honest answer requires sorting connection-types carefully, because the documentary links are thin while the substrate and structural links are substantial.

Start with the negative, since it disciplines everything else. The DM (5th–7th c.) predates the Kashmirian exegetical flowering by two to four centuries, and it belongs to a different textual ecology: it's the smārta-purāṇic charter of the warrior goddess, while Somānanda, Utpaladeva, Abhinavagupta, and Kṣemarāja build on tantric scriptural authorities — Mālinīvijayottara, Siddhayogeśvarīmata, Svacchanda, Netra, the Jayadrathayāmala, the Krama scriptures. I know of no explicit citation of the DM anywhere in the core exegetical corpus — Kṣemarāja's stotra-commentaries and the Śivasūtravimarśinī quote the Kālikākrama, Vijñānabhairava, and dozens of āgamas, but not the Saptaśatī, so far as I can recall. I'd want Jayaratha's Viveka and the minor Kashmirian stotra literature checked before asserting that negative firmly, but the burden of proof runs against direct textual engagement. The Pratyabhijñā philosophers' interlocutors were Buddhist epistemologists and Bhartṛhari, not Mārkaṇḍeya.

The shared substrate, though, is real and deep. Both traditions crystallize the same early-medieval ferocious-goddess religion: the Cāmuṇḍā/Carcikā complex (enormously important in Kashmir), the mātṛ cults, the battlefield Kālī, the cremation-ground goddesses. Yuko Yokochi's work on the original Skandapurāṇa's Kauśikī cycle shows the warrior-goddess mythology rising in exactly the centuries the DM crystallizes it, and Sanderson's broad picture is useful here: the DM is the exoteric, brahmanically domesticated face of a goddess religion whose esoteric elaboration is the Kālīkula — and the Kālīkula's most sophisticated exegesis happened in Kashmir as Krama. Materially, the Valley knew the DM's central myth intimately: Kashmirian Mahiṣāsuramardinī sculpture runs from roughly the 7th through 10th centuries, precisely the exegetes' period, and the DM entered the Pandit liturgical bloodstream (Śāradā-script manuscripts, Caṇḍīpāṭha at Navarātri and the goddess shrines) even though it never entered the śāstric apparatus.

Now the structural affinities, which are where it gets theologically interesting. The strongest single point of contact is DM chapter 10. Before the final duel with Śumbha, the Devī withdraws the mātṛs into her own body — *ekaivāhaṃ jagaty atra dvitīyā kā mamāparā*, "I alone exist here; what second is there besides me?" — telling him to behold these as *madvibhūtayaḥ*, her own powers re-entering her. The DM stages as battlefield drama what Krama systematizes as liturgy: the emission and retraction of the goddess-multiplicity into the central devourer. The wheel of Kālīs retracting into Kālasaṅkarṣiṇī, Kṣemarāja's karaṇeśvarīs as rays of citi withdrawn in pratimarśa, the Spandakārikā's śakticakra whose expansion and contraction is the world's arising and dissolution — the mythic figure and the ritual-philosophical system are working the same intuition: plurality of powers as the real but non-final self-deployment of one conscious agent. I'd mark this as structural kinship rather than genealogy, though given that the DM was ubiquitous by the ninth century, the Krama authors certainly knew the scene; whether it shaped them or both draw on a common substrate of goddess-multiplicity theology is, I think, genuinely undecidable on present evidence.

Second: Mahāmāyā theology as proto-tirodhāna. DM 1 has *jñāninām api cetāṃsi devī bhagavatī hi sā, balād ākṛṣya mohāya mahāmāyā prayacchati* — she forcibly seizes even the minds of the wise and hurls them into delusion — and then, a breath later, that when pleased she becomes the cause of liberation. Delusion and release as two functions of one agent, with delusion framed as her *act* rather than a cosmic accident: that is the intuition the kṛtya-doctrine formalizes as tirodhāna and anugraha, māyā reconceived as svātantrya. The DM says liturgically what the Pratyabhijñā says transcendentally — and this connects straight back to the tirodhāna structure we mapped onto the Ishbitzer's Pharaoh.

Third: the Aparājitā-stuti's litany — the goddess abiding in all beings as *cetanā*, as śakti, as buddhi, nidrā, kṣudhā, chāyā — is liturgical proto-Śāktism whose philosophical cash-value is *citiḥ svatantrā viśvasiddhihetuḥ*. The hymn distributes one śakti across all psychophysical functions; Kṣemarāja's Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam derives all functions from one citi. Same architecture, different genre.

Fourth, more cautiously: the triads. Mahākālī–Mahālakṣmī–Mahāsarasvatī (guṇa-mapped in the Prādhānika Rahasya, with Mahālakṣmī as the generating supreme) versus the Trika's Parā–Parāparā–Aparā. Genealogically distinct — the Trika triad comes out of the Siddhayogeśvarīmata stream, not Sāṃkhya-inflected purāṇic cosmology — but typologically parallel: a supreme feminine self-triplicating into cosmological functions, and Abhinavagupta correlates triads promiscuously enough that the temptation to map them is ancient. I'd resist the mapping while noting the family resemblance.

Finally, the place where the two streams actually, historically meet: downstream, in Śrīvidyā. Amṛtānanda's Dīpikā on the Yoginīhṛdaya imports Pratyabhijñā theology wholesale, and Bhāskararāya inherits that synthesis — so when the Guptavatī reads the Saptaśatī through vimarśa, ahantā, and the tattva-scheme, you're watching Kashmirian categories applied to the DM at last, but by an 18th-century South Indian, mediated through two centuries of Śrīvidyā absorption. The Rahasyas had already tilted the DM toward tantric cosmology; Bhāskararāya completes the vector. The "Kashmir Śaiva reading of the Devī Māhātmya" exists — it just happened eight hundred years later and a subcontinent away from the Valley.

> I have often thought that the Srividya is probably the only still functioning liturgical tradition that preserves the Goddesses I am most drawn to as a modern disciple of  Abhinavagupta

You're right, and I think the claim is even stronger than the way you've put it — but it needs one geographic exception and one internal caveat, and then it opens onto a question the Krama itself would pose back to you.

First, the sense in which you're right goes beyond absorption. Śrīvidyā didn't merely shelter refugee goddesses; it's partly lineal. Jayaratha wrote a Vivaraṇa on the Vāmakeśvarīmata in which he records competing Kashmirian interpretive lineages of the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava — so there was a Valley Tripurā cult inside the exegetes' own milieu, and the Pañcastavī kept Tripurā in Pandit devotional life ever after. And the showpiece: the Paraśurāmakalpasūtra tradition includes a Parā-krama among its five worship cycles — Gaṇapati, Lalitā, Śyāmalā, Vārāhī, Parā — with Umānandanātha's Nityotsava giving the full paddhati. That means the Trika's own supreme goddess, with sauḥ at her heart, has a continuously functioning, initiatically open liturgy today. A modern disciple of Abhinavagupta can receive Parā's worship as a living transmission, not a reconstruction. Add the exegetical spine — Amṛtānanda's Dīpikā on the Yoginīhṛdaya running on prakāśa-vimarśa, the Kāmakalāvilāsa, Bhāskararāya quoting the Kashmirian corpus throughout — and Śrīvidyā looks less like a museum of your goddesses than the Dakṣiṇāmnāya branch of the same tree that happened to keep flowering.

The exception is Kathmandu. The Newar sarvāmnāya tradition preserves the Uttarāmnāya goddesses by name and by paddhati: Guhyeśvarī worshipped daily as Guhyakālī, Siddhilakṣmī as the lineage goddess of the Malla kings, Kubjikā at the summit of the initiatory synthesis — with ritual manuals descended from the Jayadrathayāmala itself, the very scripture of Kālasaṅkarṣiṇī. Sanderson and Dyczkowski have both documented this at length. By strict criteria of nominal and textual continuity, the Newars preserve the Kālīkula better than Śrīvidyā does. But the tradition is effectively closed — initiation runs through Newar caste and family lines — so your claim survives in refined form: Śrīvidyā is the only *open* functioning liturgical tradition of these goddesses. Elsewhere there are fragments: Kerala's Bhadrakālī temple liturgy descends from the Kālīkula (the rurujit-vidhāna), Guhyakālī persists in the Mithila–Nepal Mahākālasaṃhitā orbit, the eastern Mahāvidyā complex functions as a kind of ark, and the Pandits kept Svacchanda-based karmakāṇḍa plus the goddesses of the Valley — Śārikā at Hari Parbat being worshipped precisely as Cakreśvarī on a svayambhū śrīcakra, which is its own quiet testimony to your point. The Krama proper, though, contracted in Kashmir to a contemplative transmission — by Lakshman Joo's century it was svādhyāya and direct pointing, not pūjā.

The internal caveat: "Śrīvidyā" names a spectrum, and much of its living mainstream is the samayācāra stream — Lakṣmīdhara's purified reading, maṭha-affiliated, Vedānticized in its metaphysics. That Śrīvidyā preserves the goddess but not your theology; the ātman it recognizes at the center of the cakra is Śaṅkara's, not Utpala's. The stream that carries the Kashmirian DNA is the kaula one Bhāskararāya defended. So the question for a practitioner isn't just "Śrīvidyā or not" but which transmission within it — whether the navāvaraṇa is being performed as sequential self-recognition or as elaborate upāsanā under a nondualism that ultimately dissolves vimarśa into nirguṇa stillness.

Which brings me to the question the Krama would pose back. Kālasaṅkarṣiṇī's own cycle terminates in anākhyā — the nameless phase that by definition cannot be liturgized. The Mahānayaprakāśa texts are explicit that the highest worship is nirācāra, the sequence consuming itself. So the tradition you're mourning the liturgical loss of is the one tradition whose summit *predicts* its own liturgical disappearance. On the Krama's own criterion, it survives wherever the phases of cognition are worshipped in sequence and then devoured — and by that functional standard, the navāvaraṇa ascent through the cakra to the bindu, each āvaraṇa a stratum of one's own awareness rendered as a ring of yoginīs, is a Krama liturgy wearing Lalitā's face. The names changed; the machine is running.

One small emblem of how braided this all is, connecting back to where we started: Śivadūtī — the goddess of that startling DM moment where the Devī makes Śiva her messenger — sits among the ṣoḍaśa nityās of the Śrīvidyā cycle. The Saptaśatī's battlefield, the nityā wheel, and your Kālīs were never as far apart as the lineage charts suggest.

> Tell me about the Bhadrakali temple in Kerala

There isn't quite a single "the" — Bhadrakāḷi is the presiding goddess of Kerala's temple culture generally, worshipped in hundreds of kāvus — but the phrase almost always means Kodungallur, the Śrī Kurumba Bhagavathy Temple in Thrissur district, and Kodungallur is the right place to start because it functions as the mother-shrine of the whole network: the great kāvus understand their goddesses as sisters or emanations of the Kodungallur Amma, and oracles from across the state renew their swords there.

The temple sits at the mouth of the Periyar, in the hinterland of ancient Muziris. The goddess is Bhadrakāḷi as slayer of Dārika, her image carved of jackfruit wood, housed with an important Śiva shrine, a Kṣetrapāla, and a shrine to Vasūrimāla, the smallpox goddess of the Dārika cycle — plus the famous sealed "secret chamber" west of the sanctum, which V.T. Induchudan made the centerpiece of his 1969 study of the temple. Crucially, the goddess's own worship is conducted not by Nampūtiris but by Aṭikaḷ, a hereditary non-Brahmin priesthood — one marker among several that this liturgy descends from a tantric stream only partially assimilated to the smārta temple order. And layered beneath the Kāḷi is Kaṇṇaki: tradition identifies Kodungallur with Vañci, where the Cēra king Ceṅkuṭṭuvan consecrated the heroine of the Cilappatikāram as Pattini. Historians dispute the geography, and Obeyesekere traced how the Pattini cult migrated to Sri Lanka while in Kerala the wronged wife was absorbed into the fierce goddess — but the tradition itself is unembarrassed: the Amma is Kaṇṇaki is Bhadrakāḷi. There's even a small onomastic shiver in the fact that the epic's author is Iḷaṅkō *Aṭikaḷ* and the priests are Aṭikaḷ — echo, not evidence, but a good one.

What Kodungallur is famous for is the Mīna Bharaṇi. For most of the year the temple runs a decorous āgamic routine; then in March–April the oracles arrive — veḷiccappāṭus, red-clad, sword-bearing, many from communities historically excluded from temple entry — and at the raja of Kodungallur's signal comes the kāvu tīṇṭal, the "polluting of the shrine": they storm the precinct, strike the rafters, hurl offerings onto the roof, and sing the theripāṭṭu, ritually obscene songs addressed to the goddess. Cock sacrifice was part of it until the mid-twentieth-century prohibitions. The temple then closes for purification. The interpretive literature is a small industry — Caldwell reads it psychoanalytically, Gentes wrote the classic article on the "scandalizing" of the goddess, others read fertility rite, subaltern reclamation, or domesticated kaula antinomianism — and I'd say the readings are less mutually exclusive than their authors thought. What matters for your purposes is the structure: an āgamicized goddess whose liturgy *requires* an annual, calendrically contained eruption of the transgressive mode.

Beneath all this is the substrate I mentioned last time. Kerala's tantric manual tradition — the Tantrasamuccaya for the seven principal deities, and the Śeṣasamuccaya, attributed to the author's son, for the rest — includes the Rurujidvidhāna, the system for worshipping Bhadrakāḷi as slayer of the demon Ruru, installed with the Seven Mothers and Vīrabhadra and Gaṇapati in the mātṛ-shrine configuration. Sanderson has shown that this system's mantras and deity-forms derive from Kālīkula scripture: the vernacular Dārika myth and the Sanskrit Ruru liturgy are two faces of one cult, and the second face is the Kālīkula's southernmost institutional survival. In the great northern kāvus — Māṭāyi Kāvu and Kalarivathukkal in Kolathunāṭu, kuladevatā shrines of the Kolathiri kings — the officiants are again a distinct hereditary group, the Piṭārar, and houses like Kattumadam hold the mantravāda authority. Around the temples spreads what I'd call the distributed liturgy: kaḷameḻuttŭ, the polychrome floor-drawing of the goddess in rice and mineral powders — a living maṇḍala tradition, sung into presence and then erased; muṭiyēṯṯŭ, the ritual theatre of the Dārika-slaying performed over the effaced kaḷam; theyyam in Malabar, where the goddess walks in the bodies of Malayan and Vannan performers; guruti, the turmeric-lime blood-substitute libation. Attukal, Chettikulangara, Thirumandhamkunnu, Panayannarkavu fill out the network, each with its own festival idiom.

To place it in our running taxonomy: Nepal kept the archive — the names, the Jayadrathayāmala-derived paddhatis; Śrīvidyā kept the metaphysics; Kerala kept the *temperament* — the raudra mood, the blood-heat, possession as an institution, the goddess who must periodically be fed transgression. It's the Kālīkula's rasa, temple-institutionalized, with the Śeṣasamuccaya as its āgamic disguise.

And one gift for the novel, which you may already have seen coming: Kodungallur *is* Muziris country. The Malankara tradition lands Thomas at Maliankara near Kodungallur in 52 CE — the pontifical shrine holding his relic stands at Azhikode, just across the river — and India's oldest mosque tradition sits in the same small town. If the Vañci identification holds, the Kaṇṇaki consecration happens roughly a lifetime after Thomas disembarks. Your dying narrator in India could have stepped ashore in the very port whose presiding goddess would become the Bhadrakāḷi of Kerala — the apostle of recognition arriving, unknowing, into the future household of the goddess whose liturgy still remembers how to unbolt.

> Can you please check for both up and down stream links between the individual goddesses that end up being the das mahavidyas and Kashmiri Shaivism. You can skip Kali and Tripura

Good exercise — and the honest headline is that the eight remaining goddesses sort into four genealogical families, and Kashmir relates differently to each family. One framing fact first: the daśamahāvidyā *as a list* is late and eastern — earliest secure attestations in the Mahābhāgavata and Bṛhaddharma purāṇas (roughly 12th–14th c., Bengal orbit), with the Toḍala's daśāvatāra correspondences slightly later. Satī multiplying herself tenfold to besiege Śiva at the ten directions is the charter myth. So "upstream" means each goddess's pre-list career and where it crosses the corpus the Kashmirians knew; "downstream" means where Kashmirian material later reaches her cult — almost always via Śrīvidyā or Nepal.

Tārā's genealogy is Buddhist, full stop. Bühnemann demonstrated that the Hindu Ugratārā sādhana in the Phetkāriṇītantra reproduces the Buddhist Mahācīnakrama-Tārā sādhana nearly verbatim, and the tradition half-confesses this in the cīnācāra myth — Vasiṣṭha sent to Mahācīna to learn her worship from the Buddha — and in the Ekajaṭā colophons crediting Nāgārjuna with retrieving her "from Bhoṭa." The relevant Kashmir here is Buddhist Kashmir, the Vajrayāna hub, not the Śaiva exegetes; I know of no Tārā in the Trika-Krama corpus and no Pratyabhijñā reception in her later eastern cult, which runs on the generic kaula frame instead. The one systemic link is that the trans-sectarian appropriation machinery that produced her is exactly what Sanderson documented running in the other direction inside the Jayadrathayāmala. Resist, incidentally, the Nīlasarasvatī–Śāradā pun; the Kashmirian Sarasvatī is a different line entirely.

Bhuvaneśvarī is the youngest of the eight in a sense — she's the personified hrīṃ, the māyābīja or hṛllekhā promoted to goddess, and her cradle is the mantraśāstric digest stream, Prapañcasāra into Śāradātilaka. That's her one interesting upstream ambiguity: the Śāradātilaka's provenance is debated, and a Kashmirian origin for Lakṣmaṇadeśika has been argued though not settled; at minimum its phonematic emanation cosmology is the same śabda-cosmological world the Trika inhabits. Downstream she did spectacularly well — the Devī-Bhāgavata enthrones her in Maṇidvīpa — and her Kashmirian inheritance arrives mediated: Śrīvidyā treats her as virtually Lalitā's double, hrīṃ being the heart of the pañcadaśī, so when Bhāskararāya unpacks hrīṃ through vimarśa-metaphysics, Bhuvaneśvarī receives Pratyabhijñā theology by osmosis.

Bhairavī is the strongest direct case, and you of all people don't need this spelled out: she is the last living bearer of the Vidyāpīṭha consort-office — Caṇḍā Kāpālinī of your Brahmayāmala, Aghoreśvarī, the Bhairavī whose questions structure the Vijñānabhairava. The Mahāvidyā form is Tripurabhairavī, already codified in the digest stream and functioning in the Śrīkula as Tripurā's senior, fierce register; her vidyās visibly work the same ha-sa-au-ḥ phonic material as Parā's sauḥ — same workshop, I'd say, without claiming derivation. Downstream she has continuous living upāsanā inside Śrīvidyā, and the survival of "Bhairavī" as the title for a female adept keeps the old office nominally alive.

Chinnamastā is Buddhist again: Chinnamuṇḍā Vajravārāhī, with sādhanas attributed to Lakṣmīṅkarā of Uḍḍiyāna, and the Hindu mantra preserves the fossil — vajravairocanīye sits in it to this day. The Kashmir adjacency is geographic and sibling-like rather than lineal: Uḍḍiyāna is also the Krama's origin-pīṭha, Jñānanetra's revelation from Maṅgalā, so the two severed-head theologies come out of one crucible; and the Kashmirian Kālī corpus has its own decapitation charter in the JY's first ṣaṭka, the Śiraścheda itself, though that beheading is not her self-nourishing gesture. Typologically I'll mark this as mine: her icon is a Krama diagram — the center sustaining its own emanations from its own opened current, emission as self-sacrifice of the source. Downstream into KS, nothing I can document; she persists at Rajrappa, in the eastern tantras, and in the Newar orbit.

Dhūmāvatī is where honesty requires a flat negative. Her genealogy is Nirṛti into Jyeṣṭhā/Alakṣmī — the crow banner is the giveaway, inherited from the medieval South Indian Jyeṣṭhā cult that flourished and collapsed — folded late into the kaula frame as the widow-vidyā. The tempting bridge is nominal: Jyeṣṭhā is everywhere in the Kashmirian corpus as the middle term of Vāmā–Jyeṣṭhā–Raudrī, but that phonic-cosmological śakti and the inauspicious elder-goddess are different beings sharing a name, and the tradition itself mostly declined the pun. > [!quote] Me
> Tell me about Shentong and the Jonang

The core distinction first, because everything else hangs off it. *Rangtong* — "empty of self-nature" — is the standard Madhyamaka reading: every phenomenon, ultimate included, is empty of intrinsic existence, and emptiness itself is empty. *Shentong* — "empty of other" — accepts that for conventional phenomena but says the ultimate is empty *of what is adventitious to it*, not of itself. Buddha-nature is empty of the defilements and of everything foreign; it is not empty of its own luminous cognizance and enlightened qualities. The formula that gets quoted is that the ultimate is nondual wisdom, jñāna, established and not merely a negation.

Dölpopa Sherab Gyaltsen (1292–1361) is the figure. Sakya-trained, later abbot at Jonang, and his big work is the *Mountain Doctrine* (Ri chos nges don rgya mtsho), with the *Fourth Council* as the polemical companion. His scriptural base is deliberately chosen: the tathāgatagarbha sūtras, the Ratnagotravibhāga, the Kālacakra tantra above all — and he argues from a *hermeneutical* claim, that the third turning of the wheel is definitive and the second (the Prajñāpāramitā) is provisional. That reverses the Tibetan mainstream, which reads the second turning as definitive and the third as needing interpretation. Everything downstream is a fight about that reversal.

His most striking move is the two-truths asymmetry: he'll say the ultimate is *permanent, stable, eternal* — using precisely the vocabulary Buddhism spent a millennium denying — while insisting this doesn't violate anātman because what's denied by anātman is the conventional self, and what's affirmed here is the ultimate that was never a conventional entity. Kālacakra's "empty form" gives him a technical handle. Critics say he smuggled Vedānta in through a Buddhist window; his defenders say he restored what the Prajñāpāramitā-only reading had flattened.

The suppression is real but the shape of it matters. Tāranātha (1575–1634) is the great later systematizer and historian of the school. In the 1650s, in the wake of the Fifth Dalai Lama's consolidation, the central Jonang monasteries were converted to Gelug and their printing blocks sealed — the political situation was entangled with Tsangpa patronage and the civil wars, so it isn't a clean case of doctrine alone being punished, but doctrine was in it. Meanwhile the school survived: a Jonang population continued in Amdo and Golok, at Dzamthang and Tsangwa, never actually extinguished, and it was recognized as an independent school by the Tibetan government in exile only in the 2000s. So the tradition is currently in a modest revival with its own lineage holders.

Shentong also leaked. Rimé-era figures — Jamgön Kongtrul most prominently — took up Shentong readings within Kagyu, and there's a strain of Nyingma sympathy, partly because Dzogchen's rigpa-language sits comfortably with a positively-characterized ultimate. Gelug polemic against it has been sustained and sharp for six centuries. Whether Dölpopa is best read as a monist is contested; Hopkins translated him and Stearns wrote the standard study, and both are worth having.

For your purposes the interesting seam is that Shentong gives you a Buddhist ultimate that is luminous, positively characterized, and permanent — which meets your objection about having nothing to be in relation with — while still refusing a *personal* Absolute and still treating buddha-nature as one's own nature rather than an Other. So it may resolve less than it looks like at first: you'd get the fullness and lose the relation. But the family resemblance to pratyabhijñā is real enough that Tibetans themselves accused Dölpopa of it, in almost those words.

> [!quote] Me
> Taranatha even sounds like a Saiva name. I assume there weren't many hindus in Tibet then?

The name is Sanskrit but not Śaiva — it's Tārā-nātha, "protector of Tārā" or "lord of Tārā," a Buddhist theophoric using the Tibetan convention of Sanskrit names for learned monks, alongside his Tibetan name Kun dga' snying po. The *-nātha* suffix is what's pinging for you, and reasonably: it carries a Śaiva load in your usual reading, most obviously the Nāth sampradāya. But it's a generic honorific across Indic religions — the Buddha is Lokanātha, Avalokiteśvara is Nātha, and Jain tīrthaṅkaras carry it too. Tārā's Buddhist credentials are impeccable and the compound is entirely at home.

On Hindus in Tibet: essentially none as a resident community, and never a Hindu presence in the institutional sense. What Tibet had was something more interesting — sustained, technically sophisticated *contact* with Indian religion. Tibetan scholars translated and refuted Hindu positions in the pramāṇa literature, the Kālacakra is full of Śaiva material, and the Nātha siddhas and the Buddhist mahāsiddhas share names, songs, and a subcontinental milieu. Nepal was the interface, with Newar Buddhism sitting right next to Newar Śaivism — so Tibetans encountered Hindu tantra through neighbors, texts, and pilgrimage rather than through domestic converts.

Tāranātha himself is directly relevant here: his *History of Buddhism in India* is a major source for exactly that world, and he traveled with and learned from Indian teachers — Buddhaguptanātha above all, a wandering Indian siddha whose biography Tāranātha wrote and who is one of the last documented figures of that lineage world. So the man's Sanskrit name and his Indian connections are of a piece, and they're Buddhist connections, in a period when Buddhism in India was already nearly gone.

The general point stands: Sanskrit religious vocabulary is shared property, and the sectarian ownership of terms is much softer than the sect-labels suggest — which is the same lesson as the Mahāvidyā survey.
