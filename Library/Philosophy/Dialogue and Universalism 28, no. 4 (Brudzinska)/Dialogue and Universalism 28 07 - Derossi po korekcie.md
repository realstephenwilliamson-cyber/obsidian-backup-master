## Derossi po korekcie

*Giorgio Derossi* 

BEYOND “WESENSCHAU:” 

THE MAURICE MERLEAU-PONTY “AMBIGUITY” 

BETWEEN SENSATION AND PERCEPTION 

*ABSTRACT* 

 One of the basic reasons for Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s critique of Edmund Husserl’s *Wesenschau* is represented by what has been defined as the “ambiguity” of the perceiver-perceived relationship, which is the theme of the “phenomenology of perception” developed by the French philosopher. Such ambiguity is in effect constitutive of fundamental perceptive-cognitive relationships; and—in the mature thought of Merleau-Ponty—it also extends, from an ontological point of view, to the “*chair du monde*” in which being and non-being, visible and invisible, are but two sides of the same “reality.” In this contribution we try to highlight the ambiguous characteristics of corporeal intentionality which render it incompatible with visual perception. And we propose both the necessity and the possibility of eliminating this incompatibility with a new phenomenological approach, which is also consistent with scientific “visualisation.” 

 Keywords: Maurice Merleau-Ponty, ambiguity, intentionality, corporeality, perception, sensation, visualisation, existence, essence. 

 Without doubt, one of the most significant events to occur in the philosophical and scientific fields of the last century is—as is well-known—the revaluation of the nature of perception and its role in the cognitive process. It is a revaluation that has promoted the liberation from the undervaluations, at times superficial, formulated in the successive orientations, occasionally prevalent, of empiristic, intellectualistic, idealistic thought. The feature such undervaluations have in common, apart from their manifold variants, has been the thesis (expressed paradigmatically by Immanuel Kant) that perception essentially does nothing more than supply the “intellect” with the material necessary to feed the cognitive process produced by the intellect itself. 

 It is the sensations, actually, that procure the shapeless raw material, striking the sensory apparatus of the percipient with a confused mass of stimuli emitted 

*[p. 156]* ^pg158

from the environment. As soon as they are received, the stimuli are put in order, *formed*, by “pure intuition” which, although operating in the sphere of sensitivity—as Kant emphasizes—is not itself sensitive, but is precisely “pure” *a priori*. 

 This “pure” *a priori*, however, was justifiably turned into a problem by an innovative approach, experimentally verified and theoretically elaborated between the end of the 19th and the beginning of the last century by Gestalt psychology. The results of the Gestaltists’ inquiries show that the forming of the sensitive material occurs primarily not only through the work of the perceiver, but also through the autonomous configuration of the perceived already in the ambit of sensitivity—precisely because in this ambit there are *elements* which, as Kant insisted, since they give form (Gestalt) to the shapelessness of the sensations, are not identifiable with them, and yet are not *a priori* inasmuch as they too are perceived precisely because they are not acknowledged as sensations. 

 The difference with regard to the Kantian theory is substantial: in short, it is the sphere of sensitivity to widen and reveal itself as provided with resources that refer to intellectual implementation. In the field of philosophy the phenomenology originating with Franz Brentano and Edmund Husserl, the restoring of value to intentional acts of consciousness also restored the possibility of a direct consciousness of things (not mediated by being systematically “put in brackets,” or by prejudice and bias) and, with Husserl’s *Wesenschau*, also of the essence of phenomena. Thus purified, the phenomenal appearance permits access to a knowledge that is certain because of *immediate evidence*. 

 For Husserl intentionality still remained an act of consciousness, but the phenomenal appearance which was its correlation could not but lead Husserl himself to examine the role of perception in this appearance. However, as we know, it was Maurice Merleau-Ponty who embarked upon and methodically conducted throughout the course of his philosophical reflection an in-depth examination of this role. Thus he not only rehabilitated perception with regard to the other cognitive faculties, but also went further: he made it the original matrix of all the others by reducing the intentionality of consciousness to the intentionality of “the one’s own body” (*corps propre*). This radical transposition did not fail to excite criticism and perplexity and was considered a turning point in phenomenology with an existentialist slant, because it generated a kind of confusion, an “ambiguity” as to the respective roles of subject perceiver and object perceived, of the self and the world. 

 This brings us to a considerable problem related to the appearance of new themes, epistemological and cognitive in character, that have been sparked off by revolutionary developments in science. Merleau-Ponty’s version of phenomenology seems not to be equipped to confront these, despite the fact that the most pregnant theories, e.g. of physics—such as Albert Einstein’s Theory of Relativity and the Quantum Theory—use investigative tools such as “mental experiments” (*Gedankenexperimente*) of a perceptive-visual nature in order to make clear (as Einstein says) the “physical meaning” of mathematical formulas. 

*[p. 157]* ^pg159

Drastic evidence of this inadequacy, which paradoxically renders the phenomenology of perception quite unable to thematise the role of perception in science (which it underrates), is the recollection (on which we shall later pause briefly) of the historic debate that took place in Paris between Henri Bergson and Einstein on the relativistic conception of time, which Merleau-Ponty interprets as evidence of a radical heterogeneity and consequent incommunicability between two opposing worlds.[^1] 

 I have no room here for an in-depth analysis of this crucial point, which today is perhaps of major importance from both aspects, phenomenological and epistemological. Rather, I shall try to indicate, synthetically, certain basic reasons that might explain this setback: the main one I consider to be not so much the existential curvature that Merleau-Ponty stamped upon the phenomenology of perception—which is not exclusive to him—as that which is the cause of it: the attempt to “deliver” perception from its passive status by way of reversing the roles of perceiver and perceived which begets the inextricable interweaving of “ambiguity.” 

 To this end, it is important to once more consider the meaning of the keyword “ambiguity,” already adopted by the first interpreters of the *Phénoménologie de la perception* (published in 1945), followed by many others.[^2] The word indicates the indissoluble, organic interweaving of subject and object, the self and the world, which is realised in the “one\`s own body.” The clear manifestation of this basic interweaving is provided by the correlation *toucher-touched*, in which there is total reversibility between the two roles: the toucher is at the same moment touched, and vice versa. Subject and object, soul and body, are implied organically, precisely because each performs both roles at the same time. 

 This is the basis upon which the articulated evaluation of perception is developed in Merleau-Ponty’s phenomenological-existential theory. The afore-mentioned toucher-touched correlation might seem a mere tactile sensation, which falls within the sphere of the *sensations*, generally distinct (especially for Kant) from that of the *perceptions* in that the former provides the *material* which receives *form* in the latter. The basic problem, not by chance confronted as a first step in the *Transcendental Aesthetics* at the beginning of *The Critique of Pure Reason*, and once more confronted in changed terms in the *Gestaltpsychologie,* concerns the “elements” that produce the synthesis of material and form: and such a synthesis is precisely what perception is.

Clearly emerging here is an interpretation of the perceptive act of an existential sort—similar to those of Jean-Paul Sartre and Martin Heidegger—in which the heart of knowledge is “existence” and not “essence:” hence the decisive rejection of Husserl’s *Wesenschau*. Also consciousness (or Descartes’ c*ogito*) is intimately bound to the body and always manifests itself as the consciousness of one who “recognises himself only in *contact* with things and with others.”[^4] Therefore the “world” is the assumption of whatever experience of any kind of “being.” Thus the primacy of “contact” is asserted not only with regard to one’s own body and others, but also to things, to the world. This is the original existential condition by which “we are mixed up with the world and with others in an *inextricable confusion*, which is precisely the ambiguity, namely perception as opinion or original faith (Husserl’s *Urdoxa* or *Urglaube*) in which the joining of man with the world is made visible.”[^5] 

 In this “made visible,” however, we encounter a difficult knot to untie; and in fact it is cut with a sort of act of faith, at a certain point explicitly confessed: perception, as immediacy of behaviour, in keeping with its structure, is to *believe* in a world. Being immediate, however, the belief in a world is not even an act of faith, which presupposes a doubt, but rather a pure “experience.” The knot is really cut and not untied: it is an obvious factual statement of the “presence” of the world—a statement tactile and not visible, contained in the immediate contact with the world which constitutively prevents a “detached” visibility. 

 This stringent continuance in the “original” is after all the final result of Husserl’s phenomenology itself—shared with that of Heidegger—which ends up endorsing Merleau-Ponty’s negation of the *Wesenschau*. It is not by chance that our author notices and underlines that the later Husserl, as opposed to the one of the *Grammatica pura*, understands linguistic reflection as an effort to “recognise the speaking subject, not as a transcendental subject detached from all the linguistic situations in which he may find himself, but as a speaking subject who does not gaze upon a truth and does not reach a presumed universal thought except through a certain linguistic situation and the exercise of the language.”[^6] It must be stressed that a similar conclusion is also reached by the later

Ludwig Wittgenstein in his attempt to extricate himself from the *impasse* of the *Tractatus*. But it must also be noted that in all these final outcomes the condition that makes it possible to refer to the “speaking subject,” to “linguistic situations,” to “linguistic games,” etc., namely *language*, this remains an unavoidable premise insofar as it is provided with an *autonomous* structure, with a life, that is to say a history, of its own. The language “lives” by virtue of the bipolarity constituted by the linguistic system and by its use in communicative acts, but this bipolarity does not endanger, in fact it presupposes, the autonomy of the system of signs that make communication possible. 

 This is precisely an organic correlation—in the Saussurian terminology—between *langue* and *parole*: the latter is precisely the individual use of the former, which is the “diachronic” and lasting possession of a community, guaranteed by the “laws” that govern its evolution and by the grammatical rules that preside, in synchrony, over its *correct* use. The *word* is an effective means of communication only if it is a correct use of the *language*, respectful of its rules. If a speaking subject adopts rules different from those of the other subjects, he neither understands nor makes himself understood. This fact is not contestable and is not contested by Merleau-Ponty, but it seems underrated by him to the extent to which he reduces linguistic knowledge not to the acquisition of existing grammatical rules in the language *independently of their use* through the exercise of speaking (*parole*), but rather by means of the ambiguous interpenetration of *language and speaking* in the *speaking*: it is as if, in the “own body” of language, *language* and *speaking, signifying* and *signified* exchanged their respective roles continually in an unstoppably reversible manner. 

 It is certainly true that it is in our experience of speaking subjects that the germ of universality, which permits us to understand other languages, must be found. The *historical* grammars of languages (as opposed to Noam Chomsky’s universal “generative grammar”), precisely because they are such, do not preexist the experience of the speaking subject: they are formed and transformed depending on the experience, which tends spontaneously to procure a stable system of reference suitable to ensure an unambiguous communication, as free of misunderstanding as possible. But this very origin reveals that the transition from linguistic experience in embryo to the grammatical system of reference is a genuine “qualitative leap:” the codification of the grammar inherent in the individual acts of *speaking* is not a mere formal abstraction, but is the insurance against the undisciplined innovations which can compromise effective function and communicative activity. The roles of the one and the other are certainly closely correlated, but they are not for this reason reversible, precisely because they are reciprocally indispensable.[^7]

 Likewise, as regards the systems of the rules of logic, even supposing that they derive from the pre-objective existential stratum, it does not follow that in the latter they must be absorbed as pure intercorporeal perception. This holds good also for social relationships with others, which would come to be constituted as the equivalent of the individual corporeal perceptions. Between the people acclimatised to nature and the natural environment of people—Merleau-Ponty maintains—exists a “circle” whose reality is the truth of perception. As Giuseppe Semerari emphasizes, the role of this last is revealed also in this fundamental case, because it makes 

“going down again into the concreteness of existential relationships what can only be justified in it and receive an unambiguous meaning \[…\] Thus the enigma of the *Einfühlung* is explained by the circular correlation by which, while the body of the other is something perceived, the other is himself placed as percipient.”<sup>8</sup> In procuring the concreteness of existential relations, perception—specifies Merleau-Ponty—provides the *truth* which derives from the “contact between objectivity and subjectivity” and, with it, also access to *reality* by freeing it from “concluded meanings that want to be of value in themselves, delivered from the control of things and from the struggle of the unexpressed for expression.”<sup>9</sup> 

 The preceding brief synthesis makes clear the pre-eminence of perception, and that therefore phenomenology must be essentially a “phenomenology of perception”—in agreement, moreover, with Husserl’s final analyses related to *Lebenswelt*. For such pre-eminence, however, one must pay the price of “ambiguity,” namely of another pre-eminence, this time in the same ambit as perception: that of “sensation” as regards the “perceived” endowed with a definite “form” as, in particular, the auditory and especially the visual. It is on the determination of these forms that the theory of perception of *Gestaltpsychologie* has developed*.* Making evident the factors that determine the constitution of “good” forms has supplied an innovative solution to the crucial problem of perception: namely that of the “constant” modalities in which the shapelessness of sensations receives form. It is a solution different from the classic Kantian one which, as we pointed out earlier, attributes to “*a priori* intuition” the capacity to give form to the chaotic matter of the sensations. For the Gestaltists this capacity is not *a priori* in the Kantian sense, even if it is presupposed as belonging to the perceiving subject: but that does not, however, detract from the fact that the perceived, on the other hand, “imposes” *its* form on the perceiver himself. The latter, therefore, does not give, Kant-like, an *a priori* form to the object, but rather makes it his own in the act of perception.

 It would indeed seem that the relation of reversibility between perceiving subject and perceived object which Merleau-Ponty focuses upon is also to be found in the *Gestaltpsychologie*. That he had borrowed it from that same *Gestaltpsychologie*, with which he in turn would have an “ambiguous” relationship, is a hypothesis which, however, does not agree with the fact that for the Gestaltists the “pre-eminence” is not so much due to the perceiver-perceived relationship as to the perceived (not by chance defined by Wolfgang Metzger as the “encountered”). This last, in short, does not simply propose but imposes itself forcibly on the *logos*, that is, the logic of the intellect, also contradicting it irreversibly, without the possibility of a “dialectic” synthesis.[^10] Therefore, we have a situation opposed to that of ambiguity, namely a relationship of reciprocal incompatibility, which shows itself incontrovertibly in the so-called *Enttäu-schungen* (optical “illusions”), in which the visual perceived in certain conditions is not congruent with the same perceived in “normal” conditions. Not only do the two perceived not match each other, but one of them does not even match the corresponding evaluation on an intellectual level. Therefore in these cases a hiatus is created that is unbridgeable between perception and intellection which is not compatible with the argument that would derive the second from the first. 

 The acquired awareness that the perceived has its own logic not always congruent with that of the percipient produces a crack in that organic bond between them that guaranteed the immediate presence of reality in consciousness through “bodily intentionality.” Husserl had already indicated that under representative intentionality there is a deeper intentionality (the “functioning intentionality”—*fungierende Intentionalität*), and that this coincides with “existence” itself, the real “essence” that is not so much *seen* as *experienced.* Merleau-Ponty, as we have seen, had undertaken in the *Phénoménologie de la perception* the vivisection of the *frame* of perception, after the one carried out on the “structure of behaviour.” He had tried to lay bare, in *evidence*, the essential-existential ganglia of the perceptive articulation: the “incarnation” of the c*ogito*, the primordial experience, Husserl’s *Urdoxa*, etc., weaving the weft of the intentional threads that tie the body to the world and the world to the body. But then the “perceptual evidence,” the central theme, had become ever more of a problem—and not only for the hypothetical influence of *Gestaltpsychologie—* as had also the relationship of perception with reflection, the logos: thereby, ambiguity had become problematic as a “theme.” 

 Husserl’s functioning intentionality is also based on a relationship of ambiguity between radical reflection and the “unreflecting.” But the latter, like sensation, shows itself to be essentially shapeless, so much so that in the *Krisis* Husserl is compelled to admit that “the thing in itself is actually *what in reality no one has ever seen*, because it is in continuous motion \[…\] And not even perception

really exists.”[^11] The essential connection between seeing and perceiving is without doubt inevitable, albeit negatively. Consequently the thing, the world of things, although participating in “the absolute certainty of the world in general,” prior to any explicit knowledge, for this very reason becomes “absolute mystery,” an “impenetrable opaqueness,” in which the mystery of reason is rooted. The task of philosophy, then, becomes that of “re-learning to *see* the world.” However, the final version of the phenomenology developed by Husserl and Merleau-Ponty does not seem equipped to achieve this final goal, which at this point is clearly identified. 

 A verification concerning this can be made by a synthetic examination of two emblematic cases in which Merleau-Ponty undertakes to show the necessity and the possibility of “re-learning to see”. One of these is made up of a description of the modalities and the implications inherent in the perception of the *cube*. As is well known, this is an illustration which lends itself particularly to analyses of this type, not by chance recurrent in thinkers even of a different orientation (as, for example, Husserl, Wittgenstein and others). Merleau-Ponty re-employs it several times, from the *Structure du comportement* to *Phénoménologie de la perception,* to *Le visible et l’invisible*. 

 What interests us now is to pause to focus on this last in particular.[^12] Merleau-Ponty uses it here to put in evidence once more the inextricable interweaving, the *chiasma*, of subject and object in the same *flesh (of the world)*; but this time, “ontologically,” subtracted from the problems inherent in the intentionality of consciousness and the one\`s own body. The *flesh (chair*), indeed, is not experienced directly or indirectly, since it is formed in the “aperture” generated by the “depth.” This therefore is not *perceptible*, it is a kind of *invisible* fabric, the “meeting-place” between the subject and things. The conditioning of the perceptible is thus removed, but not completely, since there remains, ineliminable, the relationship of *reversibility—*perceptible-imperceptible, visible-invisible, presence-absence. The “full” of the visible perceived is substituted by the “empty” of the invisible, and eventually this produces that “separation” which is characteristic of “seeing” but not of the perceptible, which is made up of continuously variable forms. 

 It seems that this ontological shift could free the perception-reflection relationship from the mortgage of ambiguity. But as we know, ambiguity is nothing but the product of reversibility which, once again, is looked for and found, now, in vision. This is certainly “separation” (which is lacking, instead, in tactile sensation); but it is also, at the same time, overcoming the distance between perceiver and perceived—not in the sense that a direct contact is established between them (just as in the above-mentioned sensation), but rather in the sense

that the contact takes place *at a distance* (which can never be cancelled, but only reduced in order to see better). This seeing at a distance entails that the vision is perspectival, that a cube, for example, is seen in different ways according to the varying points of view, that one can see only three sides instead of six, that the sides are seen as diamonds and not squares, etc. 

 But what of the cube composed of six equally square sides, the cube “as defined in geometry,” the “idea” of the cube, the “pure meaning” of the term “cube”? How can one grasp the issue of this pure “meaning”? Merleau-Ponty’s reply is not new and refers to the one already given earlier: to say that I have a view of the cube is the same as to say “I go from me to the cube, I exit from myself in order to enter the cube. I and my view are, together with it, caught in the same carnal world, i.e., my view and my body also emerge from the same being which is, among other things, a cube.”<sup>13</sup> In place of the subject’s own body there is now the flesh of the world, or rather the being in which the entry into me (of a cube, for instance) is reversible with my entry into the cube: the “flesh” exercises the same function as the “body.” The ambiguity implied in “see” (seen–seeing) is of the same sort as that of “touch” (touched–touching), only it has a wider range: “I see myself seeing, but by overstretching I complete my visible body, I extend my being-seen beyond my being-visible for me.”<sup>14</sup> However, the visible-invisible couple itself does not seem reducible, in these terms, to the reversibility relationship. The invisible itself, in actual fact, appears ambiguous in its turn: the invisible is obviously the “pure meaning,” the” platonic idea” of the cube; but the invisible is also the cube composed of six equally square sides. The adimensional invisible can be *rendered visible* by means of a tridimensional object or a bidimensional image. While the first shows only three of the six sides, the second can show them all: not indeed by placing them one beside the other but rather by opposing them two by two, thanks to the fact that each side can be seen both in front of and behind another, which generates the vision of depth and the figure’s instantaneous change of direction. 

 The relationship *before-behind* is reversible: or rather, the reversibility is an indication of the depth inasmuch as it renders visibility more evident in an image which is bidimensional, intended so that all the sides of the cube are visible (as with a tridimensional, transparent cube). But this reversibility is characteristic of the figure, is internal to the perceived: it is not between seeing and seen, since being seen-behind is not concurrent with being seen-in front, and vice versa. Being seen is instantaneous because the entire figure is seen. If it is not seen entire, the sides must be made to revolve and the temporal distance takes over, which is why the aforementioned reversibility cannot in any case be instantaneous.

 The invisible, then, is not the other side of the visible, but rather its condition of possibility, in the strict sense that it is the condition of possibility of rendering visible what was not. Thus to see the side of the cube which is now in front depends on no longer seeing it from behind. That does not mean that it remains invisible from behind: rather it means that to render it visible here, I must render it invisible there, and vice versa. Visible and invisible are not two stable and opposite attributes of Being, but are the results of a structural-dynamic correspondence between perceiver and perceived. The ontological shift effected in the *Le visible et l’invisible* had to extend ambiguity proper to corporeal-existential intentionality to visual perception through ambiguity proper to the ontological intentionality inherent in the flesh of the world. But not even this ontological transposition succeeds in opening the lock of the subject-object reversibility in the seeing-seen relationship: reversibility and ambiguity exist not between perceiver and perceived but rather inside the perceived: therefore the most important legacy of the phenomenology of perception is, in short, that perception is the main road to giving our knowledge an ontological value. 

 We can say conclusively that “ambiguity” based on the reversibility of the perceiving subject and perceived object produced by the intentionality of the one\`s own body is functional to the ontological transposition of conscious intentionality. This transposition is the real objective of Merleau-Ponty’s phenomenology, and “ambiguity” with all its implications is the manifestation of its actual achievement. We have tried to show that the mediation of the “one’s own body”—essential to the corporealization of intentionality—does not permit perception to produce the *forms (Gestalten)* experimentally identified by *Gestaltpsychologie*, and so corporeal intentionality remains imprisoned—as many critics have shown—in a shapeless mesh of sensations. In the *Le visible et l’invisible* Merleau-Ponty concludes his extreme attempt to overcome the limits of corporealization by transposing it into a kind of “body” of the world which is the flesh. But to do so he must retrieve visual perception and, with it, the *distance* between perceiver and perceived which characterises it. The visible-invisible ambiguity is not, however, possible, since the existence of one excludes that of the other and the two poles do not intertwine. 

 This final check to the phenomenology of perception understood as corporeal and carnal intentionality is not devoid of consequences, equally on the gnoseological as on the ontological level. By reducing the role of *Gestalt*, Merleau-Ponty also considerably reduces the possibility of giving rational form to phenomenal reality on the basis of the perceptual-visual matrix held for the original “system of reference” indispensable to all knowledge. Consequently it does not, on the one hand, succeed in undermining “intellectualism,” in particular Kantian, and, on the other, does not even suspect that the most innovative theories of contemporary science—like those of biology and physics—are expounded and explained also with a no less innovative and highly significant recourse to visual perception. In fact, the latter is no longer assigned only with 

*[p. 165]* ^pg167

the task of providing the empirical raw material (“phenomena” in the conventional sense) for experiments and mathematical elaboration, but also with that of implementing the cognitive meaning of the formal apparatus with a more sophisticated and complete “visualisation” of phenomena, which are thereby “seen” in a new and very different light. 

 One of the most instructive examples of this drastic revaluation of “phenomenal vision” is certainly that of the role reserved to it in the Einsteinian demonstration of the “relativity of time” linked to the different “systems of reference” of the observers (“perceiving subjects”). Merleau-Ponty treats it by recalling, as we noted earlier, the discussion that took place between Einstein and Bergson on April 6th 1922 at the Société de Philosophie of Paris. Bergson, Merleau-Ponty recounts, presented some ideas with which it was proposed to attenuate the paradoxical appearance of the Einsteinian theory by bringing it close to the common experience of time, for which it is unique from one end of the world to the other. But Einstein pointed out that this *lived* time does not authorise such a global extension of our intuitive notion of simultaneity. The common experience of the perceived world is found wanting when faced with its more articulated “visualisation.” 

 Merleau-Ponty imputes to Einstein the error of arbitrarily attributing to the theory of physics not simply the value of a mathematical expression, but also that of being a “direct observation of the real.” But, we may ask ourselves, is not a similar error perhaps committed also by philosophy, and specifically by the “phenomenology of perception,” to the extent that it does not manage to “visualise” the peculiar structure of the visual form itself and reduces it to the ambiguous intentionality incorporated in an invisible “flesh of the world”? 

 Common experience is certainly the primary guide in our original contacts with the world. But this does not exclude, indeed demands, further experiences that emphasize, as Merleau-Ponty himself says, the “empty” as well as the “full,” the contradictions as well as the harmonies. Basic experience is not so much the theme of a symphony as the problem of an ever-changing perspective. And the solution of this fundamental problem is not the exclusive concern of either science or philosophy, but of an ever more refined capacity for “seeing” the phenomena in depth: a capacity that only a phenomenology radically devoid of preconceptions and preliminary questions can procure for one and the other.
