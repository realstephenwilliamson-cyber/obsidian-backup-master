## 4 Kierkegaardian Wholeheartedness: Purity of Heart versus Doublemindedness

1\. 

THE REMAINING PROBLEMS FOR A REALIST 

NARRATIVE ACCOUNT OF PERSONAL AUTONOMY

The previous chapters have argued that a ‘real narravive’ theory of practical identity helps solve major objections to narrative approaches and that and that needed modifications to early hierarchical theories of autonomy show that personal identity also has stronger narrative conditions. Kierkegaard’s distinction between ethical and aesthetic “stages” or basic orientations to life can then be explained in terms of the volitional and narratival differences that caring and wholeheartedness make in one’s practical identity. And Kierkegaard’s examples of aestheticism and contrasting descriptions of autonomous self-direction help defend such a narrative account of personal autonomy from further objections, while complementing related contemporary arguments that personal autonomy rationally requires recognition of ethical norms as an implicit precondition. Strawson’s objections to ethical narrativity conditions ignore these findings about personal autonomy.

But some significant problems remain. The special problem that mortality seems to pose to narrative theories and the suggestion that narrative realism makes selves into four-dimensional wholes are closely related metaphysical objections that I will address in the next chapter. This chapter will focus on Kierkegaard’s late account of narrative self-unification to deepen the case sketched in Chapter 3 (§4.1) for the Ethical thesis (G), which faces at least two open questions. First, we need to see more clearly why an aesthetic hero who is dedicated to nonmoral goals must find that unity-2 is not enough in life, or eventually discover a conflict among his cares. Imagine a doctor who solves hard medical mysteries purely as speculative problems (as others treat logic games) or for the advancement of scientific knowledge—perhaps an extreme version of the television character Dr. Gregory House, who lacks moral concerns. Can he not have a full and rich life? Similarly, Bernard Williams off ers the famous but controversial example of the artist Gauguin who abandons his family to travel to Tahiti because this is the only way he can pursue his artistic ground project.<sup>1</sup>Imagine a version 

*[p. 132]* ^pg151

of Gauguin who is not initially concerned with putative ethical obligations to his family, even if he recognizes them as external reasons; he *only* cares about his painting, though he is authentically devoted to some goods of this practice. To explain Kierkegaard’s full answer to this sort of case, we need to consider his signed account of ethical selfhood in his discourse “On the Occasion of a Confession,” and especially the section famously known as “The Purity of Heart.”<sup>2</sup>

Second, we need to explain how wholeheartedness can be restored for agents in the kind of predicament that Lippitt describes (see Chapter 3 §4.1) in which their cares have come to disastrous ends, or they find tragic conflicts between their cares. Such experiences can push persons towards what Kierkegaard’s later pseudonyms call “infinite resignation,” in which volitional devotion to the underlying values remains but is no longer actively pursued, or is pursued without any hope of success through one’s care and eff ort.<sup>3</sup> That unity-3 naturally tends towards and is completed in infinite resignation is another central topic of “Purity of Heart,” which helps show that recognition of human limits aids rather than undermining narrative unification on Kierkegaard’s mature view.<sup>4</sup> This kind of unifi - cation that he refers to as “purity” involves synchronic and diachronic aspects, and it proves to be an especially robust form volitional wholeheartedness (unity-3), which includes both essential consistency between the values grounding one’s cares and positive dedication to each of them as parts of a single life.

Lippitt has argued that we rarely need to consider our life “as a whole,” which is correct in the sense that an Articulation condition enriched for unity-3 could not plausibly require that we are constantly thematizing our whole history or explicitly reflecting on what our entire life might amount to, like a neurotic character in one of Woody Allen’s early existential comedies. Nor do either thesis (F) or (G) evidently require anything like this. But Kierkegaard’s discourse on “Purity of Heart” implies that the structure of a life made meaningful by caring gives us good reason to take the wholistic view at least at certain intervals, even if no personal crisis compels us. The overall point of this discourse is to ask us the existential question about our lives as a whole: “*What kind of life is yours; do you will one thing, and what is this one thing?*” (UDVS 126). But Kierkegaard is clear that this question about unity-3 is fully intelligible only if we already have ethically informed positive commitments that respond to our life-circumstances, or have “chosen the ethical” in the Judge’s sense:

Before being able to answer this earnest question earnestly, a person must already have chosen in life, chosen the invisible, the internal. He must live in such a way that he has hours and periods in which he collects his mind so that his life can attain the transparency that is a condition for being able to submit the question to himself and to answer it (UDVS 126–127).<sup>5</sup>

*[p. 133]* ^pg152

Thus in Kierkegaard’s view, the kind of wholeness of purpose involved in purity of heart is available only to agents who have at least reached the stage of caring that makes ethical norms relevant; for aesthetes of most varieties, this kind of unity in life-meaning is not even at issue.

Anticipating such a response, Lippitt writes, “Of course, an aesthete could not claim unity of the kind portrayed in Purity of Heart, but then neither, arguably, could Judge William.”<sup>6</sup> For Kierkegaard arguably portrays the Judge as biased towards a Hegelian conception of social life and as not having made the movement of “infinite resignation” or fully understood the faith that is needed to balance resignation.<sup>7</sup> Yet in his discourse on “Purity of Heart,” as in his essay on “The Present Age” and other signed writings, Kierkegaard clearly supports *all* the formal theses about practical identity that I have attributed above to the Judge in *Either/Or* II (including theses F, G, and H). In his reflections on confession and “purity,” Kierkegaard’s goal is to develop the Judge’s picture of narrative unity-3 as a distinguishing feature of ethical selfhood by connecting wholeheartedness directly to infinite resignation and indirectly to a religious concern with the finality of death. Moreover, the distinctions he develops between different forms of “double-mindedness” confirm the explanation of different types of “aesthetes” that I have offered in Chapter 3 (§2.2 & 4.1). This discourse is thus important not only because it confirms a narrative conception of the stages, but because it defends the strongest plausible narratival theses.

### 2 Forms of Disunity in “Purity of Heart:” from Ethical to Religious Self-Unification

#### 2.1 The PH Thesis and Narrative Identity

In past work, I have only briefly referred to the “Purity of Heart” discourse in explaining the largely prereligious picture of authentic selfhood that Kierkegaard draws in *Either/Or* II and later pseudonymous books. But it has not been clear to commentators that this work supports a narrative conception of identity. On the contrary, Lippitt asserts that “the kind of unity Kierkegaard valorizes” in the “Purity of Heart” discourse cannot help the “MacIntyrean Kierkegaardian case” because it directs us away from all “temporal existence” towards the eternal. All finite goods in temporal life are contingent unities at best; “Thus the essential unity valorized by *Purity of Heart* is not narrative unity.”<sup>8</sup>

On the contrary, I argue that the more demanding version of unity-3 developed in “Purity of Heart” is clearly diachronic as well as synchronic; it is especially focused on repentance of past mistakes and anticipation of death as ways of strengthening our present will to form and sustain an absolute commitment to “the good.” Its recommended devotion to an eternal ideal that transcends all finite values is *not* like the mystical abstraction 

*[p. 134]* ^pg153

from temporal life critiqued by the Judge (EO II 242–249); rather, as Sheridan Hough argues, it agrees with the idea emphasized in *Sickness* that the “infinite worth” of one’s commitment to be a self is “made manifest in specific, ‘finite’ choices” for the commitment to establish “continuity in life.”<sup>9</sup> Willing the good is a temporally extended activity, a *process* that persists through lived time up to death (though we can only approach this ideal). As we will see, this involves a kind of infinite resignation: it is our eff ort that matters, because success in the outcome is never assured by our trying (UDVS 88–90), and the ever-growing robust meaning that a wholehearted life has to its agent must end in death. Thus she has to consider whether this subjective meaning can acquire *ultimate* meaning—an objective significance that no longer changes, that is eternal. In that sense, one who is dead always “remains true to himself . . . one and the same” (55). This is why “a sense of shame before one who is dead” is edifying (54), for it is equivalent to the “voice of conscience” that is the judgment of our life (as lived so far) in eternal perspective (127–128).

This link between considering the character of our life ‘as a whole’ and its potentially eternal meaning will be clearer after we see that this discourse defends a strong form of narrative unity-3. What I have called the “continuity” of cares over time and existential “coherence” among them Kierkegaard treats using the medieval virtue-concepts of “patience” and “purity.” These are closely linked from the first page of the discourse, where both are associated with “perseverance” in the face of diffi culty and suffering, and “concentration” in the face of distraction (UDVS 7). Contra the *Ecclesiastes* refrain that “everything has its time,” there is a striving that is always appropriate, a purpose and hope that should continue throughout life. We are always called forward to “the good,” and backwards to regret our past errors (13–14). Proper regret requires a “collected mind” so that repentance remains firm in the continuation of new resolutions (14); like wine, “the longer and more deeply it is preserved, the better it becomes” (19). A person who has “hardened his heart to regret nothing” might seem to have unity of purpose but is actually divided from himself (13). For example, consider Dickens’ character Ebeneezer Scrooge, who pursues wealth without limit. He is not a simple aesthete, for he *identifies* with his desires for monetary security and power over others, and he tries to maintain this identity by a second-order volition *not to regret* his past choices. But such refusal is a defense against a deeper psychological disunity. He starts to recover only when (with the help of the Ghost of Christmas Past) he realizes that he *cannot* “understand” the choice that was the turning point in his narravive up until now—the choice to let his fi anceé go—and from a more fully informed perspective, he does *not* really identify with the obscure fears and angers stemming from his childhood that motivated it. As Haufniensis would predict, Scrooge is not as wholehearted a miser as he seemed.

Consider in this light the signature thesis of “Purity of Heart” that “*In truth to will one thing can therefore mean only to will the good*” (UDVS 

*[p. 135]* ^pg154

34), which I label the *PH thesis*. Its equivalent contrapositive is that “the person who wills one thing that is not the good is actually not willing one thing; it is an illusion, a semblance, a deception, a self-deception that he wills only one thing” (25). Some commentators have seen this thesis as tendentious: in their view, it simply reasserts the old Platonic and Augustinian dogma that we should not care about temporal goods; or it loses the Judge’s balance between the aesthetic and ethical in a complete otherworldliness; it is a formal principle that turns the claim that aesthetes lack volitional unity into a mere tautology (as it simply stipulates that only one religious end is really “unified”). Read this way, the discourse seems to have a severe puritanical cast that is inconsistent with the rich plurality of earthly values we commonly recognize as worth caring about in this life: it demands a kind of unity inconsistent with such plurality. Like some ancient Stoics, it would have us give up love of our friends in the name of an eternal good that is not subject to luck. On this reductive reading, Lippitt would be correct that the PH thesis is no help to contemporary narrativists.

I argue instead that the PH thesis can be understood more accurately and fruitfully as asserting that every volitional stance or set of cares not governed by second-order perfectionist ethical ends willed to the point of infinite resignation cannot become synchronically wholehearted and remain so over time even in the face of death. This might seem as facially improbable as Socrates’s famous claim that a good man cannot really be harmed; for there are apparently plenty of materialists and sensualists who are happy pursuing their egoistic goals to their last gasp. But all is not as it seems. Scrooge’s devotion to gold, for example, turns out to be deeply “double-minded” because he cannot affi rm the choices that led to it in a more encompassing reflective stance, i.e. adequately informed reflection on the meaning of his life as a whole. Applied to his case, the PH thesis means just what Scrooge’s nephew recognized in coming to his (apparently paradoxical) conviction that somewhere inside Scrooge “there is a better man struggling to get out.” The PH thesis holds that this is *always* the case to some extent except in the ethically best possible shape our will can achieve—which is to say that, in practice, this telos of practical identity is always ahead of us. This is certainly a contentious thesis, but not due to rejecting immanent values in this life; rather, it is a very optimistic view of our nature, our potential, and our existential telos.

So understood, the PH thesis is simply a stronger version of the idea defended in the *Concept of Anxiety* that in contrast to demonic and anxious states, “\[t\]he good signifies continuity” (CA 130; compare SUD 105, 107). This is not just a diachronic but a narrative continuity: “only in the good is there a unity of state and transition” (CA 113). The clear relation between these claims and the PH thesis is supported by the similarity between Haufniesis’s distinctions among different forms of spiritual anxiety and Kierkegaard’s own typology of “double-mindedness.”

#### 2.2 A Typology of Aesthetic and Ethical “Doublemindedness”

The “Purity of Heart” discourse distinguishes several forms of what Kierkegaard calls “double-mindedness” or division in one’s identity. Though he does not make an explicit list, his examples and explanations reveal several levels and types of volitional disunity that contrast with the unified ideal of “purity.” As we will see, these distinctions agree well with the forms of aestheticism distinguished in the narrative analysis of the aesthetic-ethical distinction refined in the prior chapter.

First, Kierkegaard’s conception of “the good” or ethical ideal that is related to purity of heart agrees with the Regulative thesis (H). From the start of his defense of the PH thesis, Kierkegaard makes it clear that “the good” that is “one thing” and thus can be the object of a unified will is not one among other values to be discovered by trial and error in life (UDVS 24); rather, it underlies these more familiar goods and its importance can be discovered by pursuing them. Thus the Judge’s irenic thesis that “all roads *lead* to the good, even the road of error” is retained though also revised to include repentance (UDVS 25, 35). Much as his “Present Age” essay clarifies volitional authenticity and integrity by contrasting them with the dissolution of identity and careless curiosity, Kierkegaard’s analysis in “Purity of Heart” clarifies volitional unification by contrasting it with an array of “double-minded” agents. To each type I will first assign a number label that corresponds to their position in a psychology hierarchy, though I will also give them descriptive names. The numbering clarifies that for expository reasons, Kierkegaard’s text introduces them in an order that is slightly different than their position in the hierarchy.<sup>10</sup>

1\. As noted, “the good” for Kierkegaard refers to a regulative higher-order end. Like the “good will” in Kant, “the good” here concerns *how* other first-order goods are pursued and whether the inviolable value of each individual is respected or loved in these pursuits. Such a good will is most obviously absent from the pitiless hedonist who pursues “pleasure and honor and wealth and power” as if they were one thing when they are a changing “multiplicity” (UDVS 26–27). The problem is not just the familiar one that these desires can never be sated (27). The deeper problem is that these desires lead to consequentialist maxims that affi rm any means that serves to satisfy them; no loyalty to any limiting principle is then possible (28). Thus, as Hegel said, worldly power enslaves the master (29). Because the particular objects and states pursued under these worldly labels are fungible, or replaceable by others with similar features, there can be no loyalty to any individuals, offi ces, or social roles. Anything and anyone is dispensable in pursuit of entertainments, pleasures, leverage, status, or fame. Upon the “final change” of death, we are told, the person who has willed honor “must to his torment continue to will it when it has perished and by the torment of contradiction learn that it is not one thing” (29). This eschatological remark agrees with the Judge’s hint that in eternity, such an 

*[p. 137]* ^pg156

aesthete’s identity may “end with the disintegration of our essence into a multiplicity,” like Ovid’s demons (EO II: 160).

Kierkegaard contrasts such base aestheticism with essentially particularistic love, which is not spent in a single moment nor parceled out over temporal slices: “No, he loves with all his love; it is totally present in every expression \[of it\]; he continually spends all of it, yet he continually keeps it all in his heart” (UDVS 30). Love in the sense of commitment to a nonfungible individual is a paradigm of wholeheartedness: synchronically, the agent’s whole will is in each act flowing from her love, and diachronically, she maintains the same object over time. Thus the unity of such love is clearly narrative unity: the agent’s sense of past faithfulness to his goal helps sustain his pursuit of it into the future. Consider the classic example of Odysseus’s quest to return to his wife; he holds to his goal constantly throughout all the byways and opportunities for diversion. Similarly, Richard White argues that friendship involves botha free commitment to another person and a “profound trust” that requires ongoing acceptance of the other as distinctive and autonomous.<sup>11</sup>

2\. As noted in Chapter 3 and at the start of this chapter, the heroic aesthete who wills “*the great, no matter whether it is good or evil*” (UDVS 30) appears to have more narrative unity than the base aesthetes just described. For example, Napoleon certainly seemed to pursue military success with a dogged persistence (to the cost of many people). In response, Kierkegaard first repeats his pseudonym’s diagnosis from the *Concept of Anxiety*: such a person either still recognizes the good but is too weak to will it, or demonically rejects ethical ideals but cannot “tear himself completely loose from the good” (30). Recall that Haufniensis describes the demonic will as liable to “sudden” reversals, whereas “The good signifies continuity” (CA 130). However, in “Purity of Heart,” Kierkegaard goes on to consider more variants of this phenomenon than Haufniensis describes, thus deepening his earlier analysis.

2a. Most commonly, aesthetes who first will something *qua* great come to experience what I have called a “weakness of the higher-order will.”<sup>12</sup> Although they form higher-order volitions, they are *akratic* in maintaining them and/or acting on them:

They all have intentions, plans, and resolutions for life, indeed for eternity. But the intention quickly loses its youthful vigor and becomes decrepit, and the resolution does not stand firm and does not resist; it vacillates and is changed with the circumstances, and memory fails . . . (UDVS 31).

This “lethargy” of spirit is a failure of unity-2; unlike a demonic agent such as Napoleon who pursues evil greatness to the bitter end, these “sleepy souls” do not muster the volitional staying power that Kierkegaard calls “patience.” In another discourse, Kierkegaard describes spiritual depletion 

*[p. 138]* ^pg157

this way: “the power of the will has served its time and the enervated one has become just like dry-rot wood . . . curiosity, weary of life, seeks a more diverse task. . . .” (TDIO 92; compare SUD 58–59). Such agents have lost what Pastor Rick Warren famously calls a “purpose-driven life.”<sup>13</sup>

2b. The stronger agent who responds to ideals of greatness is determined to will one thing “regardless of whether it is good or evil.” Such a “self-willed” agent is no longer an aesthete because he recognizes the authority of the good, rather than languishing in ignorant unconcern for it, or trying to avoid awakening to it as the young man *A* does. In that sense, he is *categorically* within “the ethical” even though he opposes moral ideals. But for that very reason, he cannot be wholehearted in willing an end purely for the sake of its greatness; he is always trying to fend off the siren-call to repentance, no matter how bitter he is (UDVS 32–33). Like Saruman near the end of Tolkien’s *Lord of the Rings*, he cannot “help but turn around once more in longing for the good, no matter how dreadfully embittered he had become against this longing” (33). So it is impossible “that a person could harden himself to willing only evil” (34). This is another strong claim, rooted in a religious tradition: not every narrativist will accept that radically evil wills are necessarily torn by a lingering ambiguity such that, until death, liberty always remains to repent, however diffi cult it becomes.

2c. In between, we find heroic aesthetes who have not yet become weak-willed nor passed into demonic willfulness. Kierkegaard here repeats his familiar idea that such heroic devotion can develop through erotic love or passion for a cause:

Perhaps there was someone whom enthusiasm gripped for a specific endeavor. Full of enthusiasm, he willed only one thing: he would live and die for this endeavor, he would sacrifice everything for it, in it alone would have his happiness—because erotic love and enthusiasm are not content with a divided heart (35).

But such enthusiastic aestheticism is not a stable psychological state in Kierkegaard’s view. His claim is that it must develop in one of three ways. Either the agent loses her ardor, as in (2a), or she retains her pathos. In the latter case, she may recognize that she can be devoted to her particular cause or love only *because and insofar as* it is ethically good, beyond other concrete values—in which case her devotion to this cause embodies her deeper devotion to the ethical. Or in willfulness, (2b) she “swings off to the great instead of being lead to the good” (35). For example, consider an artist like Leni Riefenstahl whose passion for film led her to visions of greatness with little concern for justice. She clearly had doubts along her way, given that she vowed not to make more films for the Nazis after *Triumph of the Will*. Yet she did make more films for them, which she tried to deny until her contributions were proven. Her story is one of appalling division of the will, rather than its triumph. Self-deceptive versions of turning 

*[p. 139]* ^pg158

towards the great are possible too. Imagine the leader of a revolution like Danton or Castro who believes that his cause is just, but who continues it to vindictive extremes, refusing to recognize that it must then becomes unjust. He has betrayed whatever noble sentiments may have activated him at the beginning, and by the end there is no trace of nobility or even basic decency left.

The middle way from 2c that neither moves towards akrasia or willfulness leads to purity of heart. Consider a heroic aesthete who turns to the ethical rather than to greatness. Imagine a young Abraham Lincoln entering politics in the late 1830s with thoughts of improving life in his state, participating in a tradition going back to the revolutionary “spirit of 76,” and of making a name for himself. Although he considers Whig political principles just, his goal is not at first specifically moral: it is greatness through public service.<sup>14</sup> But this passion and service, both in state and federal politics and later in law practice, make him increasingly aware of moral issues involved in Westward expansion and the creation of new states. In 1854, after the passage of the Kansas-Nebraska Act, he realizes in shock during a sleepless night that “this nation cannot endure half-free, half-slave,” and he fears that slavery will slowly extend to every state, dimming the light of democratic ideals forever.<sup>15</sup> Later that year, by the time of Lincoln’s Peoria speech, his larger goal has changed: while most of his specific legislative aims remain the same, he must now think of them all within a moral framework that focuses on the enormous wrong of slavery, which he now says can never be justified by a majority voting for it.<sup>16</sup> The importance of this moral necessity to stop the spread of slavery grows ever greater for him, despite his loyalty to the constitutional order. He now wills everything else *through* willing the good. By the end of his life, the resulting moral clarity has even forced him, well ahead of his times, to recant his earlier endorsements of social segregation.

Lincoln’s progress as I have imagined it clearly contrasts with someone who finds her passion and cultivates it via higher-order volition, but loses her nerve when her acquired status starts to make her aware of ethical problems, or morally urgent needs. Say that she becomes a great doctor, but when the plague strikes, she flees rather than tending to the sick. Or she becomes a famous writer of mysteries and thrillers, but when a story of great moral import needs to be told, she puts down her pen. She becomes an Olympic athlete, but when her community needs her help to inspire children, or charities ask for help in publicizing their missions, she seeks a more private life. Such fear of moral demands that one never expected is only one way in which heroic aestheticism can lapse back into double-mindedness type 2a; commitment to the original end is given up lest it lead to an ethical conversion. As Kierkegaard’s Judge says, people fear devotion to the ethical because they “cannot really be sure what it will lead to” (EO II: 253), as Lincoln’s surprises surely illustrate. Heroic aestheticism tends to end in such spiritual lethargy or cowardice if it does not become a willful refusal 

*[p. 140]* ^pg159

of the good or earnest good will—though by self-deception, the heroic aesthete may be able to stall these pressures and remain in the unstable state of 2c for a long time.

3\. But double-mindedness extends into the ethical sphere for Kierkegaard, which helps show that its true opposite is unity-3: someone can care in an ethically informed manner, and even will the good, yet fail to will it wholeheartedly in a variety of ways. Such a person has second-order volitions and “a view of life according to which he wants to shape his life” (UDVS 56), but this view informing his higher-order will is not fully coherent.<sup>17</sup>

3a. The simplest of these are agents who choose goals for their ethical quality but also for their likeliness of success in the world. Here it looks at first like Kierkegaard is merely making Kant’s point that someone who does the right thing for the sake of some “reward” or material contribution to their well-being does not have a good will, or Plato’s point that seeming good will is not the real thing (UDVS 37). But there is more to Kierkegaard’s conception of “purity” than willing the good “without regard for the reward” (39). Someone may will *both* ethical goods and rewards as final ends, but when these goals come apart, he is stuck in indecision or volitional ambiguity: “in honesty, he does not dare to say definitely what he wills” (42). Such a person is not like Kant’s shopkeeper; he does care about the right for its own sake; but he also makes compromises for convenience, or cheats a little when the ethically best option is too hard.

3b. The same goes for a person who cares about the good, but only if it does not lead to punishment for her (45). She wills the good, but without giving it suffi cient priority or willing it “in truth;” thus she is enslaved to fears (51–52). Kierkegaard notes that someone can become double-minded in this two-ends sense by starting out with a heroic will for the good, but then weakening rather than growing wholehearted (43). It is implicit here that even a heroic striving never *starts* off wholehearted; that is something it can only become over time, if it does not succumb to akrasia. If this is right, then both synchronic coherence among one’s cares and diachronic unity in one’s development require a narrative process. The road to blessedness is “the continual transformation of the striving spirit” towards reliance on the good alone (49). There are many paths off this road into more complex ways of compromising standards and ideals to make them easier, but only by cheapening the ethical values they express.

0\. This developmental picture explains why aesthetic heroism (type 2) and ethically informed striving that is not yet wholehearted (type 3) are still far better than aesthetic “shrewdness” or cynicism that does not care earnestly about anything beyond material well-being. In this lowest of states for which we can be responsible according to Kierkegaard, heroism is dead (57); the agent is lost in the “miasma” of “light-mindedness, the cowardly whining of deference, the inflated significance of the moment” (UDVS 52)—in a word, something like Heidegger’s “everydayness.”<sup>18</sup> It is this level of aestheticism that sinks into idle talk, saying much without really meaning 

*[p. 141]* ^pg160

it (57)—for “meaning it” requires volitional devotion to something beyond oneself.<sup>19</sup> Rather than heroism, one is mired “in habit’s dull round of association with others, to the point of almost abandoning oneself while one plays with platitudes” (58). This kind of *volitional acedia* is the nadir of aestheticism; it is even farther from purity than pitiless worldly egoism (type 1). It is the result of unawakened aestheticism that never allows itself to hear the call of spirit to form positive cares and thus encounter the ethical—not even to the point where the active program of self-deception we found in the young man *A* would become a live option. As it is beneath *A*’s aesthetic bad faith and lacks even the superficial focus of a determined hedonist, I label this type 0. This spiritually sickest kind of aestheticism is not developmentally first; one dissolves into it from natural immaturity or unconcern for the ethical when one is too afraid to awaken to the primordial responsibility to care. Arguably, Judge William’s descriptions of Nero fit this type: his initial hedonism has descended into a “depression” that is really the sin of acedia in which all hint of “spirit” or the capacity for free caring remains suffocated (EO II 185–186; compare SUD 37). If this is correct, then all “shrewd” agents would become like Nero if they had his wealth or position; their spirit is in the same hysterical dissipation that comes from “not willing deeply and inwardly” even a nonmoral end in the way Gauguin does (EO II 189).

On my reading, this is the kind of ultra-aestheticism that Kierkegaard describes as willing nothing with consistency. Here “double-mindedness becomes more multifariously compounded” and there is no single “wrong road,” for the agent does not continue very long on any road (UDVS 64). By contrast, imagine a master thief who cares for his craft with a certain single-mindedness while trying to ignore its ethical problems, or a mother who cares entirely for her children and does everything possible for them . . . while trying not to think about the needs of anyone else’s children. Like Gauguin, these people do care about something; they have lasting convictions that they never (or only rarely) contradict in action (69). They can understand themselves in the mirror of their cares (67), even though they do not achieve wholeheartedness given their inattention to the ethical. But the dissipated aesthete only has the illusion of caring; he mistakes gossip for conviction and is under the “spell” of superficial busyness (66; compare SUD 33–34). So he does not recognize that he stands for nothing, and thus *is* nothing at the deeper levels of practical identity:

. . . in busyness, there is neither the time nor the tranquility to acquire the transparency that is necessary for understanding oneself in willing one thing, or for just temporarily understanding oneself in one’s unclarity. No busyness—in which one continually goes farther and farther, and noise, in which the true is continually forgotten more and more. . . . make it impossible for one to gain any deeper knowledge of oneself. A mirror . . . has the feature that a person can see his image in it, but then one must stand still (UDVS 67).

*[p. 142]* ^pg161

In other words, we have to care in a sustained way about something or someone to be capable of *existential honesty*, i.e. caring to know the truth about our cares. The “busy” person cannot even see his dissolution; he succeeds in life because his personality has an artificial gloss of integrity and unity with itself (77). Such aesthetes are capable of strong emotion, but they mistake “immediate feeling” for earnest caring. They forget that after beginning with passion, “then this feeling must be ‘kept’” (71) through ongoing eff ort of higher-order will. Kierkegaard’s example is a person who thinks he cares about justice because he is angry at being wronged, but who then wrongs others in similar fashion. He completely lacks the “firmness of conviction” that is required for sincerity (71–72). He may contemplate noble goals or profound ideas but does not engage them with his will or live them out in time; he does not even believe that the will is a first mover (74–75). In sum, “his will is not mature” (76). This diagnosis obviously agrees with Judge Williams’ themes, though his less developed distinctions among types of aesthetes are clarified in later works such as *Sickness Unto Death*, “The Present Age,” and “Purity of Heart.”

4\. On the opposite end of the spectrum, we find good agents who fail to will the good wholeheartedly or “in truth” not because of weakness or conflicting cares, but only because they are not able to will it with *infi -* *nite resignation*. We already know that in *Fear and Trembling*, Johannes *de Silentio* describes infinite resignation as a purification of the will and a unification of the self that (spiritually) must precede faith (FT 43–44). This conception is directly endorsed and clarified in several sections of “Purity of Heart.” The pure will not only gives priority to the (ethically) good above all other final ends, including the first-order goals involved in our most familiar activities and professions; it is also willing to pursue the ethical ideal even without any rational basis for believing it can be realized in the world, or any hope for success based on earthly powers. In the first part of the discourse, Kierkegaard notes two closely related ways in which an ethical will can fall short of infinite resignation:

. . . he is willing to sacrifice everything \[for the good\]; he fears nothing, but is not willing to sacrifice himself in daily self-denial—this he fears.

The double-minded person stands at the crossroads; then two visions appear, the good and the good in its victory, or even its victory through him. The latter is presumptuousness, but even the first two are not exactly the same. In eternity they are, but not in time (UDVS 63).

The first error of such an agent is volitional “impatience;” she wills the good only if she believes its victory can be secured by human agency in her time; she is not willing to wait for the future after her death, let alone for eternity (63). Among other problems, this insistence on success makes radicals liable to coerce others who stand in the way: they “will 

*[p. 143]* ^pg162

not understand the slowness of the good, that in its compassion it is longsuff ering, that in its love for the free, it will not use its power, that in its wise understanding of the weak, it shuns every deception” (62). This is a vital distinction: as Kant taught, willing the good in truth requires treating personal autonomy as inviolable. In the *Lord of the Rings*, Gandalf and Faramir are infinitely resigned and willing to take the slow road of persuasion; Saruman and Denethor are impatient for victory, and thus end up abandoning their original devotion to the good entirely. That does not mean that Gandalf and Faramir have no hope at all; faith can be combined with infi nite resignation. But if necessary, they are willing for Gon-dor to be destroyed and to lose the war rather than winning by violating the freedom of others—which of course is the temptation that Sauron’s Ring represents.<sup>20</sup>

The second error is “presumption” or pride in the archaic sense of insisting on being the hero in the eyes of the world. We might also call it volitional narcissism: “He does not will the good for the sake of \[external\] reward; he wills that the good shall be victorious; but he wills that it shall be victorious *through him*, that he shall be the *instrument*, he the *chosen one*” (61). The good is thus not willed in purity, but as the consummation of the agent’s self-realization. We might think here of Aristotle’s virtuous man who prefers to die for his friend rather than the reverse because he “thereby wins the greater good.”<sup>21</sup> When the two come apart, and the ethical ideal can only be realized if he bows out and lets another achieve it, his lack of wholeheartedness will become clear. His predicament is similar to one whose will to justice is corrupted into the satisfaction of revenge: *he* has to be the one to destroy the unjust.

Kierkegaard is surely right that this is “a powerful delusion that seems to come closest to the purity of heart that wills the good in truth” (UDVS 60); it has been exemplified by many initially well-meaning radicals throughout history. Perhaps Robert Mugabe was like this at the start, willing justice as he conceived it for Zimbabwe, but he was unwilling to share power, or let any other leader participate, even for the sake of his people’s good. Much like Saruman, he ended up caring about nothing but the shrill ideology that would give a thin patina of legitimacy to his endless tyranny and the ruin of his country. Contrast Lincoln, who issued the final Emancipation Proclamation expecting that it would cost him the next election, and then said he would never recant, “come what will.”<sup>22</sup> With much patience, like “a steady prevailing wind” (62), he pressed on in infinite resignation, without any certainty of victory, at all *or* through his own eff orts. His will became absolutely unified in infinite resignation, willing the good “in truth.”

At this point, it may help to summarize these forms of double-mindedness in an ordering that correlates them with the various types of aestheticism distinguished in Chapter 3. We have at least eight different forms to distinguish (leaving out the subforms of types Two and Three):

This list would also correlate well with the forms of anxiety and despair described in Kierkegaard’s other psychological works, though these add in forms that are defined specifically in terms of refusal of faith.

3\. 

### 3 Infinite Resignation, Faith, and 
Jest: Further Levels of Narrative Unity

We have seen that infinite resignation is a *narrative* process; it consists in a will absolutely committed to the good in the sense that it is willing to endure “the duration of time” (UDVS 62) without assurance of any earthly success. In his positive account following the typology of double-mindedness, Kierkegaard says that willing to do and to suffer “everything for the good” means “*to will, in the decision, to be and to remain with the good*” (79). The volitional state is ongoing, repeatedly renewed, rather than consisting in a single moment of decision; in other words, it is what I have called willing as striving, a “projective process.”<sup>23</sup> Infinite resignation is a kind of infi - nite “patience,” and volitional unity requires such patience. Call this thesis (I) regarding unity-3. The claim that wholeheartedness requires a pure will, which in turn requires infinite resignation, goes beyond the earlier narrative theses concerning autonomy and wholeheartedness by adding a particular and very demanding conception of the highest good that regulates our pursuit of other goods: “The good is not aristocratic; it asks for neither more nor less than everything” that we can do, however great or small (84). In other words, it involves a perfectionist standard that also rejects consequentialism, given that the probability of outward success plays at most a subsidiary role in deciding what we should do (87). What matters is the trying, the “eternal will,” not the “accomplishment” (89). The good that can be the object of our unified will has to be perfectionist or “an infinite ideal,” as Connell says, for it to present a challenge that always contrasts with any actualized state that we have attained.<sup>24</sup> Here Kierkegaard’s view connects with interesting arguments that strong narrative unity is incompatible with consequentialism, such as Williams’ “integrity” critique, though I will not pursue this here. Thesis (I) also takes us towards the connection between a wholehearted will and religious faith, because Kierkegaard thinks the only way to secure 

*[p. 146]* ^pg165

infinite resignation for the long-run is to trust in the “eternal victory” of the good “with the eyes of faith” (64).

We are now in a position to appreciate Lippitt’s insight that for Kierkegaard, complete or full earnestness includes a kind of “jest.” As he says, referring to the psuedonyms, “Both Quidam and Climacus stress the interdependent, potentially mutually supportive nature of the comic and pathos, jest and earnestness, and also the importance of their being kept in balance.”<sup>25</sup> Lippitt is right that I did not address this in my earlier account of earnest willing. But what kind of “jest” is relevant here? He suggests that one important role of humor is to lighten up our “wholehearted engagement” or goal-focus enough that we are open to being halted by values that are new to us, by “the dawning of an aspect” with ethical depth.<sup>26</sup> This is connected with his argument for objection (5) that “too ‘pre-planned’ a life” can miss much of importance: “our determination to follow our ‘life-plans,’ to pursue our ‘goals,’ threatens to lead to a kind of moral blindness, a lack of moral imagination.”<sup>27</sup> This is probably also connected to Lippitt’s thought that the unity-ideal in “Purity of Heart” is misdirected. But I do not think Kierkegaard would agree on either count.

Lippitt is certainly right that monomania, or just being too focused on our ‘agenda,’ is a distortion (or perhaps ‘vice of excess’) to which eff orts to cultivate strength of will are subject.<sup>28</sup> But as we saw, this problem is addressed by basing cares on putatively objective values, which requires that they be regulated by a higher-order care that they are well-grounded, appropriate to our circumstances, and coherent with each other—which involves a commitment to honesty about what our cares really are (see Chapter 3 §3). This higher-order care is *included* in willing “the good in truth” for Kierkegaard: we must be willing to see that some cares of ours are not ethically good; or that they are pursued in a wrong way; or that there is something or someone else that we should care about instead, or care about more than we have. Openness to novelty plays the same role towards the future that continued repentance of previous errors plays towards the past; both are *required* for wholeheartedness properly understood. When King Lear stands naked on the heath and recognizes that there are some important things of which he has “ta’en too little care,” his life appears broken, but it is actually moving closer to narrative unity-3 as a result.<sup>29</sup> Moreover, these dimensions of earnestness that transcend our actual present cares and open them to revision (or even radical rethinking in extreme cases) are essential to deep narrative continuity: they explain how *changes* in our particular higher-order volitions and cares (including ground projects), which appear to be disruptions in unity-2, are actually improved responses to the grounds for all caring—and are thus still faithful to the regulative goods that were loyally expressed, however imperfectly, *in and through* the prior now-amended cares. This deeper continuity-3 is certainly not vacuous, because it does not just define “any radical eruption in my life” as part of its development;<sup>30</sup> it includes only 

*[p. 147]* ^pg166

those that the agent can reasonably understand as *improvements* relative to the regulative highest good. As a result, there are still plenty of changes that can break unity-3, amounting to abandonment of the good, treachery, akrasia, or sheer distraction.

For example, the heroic aesthete who degenerates into weakness may tell herself that she is simply taking more time to experience what Cora Diamond calls the “adventure of life;”<sup>31</sup> the agent with an ethical cause who later compromises his principles for the sake of making the cause more successful, or (worse) for personal advancement, may think of his turn to expediency as the wisdom of experience. A man who cared for years about a loved one, but who then fears he has not experienced enough “variety” and so abandons her in a mid-life crisis, may tell himself that he has simply acquired a “quickened sense of life,” in George Mallory’s words. These phrases are all ambiguous and invite self-deception. On Kierkegaard’s view, it is precisely caring that has already attained unity-2 that makes people better able to appreciate new values; drifters and egoists are less likely to be struck by novelty in the deep way that leads to personal response through new commitments.

Likewise, earnestness sometimes requires radical changes in our cares, as we already saw with Cal in *Ordinary People*. He is not awakening to a new positive value, but he definitely experiences a gestalt-shift in value-perception. Similarly, consider the inspector in the film *V for Vendetta* who has been a loyal party member for years, and his ground project is to excel in law enforcement. But pursuit of this project leads him to revelations that force him to see the injustice and corruption of the government he serves. An aesthetic agent at this point would look for a way out, but the inspector cannot: he has to risk everything to switch his allegiance. But there is still narrative continuity in this: he remains loyal to the value of justice, only more clearly than before. These cases show how someone whose cares are regulated by a hyper-care for the good can thereby be opened to aspects of his situation that are very hard to accept. By contrast, as Annette Baier argued against Frankfurt, a fanatic is unwilling to change because he does not really will the ethical ideal above and through all else; he lacks Kierkegaard’s strong version of unity-3.<sup>32</sup> This special danger of strong purpose and determination can be tempered by the virtues of patience, humility, and critical rationality, whereas the more basic dangers of acedia and aesthetic dispersion can only be corrected by the devotions that give the agent unity-2 and make unity-3 possible. Thus we should not conceive errors that may prevent unity-2 from becoming unity-3 as reasons *not even to cultivate unity-2* through caring in the first place. It is simply false that Zenlike passivity, dispassion, and living episodically in the present will lead to keener openness to novelty and nuance that must be missed by agents with repeated daily devotion to positive cares. Kierkegaard’s analysis helps show that the best kind of narrative unity and the right kinds of openness to insight and revision go together in ethical wholeheartedness.

*[p. 148]* ^pg167

All that said, Lippitt is correct that a type of humor is essential to full earnestness for Kierkegaard. It lies in the realization that “one is nothing before God, that all personal eff ort is only a jest” (CUP 78). Climacus’s point, which is detailed in the section on suffering as the “Essential Expression” for existential pathos, is not that we should make no eff ort, but rather that even if we try our hardest to actualize the good in life, outward success never follows necessarily. There is always some gap between willing and the result that is filled only by luck; in that sense, we can do nothing without God. In a deeper sense, we may not even be able to *try* as we should without God. Embracing this is equivalent to “infinite resignation” in its widest sense.<sup>33</sup>

To clarify, “infinite resignation” in the narrower sense described in *Fear and Trembling* as the precondition of faith is a limiting case in the spectrum covered by the ethico-religious “jest”: it is the synthesis of willing an ethically good outcome wholeheartedly while accepting that it is pragmatically impossible for us, as we cannot see how our agency (on our own or through help from others that we could mobilize), can make it even marginally probable. At the other end of the continuum is the weak claim that we cannot *guarantee* any outcome that is distinct from the intention aiming at it, just because some factor we do not control can always intervene. Obviously it is *much* more likely that I will succeed in petting a cat on my lap than it was that Isaac would somehow survive to father a great nation despite being sacrificed by Abraham as ordered. Yet it is true of both these results that the agent cannot *ensure* them by intending them. One way to see this for every intended result is to realize that “the possibility of death is present at every moment” (CUP 82). It is not likely that I will fall dead before I pet the cat as I raise my hand, but it *is* physically possible. In wide infinite resignation, I accept that I cannot absolutely ensure even such small goods. We may think of this as a generalization of the special point accepted in narrow infinite resignation, which does not apply to every intended good result. When we accept the general point, we can appreciate that a tiny miracle is involved even when the cat purrs while being pet, although it is almost nothing next to the great miracles that are the specific objects of religious faith. This helps explain the example of the businessman who is a “knight of faith” incognito, who finds quiet joy in every detail, “everything that happens—a rat scurrying under a plank across the gutter, children playing.” Though his “incommensurability” with the simple aesthete is not apparent,

He drains the deep sadness of life in infinite resignation, he knows the blessedness of infinity, he has felt the pain of renouncing everything, the most precious thing in the world, and yet the finite tastes just as good to him as to one who never knew anything higher. . . . He resigned everything infinitely and then he grasped everything again by virtue of the absurd (FT 40).

*[p. 149]* ^pg168

The infinite resignation here is the widest kind: we might say that this man has learned how to look at every scene as if it might be his last sight. He appreciates just continuing into the next moment of time as a gift. His sadness or sorrow is that he can totally assure *nothing*; his faith is that he will receive something of value anyway.

Thus Silentio and Climacus are consistent on infinite resignation, and Kierkegaard agrees with them. “The person who wills the good in truth” will try as hard as he can, or suffer what he needs to, but “must in quiet patience leave everything up to the good itself, what reward he is to have, what he is to accomplish” (UDVS 97). I may have made every eff ort to convince the tax board not to cut the school budget, and I might be greatly disappointed if they do, but in the end, that is their business. In forming his intentions, the person with patience takes to heart that “what he accomplishes and what he does not accomplish are not his affair” (99). Of course an ethical agent cares about the outcome, but not to the point of thinking that only the outcome or our role in it matters. The self-willful person fails here: she has to have the victory; she does not accept the jest that is recognized in infinite resignation. Thus she lacks the earnestness of the “faithful servant” who keeps trying even though “in the end it seemed as if he had accomplished nothing for the good” (61). As Kierkegaard implies in “At a Graveside,” *all* earnest human works are necessarily incomplete in time (TDIO 80); however finished they may appear, death limits all our eff orts because death and events after it may change the effects of our striving.<sup>34</sup> This is the ethico-religious jest which recognizes with a “smile” that despite our utmost eff ort, “a human being is nothing at all and that one who works with all one’s might gains only the proper opportunity to wonder at God” (TDIO 83).

In sum, purity of heart requires infinite resignation, which in turn involves the thought of death as the limit to all human achievement in time. Thus the volitional conditions of narrative unity-3 bring us back to the special problem that death appears to pose for narrative unity. Does this mean that a free agent’s practical identity cannot in fact be “whole,” that the very conditions of wholeheartedness finally prevent the unity built up by ethical willing from being tied together in narrative completion? It now appears that our entire analysis up to this point might strengthen the death-objection to narrativist accounts of identity. Yet as we will see in the next chapter, Kierkegaard again has an answer.
