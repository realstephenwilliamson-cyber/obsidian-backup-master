## About

One of the principal Upaniṣads, belonging to the Taittirīya school of the Black (Kṛṣṇa) Yajurveda. It forms chapters 7–9 of the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka and moves from recitation and conduct to the knowledge of brahman.

- **Date:** Wikipedia's lead puts it about the 6th century BCE. It reports Olivelle (1998) as pre-Buddhist, possibly 6th–5th century BCE, and Phillips (2009) as the first half of the 1st millennium BCE. The IEP groups it with the first period of Upaniṣads, generally dated 700–500 BCE.
- **Structure:** three vallīs: Śikṣā (12 anuvākas), Ānanda or Brahmānanda (9) and Bhṛgu (10). Deussen thought the Śikṣā Vallī the earliest. The index to the Ānanda Vallī counts more sections than survive, which suggests losses.
- **Contents:** the Śikṣā Vallī covers phonetics, the syllable Om and a teacher's charge to graduating students, including the precepts to treat mother, father, teacher and guest as gods. The Ānanda Vallī calls brahman truth, knowledge and the infinite, and describes the self in five sheaths (kośas), from food to bliss. In the Bhṛgu Vallī, Varuṇa teaches his son Bhṛgu that brahman is that from which beings arise, by which they live and into which they return.
- **Canon:** a mukhya (principal) Upaniṣad, seventh in the Muktikā list of 108.
- **Commentaries and translations:** Śaṅkara (whom the IEP dates c. 700 CE) and Sāyaṇa; Wikipedia also names Madhva and Rangarāmānuja. European translations began in 1805. Wikipedia lists Swami Sharvananda's Ramakrishna Math edition, with Devanagari, transliteration and notes following Śaṅkara.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia cites it as one of the earliest accounts of education in ancient India, and reports Eliot Deutsch's view that the sheath theory is among the earliest known theories of human nature and knowledge.
