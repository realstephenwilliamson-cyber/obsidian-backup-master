## About

Thomas Aquinas's (1225–1274) systematic account of Catholic theology, written in Latin. Its prologue says it is meant to instruct beginners as well as the proficient. Aquinas died before finishing it.

- **Writing:** begun while Aquinas taught at the studium of Santa Sabina in Rome; he finished the First Part and circulated it in Italy before his second Paris regency (1269–72). Britannica dates the work to about 1265–1273; Wikipedia says he worked on it from after 1265 until the end of his life, reaching Question 90 of the Third Part (on penance).
- **Structure:** three parts, on God, on man, and on Christ (Britannica); the Second Part is itself split in two (I-II and II-II). By Wikipedia's per-part counts Aquinas's own text has 512 questions; with the Supplement and two appendices it gives 614 questions and 3,125 articles.
- **Form:** each article gives objections, a contrary authority (*sed contra*), Aquinas's answer (*respondeo*), and replies to the objections.
- **Supplement:** the rest of the Third Part was filled in after his death from his commentary on Peter Lombard's *Sentences*. Britannica calls it a 14th-century compilation; Wikipedia says it is not found in 13th- or 14th-century manuscripts. Neither names the compiler.
- **The English Dominican translation:** first issued in 1911 as "translated by Fathers of the English Dominican Province", with a revised edition in 1920–22 (Benziger). Wikipedia says it was entirely the work of Laurence Shapcote (1864–1947), who wished to stay anonymous. New Advent's online text is the second revised edition of 1920.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it a main source of Thomism, notes its influence on Dante's *Divine Comedy*, and says it remains a major reference in Catholic and mainline Protestant theological study. Britannica says Aquinas meant it to set out the sum of known learning, drawing on Aristotle, his Arab commentators and church teaching.
