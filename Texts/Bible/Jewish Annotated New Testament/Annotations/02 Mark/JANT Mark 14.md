### 14.1–2: Conspiracy to kill Jesus

*[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-1|Text]], p. 98* · ([[Matthew 26#^26-1|Mt 26.1–5]]; [[Luke 22#^22-1|Lk 22.1–2]]; [[John 11#^11-47|Jn 11.47–53]]). The three pilgrimage festivals—Passover, Shavuot (Weeks or Pentecost), and Sukkot (Booths)—brought huge crowds to Jerusalem and therefore heightened tension. *Passover* was especially explosive because of its association with the Exodus; at the festival the Roman governor moved troops to Jerusalem to discourage uprising. ^14-1

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-1|1]]:** *Passover . . . Unleavened Bread*, Mark’s phrasing suggests that he saw these as two separate festivals, as in [[Torah 31 - Emor#^23-5|Lev 23.5]],[[Torah 31 - Emor#^23-6|6–8]]. Although they arose from different starting points—Passover celebrates the release from bondage in Egypt; Unleavened Bread celebrates the spring ripening of ears of grain—they were combined into one festival with the sacrifice of the paschal lamb and the eating of unleavened bread during the seven days following. Mark here and in v 12 appears to count the days sunrise to sunrise, rather than the more typical Jewish practice of sundown to sundown (see Introduction). ^14-1-2

### 14.3–11: Anointing and prediction of betrayal

*[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-3|Text]], p. 99* · ([[Matthew 26#^26-6|Mt 26.6–16]]; [[Luke 22#^22-3|Lk 22.3–6]]; [[John 12#^12-1|Jn 12.1–8]]). A woman anoints Jesus: the action could be either that of anointing a king or of preparing a body for burial. Mark’s principle of irony would suggest both*.* A further irony is that although she is acting in a prophetic way, many of those present do not perceive the meaning of her action and respond very negatively. ^14-3

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-3|3]]:** *Alabaster jar*, a small, probably globular container of carved, translucent gypsum; its long neck would be broken so the contents could be poured out. *Nard*, the flower of spikenard, which grows in the Himalayan region. ^14-3-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-9|9]]:** The anointing will be retold *in remembrance of her*, but her name is not given. Perhaps the omission of her name is ironic: the unnamed “everywoman” understands him, while the named disciples do not. She may be compared to the unnamed centurion (15.39) who understands Jesus’ role as the divine son while the disciples again do not. She is further contrasted to the three named women who attempt to anoint Jesus’ body, but fail (16.1–8), just as the unnamed centurion is contrasted to the three named disciples who fail in Gethsemane (14.33). Women characters are also emphasized in the Jewish novellas of this period (Esther, Judith, Susanna, *Joseph and Aseneth*); they became central protagonists for the exploration of the religious concerns, as they also do in Greek novels. ^14-9

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-10|10–11]]:** Only in [[John 12#^12-4|Jn 12.4]]**–**6 are we told that Judas was the one who urged that the money be given to the poor. ^14-10

### 14.12–25: The Last Supper

*[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-12|Text]], p. 100* · ([[Matthew 26#^26-17|Mt 26.17–29]]; [[Luke 22#^22-7|Lk 22.7–20]]; [[1 Corinthians 11#^11-23|1 Cor 11.23–26]]). In Mark the Last Supper is the Passover meal (though not a Seder, which originated after the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE), while at [[John 19#^19-31|Jn 19.31]] Jesus is crucified on the day of preparation for Passover and the Last Supper is not a Passover meal. [[Torah 47 - Re'eh#^16-1|Deut 16.1–8]] established that the Passover lamb could only be eaten in Jerusalem. ^14-12

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-22|22–25]]:** Churches recite the words of the Last Supper as the Eucharist (Gk “giving thanks”); the ritual is also called “Communion,” “Holy Communion,” and “The Lord’s Supper.” Mark treats it as a full meal, as also in [[1 Corinthians 11#^11-20|1 Cor 11.20–32]]. A different account of the communal meal, found in the early Christian document *Did.* 9.1–10.15, is more similar to Jewish meals in terms of prayers (*m. Ber.* 7.1–5) and theology. Christians over the centuries have disagreed about whether the Christ is literally or symbolically present in the bread and wine, whether the wine is to be consumed by priests alone while the congregation consumes bread only, whether to use wine or (non-alcoholic) grape juice, whether to use a wafer or a loaf of bread, and who is permitted to participate. Denominations have also differed about the meaning of the practice: whether, for instance, it reenacts Jesus’ sacrifice of his life on the cross, or is only a memorial of that event. The language of consuming Jesus’ *body* and *blood* is perhaps deliberately shocking, since it suggests cannibalism; consumption of animal blood was also forbidden for Jews (e.g., [[Torah 29 - Achrei Mot#^17-10|Lev 17.10–11]]). ^14-22

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-22|22]]:** *Bread*, in keeping with Passover, this refers to unleavened bread (Heb “matsah”). ^14-22-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-24|24]]:** The *covenant* is ratified by Jesus’ blood just as Moses ratified the Sinai covenant with blood ([[Torah 18 - Mishpatim#^24-1|Ex 24.1–8]]; [[Zechariah 09#^9-11|Zech 9.11]]). Despite the use of “new covenant” at [[1 Corinthians 11#^11-25|1 Cor 11.25]] and [[Hebrews 08#^8-13|Heb 8.13]] (both alluding to [[Jeremiah 31#^31-31|Jer 31.31]]), Mark’s language (and likely Paul’s as well) suggests a concern with the present covenant, not a new or different covenant. Translator’s note *c* reflects the addition of “new” by later scribes. *For many*, see [[Isaiah 53#^53-11|Isa 53.11]] and “Jesus Dying as a Ransom.” p. 90. [[Matthew 26#^26-28|Matthew 26.28]] explicitly adds “for the forgiveness of sins” (cf. [[1 Corinthians 15#^15-3|1 Cor 15.3]]; [[Hebrews 09#^9-11|Heb 9.11–22]]; [[1 Peter 01#^1-18|1 Pet 1.18–19]]). ^14-24

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-25|25]]:** On the messiah’s future banquet, cf. [[Isaiah 25#^25-6|Isa 25.6]] and the DSS 1QM 2.11–22; cf. *1 En.* 10.18–19; *m. Avot* 3.16–17; *Num. Rab.* 13.2. ^14-25

### 14.26–31: Peter’s denial prophesied

*[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-26|Text]], p. 101* · ([[Matthew 26#^26-30|Mt 26.30–35]]; [[Luke 22#^22-39|Lk 22.39]]). ^14-26

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-26|26]]:** The rabbis grouped Ps 113–118 as the Hallel psalms, (*m. Pesah.* 5.7), and associated them with Passover. Although this is a later tradition, the early first-century Jewish writer Philo states that the Passover meal was accompanied by prayers and hymns (*Spec. Laws* 2.27.148). *Mount of Olives*, see 11.1n. ^14-26-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-27|27]]:** [[Zechariah 13#^13-7|Zech 13.7]]. God is the agent of Jesus’ suffering; see 14.36n. ^14-27

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-28|28]]:** *Go before*, see 16.7n. ^14-28

### 14.32–42: Prayer in Gethsemane

*[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-32|Text]], p. 101* · ([[Matthew 26#^26-36|Mt 26.36–46]]; [[Luke 22#^22-40|Lk 22.40–46]]). ^14-32

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-32|32]]:** *Gethsemane*, means “oil press”; it is located on the Mount of Olives ([[Luke 22#^22-39|Lk 22.39]]). ^14-32-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-33|33]]:** *Peter and James and John*, see 9.2–13. ^14-33

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-36|36]]:** *Abba*, Aramaic for father (not Heb for “daddy,” as some scholars formerly argued). Jesus’ followers, perhaps by his lead, emphasized their relationship to God as father (3.31–35n.; 10.29–30n.; 11.25; [[Matthew 06#^6-9|Mt 6.9]]; [[Luke 11#^11-2|Lk 11.2]]; [[Romans 08#^8-15|Rom 8.15–17]]; [[Galatians 04#^4-6|Gal 4.6–7]]). The image was infrequent, but not unknown, in the Hebrew Bible ([[Isaiah 63#^63-16|Isa 63.16]]; [[Isaiah 64#^64-7|64.8]]; [[Jeremiah 03#^3-4|Jer 3.4]],[[Jeremiah 03#^3-19|19]]; [[Psalms 068#^68-6|Ps 68.5]]; [[Psalms 089#^89-27|89.26]]; 103.13). “Father” by itself in Jewish tradition is more likely to be associated with Abraham or another patriarch than with God (*m. Avot* 3.12; cf. [[John 04#^4-12|Jn 4.12]]), while “king” is often a title for God. “Father” is sometimes used in combination, however, e.g., the later Jewish prayer “Avinu Malkeinu,” “our father, our king.” *Remove this cup,* see [[Isaiah 51#^51-12|Isa 51.12]],[[Isaiah 51#^51-17|17]], where God removes the cup of wrath from the people; see also 10.38n. *Not what I want*, implies that Jesus resists God’s plan, much as Peter had resisted in 8.32; Matthew and Luke lack this line. ^14-36

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-37|37–41]]:** In 13.37 Jesus told the disciples to *keep awake*, yet ironically three times he finds them sleeping. The three naps also anticipate Peter’s three denials. ^14-37

### 14.43–65: Jesus arrested and tried before the Sanhedrin

*[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-43|Text]], p. 101* · ([[Matthew 26#^26-47|Mt 26.47–68]]; [[Luke 22#^22-47|Lk 22.47–71]]; [[John 18#^18-2|Jn 18.2–11]]). ^14-43

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-44|44–45]]:** The *kiss* is an expected greeting ([[Torah 11 - Vayigash#^45-15|Gen 45.15]]; [[Luke 07#^7-45|Lk 7.45]];), though ironic here. It was perhaps necessary as an identification of Jesus in the dark. ^14-44

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-47|47–52]]:** These provocative symbolic acts have not been convincingly explained. The attack on the high priest’s slave implies that Jesus’ followers were armed. *Young man . . . wearing nothing but a linen cloth . . . ran off naked*, perhaps a remnant from an earlier account. New members were baptized nude, but it is not likely that such an act would be carried out at night under these conditions; still, the scene conceivably suggests an interrupted baptism and the panicked flight of the follower. ^14-47

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-53|53]]:** *Chief priests, the elders, and the scribes* constitute the Sanhedrin (see “The Sanhedrin,” p. 602), or Jerusalem city council. It had authority over Jewish life in Judea, but Romans reserved control over some legal aspects, especially capital punishment. For this reason, because this trial is placed on Passover when such activities would be strictly forbidden by Jewish law (*m. Pesah.* 4.1,5–6 makes clear that one may work at most until noon on the day on which Passover begins at sundown), because it is highly unlikely the Sanhedrin would convene on the first night of Passover, and because the Sanhedrin trial is absent in John’s Gospel, the scene’s historicity is doubted by most scholars. ^14-53

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-55|55–59]]:** Biblical law forbade suborning perjury ([[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-13|Ex 20.16]]) and convicting based on conflicting evidence ([[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^19-15|Deut 19.15]]), so the proceedings are presented as violations of Jewish criminal procedure. Although the *testimony* is depicted as *false*, according to Mark Jesus had predicted the destruction of the Temple (11.15; 13.2). ^14-55

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-61|61–64]]:** The titles *Messiah* and *Son of the Blessed One* are not *blasphemy*, since a Jewish king could be both, but they are politically dangerous. According to Mark, the high priest takes Jesus’ quotations (v. 62) of [[Psalms 110#^110-1|Ps 110.1]] and [[Daniel 07#^7-13|Dan 7.13]], and his identification of himself with the judging Son of Man, as blasphemy from the Sanhedrin’s point of view; see 2.6–7n. His verdict is no more legitimate than the trial. ^14-61

**[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-61|61]]:** Jesus is *silent* like the suffering servant at [[Isaiah 53#^53-7|Isa 53.7]]. *Blessed One*, the chief priest uses a circumlocution for God, which indicates a reticence on his part to pronounce the divine name. It is similar to the rabbinic “Holy One, Blessed be He” (Heb “Ha-Qadosh baruch hu”; cf. Philo, *Dreams* 2.18.130). ^14-61-2

### 14.66–72: Peter’s denial

*[[NRSVue Mark 14#^14-66|Text]], p. 102* · ([[Matthew 26#^26-69|Mt 26.69–75]]; [[Luke 22#^22-56|Lk 22.56–62]]; [[John 18#^18-17|Jn 18.17]],[[John 18#^18-25|25–27]]). By beginning the scene with Peter in v 54 and completing it here, Mark ironically conveys that Peter’s attempt at “self-acquittal” occurs simultaneously with Jesus’ conviction. Just as the witnesses against Jesus offered false testi- *crowed* mony, so does Peter concerning himself. Peter’s culminating solemn denial of Jesus is comparable to the high priest’s pronouncement of Jesus’ guilt. ^14-66
