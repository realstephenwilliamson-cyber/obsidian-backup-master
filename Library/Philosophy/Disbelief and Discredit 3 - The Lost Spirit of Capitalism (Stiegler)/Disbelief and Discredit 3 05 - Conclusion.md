## Conclusion

The fire of Prometheus is not that of the gods (Zeus’s thunderbolt, the lightning of Athena). . . . It is a perishable fire: engendered, starved, precarious, like all mortal creatures; . . . the tekhnai that men have at their disposal are not less equivocal than the Titan who provided them with these. . . . Not only because ‘the ardor of a ceaseless fire’ harbors a power that escapes human control but, more precisely, because this force contains something mysterious, a supernatural quality ... that adds a new dimension to the features of animal wildness and the assets of human culture.

\[T\]he question of writing opens as a question of morality. It is truly morality that is at stake, both in the sense of the opposition between good and evil, or good and bad, and in the sense of mores, public morals and social conventions. It is a question of knowing what is done and what is not done. This moral disquiet is in no way to be distinguished from questions of truth. . . . But within a debate rendered very real by the political development of the city, the propagation of writing and the activity of the sophists and speechwriters, the primary accent is naturally placed upon political and social proprieties. . . . 

\[I\]n pitying the orphan, one also makes an accusation against him, along with writing, for claiming to do away with the father, for achieving emancipation with complacent self-sufficiency. From the position of the holder of the scepter, the desire of writing is indicated, designated, and denounced as a desire for orphanhood

Intoxications, prohibitions, cares The fire of Prometheus - which gleams in the allegorical cave, which glows from screens (whether movie screens, or cathode-ray tubes, or ‘plasmas’), which enflames Daewoo’s microwave oven plant in Lorraine, which destroys cars and sets fire to schools in the suburbs, the image of which is repeated like a mimetic wildfire on the televisions watched by almost 98 per cent of the French public — this fire was stolen from the Olympian gods, stolen by a god, by a Titan. As such, it is no longer the fire of the gods: it has been redirected, diverted. 

And it is through such a diversion that Pandora, too, appears, the figure of desire inflamed, fashioned by Hephaestus and Athena, adorned with jewels and wedded to Epimetheus. As such, the fire of Zeus, become Promethean, is nothing other than an artefact, the energy of the pharmakon, that is, of tekhnai in general, and, occasionally, the energy of despair. But also and above all hope: this desire that is all hope, and this hope that is all desire, itself nevertheless composed of ‘instincts’, of drives from which hope would therefore be, in both senses of the word, forged \[fait et contrefait\]. It is this ambiguity in the meaning of the fire that is desire that, through this passage that is the artefact, turns it into the play of elpis (expectation, as both hope and fear) and anelpiston (the unexpected). But this also constitutes it as the pivot of elpis and anelpiston, and their escapement:\* the melancholic mechanism that this ambiguity of fire already unleashed in the liver of Prometheus, which discharges its black bile, melas khole. 

Fire, as redirected energy, becomes, through this diversion, desire, and is therefore the energy of the pharmakon. Pharmakon is the word by which, in Phaedrus, Plato designates writing insofar 

*[p. 85]* ^pg93

as it is an artifice, an aid to memory, a memory-aid, but an artifice that at the same time causes a /oss of memory, as we all experience today with mobile telephones, through which phone numbers are both recorded and forgotten. The pharmakon thus remedies and overcomes the finitude of our memories, but at the same time increases the deficiency of our memory, and in this sense it is also a drug. This concept of the pharmakon was extended by Derrida to the ‘supplement’, from which he created the logic of the supplement, but the latter is, I believe, in even more general terms a techno-logic. This pharmakon, however, both remedy and poison, but also poison and remedy, is the support of every form of addiction — whether of the fetish, a fetish from which the perversion can no longer be detached, or of memory, which becomes habituated to this hypomnesis that is writing and hence becomes dependent, as this auxiliary memory becomes the principal memory. Yet this is what also provides the possibility of philosophy, the Book, and the West itself as the hypomnesic stage of that spirit that is today lost. Or, yet again, and more recently, it is the cigarette, which Edward Bernays claimed is a ‘phallic symbol’. 

When anti-smoking laws were implemented in Italy, the number of smokers apparently declined by half a million. This is a significant reduction. And it is a great outcome. Likewise, when automatic speed cameras were introduced in France, issuing automated penalties, it led to a significant reduction in speed on French roads and freeways, and hence reduced the number of deaths resulting from the addictive and para-suicidal relationship between the car and excessive speed. Thirty-eight years after 1968, therefore, control returns in the form of prohibitions, bans on smoking or speeding that are penalized through laws and decrees and via the machines of a super-ego that has in the meantime become automatic. An epoch seems to have come to a close, and it has opened onto a spiritual poverty, in the name of which we see a return of all the forms of control that existed prior to 1968: as prohibition. 

Now, this misery and poverty is the result of a generalization and mutation of addiction, and it engenders this new monster that is an uncontrollable society of disaffected individuals, if not of unearthly beasts.\* The prevailing super-egoistic ‘hygienism” and archaism — causing so much suffering for both youth and their 

*[p. 86]* ^pg94

parents, who nevertheless demand it, helpless as they are — are merely symptoms of the regression to the drives and the repression that is its miserable response. And the latter is ultimately just as impotent as the parents are helpless - confronted with desperate logics and energies that are all constantly on the verge of rising up in the form of negative sublimations that are uncontrollable, since utterly irrational, having nothing to lose and therefore nothing to fear - as they respond to this Antigone complex that structures youth as transgressive power, that is, as future. 

The great addiction, making all others possible, is spirit. It is through industrial populism, as the industrial exploitation of libidinal energy — the latter finding itself reduced to a mechanism purely of the drives, in the absence of a libidinal ecology of the spirit — that addictive forms of technologies of the spirit suddenly become extremely toxic. Addictions, supporting and inducing all manner of pharmaka ~ fetishes, writings, technics in general as supplements in general - form a logic of the supplement. And this logic is transformed as the history of the supplement unfolds, at the same time transforming those forces that shape libidinal economy. But the supports of the play of mutual dependencies by which human beings are bound, too, are forms of addiction, beginning with love — which is the highest form of addiction — or with language (and it is for this reason that Derrida emphasized that speech is always already writing, that is, a pharmakon, contrary to the words that Plato, in Phaedrus, placed in the mouth of Socrates). In other words, addictions are the concrete realization, in the most diverse and the most ordinary, everyday forms, but also, frequently, in extra-ordinary forms, of the process of adoption in which consists the process of psychic, collective and technical individuation. 

In general organology, therefore, pharmaka form and support systems and solidarities for which the energy is desire, that is, affect, and this, as social energy, is what Aristotle called philia. The epoch of industrial populism, however, systematically and mechanically takes advantage of these pharmaco-toxic supplementary logics, and does so on the basis of a potential for addiction that is general and original (in default of origin). This ground of ‘addictivity’ results from general organology as the general process of adoption, and from the ‘viscosity’ of libidinal energy, 

*[p. 87]* ^pg95

a phrase by which Freud indicates the adhesive tendency of libido, through which it attaches to its objects (and it is in this, precisely, that the structure of fantasy as addiction lies). This epoch of industrial populism, then, is the indispensable complement of consumer capitalism, for which libidinal energy is the resource and the mechanism of escapement. Libidinal energy is also, however, what is destroyed by consumer capitalism, through being captured and through the form of diversion specific to this epoch, a form that has thus changed its ‘nature’. In this epoch, these addictive forms become toxic ‘properly speaking’, if we can put it that way: they poison the future of all humanity, through every kind of congestion and saturation, be it traffic congestion, cognitive or affective saturation, and so on. And this bears a strange resemblance to what Freud observed in the oversaturated milieu of protists, as well as realizing concretely what he described as the tendency of the super-ego to become toxic in its moral form. Marcuse extended but also inverted this analysis, by ascribing this to the super-ego in its automatized form, yet doing so without analysing the logic of the pharmakon that is the technical condition of the diversion of energy that is desire. 

2 The struggle for the life of the spirit If it is true that the life of the spirit is essentially a logic of the supplement, then spirit is an addiction and equally a form of patho-logy: a passion (and this is what is at stake in what Aristotle called nots pathetikon, which must here be extended to what I call technesis, to noesis insofar as it is ‘being-only-intermittently’).’ But it is, equally, the remedy for this condition, for this fate that, in ancient Greek thought, endures as the tragic condition of mortals. Mortals: those who hold in their hands the fire of Zeus, fire that has become Promethean, that is, artificial, and the energy generated by its diversion. Spirit is therefore also: (1) what haunts this condition, as that which incessantly returns, in a thousand ways, but firstly as fantasy, as attachment, as various viscosities, and diverse sublimable objects, through all kinds of supplements, that is, hypomnemata; and (2) what constitutes the technical system of cares of this haunting and the patho-logy that it is and that forms a socio-logy, being both the conjunction and the 

*[p. 88]* ^pg96

disjunction that is the ‘and’ of psychic and collective individuation (and this is what is at stake in what Aristotle called nots poietikon, such that noesis would be transindividuation in logos as the milieu of elementary supplementarity). 

Such a system of care is a sociotherapy, and when such a system takes on a defined form it is called a politics. A politics is itself, and before any other determination, a politics of spirit, that is, of addiction. Industrial populism has given the whole world the feeling of heading towards catastrophe, and it has been the ruination of psychic and collective individuals, which is also to say, of economics and politics, which can lead only to war. In this context, another politics of spirit must be conceived, a new sociotherapy — and it must be conceived on the basis of the specificities of the new forms of pharmaka and hypomnemata, that is, new forms of remedies as well as poisons. All politics may be sociotherapy, but the latter does not mean ‘hygienism’, because it does not oppose health to disease. Nor does it oppose the pharmakon — which is also to say, the hypomnematon, as what produces the death of the spirit — to the life of this spirit, to its vita activa. As always in the domain of this noetic soul — both embodied (as corporeality, that is, as un-conscious) and disembodied (as technesis) — that is the life of the spirit, that is, desire, the question is the passage of desire as passage of the potential to act, and for desire to here become a will. 

The challenge is thus the possibility of ‘will’ in a primordially addictive situation — which is also to say, in a situation of unconsciousness, given that the logic of the supplement in which pharmaka, hypomnemata and other fetishes consist is that of the unconscious. When addicts reach a stage where the toxin produces more suffering than relief, and where a system of dependence has developed from which there is no way out, they increase their dose, which in turn leads to a decrease in its effect. As a result, the addict both: (1) wants to detox, having identified and experienced the consequences of addiction as such; and (2) cannot and therefore does not want to stop consuming the poison yet. How then can the addict, who suffers, that is, who could potentially reindividuate themselves, and who wants to detox, that is, to stop disindividuating, find the courage to take action \[passer a Vacte\]?

Assuredly, opinion is nothing but a semblance, a caricature of essential relation, if only because it is a system organized on the basis of utilizable means, instruments of the press and pressure, the broadcast media and centers of propaganda that transform into an active power the passivity that is its essence, into a power of affirmation its neutrality, into a power of decision the sense of impotence and indecision that is opinion’s relation to itself. Opinion does not judge or opine. Radically unavailable because foreign to any position, it is all the more at one’s disposal. This justifies every criticism. Nevertheless, its panic movement escapes those critics who stress precisely opinion’s seductive and tranquilizing alienation, for its movement constantly dissipates this power by which everything is alienated into a nullity or an inalienable indetermination. He who believes that he has rumor at his disposal rapidly loses himself in it.\* 

Opinion is like fire, like that fire that must be maintained in the hearth, and at the same time monitored, since it can either heat the house or burn it down, and, beyond the house, the city itself (such are the stakes of the relations between Hermes and Hestia, goddess of the hearth’): we must take care of it. 

Now, this care is a combat within and with that which makes opinion ‘a system organized on the basis of utilizable means, instruments of the press and pressure, the broadcast media and centers of propaganda’, and so on - that is, a system for diverting the energy that is this doxa, always ready to become inflamed. Politics is this combat, and in order to lead this combat, with the appearance of new forms of hypomnesic pharmaka, we must find new weapons (and forge the arsenal of what I have called a new critique): it is a matter of polemology, of struggling for the life of the spirit.!° This will be possible, however, only if a new system of transductive relations between pharmaka and individuals is put in place, in the sense according to which a transductive relation

Television, cinema, video games, and now media players, 3G phones, personal assistants and other communications devices, all play and broadcast industrial temporal objects that are no more or less toxic than the hypomneses of speech that brought about the transition from mythos to logos, that is, from the tribe to the city. This transition opened up rational forms of knowledge and philosophy, yet also engendered those who, sometimes shameless, sometimes enemies of the good, if not of the beautiful, are the sophists, and of which Monsieur Le Lay and his ilk would be the distant heirs, and the final degeneration.’ No more or less than -writing, then, industrial temporal objects are pharmaka. Alongside this, however, an economy based on diversion, and therefore on perversion, also requires ecological measures, measures intended to prevent the loss of attention generated by that which also forms attention, reforming and trans-forming by elevating (by educating): this prevention and the attention in which it consists constitutes, precisely, this ecology that is all sublimation. 

Diké and aidos, as justice and shame (that is, ethics), are — in the nascent Greek city, and in discovering with Hermes the powers of logography, of the hypomnematon — such an ecology of spirit, conceived as the condition of all politics. And diké and aidos are also, as conditions of a diversion of libidinal energy not destructive of this energy, the super-egoistic dimensions of libido supported by its sublime forms, and as philia. In this diversion constitutive of libido, the place of the pharmakon, which is ignored by Marcuse, means that such an ecology is possible only on the condition of specifying the pharmacological characteristics of the supplement in question (that is, the organological stage of this technesis that is noesis), something that, in the case of the newest forms of hypomnemata, \\argely remains to be done. This is what public powers do not do today, leaving distraught parents to abandon their children in front of the television, dis-identified and 

*[p. 91]* ^pg99

dis-individuated, if not orphans. Such a state of affairs amounts to a situation of failing to help people in danger, a situation for which the public and private powers are responsible, and from which the children of Emmanuel and Patricia Cartier suffered appalling consequences.'’ If these infanticidal parents must be judged, it can only be with this context in mind. And it is to go more deeply into these questions of public policy that I will devote The Aristocracy to Come. 

Obviously we cannot go down the road of conducting, in the domain of the spirit, a politics of industrial detoxification comparable to what inspired anti-smoking ‘hygienism’ and campaigns against speeding. But we should still remember here that smoking was a policy of industrial poisoning in which Freud’s nephew, Edward Bernays, was a very active participant, on behalf of Philip Morris. This historical case of poisoning is now recognized as having been very costly for society, just as it is recognized that such a policy is no longer viable, something equally true for speeding, which has a direct relation with the law and the police, and which, as the object of a cult in the society of the auto-mobile, also became, as such, para-suicidal. The fact remains that the automatic or regressive super-ego that manifests itself through these prohibitive measures occurs when authority is so lacking that it is replaced with authoritarianism and police measures, which further intensifies this deficiency: the law, when it is imposed, or compulsively endured, when it is not desired as the idea of what is just, ceases to be the law, becoming merely a sterile order, that is, without future, and thus a factor in the serious disorders to come. 

The challenge is authority. And this is also to say: public power, confronted with the impotence of the rational that the industrial technical system has become. The industrialization of spirit was made possible by the appearance of devices, and in the first place by the mechanical turn in perception. The industrialization of spirit is therefore both comparable to smoking or speeding, in that it forms new types of addiction that are also generators of risk behaviour, and different from them, in that it cannot be contained within an apparatus of prohibition, nor monitored by a police force, without destroying spirit, which is always, and in all forms, the freedom of the spirit. This being the case, it is indeed a 

*[p. 92]* ^pg100

question of pursuing a politics of the sociopathology in which spirit consists in the epoch of its industrialization, and of doing so by taking action to struggle against the decline of its value. This requires new kinds of care, that is, a new type of otium. This new type of otium would constitute, in this passage from technics of the spirit to technologies of the spirit, hypomnesic practices of the industrial pharmakon that struggle against the fact that tendencies have a tendency to decompose, a tendency that plays itself out as a moment of grammatization in which the diversion of energy tends to destroy it. 

If it is true that the heart of capitalism is its libidinal economy, and that this is leading to the destruction of sublimation and the super-ego, then we must now enable this economy to move to a stage of libidinal ecology. The danger associated with any sociopathological discourse is obviously that it will maintain the illusion that there is such a thing as social health, and that this is what must be achieved: that it exists. What must on the contrary be posited as a first principle of such a sociopathology is that this kind of health does not exist, that society is always diseased, that there is no revolutionary Great Day, no horizon of redemption, whether hygienist or instinctual, but that, nevertheless, health consists, and that there would be no way to give up on it, as horizon of consistences, without giving up on everything. 

A sociopathology posits that the social is pathological, that is, perverse, diverted, constantly adopting new prostheses, new artefacts - new medications, drugs, artifices and simulacra. A sociopathology also posits, however, that, in and for this novelty, there is a constant maintenance of its metastability, which is neither equilibrium nor disequilibrium but the »ovement of individuation moved by its consistences, but where this movement, which is essentially the mobility of motives and as such e-motion, is produced, precisely, in default of a non-sociopathological situation, by the fact that there is always disequilibrium, always eris, and that it must be \[qu'il le faut\]:'\* 

At their origin Eris, or Discord, rages, engraving on the golden apple the fateful inscription: ‘To the most beautiful.’ . . . It is at the price of a wound required by the adversary powers who control man that living love triumphs.

I was 16 years old in 1968. I had met some Situationist friends in 1967, while with my older brother Dominique and our friends at the time, Alain Bideau and Saada N’Diaye, who played a large part in forming the story of my youth — as did my brother, who along with them introduced me to music, and whose example gave me the taste for reading. Our friendship revolved around the literature and ideas coming out of surrealism. I remember very clearly how stifling were the political atmosphere and moral discourse at that time, especially in Sarcelles, where we all lived. The political situation was organized around the legacy of Libération, but also all the very unhealthy consequences of the Algiers putsch and the terrible Algerian war, through which the SFIO (French Section of the Workers’ International), beginning with Francois Mitterrand, had been corrupted and tarnished forever, leaving me very distrustful of French social democracy; at the same time, tensions on the fle Seguin and its ‘workers’ fortress’ were, in 1967, extreme, and the parliamentary elections had left Charles de Gaulle in a delicate position. The events of 1968 left me perplexed, both in terms of the outcomes and in terms of its slogans, and a year later I joined the Communist Party \[PCF\] and supported the candidacy of Jacques Duclos.
