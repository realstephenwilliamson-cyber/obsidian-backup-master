Forgetting to Forget: The Curation of Memory in the Psycho-analytic Self

*By being the curator of our images, we care for our souls.*

--Thomas Moore

Throughout our reading of Charles Taylor’s *Sources of the Self* we have been investigating the origins and history of a notion not only familiar to all sentient beings, but indeed the very criteria for such categorization; the notion of “self.” This paper will be a survey of the phenomenology of that selfhood lead by Paul Ricoeur’s *Memory, History, and Forgetting.* We will start with Locke’s theory of identity, run through psychoanalysis and touch briefly on Husserl before culminating in the establishment of a new paradigm of selfhood, that of the curator.

The greatest of the Delphic maxims entreats us to know ourselves and yet we have seen in the historical analysis offered in *Sources of the Self* that the subject of such an inquiry has changed immensely throughout the ages. Within the Western intellectual tradition, however, there is a general consensus, that the modern notion of selfhood was inaugurated by the English philosopher John Locke. In his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding,* Locke famously states:

> "consciousness always accompanies thinking, and it is that which makes every one to be what he calls self, and thereby distinguishes himself from all other thinking things, in this alone consists personal identity, i.e. the sameness of a rational being: and as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person; it is the same self now it was then; and it is by the same self with this present one that now reflects on it, that that action was done."[^1]

This shows us that consciousness, particularly self-consciousness and the memories there-by entailed, is essential in Locke's formulation of selfhood and by extension identity.

What makes Locke’s theory revolutionary is that it essentially rejects two positions held since antiquity: (1) that the Man and the Person are equivalent (meaning that, for the first time continuity of body does not necessitate self-identity) and (2) that some immaterial substance, such as the soul, defines the “essence of personal identity,” allowing two bodies with the same transmigrated soul to be the same person.[^2] In position (2) he is not setting out to deny the existence of an immaterial substance, or soul, (for all we know, one could exist) but to instead affirm that if the adoption of a new consciousness through transmigration of the soul would detach the subject of the present and the one in the previous memory, then two people (or maybe two persons is the correct language here) would result: the person of the previous consciousness and the person of the newfound consciousness.

Several puzzles such as these arise from this substance and consciousness dichotomy, that is, theories in which it is the continuity of consciousness that preserves personal identity such as: Can an immaterial, thinking, substance be separated from its consciousness of the past?

If a person loses all memory of his past is he still the same person? If not, does he regain his personal identity once he recovers his memory? All of these issues are illustrated in the example of the cobbler and the prince[^3] and Locke thinks he addresses them once again in the next section:

Consciousness alone unites actions into the same person. But though the same immaterial substance or soul does not alone, wherever it be, and in whatsoever state, make the same man; yet it is plain, consciousness, as far as ever it can be extended- should it be to ages past- unites existences and actions very remote in time into the same person, as well as it does the existences and actions of the immediately preceding moment: so that whatever has the consciousness of present and past actions, is the same person to whom they both belong.[^4] [^5]

The answers Locke gives all rely on the connection of personal identity to consciousness, a connection established by the necessity of memory, or the setting aside of past actions in order to confirm the continuity of identity between the past self and the present self.

In spite of it being a revolutionary approach to selfhood there is one very serious shortcoming inherent in this formulation for both collective and individual memory. As Ricoeur articulates in regards to collective memory:

The heart of the problem is the motivation of memory in the service of the quest, the appeal, the demand for identity. In what follows from this we recognize some disturbing symptoms; too much memory, in a certain region of the world, hence an abuse of memory; not enough memory elsewhere, hence an abuse of forgetting.[^6]

But perhaps the problem is more easily understood as it is manifested in individual rather than collective memory. Essentially the issue is that if all one’s memories are continuously held in one’s mind, as Locke has just maintained, how do we account for the fact that at any given moment there are countless memories not (to use Locke’s phrase) “present to consciousness?”

The solution in the case of collective memory is exponentially more complicated. In order to address the issue Ricoeur draws on the Husserl’s *Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* and *Fifth Cartesian Meditation* emphasizing the concept of “higher inter-subjective communities.”[^7] While I find the discussion fascinating I believe that it is beyond the scope of this paper and perhaps my understanding.\]

Enter Freud

In Freud’s theory of the unconscious we find a solution to Locke’s problem albeit a seemingly nonsensical one to many in his own day. Over time, however, what was first seen as “conceptual impossibility” (unconscious thoughts) began to be accepted if, for no other reason, than that the theory of unconsciousness provided an explanation for pathologies that had heretofore been unexplained. Similar to the Copernican revolution, by postulating that underlying psychological forces in a person’s unconscious mind serve to determine a person’s behavior, Freud had demoted human agency once again.

But what bearing does this have on our phenomenology of the self? Well for one it shows us that at least some of our knowledge of the self cannot be obtained by the simple act of “looking within” or “introspection” in the sense that Locke used the terms. In fact it is this clouding of human agency, the fact that the contents of the unconscious are able to expose consciousness to “reservoirs of meaning” outside of itself, that appears to have caused Ricoeur, in his earlier writings, to coin the term “masters of suspicion” in reference to Freud. In order to garner the full benefit of the understanding of selfhood inaugurated by Freud, we must return to the role of memory, specifically the way in which the memory functions in Freud’s system.

In Freud’s two essays spotlighted by Ricoeur, *Remembering, Repeating and Working Through* and *Mourning and Melancholia* we utilize a method of investigation common in the psychoanalytic tradition, that is, to learn about the “normal” by studying the “abnormal,” in this case blocked memory as opposed to natural memory. According to Freud when memories are repressed (blocked) a resistance is formed and this resistance becomes manifest “not as a memory but as an action; he repeats it, without, of course, knowing that he repeats it.”[^8] This “compulsion to repeat” is unhealthy on the pathological-therapeutic level and, as the first essay’s title implies, the only way to eliminate the repression is by working through the memories.

In order to illustrate this process more clearly, and this is especially important in our phenomenology of selfhood, Ricoeur compares the “work of mourning” to the “work of remembering” in light of the term Freud uses for “working through” (*Durcharbeiten.*) Ricoeur points out that:

The important word here is ‘work’—or rather ‘working’—which underscores not only the dynamic character of the entire process, but the collaboration of the analysand in this work. It is in relation to this notion of work, stated in the form of a verb, that it becomes possible to speak of memory itself, freed in this way, as a work—the “work of remembering” (*Erinnerungsarbeit*)[^9]

Therefore, this “work of remembering,” as opposed to the abuse of forgetting, is an essential facet of Freud’s concept of self-identity, a function of the Ego in the repression of and the “working through” the contents of the Id.

But, in my opinion, Freud’s model offers a picture of the self that is ingenious but also incomplete. While I agree that the vast majority of the mind’s workings are not only unconscious but also impossible to make conscious, I do not believe that we are merely adrift on the seas of instinct. I believe that there are two oars available to us so to speak, individuation and volition, each developed to a greater or lesser degree by our experiences and, to some extent, our genetics.

As these facets have little to do with the role of memory, I mention them here only briefly but this is not to make lite of their importance. The first of these faculties, what the later psychoanalyst Carl Jung identified as individuation, is seen by many to be the proverbial missing link in Freud’s system. The second capacity, what I call volition or will, was elaborated by the founder of the psycho-synthesis tradition Roberto Assagioli. Although he is lesser known it is my belief that psycho-synthesis is the culmination of the Freudian systems. Finally it is my contention that these three facets—Freud’s structure of consciousness, Jung’s individuation, and Assagioli’s volition—combine to curate the “work of memory” and it is this very process that is the phenomenon that we call self
