## Chapter 8

STIEGLER CONTRA ROBINSON: 

ON THE HYPER-SOLICITATION OF YOUTH 

This chapter examines the affective disorders plaguing many young people and the problem of attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in particular. It aims to define the limits of the critique by renowned British educationalist Sir Ken Robinson in his philosophy of ‘creativity’. It will do this through a consideration of the ideas of French philosopher Bernard Stiegler, especially the notions of ‘industrial temporal objects’ and stupidity (bêtise). It makes the case for adopting elements of the seemingly distinct research paradigms as a prolegomenon to forging a social critique of capitalist-dominated, market-led educational institutions. The former, it will be seen, identifies some of the problems facing teachers in terms of the use and application of technology, the false divide between arts and the humanities, but falls short of explaining the root of the structural and psychic malaise in neoliberal regimes \[read: Integrated World Capitalism in the parlance of Guattari\] regarding classroom break-down in general. The latter, despite the apocalyptic tone of some his pronouncements, provides an update and radicalization of Deleuze’s societies of control thesis (Bradley, 2012) in terms of what Stiegler designates ‘uncontrollable societies’. Control is treated in less sinister terms by Robinson. In addressing the problem of attention and addiction to gadgets and technology in general, and the widespread ‘symbolic misery’ in what is referred to as the ‘hyper-industrial epoch’, Stiegler, it will be seen, presents a critique of technology that is all the more pressing in an age in which the loss of expectation in the lives of young people can lead to a corresponding fall off or destruction in ‘deep attention’. I want to test the hyperbole of Stiegler’s assertion that young people today suffer from a ‘colossal’ attention deficit disorder of unprecedented scale and magnitude. 

Below is an analysis of two thinkers who share the same premise – namely that something is ‘fundamentally’ wrong with educational 

*[p. 151]* ^pg166

institutions, or more concretely, in terms of learning and creativity and yet despite both sharing similar concerns – that is to say, that youth are assaulted by media and technologies to an unheard of degree – have perspectives which appear so very different and indeed antagonistically opposed from one another and which thus produce correspondingly diverse conclusions. Overall, the examination of these two views aims to zoom in on and ruminate further upon Deleuze’s claim (1990) that ‘motivation’ is an integral component of societies of control, that is to say, societies of permanent supervision and continual training. As Deleuze says at the end of his short essay ‘Postscript on Control Societies’: 

Many young people have a strange craving to be ‘motivated,’ they’re always asking for special courses and continuing education; it’s their job to discover whose ends these serve, just as older people discovered, with considerable difficulty, who was benefiting from disciplines. A snake’s coils are even more intricate than a mole’s burrow. (Deleuze, 1990, p. 182) To understand this ‘strange craving’ let us take the points of view of Ken Robinson and Bernard Stiegler into consideration on the matter of ADHD. Robinson, whose TED talks ‘How schools kill creativity’ (2006), ‘Bring on the learning revolution!’ (2010a), ‘Changing education paradigms’ (2010b), ‘How to escape education’s death valley’ (2013) have around 27 million, 4.9 million, 1.4 million and 3.6 million hits respectively (making him one of the most viewed speakers on TED.com) – asks why students are ‘turned off’ from education in many schools in the UK. His blunt answer – derived from years of empirical research (Robinson & National Advisory Committee on Creative and Cultural Education, 2001) is that excessive testing and the culture of no-saying to creativity effectively alienates students from the learning process. With students’ lives outside the classroom replete with tools and gadgets to fuel the imagination, to accelerate learning – such as every kind of education application on iPods, smartphones etc. – it is hard for teachers to keep up with the learning revolution underway. Interlinked with this idea, Robinson insists, is the divorce of language studies from the arts (drama, music and visual arts) in school curricula, and he is clearly right to bemoan this trend as it crushes creativity and aesthetic ways of comporting oneself to the world (see Misson & Sumara, 2006). 

*[p. 152]* ^pg167

Robinson Contra the ADHD ‘myth’ 

Denouncing ‘the climate of fear and risk aversion’ and the dogmatism of ‘single standardized measures’, Robinson (2001) and Robinson and Aronica (2009) suggest – considering the long-term fate of education – that one simply cannot underemphasize the gravity of the task ahead. In sum, Robinson insists that educationalists need to radicalize and experiment with their methodology for today’s students. As he says (2009, p. 16): ‘In these ways, our current education system systematically drains the creativity out of our children’. In *The Element* (2009), he outlines his antidote to the one-size-fits-all policy in schools. He searches for the ‘element’, that is to say, that sense of passion, joy and enduring commitment to a project, which he views as the hallmark of real learning. From his optimistic quasi-humanist perspective, he insists that creativity is boundless; a good intrinsic to all human beings – it is just a question of realizing the potential of all. What is required therefore is the spirit of open and free enquiry, in multimodal settings which foster meaning-making and affective responses, which inspire and invoke the ‘extraordinary powers of imagination’ (2009, p. 9). Robinson is clearly searching for that excitement, respect, hope – the sense of wonder – that is at the center of one’s being when one is young. 

The main direction of Robinson’s critique of schools is as follows (2009): while it has indeed become an everyday object of use, talk of the Internet’s impact as passé is some time off because what it is doing to the brains of users – in the language of neuroscience – is still largely unaccounted for. It is perhaps moot to say but the Internet is shaping people’s lives in ways not seen since the invention of the printing press. It is and has revolutionized educational paradigms and indeed the very way in which the young and old study. On this account, one cannot play down the Internet’s ongoing significance. It thus behooves educationalists to transform, disrupt and transcend anachronistic paradigms. In a nutshell, young people are turned off from education because ‘attention’ goes elsewhere for stimulation and sustenance. Although Robinson’s message sounds at times like the song ‘Brick in the Wall’ by Pink Floyd, especially when he points out that each student is bulging at the seams with creative potential but that this intensity and desire to learn is beaten out of them by formal schooling and continual testing, he is right to note the difficulty the teacher faces in captivating the 

*[p. 153]* ^pg168

attention of students when the latter have immediate access to vast databases of knowledge on information devices. In this way, young people are naturally distracted in class because of the myriad sources of abstract information available at the swish of a finger or click of a button. 

Undoubtedly correct in highlighting the impact of technologies on the ways students learn, Robinson however, is not alone. Indeed, the French philosopher Michel Serres (2012) recently celebrated the phase change in the way young people learn. A new paradigm has taken root with knowledge no longer bequeathed by the teacher as repository of learning. No, the whereabouts of knowledge is ‘all in computers’. Having outsourced much of her retentional memory to mobile devices, Thumbelina – as he calls the mobile phone carrying thumb generation – ‘carries her head in her hands’ and, as a result this radically transforms behavior (Serres, 2012). Although aware that the media has long seized the teaching function, and that teachers are less and less heard, Serres insists young people are learning afresh, using data differently, no longer dependent on rote learning as compared to former generations. Celebrating this fact, Serres suggests that such a situation demands teachers respond in due fashion. In *Petite Poucette* (2012), he claims the technological revolution of the 20th century has achieved the philosophical goal of education which is to impart knowledge universally as young people are learning independently, all the time, on the go. From a critical point of view, it is again this aforementioned ‘strange craving’ which therefore needs to be accounted for. 

While erring on the side of caution in acknowledging the existence of ADHD, Robinson (2010b) challenges the growing orthodoxy in medicine and claims hyper attention may well be a ‘fictitious epidemic’ because drugs like Ritalin or Dexedrine are more a way to control young people who have to endure the tedium in the classroom. Through excessive control and by repressing the natural creativity of each child, much like the modern proletariat working under neoliberal capitalist relations, teachers get children to the graduation ceremony by anaesthetizing them. On the production line or in the school system, both effectively destroy the germ of creativity. In a February 2010 TED speech entitled ‘Bring on the learning revolution!’ Robinson called for a root-and-branch revolution in education. As he says: 

*[p. 154]* ^pg169

Reform is no use anymore, because that’s simply improving a broken model. What we need – and the word’s been used many times during the course of the past few days – is not evolution, but a revolution in education. This has to be transformed into something else. (Robinson, 2010a) 

Surprisingly, it is here that he shares common ground with Bernard Stiegler. Robinson (2010a) sounds vaguely Stieglerian when he insists: ‘At the heart of the challenge is to reconstitute our sense of ability and of intelligence’. As we shall see, Stiegler calls the struggle for individuation (the process of experimenting, of becoming what one is) the battle for intelligence. Elsewhere, Robinson (2010b) further discusses his desire to forge a new paradigm shift in education. And making the case for thinking beyond a model delimited to serving economic interests, Robinson says in his speech ‘Changing education paradigms’ (2010b) that the fast-food model of education invariably impoverishes the spirit and energies of youth. As an alternative to this Victorian paradigm, schools must refashion the classroom to allow for the implementation of more experimental platforms. Contra antiquated and anachronistic education models which by and large alienate schoolchildren *en mass* through irrelevant curricula serving the economic imperatives and goals of a past era, Robinson argues: 

Our children are living in the most intensely stimulating period in the history of the earth. They’re being besieged with information and calls for their attention from every platform – computers, from iPhones, from advertising hoardings, from hundreds of television channels and we’re penalizing them now for getting distracted. From what? Boring stuff at school, for the most part. (Robinson, 2010b) 

Simply put, schools cannot compete with the learning process that is going on elsewhere and at unprecedented, unfathomable speeds. It is not only a question of the rapid development of multiple intelligences (Gardner, 1983), technical and multiliteracies (Cole & Pullen, 2010), which students intuitively gain through electronic games and different textualities, because something else is happening, students are simply learning all the time, in different spaces and times – outside the classroom, on the mobile. Regarding the thorny epistemological issue of what intelligence is, Robinson (2006) is at his best when he spells out its multilateral dimensions. As he says: ‘We think about the world in all the ways that we experience it. 

*[p. 155]* ^pg170

We think visually, we think in sound, we think kinaesthetically. We think in abstract terms, we think in movement’. Teachers have to play catch up with these phenomena and understand that as a creative profession, teaching is not a ‘delivery system’ as such. The modus operandi of teachers is not ‘just to pass on received information’ (Robinson, 2013), and it is here that Robinson echoes the New London Group (New London Group & National Languages & Literacy Institute of Australia, 1995), whose members are concerned by the disillusionment with the overall formal academic content that inheres in school-based literacy curricula. 

Engarde bétise 

The complex thoughts and extensive ruminations of Bernard Stiegler, his repertoire of concepts drawn from diverse paradigms and traditions in the multi-volume works *La Technique et le temps* (Technics and Time, 1998, 2008, 2011a) and *Mécréance et Discrédit* (Disbelief and Discredit) for example, means any attempt to reproduce or map such an intricate architectonics will produce only a poor caricature. I therefore add the caveat that I will be selective in how I apply Stiegler’s thought to principles pertaining to the philosophy of education. Despite the disclaimer, it is indeed timely to consider Stiegler’s important work in the field of educational philosophy as it updates and extends Deleuze’s influential societies of control thesis. Moreover, unlike many politically impotent philosophies, Stiegler lays down the gauntlet to respond to the ethical conundrum of a youth assaulted by ‘attention grabbing’ technologies. 

The French philosopher starts from a point of view radically different from that of the UK government backed educationalist Robinson. In much starker terms, Stiegler insists that the widespread use of drugs like Ritalin is largely a result of the fact that students suffer – borrowing from the language of Simondon – from a loss of individuation, that is to say, a loss of both psychic and collective individuation. In other words, they are becoming less and less able to care. In extremis, this leads to the shorting of transindividuation circuits (which pertains to the realm of sublimation and rule of the superego) and this threatens the very collective itself with annihilation. After all, you cannot sustain a society addicted to anti-social behavior for very long. With long circuits of transindividuation severed, 

*[p. 156]* ^pg171

widespread drug use is a matter of ‘desperate’ control in increasingly uncontrollable societies. Drug use is a method to reign in the range of attention disorders accentuated by ‘industrial temporal objects’ like the TV, cinema, violent PC games or mobile phones which ‘capture’ consciousness. 

With consciousness and memory torn away from the immediate environs and especially in the classroom, this process of *Zerrissenheit* or absolute dismemberment (see Cole & Bradley, 2014) engenders repetitive refrains which can have destructive effects on attention as they do not produce anything new per se but rather foster deadly cycles of stultifying sameness. From Deleuze’s point of view (2001), if there is repetition without difference, there is no relation to the outside. It follows that without an outside there is no engendering or fostering of multiplicities and intensities of relations – no process of enfoldment, no involution between in and out. There is no becoming other, no empathy or care for the other, which for Stiegler means that: events can turn very nasty. The scrambling of transindividuating circuits can make monsters of children – one immediately thinks of the Columbine High School massacre in 1999 and other more recent and harrowing instances of schools becoming blood baths – such as the shooting at Marysville-Pilchuck High School in Washington State, US, in October, 2014. It is here that Stiegler updates and complements Deleuze’s comments on the idea of stupidity. <sup>25</sup> 

In lengthy discussions with Claire Parnet and regarding his career as a teacher, Deleuze (2004) says he and Guattari found it ‘unsupportable’ that young people could slide over into complete blankness (*état de blanc*). He says he could not tolerate young people taking drugs to the point of collapse, or drinking to the point of falling into some ‘wild’ state (*état sauvage*). Faced with such problems, Deleuze nevertheless tried to prevent young people from being reduced to pulp (*état de loque*). Writing against the ‘cracking up’ of young people, he says: ‘je ne le supporte pas’ \[I cannot stand it\]. Deleuze thus denounces the turning to pulp of youth through addiction and excess. meanwhile, Stiegler finds in the addiction to industrial temporal objects – such as excessive Internet, video game and mobile phone use – a turning to pulp of young people’s subjectivity. Simply put, for Stiegler, the primacy of the object of addiction can lead to the annihilation of the subject – the loss of individuation. His view is that industrial temporal objects foster addiction through a kind of occupation of time, a ‘flattening’ 

*[p. 157]* ^pg172

of consciousness – a point that resonates with the sense of the flattening of capitalist subjectivity (*laminage de la subjectivitécapitalistique*) of which Félix Guattari speaks (1995). Writing against the ‘dictatorship of shorttermism’ (2010b) and the ensuing carelessness and precarity of short-term thinking, Stiegler resolutely opposes ‘control’ by the drives \[pulsions\] – or the satisfaction of our lowest common denominator through advertising and marketing (shopping, pornography, violence) – as this domination short-circuits the libidinal economy, derails the transformation of the drives into desire, into fidelity, into care, and can turn subjects suicidal as a consequence. He thus warns that the process of desublimation, no doubt derived from Marcuse’s Freud-inspired notion of repressive desublimation (1968), can liquidate the authority of the superego and give way to the ‘hideous beast’ (2012, p. 48) – a form of a negative sublimation. The destruction of the superego finds a correlative in the destruction of libidinal energy that capitalism exacerbates. Stiegler insists those who are given over to the control of the drives are the victims of the suicidal organization of society itself. It is worth quoting Stiegler at length on this point: 

These manifestations of hopelessness and despair, having become ordinary, having become our everyday reality, these pathological behaviors – which are nevertheless faithful reflections of drive-based models that marketing promotes, and which make it seem as though suicidal actions are seeping into everyday life, a manifestation of the slow decomposition of the motives for living, in the ruined milieu of what had previously contained possibilities of existing, signify that the reign of despair, that is, the destruction of every reason to hope, has now become immense and terrifying. (Stiegler, 2012, p. 62) 

Here, the sense of the ‘slow decomposition of the motives for living’ and the ‘destruction of every reason to hope’ offers a crucial update on Deleuze’s scepticism of the contemporary. As we know, writing on the transformation of movement and time images in his two books on cinema, Deleuze points out that the ‘I’ in post-WWII societies senses he or she is not living its own life. As he says: ‘The modern fact is that we no longer believe in this world. We do not believe in the events which happen to us, love, death, as if they only half concerned us. It is not we who make cinema; it is the world which looks to us like a bad film’ (2005, p. 166). After the end of WWII, Deleuze finds not only the West but also further afield in 

*[p. 158]* ^pg173

Japan in the grip of a mutation. He perceives abrupt changes in Europe after the war, in an Americanized Japan, and in France in 1968. There is a sense in which characters in auteur cinema – on which Deleuze draws heavily – no longer believe in the milieu in which they inhabit. It is our lot to see rather than act and as such we are seers of our own in-existence. Discerning a similar sense of disillusionment, Stiegler (2014a) labels this ‘symbolic misery’ and describes the present epoch as one which is out of love with itself. Unpacking this meaning he explains it thus in *Uncontrollable Societies of Disaffected Individuals* (2012, p. 82): ‘A world that does not love itself is a world that does not believe in the world: we can believe only in what we love’. Our era is one which exhausts its own viability and belief in itself, with deleterious results: there is a passage from symbolic to spiritual misery. Uncontrollable societies populated by massified and disaffected individuals become more and more unliveable. The atmosphere of our hyper-industrial epoch – hyperactive control societies – a world of registers and barcode readers – is ‘heavy, stifling, and anguished’ (2012, p. 82). 

For Stiegler like Deleuze, control societies exploit the tendency of noetic beings (mindful selves) to regress to the level of drives, to the level that they become furious. As desublimation liquidates the authority of superego, the self becomes hideous, a beast of all abominations. The loss of a figure to believe in leads to a crisis of authority at the very heart of society. The vacuum is filled by ersatz forms of meaning: shopping, consumerism to the nth degree, more and more violence, all for any absurd purpose. For Stiegler, commodification is precisely related to the liquidation of superego and the loss of sublimation. Generalized proletarianization (Stiegler, 2010a) or deskilling – a term which emerges out of a reading of Simondon and Garelli (2005) – which Stiegler takes to mean the dumbing down of workers, the reign of base drives and the proliferation of addictions – leads to a loss of feeling of existing – one could say a loss of creativity – at worst to despair, with spiritual misery and negative sublimation the effects of a hyper-vulnerable technological system. Manifestations of hopelessness and despair – pathological behaviors – are seeping into everyday life, according to Stiegler. Again in apocalyptic terms, he insists that the manifestation of the slow decomposition of the motives for living has become ‘immense and terrifying’ (2012, p. 62). 

*[p. 159]* ^pg174

Consumer behavior can become pathological, with the social order plagued by malignant and excrescent growths. Stiegler’s antidote to this is a noopolitics to contest what he terms psychotechnologies and neuropower —a battle using technologies of the mind rather than the industrial temporal objects. His tactic is to subvert the ‘technologies of the spirit? which dominate our spiritless age. For Stiegler, suicidal control societies — which operate through the exploitation of libidinal energy that exhausts this very energy itself — and the ‘culture industry’ (Adorno & Bernstein, 2001) — are two sides of the self-same hyper-industrial epoch. They both rechannel technologies of the spirit. Cognitive technologies are thus ultimately complicit in sustaining psychotechnologies which destroy attention.

Borrowing language from Husserl and tracing the construction of primary, secondary and tertiary retentions in consciousness, Stiegler finds that the latter retentions underpin the transindividual. Long circuits of transindividuation (see Simondon, 1989) are constantly under assault by new media technologies which fundamentally restructure intergenerational connection, memory and reorder retentions and protentions (that is the experience of the future in trepidation — with hope). The critique of what Stiegler calls the retentional economy resonates with Franco Berardi’s work (2009) who insists that young children from the 1970s learn language more from technical objects than their mothers. Drawing on anthropologist Rose Golden’s comment from 1975 (Berardi, Empson, & Shukaitis, 2009), he highlights the way dependency on the machine upsets the affective relation to language itself. Again, if there is no sublimation, then there is no transindividuation because the latter is more and more short-circuited by marketing and advertising — which act to desublimate. Pharmacologically, Stiegler is searching for ways to restore ‘deep attention’ (see Hayles, 2007) to move from poison to remedy to cure, to create transindividual circuits which reinvigorate society.?\*° For Stiegler (2013), following his teacher Derrida, the pharmacological — following an interpretation of Plato — means a paradoxical phenomenon that is both poison and cure, bringing both benefit and harm. So, for example, for Stiegler and Guattari too, computers are pharmacological because they introduce both emancipatory possibilities as well as newly found repressions. Stiegler is no Luddite because his analysis emerges from a close reading of André Leroi-Gourhan (1993), who

*[p. 160]* ^pg175

insists that techné has always been an ontological aspect of the human. There is no escape from this fact – our facticity is technical. 

Writing against the perverse effects of pharmaka, and borrowing Foucault’s language, intelligence signifies the taking care of pharmaka through careful use. Here, Stiegler it appears is always looking for signs of hope as he treats care – derived from Foucault’s notion of care of the self – as the care one takes of oneself through the care one takes of others. The reconstitution of a politics of attention and noopolitics is ‘grounded in the psychopolitical regulation of economic psychopower’ (Stiegler, 2010b, p. 182). Nonetheless, he is critical of ‘industrial temporal objects’ like mobile phones which capture, monopolize and penetrate attention – through the control of time – in unheard of degrees. Industrial temporal objects like mobile phones are key components of societies of control because they work to capture attention and dominate the sensorium, even more so in the so-called uncontrollable societies. In systematically implementing grammatization iterations \[the outsourcing of memory, a term usually attributed to Sylvain Auroux; see Stiegler, 2011b\], societies of control thereby open new possibilities for the control of the central nervous system through a powerful stimulation of retentions and protentions. The point here is that issues of ADHD need to be put into context because they are a response in part to societies already mad or schizophrenic. It is too simplistic to insist, as Robinson appears to do, that once children are given a space to be fully creative vis-[illegible]-vis the arts, everything will turn out well. The issue of Deleuze’s notion of the ‘strange craving’ needs to be contextualized in terms of neoliberal, cognitive semiocapitalism, and the endemic madness and speed of social and economic life. Yet, in remarks not inconsistent with Robinson’s, Stiegler (see Corcoran, 2010) in his keynote speech ‘The Age of De-proletarianization: Art and teaching art in post-consumerist culture’ states that the raison d’être of the arts in contemporary societies is the ‘de-proletarianization’ of knowledge as such. He reasons that in consumer society not only the know-how (*savoir-faire*) of workers, but also the knowledge of how to live (*savoir-vivre*) of citizens is under threat. That ‘a good consumer’ (Corcoran, 2010, p. 11) is both utterly passive and irresponsible signifies for Stiegler the complete opposite outcome of what Kant calls ‘maturity’, that is to say, the attainment of rational faculty through knowledge of reading and writing. 

*[p. 161]* ^pg176

Following Simondon, Stiegler is clearly concerned with the disintegration of the transindividual circuits and how control societies seek to maximize what he terms the ‘available brain time’ of the general public (2010b). The destruction of transindividual circuits is brought about through the use of new forms of hypomnemata or mnemonic devices. As industrial temporal objects function to control the ‘perception-consciousness system’, the consequences are stark. As Stiegler puts it, power belongs with control. In concrete terms, control functions by way of the flux of ‘industrial temporal objects’ such as TV. Berardi spots this too by claiming that the cell phone functions to effectively call the worker back to work, to ‘reconnect their abstract time to the reticular flux’ (Berardi, 2011). In terms of semiocapitalism, Berardi argues that the mobile phone is a tool appendage that connects the needs of semiocapital and ‘the mobilization of the living labour of cyberspace’. Indeed, noting the affective effects of technological addiction, which include psychopathological types of panic, Berardi et al. (2009) picks up on the issue of cognitive overload, writing in *Precarious Rhapsody*: 

Individuals are not in a position to consciously process the immense and always growing mass of information that enters their computers, their cell phones, their television screens, their electronic diaries and their heads. (Berardi et al., 2009, p. 40) 

The catastrophic result is the engendering of disaffected psychic and social individuals, of which the December 2012 Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting is one such horrifying expression. This is the aforementioned beast of all abominations. Moreover, industrial temporal objects qua psychotechnological apparatuses detrimentally affect the intimate relations between the generations – as it is through parents and teachers that knowledge is handed down. Psychotechnological systems hone in on the control of attention – and distort or short-circuit the psychic system for the production of desire. It is in this sense that psychopower destroys the transmission of knowledge. An affect of the reordering of the organology of the central nervous system and combined with the fall off in ‘deep attention’, we are witness to a distinct rupturing of the psychic and synaptic circuitry of juveniles and the so-called acceleration of hyper attention disorders. For example, multitasking – and the corresponding loss of ‘deep 

*[p. 162]* ^pg177

attention’ reached through reading literature for example – is blamed for accelerating a generational mutation, which leads to the forgetting of old systems of retention, memory and learning. In China, *tibiwangzi*, or ‘take pen, forget character’ is a striking instance of this trend through excessive reliance on mobile phones to write Chinese characters (Moxley, 2010). 

Through the conquest of psychotechnological apparatuses which serve the time of capital and lead to a ruination of the affective capacities of youth, Stiegler discerns the tendency to withdrawal (*désaffectation*), to a loss of ethos or place, and for him, this is a matter of ethics. While Stiegler’s analysis (2012) of Japan is sketchy in parts and derivative to a large degree, he uses it as an excrescent model, and finds instances further afield (see Kato et al., 2012) <sup>27</sup> where the ‘I’ is disindividuated, concretized in the mushrooming of cases of hikikomori or acute social withdrawal.<sup>28</sup> As Teo (2010, p. 7) warns: ‘Japan is in the midst of an epidemic of adolescents and young adults who have retreated into their bedrooms, in effect vanishing from the eyes of society’. In China and Japan, Stiegler notes, psychotic disorders have been produced by a hyper-proletarianization managed through drugs. <sup>29</sup> Not limited to one or two special societal structures, children across the world’s continents are becoming more and more disturbed and disruptive, with widespread attention deficits on both psychological and social levels. In effect, children are more and more depopulated of life by TV, video games and other disorders emerging from hypermarkets and hyper-industrial societies. Such industrial populism is ‘poisoning’ the world, and in this respect, Stiegler says of his work (see Lemmins, 2011): ‘My books want to serve struggles’ to win the ‘battle for the mind’. We see here the very different sense of the term revolution. While Robinson invokes ‘revolution’ as a synonym for a transformatory education battle – or more critically perhaps, to win back creativity as a means to exploit the available surplus value of youth-soon-to-become-workers, Stiegler insists there is a revolution already taking place in terms of cultural and cognitive technologies which assault ‘the very foundations of knowledge’ (2010b, p. 16). 

*[p. 163]* ^pg178

Neuroscience Struggles to ‘capture the mind’ 

Contra the ‘massive capture’ of children’s attention from the earliest age through widespread ‘organological disorders’ – the literal destruction of children’s affective and intellectual capacities – in summa to feel empathy – and opposed to an epoch perceived to be heading more and more towards the destruction of attention and hyper-cynicism – a performative nihilism expressed in the form of ‘I don’t give a damnism’ (2010b, p. 43) or words to that effect – Stiegler claims TV makes us dumb as it targets the drives, curtails the libidinal economy and destroys desire. The point being that capitalism and marketing work in tandem to seize control of this libidinal energy and decompose the drives. On this view, they short-circuit the role of parents, education and even national education as a whole. The message is that teachers and students do not live in a social vacuum – a point conspicuously missing in Robinson’s neoliberal analysis. The creative economy is immanent to an ideological apparatus beset on cultural hegemony. Stiegler (Corcoran, 2010, p. 12) puts it thus: 

What the creative economy model really proposes is a method for resuscitating the ever-weakening desire of consumers, by drawing together marketing and artistic creation to produce a kind of social Viagra. The creative economy, Stiegler insists, does not serve to liberate the self from a world of alienation and suppression of potential. It has little to do with what he calls ‘re-engaging the life of the spirit’, or the ‘reconstitution of responsibility’. To concur with this point means to understand that Robinson’s blue sky, to ‘think the unthinkable’ (a 1990s New Labour mantra if there was ever one) – is ultimately ethereal, utopian and practically impotent. Kids return home from school where the refrain of the TV dominates their home space; it dominates the room and destroys attention. It is ‘the zero degree of thought’ (Stiegler, 2010b). Vehemently opposed to the cognitive and affective saturation of youth in hyper-industrial societies, by a media and culture industry beset on capturing ‘available brain time’, Stiegler criticizes the audiovisual industries for systematically destroying attention with ‘the most brutal and vulgar techniques’ (2010b, p. 72). 

What Stiegler ultimately discerns is a loss of the feeling of existing – a consequence of psychic and collective disindividuation which is itself 

*[p. 164]* ^pg179

formed through the commodification of attention. It is this state which currently afflicts otaku and hikikomori and other societal addicts in Japan and elsewhere.<sup>30</sup> On the fate of the hikikomori and otaku in general, Stiegler writes that the ‘congested reality’ of psychic and collective disindividuation may lead to ‘shameless acts of violence’. <sup>31</sup> Hikikomori and otaku are extreme cases and worrying proportions of disaffected youth. Torn away from the social of the school, they become ‘profoundly cut off from the world’ (Stiegler, 2012, p. 88). Living a kind of social autism, shriveled up in their domestic and televisual milieu, and hermetic within a social milieu which is in large part ruined, <sup>32</sup> Otaku, who suffer a loss of psychic and social individuation, are deprived of an ethos, as they reside nowhere other than in the nonplace of the world, in a kind of enclosed, virtual world of computer games and comics. The result is ‘a perfect indifference to the world’. Faced with a global loss of *savoir-faire* (knowledge of how to do) and *savoir-vivre* (knowledge of how to live), Stiegler critiques the becoming-herdish, becoming-wild (*devenir-inculte*), and the growing subsistence without existence. He despairs of a society which has lost the joy of life (*joie de vivre*). 

On the Construction of Attention 

Stupidity belongs to the realm of irresponsibility and incivility, Stiegler insists. And faced with the destruction of singularity, the ‘motive of all motives’, Stiegler warns of the societal slide to the pure and simple loss of every reason for hope – that is, to populism and terror. Informed by Foucault’s technologies of the self thesis (Foucault, Martin, Gutman, & Hutton, 1988), Stiegler examines the act of writing in history and finds it to be a psycho technique which sustains attention formation. It is capturing of the self by the self. Here, attention is a technology of the self. There is thus a nootechnics of the book – a *hypomnematon*. Writing, as a psychotechnology of attention is pharmacological because it constructs an apparatus of individuation. In Kantian terms, writing and reading are thus mechanisms for the production of maturity in an ‘organological age of attention formation’ (Stiegler, 2010b, p. 166). If to learn is to retain, then education is attention formation through social accumulation. All teachers then must be concerned with the loss of attention among the young because 

*[p. 165]* ^pg180

attention formation is transindividual – in the sense that it is the keystone of the social arch. So a reminder for teachers would be to inculcate and protect the attention of children and adolescents and resist complicity with the overuse of ‘industrial temporal objects’. Contesting the organological forms of stupidity of the age, Stiegler forecasts the emergence of new pharmacological and organological conditions that could be in some way new forms of individual and collective intelligence. He calls this the organological reformation of *Bildung*. Stupidity is equally important for Deleuze because it is precisely stupidity that compels us to think. It is at the edge of the ridiculous that thinking takes place. It is also a question of shame, the shame of being human; the condition which demands that we think fundamentally and face up to our collective bêtise. 

Throughout Stiegler’s corpus, there is a consistent message against the systematic adult infantilization and the prematurely becoming-adult of children – something which all teachers are concerned within some way. He concludes in his *Uncontrollable Societies* (2012, p. 123) that young people have a right to a future not built on fear and insists they have a right to something other than being policed, a right to a future that is terrestrial, spiritual and elevated. Although, for Stiegler, young people perhaps may no longer believe in the authority of their parents and teachers as they once did, and when confronted with and confused by the structural cynicism of the society in which they live, a society that produces a world without shame, and who have no other avenue than anti-social withdrawal, he finds a genuine possibility of sublimation through the reconstitution of the life of the spirit – comprised of an ecological economy of cognitive and affective functions. He calls this a ‘technology of the spirit’. This is the optimistic thread which runs throughout his work. 

Conclusion 

In their own ways, Robinson and Stiegler both make significant contributions to the topic of attention disorders and its connection with creativity. Yet, Robinson makes no sustained critique of the desire for constant supervision and learning in control societies as Deleuze did or in hyper-competitive environments where the widespread use of Prozac, Ritalin or Provigil is believed to improve emotional and psychical life and 

*[p. 166]* ^pg181

boost cognitive performance. In his work, there is no sustained examination of systemic machinic enslavement the likes of which Lazzarato speaks (2006), which could add another dimension to his work.<sup>33</sup> For Lazzarato, techniques of control operate through the modulation of brains, that is to say, the tinkering with the psychic life of memory and attention, general intellect and mental disposition – a new kind of knowledge/power micro-technology. Lazzarato writes (2006): 

The television-machine also acts as a device of machinic enslavement by investing the basic functionality of perceptual, sensory, affective, cognitive and linguistic behaviors, and so can work on the most fundamental impulses of human activity and of life itself. 

Nor is there in Robinson’s work a critique of the social subjection operating at the molecular level (pre-individual or infrasocial) – which modifies affects, sensations and desires – that Berardi writes against (2009). While Stiegler argues for an ‘industrial politics of spirit’ (Stiegler, 2014b) to confront the disaffection of youth, the content and focus remains unclear. To remedy this, one may return to Guattari (2000, p. 68) and his mental ecology which spells out proscriptions on this point: ‘We need new social and aesthetic practices, new practices of the Self in relation to the foreign, the strange’. Wedding the work of Deleuze, Guattari and Stiegler, the above has been an attempt to write a prolegomena of a schizoanalysis of neuroeducation, to question ‘the production of brains without consciousness or conscience’ (Corcoran, 2010, p. 19). Such a work looks at the supposed strange craving for dopamine euphoria intensified in prolonged video game use, the architecture of which increasingly uses sophisticated psychological and cognitive devices to seduce and manipulate the mind and emotions – that is to say, a process that keeps the learner or player coming back for more – like laboratory rats – ad libitum – a ‘strange craving’ indeed.
