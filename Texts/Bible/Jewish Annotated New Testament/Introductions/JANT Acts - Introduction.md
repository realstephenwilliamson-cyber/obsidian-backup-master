## Title of Book and Meaning

The Acts of the Apostles (Acts) appears fifth in the canonical order of the books of the New Testament, but originally it was the second part of a two-volume work (1.1) written by the author of the Third Gospel, traditionally identified as Luke. The two works (Luke-Acts) have a common literary style, narrative parallels, and thematic similarities. While the title “Acts” refers in general to all the apostles, the work focuses on Peter and Paul.

## Authorship

The attribution of Acts to Luke, often considered a physician and companion of Paul ([[Colossians 04#^4-14|Col 4.14]]; [[2 Timothy 04#^4-11|2 Tim 4.11]]; Philem 24), first appears in the second century (Irenaeus, *Adv. Haer.* 3.1.1; 3.14.1; Tertullian, *Marc.* 4.2.2; Clement, *Paed.* 2.1.15; *Strom.* 5.12.82). The author had considerable familiarity with the Hebrew Bible through its Greek translation (the Septuagint), Jewish customs and institutions such as Sabbath restrictions on travel (1.12), Greek literary traditions (Paul quotes from Greek poets in 17.28), and Roman political structures (e.g., legal proceedings in ch 24).

## Date

The Gospel of Luke probably alludes to the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple (see 19.41–44; 21.20–24), which places its composition after 70 CE. It is also possible that the author of Acts had access to the writings of Josephus, which were also composed after 70. While a precise date is impossible to establish, Acts was most likely composed early in the second century CE.

## Literary History

Acts has long been understood as a history of the early church. The author describes the work as an orderly narrative based on careful investigation (see [[Luke 01#^1-3|Lk 1.3]]). Several characters, such as the Roman proconsul Gallio (18.12) and Felix and Festus, the procurators of Judea (ch 24), are historical figures. Other details, such as the sailing routes and nautical practices (e.g., 27.9–44), references to Roman law (ch 24), and beginning in chapter 16, the frequent use of first-person plural (“we”), have suggested that the author was present at the events narrated or had access to firsthand accounts.

Modern scholarship, however, has called into question the historical reliability of Acts. Many of the deeds and words of Peter, Paul, and the other apostles cannot be confirmed, and in several instances the information presented in Acts contradicts what we know from other sources, including Paul’s letters. For instance, the Jerusalem council’s decision on the admission of Gentiles to the community, including abstention from eating “whatever has been strangled” (15.20), in keeping with the Torah’s prohibitions of eating carrion (e.g., [[Torah 29 - Achrei Mot#^17-15|Lev 17.15]]), is not borne out in Paul’s letter to the Galatians (2.10), which mentions only service to “the poor” as the requirement. Whatever historical information may be present in Acts, the selection of events, their ordering, the content of the speeches, and many “historical” details were determined by the author’s theological and literary interests.

## Structure and Contents

Acts presents an account of the expansion of the church from its origins among the small group of Jesus’ followers in Jerusalem to a movement spread throughout the Roman Empire. The narrative trajectory is summarized in the initial instructions that the resurrected Jesus imparts to his disciples: they should be his “witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth” (1.8). The action unfolds in two main units. The first, chapters 1–12, focuses largely on activities that take place in Jerusalem, Judea, and Samaria. Peter, the central figure, delivers speeches, performs healings, and, as the climax of this section, baptizes the first Gentile convert, the Roman centurion Cornelius. (Philip had previously baptized an Ethiopian eunuch, 8.26–40, but he is characterized as already worshipping God in Jerusalem.) Beginning in chapter 13, the focus shifts to Paul, his missionary activity in Asia Minor (present-day Turkey) and Greece, his arrest by Jewish authorities, questioning before Roman and Jewish authorities, and his journey to Rome, where he is to plead his case before the emperor. Although the author surely knew that Paul was dead, likely killed during Nero’s persecution of Jesus’ followers in 64, the book concludes with Paul preaching openly in Rome.

## Interpretation

Scholars have long attempted to identify the genre of Acts, whether as history, biography, or Hellenistic novel. Considerable attention also has also been paid to ascertain its purpose: a defense of Christians in the eyes of Romans, a defense of Romans in the eyes of Christians, a defense of Paul’s memory, particularly against charges of antinomianism (not following the \[Mosaic\] law and its interpretation), an explanation or reassurance to Christians who grew anxious about the delay of Jesus’ return, and/or an attempt to harmonize di-verging opinions among early Christians. The attitude of Acts toward the Roman Empire is itself perplexing. While the author may have wanted to show the Roman government as legitimate or to reassure Romans that Christians were not a threat to their rule (as in the treatment of Paul in the latter part of the book), the author is in places highly critical of certain Roman political claims, such as Rome proclaiming itself ruler of the world.

## Guide to Reading

Acts shares with other early Christian writings many ideas that become central to later Christian tradition, although it presents them without the more developed argumentation such as is found Paul’s letters. Acts highlights the power of the Holy Spirit to guide and protect Christians (e.g., 19.21), the resurrection as the core proof of Jesus’ identity (e.g., 2.22–32), and the offer of salvation to all persons (10.34–35). Other themes, such as martyrdom (chs 6–7) and the sharing of possessions (2.45), find widespread acceptance in later Christian thought.

Acts presents an idealized church expanding in an orderly, harmonious fashion, from Jerusalem to Rome and from Jew to Gentile, and comprising persons who live according to a common set of values and observe an agreed-upon set of ethical norms, as members of God’s people.

One of the major themes in Acts is the inclusion of Gentiles among the people of God. According to Acts, Gentiles are now full participants in God’s promises, and any distinction between Jew and Gentile as members of God’s people has ceased. As Peter says, “God shows no partiality” (10.34). Although the newly formed relation between God and Gentiles comes as a divine act (ch 10), Acts also depicts Jewish rejection of Christian claims as causing the gospel to come to Gentiles (13.46; 18.5–6; 28.28). The ending of the book, in which Paul emphatically declares that the Gentiles will listen to the proclamations of Jesus, reflects the time of the writing of Acts in which former pagans and not Jews comprised the majority of most Christian communities.

While Acts conceives of all persons comprising the people of God, it creates new categories of divine judgment by distinguishing between those who believe in Jesus as resurrected Messiah and those who do not. Those who reject Jesus are excluded from God’s community, and they do not share in the blessings of salvation. Only those who believe can expect salvation (4.12). Acts thus replaces an ethnic distinction with a theological one that comes to define the new people of God.

Aspects of Roman government and culture constitute another important element in the narrative. The Roman government is represented by its magistrates, soldiers, and legal practices, and Paul identifies himself as a Roman citizen (22.25–27), a claim not verified by his own letters. Generally, Roman figures treat Christians with respect. Several Romans (e.g., Lysias, 23.26–30) show sympathy with Christian figures, and a few, such as the centurion Cornelius (10.44–48), convert to Christianity. Beyond any historical reminiscence, the author may have used this largely positive portrayal of Roman power to convince Christian readers to see their religious community as a legitimate and welcomed part of the Roman world. Acts also employs language (e.g., “Lord of all” \[10.36\]) used by Roman authorities and writers to justify Roman imperial power, and it applies such language to Jesus and the early church. In so doing, Acts presents Jesus, rather than the emperor, as the true savior of the world.

Historical Jewish figures such as Rabbi Gamaliel, Agrippa I, Agrippa II, and Jewish institutions, such as synagogues and the Temple, occupy a prominent place throughout Acts. Acts identifies the Pharisees as those who believe in resurrection, in contrast to the Sadducees who do not (23.6–10), and provides impor tant information on contemporaneous Jewish institutions and customs. The Jerusalem Temple is the site of worship and sacrifice (2.46; 3.1; 5.21; 21.26), and Paul participates in rituals of purification (21.26). Other Jewish customs mentioned include the taking of vows (18.18), the festivals of Passover (20.6; see also [[Luke 22#^22-1|Lk 22.1]]) and Shavuot/Weeks (2.1), and circumcision (16.3); Acts frequently portrays synagogues as important communal institutions and describes Jews participating in synagogue activities such as reading and discussing scripture on the Sabbath. It also depicts Gentiles who themselves are engaged in Jewish religious activities, persons who are often referred to as “God-fearers” (Gk *sebomenoi/phoboumenoi ton theon*), such as the Ethi- opian eunuch who worshipped at the Temple and was reading Isaiah (8.27–28; 10.2) or the Gentiles who are present in the Diaspora synagogues.

Relying on the Septuagint, Acts includes quotations from several biblical books, most commonly Psalms and Isaiah, but also verses from Exodus, Jeremiah, Joel, Amos, and Habakkuk. Characters are described as reading from the Law (i.e., the Pentateuch) and Prophets (13.15; 28.23). Acts makes clear that scripture, properly understood, foretells who Jesus is and how people will come to respond to him (13.41,47; 15.16–18; 28.26–27). Jewish figures in Acts respond to these teachings in different ways: some accept what the apostles preach (2.41; 13.42), and others reject it (7.54; 17.2). The book culminates with Paul’s fiery denunciation of Jewish unbelief, punctuated by a quotation from Isaiah that both predicts Jewish intransigence and focuses on God’s promises to Gentiles (28.25–28).

Luke-Acts depicts the believers in Jesus as possessing the proper understanding of scripture, obedient to God, and serving as the true recipients of the divine promises and blessings. Jesus’ followers point to the fulfillment of prophecies (e.g., 2.14–28) as they exhort both Jews and Gentiles to accept Jesus as the Messiah. Some Jews, however, generally prove unwilling to convert or incapable of comprehending God’s actions. In contrast, the ease with which many Gentiles, including God-fearers, come to this recognition casts further reproach upon Jews for their unbelief.
