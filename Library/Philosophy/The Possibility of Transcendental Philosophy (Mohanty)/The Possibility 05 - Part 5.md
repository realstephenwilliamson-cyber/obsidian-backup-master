The problem thus presupposes a historically accomplished framework of thinking. Once this is recognised, it would seem as though by *overcoming* this tradition - at one stroke, as it were - one gets rid of the problem. However, this is a move which, because of its radicalness and yet because of the ease with which one ,desires to eliminate a nagging problem, has proved tempting to many philosophers. But it is precisely the ease with which so radical an amputation is to be achieved, which makes it suspect. What is needed, most importantly, is to exhibit how the problem as well as the framework, i.e., the Cartesian distinction, come to be constituted, how they come to emerge *from within phenomena* themselves. The mind body dualism is not merely a philosophical theory. Rather, the philosophical theory lays its hold upon and radicalises modes of interpretation, i.e., noematic senses under which we apperceive ourselves and others. If Cartesian dualism is grounded in phenomena themselves, it is not for that reason a phenomenological theory, for it does not see the genesis and the limits of those phenomena and ascribes to them a primordiality that does not belong to them. If this be so, then to characterise Cartesian dualism - as I have done - as a framework that is a historical accomplishment cannot be the whole truth about it. To the extent it is also grounded in phenomena, it transcends history. 

In our pursuit of the problem of the constitution of the body, we have distinguished between: body as a material object, body as a living organism, body as expressive, body as a cultural object, the lived body in its pre-reflective self-experience, and the constitutive transcendental subjectivity with its structural moment of corporeality. (The last does not concern us here, its introduction earlier was meant only to counteract an erroneous but widely held view about Husserlian transcendental philosophy.) 

(a) Lived body in its pre-reflective, pre-objective self-experience is a directedness towards the world, the world being intended as having its centre around the body and the things belonging to it as well as the dimensions and relations (space, time and causality) in which they are ordered being characterised in terms of the powers and abilities constituting the lived body's self-understanding. This world is not a totality of objects, but a structure of references. At this level, body and mind are not yet distinguished. It is not the case that body is lived as something outer and extended while mind is lived through as something inner and unextended. There is rather one, undivided experience of being in the world and with others. If the Cartesian dualism has not yet constituted

The lived nondistinction between the outer and the inner is threatened by objectivating intentions, so that the body, or rather parts of it, tend to emerge as distinct objects, though always against the background of anonymity, in which role the body as a whole functions. The body functions in this unstable manner of anonymous background and objective figure, though the totality of one's body never quite abandons the former role. Plessner sought to capture this nature of body by the expression *"Leib in Korper,"* suggesting an ambiguity which is also "an actual break in his way of existing," which accounts for both the locutions: a man *is* his body and also *has* it. 18 I am trying to isolate these two as two distinct strata in the constitution of body, and the constitution of body as thing, it seems to me, has its phenomenological basis right within this unstable texture of lived body. Reflective thought lays its exclusive hold on this fissure and objectifies the body as a whole (which presupposes, as Husserl has said on various occasions, the mediation of the other: *"wie sein Leib fur mich, so mein Leib fur ihn").* Except in theory, however, the body - never quite becomes a *Korper.* The other's 

even the other's body - body, perceived as a thing, continues to be apperceived *as* what the other lives as his *Leib.* Even the corpse is perceived as what *was* another's lived body but is no more. 

(b) If lived body is pre-Cartesian, so also is body as expressive. To be sure, expression expresses something. The body "mirrors" the soul. Verbal utterances and also non-verbal gestures express thought and intentions. But in so far as body is expressive, the inner is not the Cartesian inner, the *res cogitans,* the invisible substance or process, but the "sense" or meaning *(Sinn)* of the expression. The outer-inner distinction here is pre-Cartesian, it is the distinction between the sense and its concretisation. One often suspects that the talk of "meaning" is founded on the Cartesian myth of the" ghost in machine." This suspicion is unfounded, for the sense of expressive behavior is "expressed," realised, concretised, so much so that the two - the sense and its concretisation - are inseparable, their unity is the expression. It is here that Strawson's concept of "P-predicate" becomes particularly helpful: for example, the concept of "depression" spans within its structure, both the inner and the outer in one - with the addendum that the outer that is so inseparable from its inner is not yet the Cartesian *res extensa.*

With the constitution of body as *res extensa,* the other member of the pair, mind, as *res cogitans,* also constitutes itself. The two are indeed correlatives. Only after these terms are constituted, is such a philosophical theory as identity theory (of whatever variety) first meaningful, but then one also realises that it cannot be valid as asserting the identity of two terms which are distinct but correlative. The more sophisticated version of the identity theory makes use of the sense reference distinction and asserts the identity of referent but does not want to deny the difference between the two concepts' 'bodily" and' 'mentaL" From the phenomenological point of view, this presupposes that one can lay one's finger on the referent without the mediation of some sense or other, whereas the referent is identifiable only through some sense or other. The pure, unadulterated, bare particular which is the identical referent is a fiction, if neither of the senses "bodily" and "mental" makes any difference to it. The identity of referent through variations in sense is a basic theme for phenomenology, but that identity is not indifference to sense variations, but is constituted by them. The modern identity theory has no place for this notion of constitution, for then the senses would be constitutive of the reference, and the ontology of bare particulars would have to go.

This sense or content - e.g., in the case of an act of thinking, its propositional content, that s is *p -* is the recalcitrant element that refuses to be absorbed into the identity of the referent. The brain state theory in this respect is from one point of view weaker, but from another point of view stronger than behaviorism. It is weaker, for the concept of behavior is more amenable to and in fact is in need of an intentional interpretation; it is stronger in the sense that the most viable models, generally information-theoretical, of neural processes seem to require an intensionallogic. [^19]

Even Strawson's thesis, illuminating up to a point, founders. To have the concept of "depression," according to this thesis, is to be able to ascribe depression to oneself independent of observation *and* to ascribe it to the other on the basis of observation (of the other's behavior). Although this does span the gap between first person ascription and third person ascription, between Cartesianism and behaviorism, what one can ascribe to the other, on the basis of observation, is depression but not what he is depressed about, i.e., not the noematic content of depression. In other words, it is only the possession of a state such as depression or anger that one can ascribe to the other on the basis of observation of his behavior, but one cannot therewith ascribe to him that his mental state has such and such noematic content. It seems then that an entire dimension of the other's mental life remains inaccessible except through his appropriate verbal utterance or through inference on the basis of circumstantial evidence. 

The empirical *theory* which, ideally considered, succeeds in eliminating all intentionality by reducing body to *res extensa* and replacing all talk of the mental by talk of appropriate bodily states, in so far as it is a scientific theory, is, or shall be (when it is completed), a historical achievement of intentional acts of a community of scientists. Thus the denial of intentionality is its own refutation and itself an accomplishment of intentionality. At the same time, there is no reason to cast doubt on the success of scientific theory in achieving its own projects; nor is there any reason to doubt the validity of its theses, for the only criteria of validity to which it can be submitted are internal to the project itself.

v 

The preceding, cursory account of a phenomenology of body, projected against the background of two contemporary analytic discussions of the mind/body problem - those of Wilfrid Sellars and P.F. Strawson - is intended both to show the conceptual limitations from which analytic philosophy's discussions suffer as well as to make it possible to appreciate the contribution that phenomenology can make towards the understanding and the resolution of this classical problem. 

I conclude with one last remark to ward off a possible misunderstanding. One of the central theses underlying the phenomenology of body is that of bodily *subjectivity.* It might appear as though by speaking of "bodily subjectivity" one is in effect assimilating "body" into "consciousness," so that instead of the real body one is talking about the thought or idea of the body. Nothing could be farther removed from our intentions. Body is neither a modality of consciousness, nor is subjectivity coextensive with consciousness. In fact, one of the implications of the concept of bodily subjectivity is that the concept of subjectivity is wider than the concept of consciousness. It also entails that intentionality is a distinguishing feature, not of the domain of consciousness, but of the larger domain of subjectivity. The concept of subjectivity should also be dissociated from the epistemological concept of "subject." Nor do the concepts of subjectivity and consciousness necessarily hang together with the concept of "representation" (of reality) and/or the priority of the temporal dimension of presence over the other modalities of time as Heidegger would have us believe. Released from these historical and metaphysical preconceptions, the phenomenological concept of subjectivity is multifaceted: it is both pre-reflective and reflective, both bodily and intellectual, aesthetic as well as logical, non-temporal as well as temporal. We cannot pursue that line of thought longer, only this much needs to be said now: the phenomenological subjectivity is not the Cartesian inner, but that domain of experience within which the Cartesian dualism has its genesis.

CONSCIOUSNESS AND LIFE-WORLD\* 

I wish to take this opportunity to offer a few of my reflections on what has always seemed to me to be one of the late Professor Gurwitsch's most fundamental philosophical insights. In many of his writings he has emphasised what he has called the ambiguous nature of consciousness, I by which he meant that consciousness lends itself to both causal-explanatory account in terms of functional dependencies on natural phenomena and phenomenological-descriptive account. The ambiguity consists in this very possibility of different points of view. [^2]At the same time, for Aron Gurwitsch, consciousness as "the medium of access to whatever exists and is valid" constitutes "a unique realm of absolute priority.,,[^3] It is on these two theses that I propose to reflect, taking into account the concept of the life-world developed in continuation of Husserl's later writings. 

THE CAUSAL-EXPLANATORY APPROACH 

The ambiguity in the nature of consciousness is said to lie in the possibility of describing it from different points of view. These are: the causal-explanatory and the phenomenological-descriptive. From the first point of view, a conscious state of mine - for example, my thinking

It should be obvious that such an account of my state of consciousness is not strictly speaking a description of it, but gives instead what may be called its "theoretical equivalent" which, for purposes of the theory under consideration, is identical with it. I do not feel competent to develop even the barest outlines of such a theory and the corresponding "theoretical equivalent" of my present state of consciousness, but irrespective of the precise form such an account takes, the following remarks may be in order. 

First, the account is, in the strict sense, relative to the system. If the system be *T,* then what we get is a T-equivalent of my present state of consciousness. In the second place, the T-equivalent shall contain only those terms, concepts, and formulae which are admitted into *T* either as undefined primitive concepts, defined concepts, postulates, or theorems. It is important to bear in mind that the relationship of such a theoretical system as *T* to a pretheoretical experience such as mine (expressed in the sentence "I am trying to formulate this argument") is tenuous: the pretheoretical state C cannot as such enter into the system, its pretheoretical report cannot function within *T* as a well- formed formula of *T.* For *T,* by the very requirements of the system, lays down, prelegislates the requirements which a formula has to satisfy in order to be admitted into the system in any specific role. C, let us then suppose, has to be transformed into *A* in accordance with the requirements of *T,* so that *A,* and not C, shall belong to *T* (even as an explicandum). The theory that is built up on such transformed (or idealised) formulae as *A* derives its explanatory value partly from its predictive power, but that predictive power is inseparable from the possibility of translating back and forth (idealisation and deidealisation) from the language of the system to the pretheoretical language. 

If this be the situation at which a causal-explanatory approach reaches its theoretical completion, precisely at this point it is easier to see what one suspected right at the beginning - namely that a description in terms of functional dependencies is only a halfway house between the pretheoretical report and an incipient theory, so that the" *T-* equivalents" in the completed theory *T* are not descriptions of C but its equivalents in the sense that within the theory they are to replace it altogether. At this state of our thinking, two further points are pertinent. The scientist's replacement or identity statement of the form "C is *A"* is not descriptive but bears the load of the entire theory *T.* At the same time, the fact that the theory *T* does not describe C is no defect of the theory but rather precisely its constitutive sense. It is always a hasty judgment on modern scientific theorising that it removes us from the concrete richness of everyday experience. Such removal or distancing, this emptying of sense or *Sinnentleerung,* precisely constitutes the sense of this enterprise. Husserl therefore in the *Crisis* was as much sitting in judgment on modern science as describing the constitutive sense of Galilean science. To be fully aware of its sense, then, is to be aware of its distance from the concrete lived experience. To be aware of this distance is to be aware of the problem of the genesis of scientific theory. We need then to bring to consciousness the fact that scientific theory which excludes from within itself all reference to conscious acts is itself a product, a historical accomplishment of acts of thinking carried out, in intersubjective communication and in relation to the tradition handed down by previous investigators. Thus, if I may use a Hegelian metaphor, it is the "cunning" of intentionality that it constitutes a sense - the sense of the scientific theory like *T* - to whose noematic structure belongs the denial of, rather the emptying of, alJ intentionality. 

THE PHENOMENOLOGICAL-DESCRIPTIVE APPROACH 

The causal-explanatory approach then does not describe consciousness at all. It would seem to describe it only so long as this approach has not perfected itself into a theory and consequently so long as it occupies an ambiguous position between the informal and the theoretical. For a description of consciousness we then have to turn to the approach of phenomenology. Strangely enough, it is here that the ambiguity of consciousness shows itself. This is because the phenomenological approach itself discloses the possibility of three different descriptions of conscious life. Of these three, two - the phenomenological-psychological and the transcendental-phenomenological - pervade the writings of Husserl and Aron Gurwitsch. The third - the life-woddy (if I may use that construction) - owes its inception to Husserl. To students of phenomenology a detailed account of these three approaches would be redundant. Nevertheless, for the purpose of developing the sequence of my thoughts, I shall briefly characterise them. 

*[p. 142]* ^pg170

(a) As a phenomenological psychologist, I am interested in describing the essential structures of my psychic life, and in order to be able to do so I put within brackets all considerations of psychophysical causality, functional dependencies on natural events and processes. In fact, I decide not to make use of my belief in the world, although the external world remains there. In addition, I do not make any use of the knowledge or beliefs I may have acquired or inherited. Taking these methodological precautions in order to preserve the "purity" of my reflection, I turn my reflective "glance" toward my psychic experiences under consideration. Searching for their essential structures, I vary possible circumstances, objects, etc., and look for a set of unvarying characteristics. If I am studying outer preception, I may thus arrive at its perspectival character, among others. But, if I am interested in consciousness in general, I am struck most of all by the central phenomenon of its intentional directedness to its object, as well as by the peculiar way in which every conscious act intends its object in a specific manner. In other words, I hit upon the structural phenomenon of noetic-noematic correlation described so lucidly and in such great scientific detail by Aron Gurwitsch. I also perceive within me, in that "pure" inner reflection, that my conscious acts are involved in a temporal flux, not a succession of perishing moments but in the continuity of a vanishing comet tail such that each now, with its total temporal horizon of retentions and protentions, is sinking back into the past. I find also the theme-thematic field structure of conscious life, and confirm in my own reflective observations the various laws of transformation formulated by Aron Gurwitsch, developing certain seminal ideas of Husserl. On the whole, as a phenomenological psychologist, my primary point of departure is my own experience, with its noetic-noematic structure, precisely as it is given to me; but also as an eidetic scientist, I apply the method of eidetic variation and seek to arrive at the corresponding eide. In any case, this description is still concerned with the essence of the mental, conscious life as a stratum within the total domain of the world,[^4] even if the rest of the world has been "bracketed out." 

(b) Motivated by the theoretical interest of a radical "founding" of philosophy, and an ultimate *clarification* of the genesis of the sense of my concepts, I may not want to stop at this level of description. (c) It is necessary to state at the outset that I do not intend to refer to any particular concept of the life-world, certainly not to that expounded by Husserl in the *Crisis.* But undoubtedly it is Husserl to whom we owe this concept, irrespective of the form or forms it has taken. THE LIFE-WORLD 

Faced with these three possible approaches to the study of conscious life and the consequent ambiguity in the nature of consciousness, one has inevitably to ask: Which of these is the prior and more fundamental approach? Please note that I am not exactly asking: Which one of the two, consciousness and the world, is primary? I am rather asking: Which of the three approaches to consciousness is the primary one? The relation between that question and this one will become clearer as we proceed. While concerning ourselves with questions such as these, we have to bear in mind that insofar as each of these domains is a field of subjectivity, it is characterised by its own type of intentionality, and in raising the question about their relationship, we also want to make intelligible for us the relationships between life-worldly intentionalities, the psychic intentionalities, and the transcendental intentionalities. This is so difficult a problem that only if it were possible for me to understand the problem correctly, not to speak of solving it, I would regard it a great progress on my part.

TRANSCENDENTAL CONSCIOUSNESS 

Let me now return to the question of priority raised a little while ago. The question of priority may concern either priority in order of givenness

*[p. 149]* ^pg177

, or priority in order of constitution. If the question is, what is the most original way of given ness of consciousness, a mode of givenness that does not presuppose any other or any methodological operation, then our answer would be consciousness as involved in life-worldly experience. This is not to neglect the other and more founded modes of given ness that are made possible by either acts of reflection or higher-order idealising acts - all of which refer back, for their *Sinnesfundament,* to life-worldly experience. If this is so in the case of other concepts (e.g., the concept of "circle"), this is also the case with our concept of consciousness. My bodily subjectivity (and not my reflective consciousness of it) provides the first and the most primitive awareness of consciousness's freedom from, while it is still engaged with, the world. [^15]But the body's prereflective and nonpositional awareness of itself is ever on the verge of being transformed into a reflective attending to ... , and with this reflective attending to, the body freezes into an object as also the attentive glance becomes the incorporeal inner glance. We then have the beginning of that process of reflection which leads to isolating the pure consciousness as an autonomous region of its own, thereby bringing it to its own mode of givenness and realising the possibility of describing it on its own terms. 

But when one asks about priority in order of constitution, the answer would seem to be different. Since constitution for me is constitution of sense (and even constitution of being is nothing but constitution of the meaning of "being"), constitutive phenomenology is really tracing the genesis of meanings to their last origin - such an origin can only be in a domain that is: (1) amenable to pure reflective glance without losing its "originary" characters, (2) in that sense can be brought to *reflective self-evidence* (as contrasted with the merely prereflective transparence), and (3) can be exhibited as a self-complete autonomous region. I want to maintain that only transcendental consciousness satisfies these three requirements, and therefore transcendental consciousness and not life-worldly experience is to be accorded priority in the order of constitution. In other words, I am maintaining the thesis that life-worldly experience is constituted in transcendental consciousness and rejecting the opposite thesis that transcendental consciousness is but a constituted possibility within life-worldly experience. 

Life-worldly experience, being the most primitive mode of givenness, is also the most unstable. Higher forms of consciousness arise out of it, presuppose it as their basis. They can thus arise because life-worldly

AMBIGUITY IN THE STRUCTURE OF CONSCIOUSNESS 

We thus find a profound ambiguity - what is really a pointer to its many-layered nature - in the very structure of consciousness. First, there is the great and ambiguous divide between the transcendental and the mundane, which are, paradoxically enough, not quite the same and yet also not two different things, regions, or processes. Second, there is the undeniable *discontinuity* between reflective consciousness and prereflective consciousness. The prereflective, again, is either the prereflective but transparent consciousness (e.g., of Sartre), or the prereflective bodily behavior, or the prereflective, anonymous, functioning of consciousness as *constituting* the naIvety of the" given." Third, there is the two-tiered structure of consciousness at every level, to which Aron Gurwitsch so convincingly drew our attention: its temporal, historical flow and its atemporal, ideal, meanings. There is also, last of all, the twin aspects of intentionality and reflexivity: in one aspect, consciousness is directed towards the world and, at a deeper level, world-constituting; in the other aspect, it is given to itself even prior to an explicit act of attending or reflective glance. I shall conclude this paper with some brief comments on these points. 

1\. The tension between intentionality and reflexivity, other-directedness and self-transparency, appears at all levels of conscious life. Even bodily behavior, as Merleau-Ponty has shown, is intentional and is not quite opaque. But as these concepts move from one level to another, from the life-worldly to the purely inner, and from there to the transcendental consciousness, their meanings change. To trace out these changes in meaning is a task that we cannot accomplish here.

2\. The structural correlation between the temporal series of conscious acts and their ideal *Sinne* appears equally well at all levels of conscious life - but again with profound changes in the nature of the correlation itself. The language of "noetic act" and "noema" is best adapted to the intermediate level of phenomenological psychology. The bodily behavior is not a noetic act, just as the significance its intentional object has for it is not yet an ideal *Sinn.* Nevertheless, the notion of "sense" or significance can be carried over to the way the world presents itself to the body's responses. At the level of transcendental subjectivity, the notion of act-intentionality itself suffers a serious limitation; acts are now shown to be constituted in the "stream" of inner time-consciousness. How the notion of "noematic sense" has to be recovered here is a most difficult question. In one of the last philosophical conversations I had with Aron Gurwitsch, he assured me that this was one of the problems engaging his attention then. But I have no doubt that each temporal phase constitutes its temporal horizon and sinks into the past with this horizon, in such a manner that that horizon would be *its* sense. To identify a past phase is to identify through its temporal horizon. Through overlapping of these horizons, there is a sedimentation of senses, making time-consciousness possible, just as the sense of an identical object of outer perception is constituted by the overlapping synthesis of the noemata of many different acts. 

3\. The *distinction* between prereflective and reflective consciousness appears within both pure inner experience and transcendental experience. Bodily behavior has no option of raising itself to the reflective level; it is condemned to prereflective intentionality. Reflection on it belongs to a different dimension. Within the domain of the pure (i.e. phenomenologically reduced) inner experience, the prereflective and the reflective fall apart; among other points of difference, the former is nonegological, the latter egological. Within the domain made free through transcendental reflection, an entirely new mode of subjectivity comes to light: that is, as the anonymous functioning in constitution of the pregiven life-world. 

4\. As a consequence, we have a concept of transcendental subjectivity which is largely novel. It spans the gap between reflective consciousness and prereflective constituting subjectivity. It is not the nontemporal principle of validity *(Geltung),* but a region of experience - it therefore does not ground the *truth* of the sciences, but constitutes their *sense* (as Hans Wagner has insisted). 18 It is neither the concrete subjectivity

By emphasising the structural isomorphism between consciousness philosophy and existence philosophy, I have sought to draw attention to the often forgotten situation that "the facts of the matter" in both are the same. First, both have to recognise that philosophical reflection - existential or phenomenological - is an activity which is undertaken by a human being in a historical situation. It has also to be recognised that as a reflective endeavour, philosophy is not coincident with living or existing in any of the more philosophical or even non-philosophical senses of those words. I. QUINE AND THE PSYCHOGENESIS OF REFERENCE 

Quine gives us an account of the "psychogenesis of reference." This is an account of the way we learn to refer to objects, or to speak referringly of objects. It is necessary first of all to specify what Quine means by "reference" or "referring to objects" and also by "psychogenesis." To begin with "reference," there is no one sense in which philosophers use it. One may distinguish among at least three different senses, the first two of which are the opaque and the transparent senses. In its opaque sense, it is not the case that an expression (or the occurrence of an expression) refers only if the object referred to exists. In the transparent sense, however, this implication does hold. There is no doubt that Quine'S use of "reference" is in the second, transparent sense. The principle, "Don't refer to what isn't," appears to be a piece of moralising advice but, in fact, gives expression to a theoretical commitment: the alleged reference to what is not is not genuine reference at all. Reference is not aboutness, referential opacity is only seemingly referential. Not all transparent senses of "reference" are Quinean, however. It is possible to subscribe to the transparent sense of "reference," while at the same time holding that naming may be a referring act, or that names, *qua* names, refer. One only needs a certain theory of proper names, such as Russell's (or Kripke's), to be able to hold the latter view. Now this surely is not Quine's. For Quine, reference not only is transparent but is achieved only through the logical apparatus of quantification and identity. Use of proper names does not show the existence of their objects. "Pegasus" not only may be used by persons who deny the existence of Pegasus but is in fact used in that very act of denying. The existential import is carried not by the name but by the existential quantifier - which is particularly evident in the case of, "There are unspecifiable real numbers" where the values are not objects with names. II. QUINE AND KANT: IS "OBJECTIVE REFERENCE" PAROCHIAL? 

Since Quine'S idea of objective reference hangs together closely with the regimentation of language and with the structure of formal logical theory of predication and quantification, the psychogenesis of reference amounts to the psychogenesis of that formal logical theory. It should be recalled that there is, in the tradition of Western philosophy, another theory which ties the concept of objective reference to the structure of formal logic - not, to be sure, to the apparatus of quantification, but to the interrelated structure of forms of judgment. But, unlike Quine, Kant, his precursor in this regard, would consider it perverse to speak of the psychogenesis of that innate structure. For Quine, "The whole apparatus, and with it the ontological question itself, is in this sense parochial - it is identifiable in other languages only relative to analytic hypotheses of translation which could as well have taken other lines." 13 It may be useful to pursue this point, and the contrast with Kant, a few steps further.

The nature of the objects referred to may differ from community to community; they may be material objects, spirits, gods, demons, things, events, or processes. In effect, as contrasted with the Brentano thesis, one refers to objects only in judging. The object, truly, is the object of judgment; and the forms of judgment and the underlying modes of synthesis (or categories = concepts of object in general) - although they come to their own in the intellectual achievements of Aristotelian logic and Newtonian physics - are not mental in the sense in which feelings and desires are. The Quinean requirement for objective reference is to be understood in the context of the development not of physics but of formal logic since Kant. Quantification theory, Quine writes, is less than a century old.

This over-all strategy is all the more interesting because a very similar movement of thought is to be found in so non-Skinnerian a theorist of child psychology as Piaget and in philosophers as different from Quine as Husser! and Merleau-Ponty. In the succeeding sections I propose to institute comparisons, in brief outline, of Quine's strategy with those of Piaget and H usserl with a view to isolating those elements in his thought which a cognitivist and a phenomenologist may find worth salvaging from the Skinnerian framework, and also to indicate directions in which Quine'S over-all theory needs to be supplemented. Let us begin with Quine.
