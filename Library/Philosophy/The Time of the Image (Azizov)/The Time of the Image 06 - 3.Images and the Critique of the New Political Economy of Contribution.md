## 3.	Images and the Critique of the New Political Economy of Contribution

3.1	

A New Economy and the Post-Industrial 

### 3.1	A New Economy and the Post-Industrial Proliferation

On the level of criticism, Bernard Stiegler’s theory insists on the possibility of a new economy as the response to the post-industrial proliferation. He calls this economy the contribution economy. Stiegler also insists that all kinds of knowledge, including linguistic knowledge, are now transformed into technologies and industries of language by what he calls grammatisation. Grammatisation plays an important role in the formation of contemporary consciousness and becoming. Immersed in the multimedia of an over-connected world, with time and space as we know them abolished, we no longer have ›cardinal points‹ to guide us and may even be led where we do not wish to go to. We must therefore prepare to confront new spheres of ideological control and discover new possibilities in the digital environment. Stiegler’s work in some ways has a double-sided approach, with a critical attitude on the one side and possible ways of discovering something new on the other. It is also what he theorises elsewhere as ›the pharmacy effect‹.[^138]

Jean-Luc Godard’s *Historie(s) of Cinema* is a good example of the montage of quotations. By making this film, Godard also creates the ground for the legitimacy of quoting. In the same way as modernist artists struggled for the legitimacy of the direct use of objects, rather than allusion to objects, contemporary art should be a struggle for the legitimacy of quoting.[^141] Modernism’s release of broken lines, empty canvases and bicycle wheels among many other objects created the revolution in the use of images. In the 21st century this revolution will depend on the correct use of quotations. There is in fact increasing a struggle in relation to issues of copyright. Aesthetic and conceptual reinvention of ›a new critique‹ of the »dominant tendencies of contemporary industrial techno-culture are needed capable of avoiding easy recuperation by the immense adoptive capacity of individuals« (Stiegler 2009a: 65). Adoption is in this case quoting or the citation of the other’s experience. I will return to this question below.

This new ›contribution economy‹ is theorised by Stiegler in his close examination of technics as an association, technics as something associated with other activities of human being (Stiegler 2009b: 10). Under the conditions of the global proliferation of images and the disorientation of coordinates, the problem of the correct use of technics should be crucial to any understanding. The evolution of culture is accompanied by the impact of the shifting of each other technology and technics on humanity’s memory (Stiegler 2009b: 9). This memory is above all memory of culture, because these technologies recorded everything what happened and which might be easily forgotten. Historically, while developing from the age of orthography to the age of ›informatics‹, the use of technics first aided memory and soon after became memory. Technics has replaced memory, and this is the reason of our increasing dependence on technics as recorded memory. Technologies have memorised everything what happened as temporal objects, and these objects are now assisting us in remembering.

Another reason for the necessity of this assistance is connected to the very nature of the function of technics. Technics functions in a way of enabling events of memorising to happen. This is true from the early technics of writing to the recent digital technologies. Stiegler insists that writing

Information compared to knowledge functions on value and speed, and with the loss of immediacy its value is also lost. Disorientation occurs when events are not preserved any longer in their recordings but used instantaneously for reasons of immediate convenience, as in modern broadcasting. If writing preserved memory of an event in texts and cinema recorded it in films, the media uses an event to support the current industrial need. Historically any recording of memory is also the recording of the distance of this memory, the distance of the past event from now. When we are watching films or reading books, we are aware of this distance. The media eliminates this distance and creates the ›simulation‹ of now. The report eliminates even the loss of the distance when the event is indistinguishable from the report. The loss of the distance is replaced by the speed and by the new acceleration. Together with this memory the value has also disappeared. But the difference persists. Of course, since the difference persists, there will always be a way out of this completely difficult situation. In fact, Stiegler is already proposing a useful critique and a distinct theory of the ›tertiary retention‹ of memory. I find that his theory insists on the necessity to increase attention to the already existing knowledge and to what has been preserved by memory. In the previous chapter I have demonstrated how one needs to turn to the ›recorded memory‹ for any solution. Up to now the modernity encouraged the moving forward to the future, but with the disorientation the return to a primal impulse has also become necessary. What are possibilities for critically working with images? First of all, Stiegler calls the creative industry to ›get out of the golden ghetto‹ (Stiegler/Ars Industrialis 2010: 18). It is in the article based on his lecture given in Nantes in 2010, which addresses critically the proletarianisation of culture. As I described above, proletarianisation is another name for the massification of knowledge. The knowledge in question is not based on the principles of learning or teaching but on something called ›know-how‹, knowledge based not on the education but on the words of the mouth. It is not knowledge that provides the possibility for ›maturity‹, as Kant suggested in his work on the Enlightenment (Kant 1996). For Kant learning by using the possible technics such as writing and reading is the possibility to retain knowledge, which would help to understand and therefore to provide an assistance in becoming mature. On the contrary there is a certain knowledge which prevents from becoming mature, and this sort of knowledge is a matter of the misuse of technics. Instead of maturity it creates minority, since »consumerism liquidates the desires of individuals«, which means that »the devel

### 3.3	Technics as a Remedy

Technics as a Remedy

Technics and technology, as I said from the start following Stiegler, are an ›essence‹ and are contradicting the essential nature of the contract between the human and technology. This contract is expressed in the understanding that an essence of being human is having no essence and that technology is always complementing this default. This default and non-essential being makes evolution possible.

Historically any knowledge and any technics of operating knowledge are adopted from the outside. The relationship between the inside and the outside, the internal and the external and their mutual transformability always created the possibility to spatialise the thought and to extend what is spatialised to form a new space of new technics. The role of memory in this process is undeniable, as already discussed above.

Space, time, place, and all of them in their transmutability, are vital questions in trying to locate the intelligent work, to set to work, to install philosophy in the new space created by technologies. The solution for any problem is possible, because it is known that the real is self-made. Reality itself consists of timeless, spaceless substances, whose attributes come from knowledge alone. Knowledge, before anything, is re-collection, and it receives different forms alone through its use, such as a discourse or an argument. Encoding and decoding the space created by this process is the way towards an understanding of the problem. My understanding of the notion ›placing‹, which I will explore below, derives from this kind of understanding. Bernard Stiegler didn’t explore the question of the place, because he is more concerned with the question of memory. The question of memory creates a certain spatial mechanism.[^143] I will also argue that from writing to digital technologies all the activities are becoming prominent through their being ›placed‹. It is an unfinished system, which may always be combined with other systems to create the whole and then be fragmented again. In this sense the machine has similarity to the human as they are both in default. This may also be applied to the concept of programming. In programming one has the possibility to invent on the one hand and to adjust on the other. Stiegler insists that the technical system in general is the system which can be treated as individual and object (Stiegler 1998: 28). This is because any technics that is invented is the result of knowledge. Knowledge consists of concepts, and these concepts are individual and receive their complexity by becoming structured. The system finds its full realisation in the structure through the experience.

This is why it is reasonable to say that the structure itself is through and through cinematographic. The subjective position is different only in the manner of making a choice, because what becomes the commitment shifts depending on context and time, whereas compromising is embedded in the context only.

The task of defining becomes the matter of using things according to one’s choice. But, in the age of proliferation as ours, to make the choice of one thing is not enough, because there should be the choice of something else in order to combine them together. It is not an easy task, because the choice should be the combination of different choices, and this combination is the possibility for the user to be able to say anything at all. Combining things is not simply putting together differences but involves the engagement with them, to make attempts to discover them and to learn the essence of these things.[^162]

It is even more difficult because the making of the choice became the political question, since there is an increasing internalisation of the control, which is »worse than the censorship« (Deleuze 2005). Subjects control themselves in order to play safe, not to say anything that may irritate the police. Because of this the choice of the use is restricted to specialised people. But with the emergence of new technologies there is a larger access to the use today. A good example is the use of the sharing devices of the internet, which is occupied by anyone who wants to be an ›artist‹ at least by sharing what already exists.

A New Mechanism and Grammatisation

The powerful mechanism, as powerful as, let’s say, the alphabetic ordering, that punctuates the space of knowledge and programming consciousness could be considered as such a mechanism. The alphabetic ordering is used even more increasingly today, and the use of the alphabetic ordering in the form of lists and indexes has opened up its arbitrary nature. When used to classify things in lists, the only connections between things are made by the ordering of letters. Letters of the alphabet connect things which otherwise have absolutely no connection. Lists combine different words connected to each other by the first letters of the alphabet only, often having nothing in common in terms of the meaning.

There is also the visual order, and both the alphabetic and the visual together form the basis of the digitalisation. Today the use of the digital media and its elements in the sharing devices of the internet, like YouTube, challenges the hegemony of the visual order: on the one hand there is the legitimacy of the ›techniques of the self‹ and the subjectivity, on the other hand there is the legitimacy of the subjective expression of sharing, rather than producing or consuming. What is so interesting here is the possibility to become engaged in a constant stream of knowledge and become immersed in this stream. There are two dilemmas here that should be noted for their paradoxical nature. First: although initially programmed for the access of the users of YouTube, themes are disorganised and chaotically put together according to their technical properties. Usual prejudices are abandoned here; it is purely for the use. YouTube creates the space of equality; it is a space of co-existence of all subjects equally: physics, politics, philosophy etc., visual, textual and audio, and everything else. It is also the space for the freedom of choice. The second dilemma is the manipulated user who produces metadata, the malaise of the technology.

In this way the uses of the alphabetical ordering combined with the visual one are creating both the challenge and the confirmation to the consumer society. Indexing and classifying adopted from the alphabetical ordering are forms used ubiquitously in the digital media. This kind of use of the order, where connections are made according to the form rather 

*[p. 128]* ^pg129

than meaning, ends up with the loss of the link between the subject and the substance. The complexity of the choice and the engagement is resided herein. This loss of the link between substance and meaning effectively challenges the materialist vision of the world.[^163] The confrontation between the materialist vision, which is also called an objective vision, with the subjective one helps to proliferate means, ideas and images and increases the possibility for their use.

In our times to learn the skills of the alphabetical and visual ordering, combined with the complex philosophical ideas explaining the media use, may be one of the possibilities of overcoming the crisis created by this process. The characteristic feature of the alphabetic and the visual order is that they are temporal and changing depending on the context. In this sense they are potentially helpful in restoring the lost substance, the missing subject, to grammatise the visible by combining the product of the machine with the product of the intellect. As an example, I would like to stress the possibility of combining the story with the database (Manovich 2005, 2006). Both the story and the database may exist as texts or pictures. As I explained above, the link between them is their being an expression of the subjectivity. The first group of texts is called ›database‹, and the second is called ›story‹. The database is usually ordered, regulated and classified. It reveals the structure of life on the level of its organisation; for example, the manual of the quantum mechanics, the timetable of trains, the list of names, the listing of events, the newsreels etc. are databases. These kinds of texts are usually static. If they describe the movement, then this movement is regular and correct. They are products of machines. Stories are usually describing an event which never happened before. The story is the struggle against the

### 3.9	The Case of Harun Farocki

I would like to look at Harun Farocki’s work, which in some ways is an example of what is said above. Harun Farocki describes his work as a way 

*[p. 130]* ^pg131

of documenting ›the industrialization of thought‹: the development of technologies and artificial devices that encroach on the activity of the human mind and senses. He also uses the montage in order to comment on the society, which is already saturated by commentary and where the thought is industrialised.

Farocki makes films about the industrialization of thought. In this he is also close to the thinking of Stiegler, who is actively engaged in the critique of the industrialisation of knowledge and looks at editing as an alternative. Influenced by the critical theory, which is engaged in the critique of the industrialisation of knowledge, as well as by the most critical filmmaking and other visual forms, Farocki’s work can be characterised, preciously as thinking with images.[^165] The explosion of visual means and their meaningful strata have created space from which to speak about images. It is the confrontation between the natural order, which can also be called the panoramic world, and the visual order, which can be called the discrete filmic world. On the one hand there is the machinality of perception of images surrounding us, on the other hand there is a space created by artists’ perception of this machinality. Farocki positions himself in between working like a machine and working as an artist. As a result, his work is joining two separated elements of art: work and subject. Work ends up reproducing the subject.

In a filmic reality one aspect is providing continuing transformations: here we have a separateness of images. From its inception cinema is separated from its origin, which is photography.
