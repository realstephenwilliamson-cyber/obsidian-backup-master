## About

A Sanskrit hymn of a thousand names of the goddess Lalita (Tripura Sundari), chanted as a hymn or recited name by name. Wikipedia calls the names sacred in Hinduism, especially in Shaktism. The vault's book translates the hymn with the commentary of Bhaskararaya.

- **Source and frame:** the hymn belongs to the *Brahmanda Purana*; the Leipzig University Library catalogue places it in that Purana's Uttarakhanda. In its frame story Hayagriva, a horse-headed form of Vishnu, teaches the names to the sage Agastya. Tradition credits the composition to eight goddesses of speech (Vasini, Kameshvari and others) at Lalita's command.
- **Date:** Wikipedia gives no date of composition. Leipzig holds a copy dated 1672, in which 49 opening verses of the Agastya–Hayagriva dialogue and 84 closing verses of praise surround the names.
- **Form:** 1,000 names with none repeated. Wikipedia says it avoids the filler particles (*tu*, *api*, *ca*, *hi*) common in other sahasranamas. Meditation verses (dhyana) come first; the names run from the goddess's head to her feet, and names 475–534 treat the chakras and kundalini.
- **Commentaries:** Wikipedia lists five, by Vimarshanandanatha, Vidyaranya, Bhatta Narayana, Shankara and Bhaskararaya, who draws on the earlier ones.
- **Bhaskararaya:** Wikipedia gives his dates as 1690–1785; he was born at Hyderabad to a Maharashtrian Brahmin family and later settled in Tamil Nadu. His commentary, the *Saubhagyabhaskara*, was completed in 1728. More than 40 works are attributed to him.
- **Translation:** R. Ananthakrishna Sastry's English translation of hymn and commentary had reached a revised and enlarged second edition by 1925, published at Madras by the translator (University of Pennsylvania catalogue). Wikipedia cites later printings by the Theosophical Publishing House (1970) and Gian Publishing House, Delhi (1986).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia notes that the hymn presents the goddess as performing the five cosmic acts of creation, protection, destruction, concealment and grace, which Shakta readers take to place her on a level with, or above, Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.
