## About

A dialogue by Plato on erōs. At a drinking party in the house of the tragic poet Agathon, the guests each give a speech in praise of Love; Socrates reports what Diotima taught him; and Alcibiades arrives drunk to praise Socrates.

- **Frame:** Apollodorus of Phalerum, a follower of Socrates, retells the evening to an unnamed friend. He was a boy at the time and had the story from Aristodemus, who was present, checking details with Socrates.
- **Dramatic date:** 416 BC. Wikipedia's Symposium article says the party celebrated Agathon's first victory "at the Dionysia of 416 BC" (its separate Agathon article says the Lenaia); the SEP says his tragedy had won the day before.
- **Speakers (Stephanus pages):** Phaedrus 178a; Pausanias, who separates common from heavenly love, 180c; the physician Eryximachus 186a; Aristophanes, with his myth of the original round humans split by Zeus, 189c; Agathon 195a; Socrates, reporting Diotima, 201d, including the ascent from one beautiful body to beauty itself (210a–211d); Alcibiades 214e. It ends with Socrates leaving for the Lyceum while the others sleep (223d). The whole runs over Stephanus pages 172–223.
- **Composition:** Wikipedia gives c. 385–370 BC in its lead and "no earlier than 385 BC" in its body; the IEP puts it at the start of the middle period, c. 380–360 BC. The SEP says most scholars order the erotic dialogues *Lysis*, *Symposium*, *Phaedrus*. Thrasyllus placed it in his third tetralogy and gave it the alternative title *On the Good*.
- **Text:** the principal manuscript is Codex Oxoniensis Clarkianus 39 in the Bodleian. The first printed edition came in Aldus Manutius's complete Plato, Venice, 1513.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia counts it among Plato's major works. It became the model for the symposium genre, and its readers run from Xenophon and Aristotle through Ficino's Latin translation and commentary (1484) and Shelley's translation (1818) to Kierkegaard, Freud and Lacan.
