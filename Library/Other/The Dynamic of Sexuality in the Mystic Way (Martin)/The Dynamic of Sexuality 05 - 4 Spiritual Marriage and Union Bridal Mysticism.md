## 4 Spiritual Marriage and Union: Bridal Mysticism

This chapter will situate Mechthild’s life and work within the context of the spiritual condition known as *unio mystica*, or mystical union: that which is enthusiastically sought between soul and God. This will then provide the background for exploring in some detail the text of *The Flowing Light of the Godhead*, with its manifold and profuse imageries. 

### I. Unio Mystica

From the thirteenth century access to education and literacy for women improved, especially for those of the upper and middle classes; and consequently, writings by and about women increased.[^1] During this time, many new female religious orders were granted ecclesiastical approval; for example, the Premonstratensians, Cistercians, Carthusians, Franciscans and Dominicans. Apart from these approved forms there were many varieties of less structured devotional forms of life populated by the *mulieres religiosae*, or religious women. The foremost problem for women mystics was the issue of authority; and, lacking the necessary theological background, they tended to support their claims to authority by appealing to visionary revelation. Augustine had divided the showings produced by special divine action into three ascending forms based on their relation to materiality: corporeal visions, which are naturally of the sensual grade; spiritual visions, which are images given interiorly to the soul; and intellectual visions, which constitute an immediate grasp of infallible divine truths. The visionary experiences of, especially, mediaeval female mystics tended to ‘collapse the Augustinian hierarchy . . . \[by\] meld\[ing\] all three modes of vision into direct forms of “total” conscious experience of God realized as much in and through the body as in a purely spiritual way’.[^2] For Augustine, Bernard and Eckhart the so-called intellectual visions are privileged over the other kinds, because they involve going beyond ‘images’, which are only based on the imagination, and which Platonically speaking are said to occupy an intermediate rank in the hierarchy between sense perception and intellect. As for Mechthild, most of her visions seem to fall into the traditional category of what Augustine would call spiritual, or symbolic, visions.[^3]

This ‘specular Minne’ is an irresistable force for Hadewijch, and for Mechthild is the object of intense desire as embodied in the bridegroom, Christ. In extolling her virtuous life, James sought to offer those women who might otherwise be tempted to join the Cathar heresy an acceptable alternative outlet for expressing their religious piety.[^17] His book, considered a ‘manifesto for the new form of beguine life and its mystical piety’, influenced subsequent accounts of *mulieres religiosae*.[^18] Individual beguines also found voice in their own writings; yet these ‘should not be understood as introspective self-analyses, \[but rather\] as theological and pedagogical texts geared toward a larger mixed audience’.[^19] Indeed, Mechthild’s book, *Das fließende Licht der Gottheit*, is composed of ‘poems, various forms of dialogue, aphorisms, prayers, short pieces of prose, and sequences that seem to function as commentaries on theological topics’.[^20] This diverse range suggests to Wiethaus that the texts were composed in the circumstance of oral discourse to a community.[^21]

#### Desire and Love

In an extended metaphor in Chapter 44 of Book I Mechthild ‘exquisitely describe\[s\]’ a consummated mystical desire.[^60] Accompanied by her chamberlains, the five senses, and suitably clothed in the virtues, she contrives to dance with her lover (Christ), here represented as a ‘young man’.[^61] For Mechthild it is a ‘game of love’ she plays with the divine, as seen by her reluctance to join Christ for a tryst in ‘the shade of the spring, into the bed of love’, whence he invites her, because she claims to be unworthy. The soul responds that it is as according to her nature to desire to go to God. She then relents and proceeds to her lover ‘in the secret chamber of the invisible Godhead’, where she strips from herself ‘fear and shame and all external virtues’ leaving only the interior virtues, though it is not clear what exactly these are, apart perhaps from the irreducible longing for God. This image of the mystical dance of the soul has its origins in Neoplatonism, once adopted by Dionysius the Aeropagite, and then taken into Catholic mysticism.[^62] Such fervent expressions of love here, and elsewhere, are also echoed in *bhakti* devotion, where the milkmaids earnestly desire their Lord Kṛṣṇa in the groves of Vṛndāvana.

*Elementary Images of the Divine* 

The mediaeval cosmological system classified all existing substances according to their primary attributes, and all substances of a particular elemental group were considered to possess inherent similarities. So for example, ‘heat from the sun and heat from a burning candle would both be identified as forms of the element fire; \[and\] both wind and fog would be identified as forms of the gaseous element air’.[^81] Moreover, a group of diverse elements such as water, blood, wine, milk or honey are all watery elements. Not only have they a common physical attribute, namely fluidity, but they can also be said to have a symbolic affinity as well.[^82] Only selected elementary images will be examined here.

5 Identity and Union: Tantric Mysticism 

In the previous chapter a special type of mystical endeavour, which aspires to a union of the individually separate soul with God, was considered. Love is the motivating factor for seeking this symbolic embrace of the bridal soul and bridegroom spirit (Christ). In the tantric tradition to be considered in this chapter, the individuated soul (*jīva*) aspires to an identificatory experience with God, through its own realizing endeavours. 

### I. Polar Creation

Indian philosophical thought is premised upon the concept of the universe as being in a continual process of becoming. In Vedānta there is the notion of *māyā* as *anādi bhāva rūpa* (of a form of a beginningless becoming). And in Western thought this notion has been expressed in the ‘river of time’. As Heraclitus opined, ‘one cannot enter in the same river twice’.[^1-p50] The endless cycle of creation is apparent in the work of the Hindu Trinity of Brahmā, the creator, Viṣṇu, the maintainer, and Śiva, the destroyer. In Śākta Tantrism these roles are collectively assumed by the Goddess, who is named in the Śrīvidyā tradition under study here, Lalitā Tripurasundarī. 

Theologically, Tantrism conceives of an Absolute, or Supreme One, which at the phenomenal level appears diametrically opposed; and these two, Śiva and Śakti, are representative cosmic versions of man and woman.[^2-p50] Brooks sums it: ‘\[c\]reation is understood as a process of self-expansion (*prapañca*) in which the universe is considered identical to *and* different from the Absolute. Put differently, the Absolute creates a reflection (*vimarśa*) projecting its own inherent self-illumination (*prakāśa*)’.[^3-p50] This creation, or rather emanation, is mediated by *kuṇḍalinī*, a subtle form of pervasive energy. There are some affinities with the older philosphical system of Sāṁkhya, in which the universe is said to consist of only two principles, namely matter (*prakṛti*) and pure consciousness (*puruṣa*). Metaphysically, there is a radical dualism between these two reference points; and it is the realization of their proximity, yet ultimate distinctiveness, which leads to the cessation of suffering and the round of rebirths. The real self is transcendent to the cognizing self of ‘I’ statements, and is thus beyond *prakṛti* (a concept that actually includes the Western idea of ‘mind’).[^4-p50] As with Śakti and Śiva, *prakṛti* and *puruṣa* are representative of the feminine and masculine order; and they are mutually dependent and activating principles. The binary opposites, represented principally by Śiva and Śakti, combine and so create a triadic universe which takes on the various forms of ontological being and epistemological experience that make up reality as we understand it. Śākta Tantrics conceive the universe as existing at three levels of being: everything exists in gross (*sthūla*), subtle (*sūkṣma*), or transcendent (*parā*) forms. These three levels of being take shape materially through the three *gunas* – *sattva* or goodness, *rajas* or activity, and *tamas* or darkness. The three *gunas* characterize the names and forms that take shape in the material universe (*prakṛti*). As forms of Śakti and Śiva, everything can be understood to exist in varying degrees as proportions of being (*sat*), consciousness (*cit*), and bliss (*ānanda*).[^11-p52] 

The triune nature of reality is reflected in the three aspects of tantric meditation: the *mantra*, the *yantra*, and the *cakras*. Each of these will be considered briefly. ‘Mantra’ derives from the root *man*, ‘to think’, and the suffix indicating instrumentality.[^12-p52] Mantras are words or phrases, usually in Sanskrit, that ‘\[i\]n the orthodox vedic tradition . . . have been used to evoke deities, for protection, and to magically affect the world . . . in tantric traditions they are themselves regarded as deities, or as embodying the power or energy (*śakti*) of a deity’.[^13-p52] The ‘seed syllable’ (*bīja*), or unit of sound, *auṁ*, is fundamentally important, as it is identified with absolute reality in *Taittirīya Upaniṣad*, and with the structure of the cosmos in *Maṇdukya Upaniṣad*.[^14-p52] As a string of mystic syllables mantras are empowered and imparted by the guru to the disciple, who, through recitation, verbal or mental, energizes his or her consciousness into awareness of the sounds of the universe. Moreover, it serves to establish the identity of the individual with the cosmic soul; and in the Vedānta this affirmation is given as *ahaṁ brahmāsmi*, ‘I am Brahman’.[^15-p52] 

Structures in the universe are representable in graphical form, known as *yantras*. The word ‘yantra’ derives from the root ‘yam’, meaning to sustain, hold or support the energy inherent in a particular element, object or concept; this being so, it can refer to any machine or instrument used in architecture, astronomy, alchemy, chemistry, warfare or recreation. When used in a theological sense the mystic *yantra* becomes an aid and chief instrument for meditative discipline. It is configured as a geometrical shape of certain proportions, and understood as a ‘dwelling’ or receptacle of *iśṭādevatā* (the chosen, tutelar deity); it is, again, an iconographic, rather than anthropomorphic, representation.[^16-p52] The diagram moreover is reality itself in pictorial form; and by its worship, the devotee is enabled to realize his or her identification with absolute being. The optical focus of the *yantra* is its centre, and the *devatā* image is depicted by the mathematically extensionless dot, the *bindu*. This symbolically represents the One, out of which the unity of the static (male, Śiva) and the kinetic (female, Śakti) cosmic principles expand to create the universe of matter and spirit. A basic concept in Śaiva–Śākta Tantrism is the system of *cakras* (‘circles’ or ‘wheels’)*.* The cosmos is thought to consist of seven planes of existence, from the gross through to the most subtle, and this is mirrored in the seven psychic vortices of the human body matrix (see Appendix A). *Çakti and Kuṇḍalinī* 

The word *śakti* is from the verb root *śak* (or *śakḷ śaktau*), and simply means ‘the power to produce an effect, capability, efficiency, or potency’. In the Ṛg Veda the word occurs in the sense of ‘capacity’; as *vajra*, the thunder-bolt; as *karma*, the power to act; and as the proper name of a type of weapon. II. *School of Çrīvidyā* 

Śrīvidyā, or ‘Auspicious Wisdom’, is a prominent school of Śākta Tantrism in India, and is intellectually and geographically catholic, unlike some sectarian and regional groups.[^56-p57] The grammatically feminine compound *śrīvidyā* refers to the *vidyā* of Śrī, that is, the wisdom or knowledge of the goddess (*Śrī*) who embodies auspiciousness (*śrī*). This knowledge is liberating, and bestows the wisdom that can lead to material prosperity.[^57-p57] Śrīvidyā has a volumnious canon in Sanskrit, which emerged in written form probably around the ninth century; consequently, there is a well-developed theology. The Goddess is conceived triadically as gross (*sthūla*), or anthropomorphic; as subtle (*sūkṣma*), or mantric; and as transcendent (*parā*), or yantric. These three are interpreted distinctively and characteristically as, Lalitātripurasundarī, *śrīvidyā*, and *śrīcakra*.[^58-p57]

The hallmark of tantric worship is to be found in its ritual activity, and so a short discussion on this is necessary. Lakṣmīdhara rejected completely the notion of external worship, and disdained ritual practices that otherwise broke conventions. III. *Saundaryalahar[illegible]* 

Composed of one hundred stanzas in metrical form, the *Saundaryalaharī* consists of three parts. The first part comprises stanzas one to forty-one and is known as the *Ānandalaharī*, ‘Flood of Bliss’; it is usually regarded as the most important part of the whole work. The second part comprises stanzas forty-two to ninety-one and is a eulogy of Devī’s beauty; it is for this reason often designated as the *Saundaryalaharī*, ‘Flood of Beauty’. The third part comprises stanzas ninety-two to ninety-nine and is a prayerful invocation. In stanza one hundred the poet acknowledges Devī as the inspired source for the hymn.[^99-p63] In Sanskrit, *laharī* means ‘intoxication’, and the whole poem should be understood as an appreciation of the Absolute, personified as the Goddess. Further, *ānanda* (delight) and *saundarya* (beauty) refer respectively to the Absolute as subjectively perceived and the Absolute as objectively perceived.[^100-p63] 

An illuminating description of the meaning of *Saundaryalaharī* for contemporary *tāntrikas* is given by a senior member of Guru Mandali: 

*Saundaryalaharī* is a poem extolling the praises of Devī \[i.e., Tripurasundarī\] and giving some of the details of Śrīvidyā without going into the intricacies of the *pūjā* or the theory. This is why it lacks the sort of technical descriptions you see in other texts such as *Vāmakeśvara Tantra*. We must remember that this is a *stotra*, not a Tantra, it was meant to be read with secret and special teachings included *only* in the oral tradition. Lakṣmīdhara provides many of these details. Those who view the text from the purely literary point of view will not be able to appreciate these hidden and unstated matters. In fact, we can say that those who reject that these secret or implicit teachings are actually in the text have been led purposefully to this conclusion by Śaṅkara. He wished to protect the uninitiated and leave the text open to all. He accomplished this by leaving out all the technicalities and instead using the secret and suggestive method of teaching. Those who think it is just a poem praising Devī and not an exposition of Śrīvidyā have seen only the surfaces, they appreciate merely the waves of beauty (*Saundaryalaharī*) but not the ocean of meaning.[^101-p63] As this comment admits, there is an exoteric and esoteric meaning to the *Saundaryalaharī*, as with all tantric poetry and verse. *Çrīcakra and Çrīvidyā* 

Verse 11 of *SL* refers to the *śrīcakra*, the sacred *yantra* of the Goddess: 

By reason of the four \[triangles called\] śrīkaṇṭhas and the five 

\[triangles called\] śivayuvatis,

dwelling place are evolved.[^115-p67] 

Lakṣmīdhara and his followers interpret this verse differently than the Kaulas, in two ways: by the orientation of the triangles; and by its order of construction. In the Samaya version of the *śrīcakra* the *śrīkāṇṭhas*, or Śaiva triangles, are drawn with their apex downward, while the *śivayuvatis*, or Śakti triangles, point upward. In the Kaula version the orientation of the angles is reversed: the Śaiva triangles point up, and the Śakti triangles point down.[^116-p67] (See Appendix D.) The construction of the *śrīcakra* proceeds, for Lakṣmīdhara, in a geometric progression from the *bindu* (central point) outwards; this is the so-called ‘order of evolution’ (*sṛṣṭi-krama*). For the Kaulas, the order of construction is from the outer circle moving inwards; the so-called ‘order of dissolution’ (*laya-krama*).[^117-p67]

these \[three groups\], when completed at their \[respective\] terminations 

with the three syllables hṛṅ,

#### Tripurasundarī

The second part of the *SL*, from verses forty-two to ninety-one is a meticulous encomium on the physical beauty and presence of the Goddess. Her features are described in detail: her diadem (42); the locks of her hair (43); the parting line of her hair (44); her smiling lotus-like face (45); her brilliantly shining forehead (46); her eye-brows (47); her eyes, which, as they open and close, create and dissolve the universe (48, 49, 51–57); her ears (50); her eyebrows (58); her ear-rings, which reflect in her cheeks (59); her response to the songs of Sarasvatī (60); her nose, which holds pearls created by her cool mouth (61); her red lips (62); her sweet smile in her moon–like face (63); her China rose tongue, on which the ruby-like goddess of speech sits (64); the cooling flakes of betel and camphor that drop from her mouth (65); the sweet tones of her applause (66); her matchless chin (67); her neck, which has the beauty of a stalk (68), and whose three creases define the limits of the three musical scales (69); her four arms (70); her lovely hands (71); her prominent breasts, like ruby jars of nectar, from which her sons suck (72–74); her deep navel (76); the line of abdominal hair (77, 78); her slender waist, which is burdened by overhanging breasts (79, 80); her wide hips and buttocks (81); her thighs like elephant ears (82); her shanks (83); her feet (84–90); and finally her elegant gait (91). 

She is the direct inspiration for Śaṅkara, and the milk of her breasts nourishes him:

as if from your heart 

there flowed an ocean of the milk of poesy; when the Draviḍa child tasted this as you gave it to him in compassion, he became the poet laureate of the master poets.[^123-p68]

The tantric outlook is essentially one of seeing unity; and the appearance of polarized opposites is just that – an appearance. This knowledge once realized paves the way for identifying the self (*ātman*) with *brahman*, or its reflective aspect, which in Śrīvidyā is the Goddess. The means for achieving this goal is through mantric repetition, yantric visualization, and activation of *kuṇḍalini-śakti*. Although the *SL* may not refer directly to this doctrinal outlook, it is none the less seen by its devotees as pointing to them. What is abundantly clear about the text is the function of beauty in assimilating to the divine.
