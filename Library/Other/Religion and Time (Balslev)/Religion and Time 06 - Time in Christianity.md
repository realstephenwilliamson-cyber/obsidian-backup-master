## Time in Christianity

*Peter Manchester* 

Christianity is a religion *about* time. One cannot approach the title theme therefore without some view of what is fundamentally characteristic of Christianity as such. In my view, time and Christian experience come together in a distinctive way in the Christian view of *revelation.* 

The point of entry for my effort below is the idea of revelation, as it illuminates two artifacts of earliest Christian history: the New Testament, and the Christian movement defined as liturgical communities ( defined 'eucharistically'). These communities trace themselves back to a 'last supper' and therefore to a table fellowship, that of one Jesus. The notion of a 'last' supper points to a preceding series of communal suppers, which it is very instructive to assume had already been liturgical in an on-going way, specific to the Judaism of the time. 

The reaching back implied in designating anything as 'last' has in the case of Christianity opened up a particular space of time to an astonishingly intense imaginative inspection. The period is that of the discipleship of Jesus, meaning from his baptism to his entombment. Christianity began in a sudden transfiguring movement of recollection, of recollective continuation in that discipleship, quite quickly after the end. The time of Christian revelation is not a time *after* that of the historical discipleship of Jesus, but that same time in a new light. 

The four studies below explore ( 1) the structure, (2) the intellectual context, (3) the historical origin, and (4) the distinctive contribution to the history of philosophy of the foundational Christian experience of time. A brief epilogue (5) traces the later history of Christian adaptation to other forms of temporal experience. 

I. THE TRANSFIGURATION OF TIME 

When does Jesus Christ say, "Before Abraham came to be, I am" ([[John 08#^8-58|John 8:58]])? 

How might one answer this question? The question "When?" addresses time, and time is the dimension of history. Perhaps first 

*[p. 110]* ^pg118

we should consider this as an historic text to which a date and provenance can be assigned, a text deriving from an institutional history about which we are increasingly well informed and underneath that, from the discipleship of an historical figure, Jesus son of Mary. 1 The text purports to quote thisJesus making the statement in question on a definite occasion in his actual career, hence somewhere around the year 30 of the common era. 

Yet almost certainly the historical Jesus never uttered such words. Nor did he utter many-maybe not any--of a whole cycle of statements characteristic of the way he is made to talk in the Gospel of John: speaking of the glory he had with the Father since before the foundation of the world, of his unity and equality with the Father, of his coming from and returning to the Father so that a third factor, the Holy Spirit, can come. In such discourse, almost entirely confined to this one New Testament text, the pattern of what will later become the distinctively Christian doctrine of divine 'trinity' is already prefigured. 

The anachronism of the discourse it attributes to Jesus, its embrace of speculative or 'gnostic' themes and images, and many internal indications of the historical situation in which it was produced allow historians with confidence to date the Gospel of John to sometime close to the end of the first century. And so Jesus Christ, now a spiritual ideal, a literary figure in something like 'theology fiction,' says "Before Abraham came to be, I am," in the year 100. 

But what is the relation between the Jesus who did gather and speak with disciples before his death in the year 30, and the ideal figure of the year 100? Christian faith takes them to be the same, without at the same time denying the transformation of viewpoint that separates them. Indeed far from denying it, the shift between what was apparent to his discipleship during the career of Jesus and what they proclaimed about him after his death is the central theme of the New Testament: the resurrection of Christ. Whatever one might say about the concrete character of the experiences that produced this proclamation, it is clear that they effected a radical and retroactive transformation of understanding on the part of Jesus's disciples, which is to say, they accomplished a revelation.

That time, plainly interior to the revelation from which the text means to speak, is not a breach with older traditions of Christian expression, but a central point of contact and continuity with them. Already in the synoptic gospels a generation earlier, authentic sayings and doings of the historical Jesus had begun to be adapted to express, or wholly to be overlaid by, revelations of the resurrected Christ. It used to be thought that the gnosticizing direction in which John plainly moves amounted to a 'Hellenizing' of Christianity, which at the speculative or philosophical level meant Platonizing. Platonic eternity as pure timelessness, according to the critics, cancels or at best radically distorts the primitive Christian view of time, especially time as *aion,* 'age' or 'aeon'. It seems to them unfortunate in the fashion of a very bad pun that the gospel of John induces us to use the same word to mean 'eternity'. 

In work I have done on time and eternity in Plotinus and Neoplatonism, I have been struck by two anomalies in the picture of 'Platonic eternity' this criticism presupposes. 

First, the full concept of eternity as we know it from Augustine and Boethius is not demonstrable in the Platonic tradition before Plotinus. The classic place in Plato, *Timaeus* 37D where time is made to be "an image of eternity, moving according to number," states that it is with respect to presence alone, not pastness or futurity, that time can be said to be. III. CHRIST IN TIME 

I assume that the entire public career of Jesus son of Mary lasted less than a year. I tend to represent it to myself in liturgical time; from about one Yorn Kippur, say, to the following Passover. I discount the three years suggested by the three trips to Jerusalem recounted in the Fourth Gospel. The first one it mentions Uohn 2: 13) is the one where Jesus acts in anger in the temple, the one fatal trip to Jerusalem of the synoptic narrative. The passion narrative in John does not then ensue until chapter 18. Chapters 3 through 17 take place, I am supposing, in a kind of interpolated time, which tracks not the career of Jesus but that of the community of the gospel, eucharistically identified with the risen Christ. [^10] In my image, to have been a member of Jesus's entourage was to 

have been jolted into astonishment and perplexity-along with fear-by the *suddenness* of his arrest, crucifixion, and burial in Jerusalem. One had just begun to grasp the *size* of what was going on, and crash, he was crucified, died, and was buried. "What just happened here? What was that all about? Did you follow what he said the time when ... .?" 

Jesus spoke enigmatically. He used parables in a provocative way and seems *not* to have taken his disciples backstage to amplify or allegorize upon them. He was given to challenging one-liners, and to a highly original discourse using the terms and images of the apocalyptic literature, notably its 'son of man'. He was not at all easily understood the first time round. From the beginning, comprehension of Jesus has been *retrospective,* however immediate the initial consultations with memory will have been. 

And of course, quite quickly after the initial seizure of surprise and dismay, come the revelations ofresurrection, an understanding that is not just retrospective but retroactive. That transfigures memory. 

No critical reflection on the scope of this transfiguration can be plausible that does not acknowledge that the memories in question were initially those of Jesus's actual companions and disciples. IV. TIME IN CHRISTIANITY 

In centering these reflections in the problem of revelation, asking about the temporal structure of the revelation the New Testament purports both to draw from and exemplify, I have deliberately directed attention toward what I called the 'inside' of time and away from another aspect of time much discussed in relation to Christianity, what I would call the 'epic' dimension. 

This is the dimension in which biblical 'salvation history' unfolds. Genesis: creation, the patriarchs, the calling of Abraham; Exodus; Moses, the Exodus, the giving of the Law; Christ: the coming of Christ as fulfillment of the Law and of the promises to Abraham; Church: the life of faith, hope, and love 'between the times'; Apocalypse: the second coming of Christ as eschatological judge, the parousia and consummation. 

Biblical imagination does join historical time and mythical time in a characteristic and philosophically influential way. It moves from the entirely mythical time of creation's seven days, through the still cosmogonic time of the mighty deeds of the Lord in Genesis, in which the stories (Noah and the flood, the tower of Babel, etc.) begin to be at least ethnographically instructive given the visibility of their derivation from older myth-cycles, into the legendary times of Abraham and the patriarchs, Moses and the Exodus, and finally, from about the triumph of kingship and the Jerusalem cult in Canaan, into times we can coordinate with 

*[p. 127]* ^pg135

archaeologically and philologically constructed chronology. The transition is so smooth, the horizons of mythical time and historical remembrance stretch across one another so far into one another's domains and modes of exhibition, that still today the critical decoupling demanded by historical consciousness remains widely unattempted in the cultures shaped by biblical religion. 

At the other end from Genesis of the mythical history that this technique produces, especially well-developed in Christianity, lies the End *(eschaton,* whence eschatology). The function of eschatology has been to project time in the horizon of the future, by which I mean, to consider how time lets the future appear in the present. This of course can be understood in various ways. Does it do so in the mode of simple expectation-some Thursday afternoon, say, and soon, the Lord will appear in the air? Overshadowed by the future in this mode, the present is full of anxious but electrified preparations. It lives the apocalyptic mood of paranoid exhilaration: "It's us! We're the ones who get to see!" Once Christianity got past the first couple of 'ends of the world' that were concrete in this fashion (the death of Jesus, the destruction of Jerusalem) and began to realize that in some permanent way the Last Judgment has still not come, individual death began to take over as the end of the world most relevant to eschatology. [^16]Eternal life as afterlife began to detach from its original apocalyptic context, the *aion* to come with its resurrection of the body, its new heaven and new earth, and to become mainly a matter of individual and personal survival, finally merging with Platonic ideas about restoration of the soul to contemplative union with the divine in the Platonic *aion,* eternity. Individuated in this way, the question of the bearing of the future on the present was interiorized, detached from historical experience, producing in the end the mysticism and otherworldliness ascribed to medieval Christianity. 

Efforts to recover the worldly, political-historical context of original apocalyptic imagination are so important in contemporary Christian theology, [^17]and build so productively on a century's work

In order to translate this argument into the terms of temporal problematic directly, we must appreciate why the unifying and transformative act of the will is love. Will is *voluntas* in Latin, the voluntary. The problem of freedom that is authentically Christian in Augustine has nothing directly to do with another problem, a perennial philosopher's favorite to whose discussion Augustine also contributes, 'freewill' or better free choice, *liberum arbitrium.* The issue in *Confessions* and *The Trinity* is not 'choosing' but *having a* will-wholeheartedness. What Augustine believes he comes to in the exercise of recourse to memory in *Corifessions* is the love that was all along the true will operative in him, quite apart from his own conscious intentions at the time. That love he confesses to be God's gift, not his own doing. And it is love that has the future. 

Augustine reasons like this: We only know as we remember. But we only remember as we love. His illustration of the priority of the future with the love that comes from God and goes to God brings us to his deepest discoveries about time and temporality in Christian experience. The priority of the future in Christianity is expressed not in some turning from the past toward the future, and certainly not in any dismissal of the present in favor of an expected utopia, but in the power of the future, as advent of God, to transfigure the past and from that reveal in the µresent the resurrected life of Christ. 

It is to the *temporal structure* of this characteristic Christian way of speaking from within the revelation that I want to draw attention. I know best to make this clear from a temporal interpretation of the triad, taken from Paul, traditionally called the 'three theological virtues': faith, hope, and love.

Faith is the relation to the past, to things done in time to which one is related by birth and tradition and gift. It is a mode of memory, and like memory is foundational, the farthest thing from merely 'entertaining as true'. 

Hope is the relation to the present. It is a mode of understanding, directed to the present situation, grasping what is promising in it. If there is a component of expectation in hope, this is because the time that continues, into which planning and expectation project themselves, is itself a structure of the present, not the phenomenon of the future at all. Efforts to fashion a theology of the future as a 'theology of hope', popular as they have been, systematically blind themselves to the authentic and original future so distinctive in Christian experience of revelation and time. Hope is understanding or it is wishful thinking. Christian hope is not a species of optimism. 

It is love to which the future belongs, and the reverse, the future belongs to love. The question that opens to the future is, what is worthy of love? What in the present situation, entered in faith, understood with hope, elicits and confers the wholehearted self-gift that is love? 

To this the most certainly authentic of the parables of Jesus speak. It is like the little bit of leaven, from which the whole loaf takes rise ([[Matthew 13#^13-33|Matthew 13:33]] = [[Luke 12#^12-31|Luke 12:31]]). It is like the mustard seed, now the smallest of seeds, but look again, the birds of the air are roosting in its branches ([[Mark 04#^4-31|Mark 4:31-32]], cf. Thomas 20:2). 

I argued in the previous section how there grew around Jesus, in a few short months cut short by catastrophe, not just a discipleship but a table fellowship. At this table the word of God as embraced by Jewish faith began to hint at an extraordinary promise. From the point of view of any possible expectations or laying of plans, this movement was hopeless and doomed-as events quickly made clear. But the love by which God's word was begotten from the Father did not enter time in the same way as plans and expectations. Like the future itself, it completed itself around behind the back of time. The transfigured past, the resurrected Christ, were revealed. 

V. EPILOGUE: CHRISTIANITY IN TIME 

I have shown from work of Augustine and Kierkegaard how the Christian experience of temporality could give rise to philosophical thinking of originality and influence. This is by no means equivalent to tracing the history of Christian contributions to the philosophy of time. Other powerful modes of temporal experience have absorbed the Christian experience back into themselves, and some 

*[p. 134]* ^pg142

of these hybrids have been at least as influential as the distinctive mode I have tried to characterize. 

Two in particular deserve brief review: in interior or mystical theology, a dissolving of futural temporality back into the pure presence-eternity of late Platonism; in theology of history, a reversion to covenant-history or 'salvation history' based on Old Testament models of how the divine becomes a factor in time. 

The first is the more difficult to analyze, because as I have emphasized Platonic eternity is a temporal phenomenon, not abstract timelessness. One must not overstate this denial of an eternal aspect to historical finitude, especially in Plotinus. It is not a Vedantist all-self or Buddhist no-self position: he expressly teaches that souls remain individual in eternal contemplation. But in fact he means by this only a kind of naked plurality, whose joy is not the consummation of personal uniqueness or vocation, but a kind of multiple concentricity in which each soul, as center of the whole intelligible world, is in interior communion with every other. 

From Plotinus evolves a beguiling and important mysticism, made progressively more theological through Iamblichus into Proclus, then Christianized in pseudo-Dionysius and widely influential in the Middle Ages, coming finally to a kind of head in Meister Eckhart. Even Christian writers as deeply and programmatically based in Augustine as Boethius and Bonaventure no longer understand interiority in a fully temporal way, but rather on the model of Platonic contemplation. Thomas Aquinas expressly denies that the Augustinian temporal triad, memory, understanding, will, are an image of the divine life in anything but the most figurative and preliminary way. [^27]

While in this way the interior or what we today call 'existential' appropriation of the temporal structure of revelation was absorbed into Platonic contemplative mysticism, the exterior or historical aspect gave way to what I earlier called "epic" salvation-history. Again the pivotal figure is Augustine, here in *The City of God.* The theoretical view of time achieved in this work is of course linear, "once-for-all" time, the dimension in which the career of human freewill is configured by divine providence to be the story of the final triumph of the 'City of God'. Rejecting fatalism and eternal recurrence, Augustine conclusively embraces for Christian imagination what is originally the attainment of Old Testament Covenant theology, as integrated into historical experience by the Hebrew prophets: a finite, one-way drama beginning in a singular and absolute creation, drawn by epochal stages into a more and more explicit manifestation ofa kingdom of God whose final consummate realization will mark an equally singular and absolute end of history.

Re-absorbed in this way into Covenant experience, the Christian epic became of course the principal channel through which biblical historicism became so important a source for the modern European conception of history. The apocalyptic experience by which Christianity was marked in its origins nevertheless continued to potentiate new outbreaks of the distinctive temporality I have described. Alongside ever-recurrent efforts to read events of mundane history in a literal way as portents of the End, the Middle Ages produced proponents of an apocalyptic spirituality in which the experience of time as the interiority reached by revelation was reawakened.
