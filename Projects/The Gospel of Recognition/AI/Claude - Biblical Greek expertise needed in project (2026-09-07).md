**1. Does John keep "fulfillment" and "recognition" in separate vocabularies?**

The Gospel relates events to scripture two ways. One uses an explicit formula — *so that the scripture might be fulfilled* (ἵνα πληρωθῇ, once τελειωθῇ) — and these cluster in the passion: the soldiers dividing the garments (19:24), *I thirst* (19:28), the unbroken bone (19:36), *they will look on the one they pierced* (19:37). The other reports the disciples understanding something only afterward, with no formula at all:

- 2:22 — when he was raised, his disciples remembered that he had said this
- 12:16 — his disciples did not understand these things at first, but when he was glorified they remembered that these things had been written about him
- 20:9 — for they did not yet know the scripture, that he must rise from the dead

The claim I'm building on: recognition is never said to fulfill scripture, but misrecognition is — 12:38–40 puts the Isaiah quotations about unbelief and blinded eyes under a fulfillment formula. The theological payoff is that the resurrection changes nothing in the text and only produces a vantage from which it can be read, which is a strong position against supersessionism.

**Ask:** run the distinction exhaustively; is 12:38–40 a genuine counterexample, and does the pattern survive 18:9 and 18:32, where the formula is applied to Jesus's own earlier words rather than to scripture?

---

**2. Can the absolute ἐγώ εἰμι bear the weight I'm putting on it?**

The unpredicated "I am" — 8:24 (*unless you believe that I am, you will die in your sins*), 8:28, 8:58 (*before Abraham came to be, I am*), 13:19, and the arrest party falling backward at 18:5–6. My criterion is that nothing stands in front of the "I": it is not owned or attributed by the speaker. Relevant comparanda are [[Isaiah 43#^43-10|Isaiah 43:10]] LXX rendering *ani hu*, and the Synoptic warnings that impostors will come saying *I am* ([[Mark 13#^13-6|Mark 13:6]], [[Luke 21#^21-8|Luke 21:8]]).

**Ask:** is this a defensible grammatical observation about Johannine Greek, or only a feature of John's composition? The distinction matters because I use the criterion Christologically while elsewhere treating stylistic elegance as evidence of redaction, and I need to know whether that's inconsistent.

---

**3. Is ἀφ' ἑαυτοῦ / ἀπ' ἐμαυτοῦ a technical idiom in John?**

The recurring refusal of self-derivation: *the Son can do nothing from himself* (5:19), *I can do nothing from myself* (5:30), *whether I speak from myself* (7:17), *I do nothing from myself* (8:28), *I have not come from myself* (8:42).

The verse that matters most is 11:51, where the narrator says Caiaphas *did not say this from himself*, but prophesied as high priest that year. Also 18:34, where Jesus asks Pilate whether he says this *from himself*. My reading of the priests and Pilate as acting in good faith depends on the construction at 11:51 being the same one that marks divine speech everywhere else.

**Ask:** identical idiom or ordinary Greek coincidence? If the latter, a chapter needs rebuilding.

---

**4. Is χωρέω a deliberate thread?**

Three occurrences: the stone jars at Cana *holding* two or three measures each (2:6); *my word makes no room in you* (8:37); and the closing hyperbole that the world itself could not *contain* the books that would be written (21:25). The verb means to make room for, to have capacity — not merely to hold spatially. If John is using it consistently, capacity rather than content is the limiting factor throughout, which is the structure my whole argument runs on.

**Ask:** two things — a lexical judgment on whether "make room for" is live in all three, and a literature check on whether anyone has connected them. This is the cleanest philological point in the project and the one most likely to be original.

---

**5. Are the advocate and the accuser one function?**

At 5:45 Jesus says *do not think that I will accuse you to the Father; your accuser is Moses, in whom you have hoped* (ὁ κατηγορῶν). Moses is Israel's paradigmatic intercessor, so the advocate has become the accuser without changing anything about Moses — the change is in the standpoint receiving him.

Against that, the παράκλητος passages (14:16, 14:26, 15:26, 16:7), where the same figure *will convict the world concerning sin* (16:8), and the sin is specified as not believing in him (16:9). [[1 John 02#^2-1|1 John 2:1]] uses παράκλητος of Jesus before the Father.

**Ask:** does the semantic range of παράκλητος support a single function differentiated by reception, and has anyone published this identification?

**6. Is the singular/plural alternation of ἁμαρτία doing theological work?**

At 8:21, *you will die in your sin*, singular. Three verses later at 8:24, *you will die in your sins*, plural — and the plural is resolved by a single condition, *unless you believe that I am*. Then 9:41, at the close of the blind man's story: *if you were blind you would have no sin; but now you say "we see," so your sin remains*. And 16:9 again defines sin as unbelief.

I want to say one sin, a failure of recognition, generates the many. Bultmann's unbelief-as-sin is adjacent but he arrives existentially rather than through 9:41.

**Ask:** is the alternation meaningful or just Johannine stylistic variation? If it's style, I have to reformulate rather than defend.

**7. Does the [[Psalms 082#^82-1|Psalm 82]] argument require the predicate to be true?**

[[John 10#^10-34|John 10:34–36]], answering the charge of blasphemy: *Is it not written in your law, "I said, you are gods"? > [!quote] Me
> As far as Jesus the Nazorean. I have read a theory that the reason Jesus is remembered as being associated with Nazareth is because he was a Nazarite by oath. What do you think of the idea?

The theory has exactly one good argument and several bad problems, and it's worth knowing which is which because the good argument is genuinely good.

**The point in its favor is the zeta.** Nazirite is נָזִיר, from the root n-z-r, with zayin. Nazareth is נָצְרַת, with tsade. The Caesarea priestly-courses inscription confirms the tsade for the town. In Greek transliteration tsade normally comes out as sigma (Zion צִיּוֹן → Σιών, Sidon → Σιδών), while zayin gives zeta. So Ναζωραῖος / Ναζαρηνός with a zeta is phonologically odd for Nazareth and entirely natural for *nazir*. That anomaly is real, it's old, and it's why the theory keeps resurfacing rather than dying. The usual answers involve Aramaic dialect phonology or a voiced allophone of the tsade, and they're plausible without being decisive.

**Everything else runs against it.** A Nazirite abstains from wine and from corpse contact (Numbers 6). Jesus is remembered doing both, in the tradition's own words: *the Son of Man came eating and drinking, and they say, look, a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners* ([[Matthew 11#^11-19|Matt 11:19]] / [[Luke 07#^7-34|Luke 7:34]]), stated as a contrast with John the Baptist. And [[Mark 14#^14-25|Mark 14:25]], *I will not drink again of the fruit of the vine until that day*, presupposes that he had been. On corpses: he takes Jairus's daughter by the hand, touches the bier at Nain, and stands at Lazarus's tomb.

Meanwhile the Gospels do give us a Nazirite, and it isn't Jesus. [[Luke 01#^1-15|Luke 1:15]] says of John that he will drink no wine or strong drink. If the epithet marked the vow, the tradition attached it to the wrong man.

**Two better competitors for the etymology.** Both share Nazareth's tsade, which the Nazirite proposal doesn't:

- *netzer*, branch — [[Isaiah 11#^11-1|Isaiah 11:1]], *a shoot from the stump of Jesse, a branch from his roots*. [[Matthew 02#^2-23|Matthew 2:23]] says *he shall be called a Nazorean*, attributes it to "the prophets," and no such text exists. A pun on *netzer* is the most economical explanation of that verse.
- *notzrim* / n-ṣ-r, to keep or observe. This is the root behind the Mandaean *naṣoraia* and behind the later Hebrew and Arabic terms for Christians. It also handles [[Acts 24#^24-5|Acts 24:5]], where Paul is called a ringleader of the sect of the Nazoreans, since a sect name built on "observants" transfers to followers more naturally than a personal vow does.

**Where I'd land:** the epithet is probably geographic in origin and then exploited. Matthew clearly hears *netzer* in it. Whether anyone in the first century heard *nazir* is unfalsifiable, and the behavioral evidence makes it unlikely as the source.

**For your commentary, though, the interesting thing is what John does with the name.** He uses the ambiguous form Ναζωραῖος, not Mark's straightforwardly geographic Ναζαρηνός, and he uses it at exactly two places. First at 1:46, where Nathanael says *can anything good come out of Nazareth?* — an objection that is correct on its own terms and reaches the wrong thing, which is your pattern. Then at 18:5–7, where the arrest party says they are looking for *Jesus the Nazorean* and receives ἐγώ εἰμι in reply, twice, and falls backward. The place-name identification is met by the unattributed I. And then it goes onto the placard.

So John has the epithet functioning as the name under which he is *not* recognized, answered each time by the name under which he is. That's usable whatever the etymology turns out to be, which is a good position to be in, since the etymology isn't going to be settled.
