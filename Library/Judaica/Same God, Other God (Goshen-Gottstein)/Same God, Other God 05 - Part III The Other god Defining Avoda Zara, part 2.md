One might consider whether worship applies properly to Brahman and whether the Shituf construct does justice to the various theological views of Hinduism. But even if this is not an appropriate appreciation of Hinduism on its own terms, it does provide a mechanism for viewing the religion, considered broadly as a single entity, in a way that takes it out of the bounds of Avoda Zara for its non-Jewish practitioners. 

 Even more explicit than Rabbi Herzog is a present-day authority who is, in fact, the only rabbinic author to have discussed Hinduism with some degree of familiarity with its doctrines. While the discussion is not extensive, it at least is based on an attempt to portray Hinduism in its own terms. The following passages by Rabbi Adin Steinsaltz, a leading Talmud scholar, show us how a contemporary halachic perspective on world religions could be constructed. We are able to follow the moves made by Rabbi Steinsaltz and to see how each in turn addresses the fundamental considerations that have informed our discussion up to this point. The sum total of his discussion is, as a respondent to his piece correctly observed, extraordinary, if not absolutely exceptional. <sup>13</sup> As the respondent continues, while making no concessions to modern liberalism or even ecumenism, and while characteristically identifying his position with that of the Talmud, Rabbi Steinsaltz reassesses current world religions, including the various forms of Hindu and Buddhist religions, as adequately monotheist, adequately nonidolatrous, and at least adequately ethical to qualify as compliant with the noachide laws. Rabbi Steinsaltz’s article represents an approach so open-minded that it would not be followed by more than a few orthodox rabbis currently. With these words of introduction, let us consider the teachings of Rabbi Steinsaltz, and how they relate to the present discussion of Avoda Zara, Shituf, and a Jewish view of Hinduism. <sup>14</sup> Steinsaltz begins by acknowledging the changed nature of contemporary reality; interactions that are presently possible between Jews and non-Jews are fundamentally different from those of any previous era in Jewish history. This poses the challenge of religious tolerance, a term that Steinsaltz is not enamored with. His charge is to find a way of accommodating other religions from a Jewish perspective. Significantly, he speaks of “recognizing” other religions. The choice of language is worthy of note. It implicitly appeals to the language of diplomacy and to ways in which states recognize each other’s legitimacy. Can Judaism recognize other religions, and how does that relate to its monotheistic faith and its attendant truth claims? Steinsaltz seeks to create a model that would allow Judaism to recognize other world religions. In order to appreciate Rabbi Steinsaltz’s suggestion we should recall that the standard view of Judaism’s message to the world are the seven noachide commandments. <sup>15</sup> Now, Steinsaltz’s presentation takes as its departure point the existence of two tracks to spiritual reality, to heaven—the Jewish one, which is the more stringent one, consisting of 613 commandments, and the one meant for the rest of the world, known as the noachide path. The noachide path serves as the yardstick for assessing world religions and for determining what is demanded of them. This includes the expected standard of God-knowledge and how it coheres with the demands on purity of approach to God, as these are made of Jews. 

*[p. 85]* ^pg92

 Steinsaltz bases his argument on the broader recognition that law is not a universal phenomenon, and that it applies to different groups in the community in different measures:

 The idea that certain laws of Judaism do not apply to all is an essential feature of the *halakhah* . Special standards of religious practice apply to men, while women are exempted from all commandments that must be practiced at a fixed time. The people of Israel are not bound by the special obligations incumbent upon the priesthood: *kohanim,* the descendants of Aaron, must keep from contact with the dead outside their immediate families in order to preserve the ritual purity of the priesthood. And the priesthood is not bound by the same rules of purity that must be observed by the high priest, who cannot attend the funeral of even his own parents and children. The high priest would not think to censure his fellow priests for attending their parents’ funerals; a common priest, a *kohen* , would not think to censure an ordinary Jew for attending the funeral of a friend, teacher, or cousin (indeed an ordinary Jew might be censured for not attending). Different standards apply to different groups even within the Jewish community. The Noahide laws operate on the same principle: differing standards apply to different groups. <sup>16</sup> 

 To this must be added a deeper philosophical understanding that respects the multiplicity of religions. In addition to the appeal to the different paths for Jews and non-Jews, Steinsaltz makes a more radical claim that touches upon Judaism’s future vision and ultimate hope. Judaism’s ultimate vision does not, according to Steinsaltz, consist of all of humanity adopting Judaism: 

 Judaism, despite the absolute and exclusionary quality of its monotheism, has a side that tends toward openness and toleration. This side of Judaism has also an expression in the Jewish abstention from proselytizing. Even ultimately, Judaism does not view itself as the religion of all people. It is the religion of the Jews alone and is, for almost all its practitioners, inherited. The assumption that Judaism is the religion of one people (and a few unsought converts) is emphatically a normative principle and is important to our discussion because it suggests that within Jewish doctrine there is room for the religious beliefs of others. 

 This principle applies not only to the world as it is today, but also to the messianic projections that Judaism makes for the future. Although the messianic era represents an ultimate vindication of truth as Judaism understands it—a time when the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob will assert his dominion over all the world—at that time the peoples of the world will not embrace Judaism and will not come to observe Jewish law. In the closing chapters of his monumental Code of Jewish Law, Maimonides gives an account of the end of days. In his portrayal, the messianic realm is one of peace, but not uniformity of faith. According to Maimonides, when Isaiah saw the wolf and the lamb lying down together, what he envisioned was not a change in the nature of creation. Wolves will still be wolves, and lambs will be lambs; what will change is the relationship between them. At the end of days, the different peoples of the world will not become less different. And because they will not embrace a single faith, the prohibition against gentiles undertaking distinctively Jewish practices will continue. However, each religion will come to share with all the others a small set of fundamental truths, and people everywhere will abandon violence, theft, and oppression. <sup>17</sup> 

*[p. 86]* ^pg93

 This is a stunning statement. Abstention from proselytizing is not seen as a consequence of political circumstances but as a fundamental characteristic of Judaism, which is supported by its messianic vision. As Maimonides’ description of the messianic era does not include a description of Judaism prevailing, Steinsaltz concludes that all religions will remain in the messianic era and that their relationships will be harmonious and characterized by mutual exchange and enrichment, or at least the sharing of a common core of moral and spiritual truths. <sup>18</sup> 

 With these foundations in place, Steinsaltz moves on to develop the notion of different approaches to God that characterize the Jewish track and the noachide track: 

 “Toleration” would not be an accurate name for this doctrine, and certainly the doctrine is not one of religious equivalence. However, the approach that Judaism takes toward righteous gentiles offers a partial solution to the problem of intolerance in monotheist religions. By establishing different sets of expectations for different groups, Judaism makes room for adherents of other faiths to perform their own religious obligations in a way that entitles them to salvation by the God of Israel. While Jews are enjoined to follow 613 commandments of the Torah, the demands that normative Judaism makes of gentiles comprise only seven laws. These six prohibitions and one positive commandment are together known as the Noahide laws because (according to chapter seven of Sanhedrin) they were the series of laws given to Noah after the f lood (though they differ little from the basic laws given to Adam). The Noahide laws set a universal standard for gentile religions and embody the truths that, according to Maimonides, the peoples of the world will come to recognize and share at the end of days. Thus, the Noahide laws delineate the boundaries of Jewish religious toleration: failure to observe these laws would bar a person or a people from entering their own gate into heaven. One of the highest principles of the Noahide laws is belief in the one God. Both Islam and Christianity (though trinitarian doctrine presents a complication) satisfy this key demand and clear the way for Jewish recognition of these religions. It is an entirely normative principle in Judaism that the monotheism expected of gentiles by the Noahide laws is of a less absolute kind than that expected of Jews. In the Middle Ages, many authorities indeed recognized Christian doctrine (even the doctrine of the Trinity) as basically monotheistic belief. One can readily understand how the doctrine of a triune Godhead could contaminate Christianity’s claim to be monotheistic. However, Christianity was generally not considered polytheistic or idolatrous, though Maimonides—who did not live in Christendom—dissented from the widespread rabbinic agreement on this point. The concept of the Trinity was represented in the church as a mystery or paradox because it apparently contradicted a central component of their faith in the one God. Thus the Trinity, even though it is an essential feature of Christian theology and not merely one of folk religion, could be taken by Jewish scholars as a supplement to, rather than a replacement for, the idea of God as one. By Jewish standards as applied to Jews, trinitarianism is not monotheism. But by the standards of the Noahide laws, the doctrine of the Trinity is not an idolatrous belief to which Judaism can express an objection. <sup>19</sup> 

 Steinsaltz first establishes the rule that expectations differ for Jews and non-Jews. Not only is the number of commandments different, but what is actually 

*[p. 87]* ^pg94

expected of Jewish believers is different from what is expected of non-Jewish believers, even in relation to the very same commandment. Thus, the demand to worship one God alone may be interpreted and applied differently to Jews and to non-Jews. I believe this statement is unique. It grows out of a lengthy tradition of Ashkenazi dealing with Christianity and the development of a de facto lower standard for non-Jews, captured in the recognition that a noachide is not commanded to avoid worship through Shituf. However, I am not familiar with any prior statement that grounds this view of the noachide’s theological obligations in a broader view of Judaism’s relationship with world religions and its future hopes as well as in a theory of how the noachide laws function as a code of law and their relationship to what is expected of Jews. The statement is not only novel in terms of the broader context that it offers, in trying to make sense of a tradition that existed in practice for hundreds of years, but also in terms of how Rabbi Steinsaltz grounds it. He presents this view as the normative view, indeed as a majoritarian view. He thus turns the principle permitting Shituf for the non-Jew into Judaism’s representative statement. 

 For all its thoroughness and for how systematic it is, this statement does rewrite the history of Jewish law. Indeed, the view that Shituf is permissible to the non-Jew does create parallel tracks, while maintaining a hierarchy that affords Judaism the superior spiritual status. But this view is far from representative. The debate still rages today as to whether Shituf is permissible for a non-Jew or not. More significantly, the Maimonidean position may well be the majority position in relation to Christianity. <sup>20</sup> Steinsaltz casts Maimonides into a minority position and even hints at historical factors that may have led to his not understanding Christianity sufficiently: he never lived among Christians. <sup>21</sup> This is itself an interesting argument, the likes of which is rarely heard in halachic circles. Perhaps what is most interesting is that Steinsaltz develops a view that is based on Shituf, without ever appealing to the term. In fact, in note 6, where he offers his justification for his theory, he quotes Meiri, to whom we shall turn following the present discussion. Now, Meiri is outside common consensus and certainly cannot provide the basis for such a far-reaching view being considered majoritarian, even if one resonates with his views. Meiri does not build on Shituf, but on another logic, to be presented in a later chapter. Apparently, Rabbi Steinsaltz does rely on Meiri, leading him to avoid reference to Shituf, but the theological construct he presents is that of Shituf. In any event, he has taken a disputed, likely a minoritarian, view and constructed a broad theory, presented as Judaism’s representative message that accommodates lower religious understanding within a hierarchical view. Conceptually, this is really an inclusivist move, one that allows him to include lower forms of approaching God within a framework of legitimacy. 

 One of the arguments contained in Steinsaltz’s presentation relates to the question of who speaks for the religions. Religions, as we have already seen in the case of Hinduism, are complex entities. Steinsaltz’s discussion proceeds in terms of unified wholes, upon which the halacha must pronounce its judgment. This gets him into the question of what is essential in a religion and what is secondary. His decisions may not be shared by the scholar of religion or the theologian, 

*[p. 88]* ^pg95

but they are important both in terms of raising these questions <sup>22</sup> and in terms of the positions he adopts, which are important in and of themselves. Steinsaltz’s answer is clear. It is theologians who speak for the religions. <sup>23</sup> 

 Steinsaltz seems to assume that there is a core religious teaching that one can identify. This is identified with the higher form of the religion, presumably as preached by theologians or the officials of the religion, as distinct from what he terms “folk” religion. Thus, he recognizes that under the name of a given religion we may encounter a variety of phenomena. However, the halachic judgment is made upon the essence of the religion. Thus, there seems to be one pronouncement per religion, rather than multiple rulings, depending on the specific form or practice under discussion, and it is this one ruling that determines Judaism’s fundamental attitude to that religion. It is this strategy that will allow Steinsaltz to make his breakthrough statement in relation to Hinduism. 

 To dwell for a moment longer on the notion of religion in essence and in manifestation, it is worth noting the following passage in his article:

 Judaism does not concern itself with the internal heresies of other religions even when these have arisen to defend standards of monotheism to which Judaism itself adheres. The struggles over iconoclasm, in the seventh century, struggles that touched the core of Christian monotheism, left no mark on Jewish writing about Christianity in the period; and the Jewish attitude toward Christianity was unaffected by the emergence of the Protestant sects in the sixteenth century, some of which have practices that might be said to resemble those of Judaism more closely than do those of the Catholic Church . . . And Judaism has likewise nothing to say about the Sunni Wahabi struggle to purify Islam of folk practices (for example, grave worship) that contradict the basis of Islamic monotheism. . . . Judaism is not affected by these internal struggles because they are recognized to occur beyond the boundaries of Jewish law. <sup>24</sup> 

 The truth is that Judaism does not concern itself with all these details because Jews have been either insular or conservative in their approach to other religions. They have indeed shown little interest, because interreligious relations have not been as vital as they have become today. Steinsaltz, in a brilliant move, stands this historical reality on its head, and offers it as proof for Judaism’s judgment concentrating only on the essential. Details do not seem to matter in this scheme. Once a fundamental decision has been reached, it remains valid, notwithstanding different strands within the religion or the history of transformations within the religion. 

 This allows us to move to his presentation of Indian religions: 

 What about indic religions and various kinds of Buddhism. Again, I do not believe that a definitive solution is possible, but a partial solution may be considered. It is important to introduce a distinction between theology and religious practice. In the ancient religions grouped under the name of Hinduism, there are many gods and local shrines, but the theological principles that guide belief and provide a uniformity of moral standards assume that all the deities revered in India or elsewhere are forms of, expressions of, or names for, one ultimate reality or God. Saivites 

*[p. 89]* ^pg96

propose Siva as the best name (among many names) for this ultimacy; Vaisnavites prefer Visnu or Krishna; *atman* is an Upanisadic word for the same principle—and Brahman is perhaps the most common way among non-Muslim, non-Christian Indians of naming ultimacy. 

 As for Buddhism, the difficulty is not that there is a plethora of gods, though Siddhartha Gautama and other buddhas, bodhisattvas and “incarnate” lamas are often treated as godlike. The difficulty, from the perspective of the Noahide laws, is that it is unclear whether Buddhism is theistic at all. Buddhist thinkers tend to argue that metaphysical beliefs are among the causes of human suffering. . . . Still, it is not necessarily atheistic to conclude that because holding metaphysical beliefs leads to pain, it is best to concentrate our attention on proper human behavior. In any case, however controversial the question of whether Buddhism is theistic, it is certainly not *poly* theistic. 

 By the standards of Jewish law as applied to Jews, Hinduism and Buddhism do not count as monotheistic traditions. However, the essential point of the Noahide laws is that the standards of Jewish law do not apply to non-Jews. Radically pure monotheism is expected by Judaism only from Jews. The Noahide laws do not preclude gentile religions from developing softer, more complex, and compromised forms of monotheism. Under the Noahide laws, it is possible to assume that Hinduism and Buddhism are sufficiently monotheistic in principle for moral Hindus and Buddhists to enter the gentile’s gate into heaven. Jewish law regards the compromises made or tolerated by the world’s major religions as ways of rendering essentially monotheistic theologies easier in practice for large populations of adherents. The fierceness of Islamic opposition to such compromises has no counterpart in Judaism. In Islam, it is seriously blasphemous for anyone of whatever faith to combine belief in the one God with popular ideas about other heavenly powers or with subtle theological doctrines such as the Trinity. Islam cannot tolerate such compromises because the truth that they violate is applicable universally and not simply to Muslims. The problem is that Islam is radically monotheistic (like Judaism) yet is also (unlike Judaism, which is the religion of one people) universalistic as well. <sup>25</sup> 

 With lessons learned and paradigms established in relation to Christianity, Steinsaltz now extends these to Hinduism and Buddhism. Doing so requires one more move. That is the move of focusing on theology rather than worship. We have already noted the dual meaning of Avoda Zara as encompassing both the wrong object of worship and strange methods of worship. We have also noted the propensity that Jewish viewers have to focus on the action at the expense of the understanding of that action. Steinsaltz offers us a corrective. He puts aside the ritual, which means putting aside the worship of images and the myriad gods of the Hindu pantheon, and focuses on the philosophy that underlies these. <sup>26</sup> The philosophy he offers us is fully vedantic. The vedantic view is indeed a hierarchical view that considers the vedantic teaching of the ultimate unity of being as the highest form of Hindu teaching. For vedantins, lower forms of understanding may be tolerated and accepted, since Vedanta holds the key to ultimate meaning. It thus makes it possible to accommodate the lower forms of religion as well. Such understanding is also patient. Hindu belief in reincarnation and in gradual evolution takes away the pressure to resolve philosophical and 

*[p. 90]* ^pg97

theological differences immediately and creates a space for processes that are long term, leading to an attitude of tolerance. It seems no accident that someone who seeks to understand religion from the perspective of hierarchy and offers a hierarchical reading of Judaism’s relations to world religions should appeal to a highly hierarchical view of Hinduism itself. Steinsaltz in turn also develops an attitude of tolerance, while rejecting the term, without even awaiting the final messianic fulfillment of religions, as they come to know God fully. In Steinsaltz’s view, religions may hold on to their imperfect views even in the eschaton. The accommodation of other religions stems from the election of Israel and from the different tracks that have been established for Jews and non-Jews. Consequently, one can tolerate compromises to monotheism. Steinsaltz uses a striking phrase: softer, more complex, and compromised forms of monotheism. <sup>27</sup> Hinduism is thus compromised monotheism, and as such is valid. 

 Something further is gained by this move. These religions are only valid for non-Jews. In the contemporary context this provides a protective mechanism against Jewish attraction to Eastern religions. <sup>28</sup> What is permissible for non-Jews would be considered idolatrous for Jews. Here again respect and protection of identity are achieved in a single move. 

 Steinsaltz takes his knowledge of Hinduism from some textbook, and in the process distorts a fact or two. That textbook, written from the perspective of how representative Vedanta takes on interpretive power, allows him to deal with the entire scope of Hinduism within a few lines. Perhaps too much knowledge is a dangerous thing. Perhaps it is advantageous to take up a single perspective and to develop a halachic position according to it. At the same time, one wonders whether more detailed study of the religion might upset the facility of making broad pronouncements based on such a view. Even more doubts are cast on his treatment of Buddhism and his attempt to define it as theistic. Perhaps the genre dictates the method. Steinsaltz authored these words for a panel presentation on religious tolerance, where he represented Judaism. Such situations seem to have their own dynamics, leading to results, even if positive in and of themselves, that are not always commensurate with the message that emerges from other contexts and genres. They bring out the best in a given presentation, but they are written in English and spoken academically. Would Steinsaltz also say these very things in Hebrew, in the framework of a *pesak* , a ruling of the halacha? <sup>29</sup> Whether he would or not, he has certainly taken us a long way into thinking about Hinduism as Avoda Zara and has offered us a way of thinking that is systematic, grounded in theological principles, and above all an alternative to the impressions generated by the visual dimension of Hinduism, reminding us of the priority of theology over practice. 

 In the tension between theology and practice, and once one has identified the true nature of a religion, all deviations, including theological deviations, become irrelevant from a Jewish perspective:

 The less than absolutely monotheistic folk beliefs of Christians or Buddhists are taken in Jewish law to be violations of Christianity and Buddhism, religions that are in themselves adequately monotheistic; hence such beliefs are only problematic 

*[p. 91]* ^pg98

internally—solely within the discourse of another religion. Such violations do not affect what Judaism has to say about Christianity or Buddhism or any other religious tradition. The standards of Jewish law cannot be violated where they do not apply. <sup>30</sup> 

 One returns to the question of how one knows these religions are in and of themselves adequate and what provides us with the key to recognize their essence as acceptable. The attempt to distinguish the essence of a religion from its secondary folk accretions is problematic. It ignores the complexity of religions, in favor of a simplified model of the religions, seen from a particular vantage point. But whether true or not, it certainly addresses beautifully the challenges of tolerance and religious pluralism. It is fitting to cite here Steinsaltz’s conclusion:

 This hierarchical aspect of Noahide legal thought might be viewed as an unpromising model for regulating relations among the monotheist faiths, but its illiberal quality is, I want to conclude by arguing, what is most promising about the Noahide model. Basically, it does not require most religions to give up, or modify, the meaning of such words as *true* and *truth* . <sup>31</sup> It provides a basis for conversation among religions without the expectation of compromise between or reconciliation of claims. It requires no concession to objectionable beliefs and practices, though the Noahide model does sharply restrict the applicability of one religion’s laws to any other’s. The Noahide approach, in other words, is a formula for no more than peace. But then, peace is in itself among the very greatest of religious values. <sup>32</sup> 

 Steinsaltz is the only rabbinic authority to attempt an application of the principles formulated initially in a Christian context to the context of Hinduism, as well as of Buddhism. <sup>33</sup> For most rabbinic authorities there has been little incentive or interest in finding constructive theological and halachic views that would accommodate Hinduism. Given that most of them follow the halachic opinion that even Christianity is Avoda Zara, there is little to discuss in relation to Hinduism. Steinsaltz should accordingly be appreciated for the audacity and novelty of his theological and halachic approach.

### 9 Shituf: Critical and Constructive Reflections

 Shituf provides the basis for the few contemporary efforts at recognizing Hinduism as a valid spiritual path for its followers. While appreciating the 

application of this category to the context of a new religious encounter and recognizing the possibility that such application offers for contemporary interfaith relations, we must also consider whether this category really does justice to and describes adequately the views of the religion it seeks to describe. My own sense is that Shituf is a useful internal category, but one that does not do justice to the theological nuances of the religions it seeks to describe. The category may therefore remain useful for future discussion as a purely internal one by means of which Jews come to terms with another religion. But if we chose to employ it in order to make gains in relation to Hinduism, as previously made in relation to Christianity, we still ought to consider how adequate it really is to a description of Hindu religious reality. We may therefore have to reconsider and reinterpret it or, at the very least, use it with awareness of the discrepancy between our own internal theological and halachic categories and what should be an adequate description of the religious reality of other religions. 

 The use of Shituf is complex. Its complexity stems from its tortured history. What started as a local note in a discussion of the Tosafot ended up as a principled view of other religions. Consequently, we find various understandings of Shituf and various positions in relation to it. Beginning with the Tosafot, there are two possible readings of what the permissibility of Shituf for non-Jews refers to. <sup>1</sup> According to one reading, what is under discussion is taking an oath in God’s name alongside the religious power or authority of something that is clearly non-divine, such as saints, or the gospel. This reading, which I consider the correct reading of the Tosafot, leads us to consider the entire evolution of Shituf as a category through which we consider other religions as an accident of the history of Jewish halacha and its views of other religions. If Shituf referred to taking an oath, say, in a saint’s name, alongside God’s name, it addresses issues of religious propriety, rather than of theological correctness, relevant to the concerns of Avoda Zara. This is indeed the early rabbinic application of the verb from which Shituf is derived (the abstract noun does not yet exist in rabbinic literature 

*[p. 94]* ^pg100

and only comes into existence with the discussions of the Middle Ages). <sup>2</sup> If so, we do not know much from the Tosafot concerning how a view of God, understood as a complex, rather than a simple, unity would be considered, in terms of Avoda Zara. The discussion in chapter 1 of this book, concerning the possibility of recognizing the same God, applied to the various positions articulated in the Middle Ages, suggests that it is possible to consider the Christian God the same God as the God of Judaism. One way of understanding this with reference to the Shituf construct is that Shituf describes a relationship to a being *other* than God alongside God. Consequently, belief in a complex notion of divine unity does not necessarily mean one is worshipping another God and falls outside the scope of the original intention of Shituf. According to this possibility of understanding the Tosafot, applying Shituf to an intra-divine plurality may represent a deviation from the original intention. If so, Shituf may indeed not be suitable for the theological challenge. One may either consider complex monotheism, referring to several personalities within the Godhead, as theologically acceptable or not, but Shituf is relevant to this discussion only through a process of felicitous historical misunderstanding. 

 The alternative understanding of the Tosafot is that they are indeed referring to Jesus, when they speak of Shituf. It is here that a series of questions arises regarding the appropriateness of the category and its application to other religions. The first question has already been raised. Whose perspective are we appealing to, that of the worshipper or our own? Should the matter be approached from the perspective of believers or of outside viewers? The difference in perspective will determine the judgment placed on the act of faith. In our case, it may be that evolution of Shituf as a halachic category forced later authorities to contorted understandings of Christian faith by virtue of the very category. Thus, once it is assumed that Shituf refers to Jesus, one is forced to also assume that the problem is that a human being is worshipped. This means that we exclude the intention of the person worshipping and view Christian faith exclusively from our own perspective. Jesus is accordingly viewed as a person. <sup>3</sup> We, the halachic observers, insist on his humanity as part of our assessment of what is worshipped, rather than taking the believer’s view that starts with a certain understanding of divinity, from which it proceeds to recognition of Jesus and his divinity. 

 In fundamental ways this goes against the grain of halachic thinking itself. Halachic thinking emphasizes intention as the foremost basis of defining and appreciating any act. As already suggested, with reference to Avoda Zara we have explicit talmudic discussions concerning the status of someone who worships Avoda Zara, while his intention is directed to God. <sup>4</sup> This and similar discussions ought to have played a major role in assessing whether the worship of Jesus as God should be considered Avoda Zara. The contemporary context, which calls for a fresh restatement of Judaism’s views of other religions, makes the fundamental methodological and philosophical challenge much more poignant. Continued application of the category of Shituf, with reference to another religion, runs the risk of violating the believer’s own faith perspective, by forcing upon it the outsider’s views and categories. This is a subtle form of religious violence. <sup>5</sup> Even if we are of the opinion that non-Jews are not forbidden to worship through Shituf, 

*[p. 95]* ^pg101

thereby recognizing Christianity, the very category by means of which the issue is determined constitutes a judgment that carries within it some potential or actual misrepresentation either of the theology or of the intention of Christian worshippers. It is therefore in some fundamental way inadequate to the task of fair theological representation of Christianity. 

 One of the factors that leads Jewish authorities to this total disregard for the intention of the Christian believer is the fact that they are “stuck” with the category of Shituf, following one particular reading of the Tosafot. As this category provides them with the tools for reflecting upon Christianity in halachic terms, they have almost no choice but to take the position that Shituf, whether allowed or forbidden to non-Jews, refers to the worship of a person, proclaimed as divine. This theological and halachic straight jacket is not really called for by the sources that gave birth to this halachic tradition. 

 The suitability of Shituf to a view of Christianity was already queried, albeit from an entirely different perspective, by Rabbi Yakov Emden. <sup>6</sup> Emden accepts the position that non-Jews are permitted to worship God through Shituf. In passing, he does, however, raise some fundamental queries in relation to this position. Emden considers the kind of association that is permitted along the lines of Nachmanides, quoting Deut. 4,19. In other words, what is permissible is to worship the highest God along with a lower rung, an intermediary, on the metaphysical ladder. The reason one might want to worship an intermediary echoes Maimonides’ thinking, namely that the king wishes to honor his servants, rather than Nachamanides’ reasoning wherein such worship is either required for continuing provision of needs or is a natural religious response. As both Nachmanides and Maimonides share the same hierarchical worldview, Emden’s hierarchical understanding of Shituf draws on elements of both teachers in presenting a view of Shituf that relates to the worship of intermediaries. Emden questions how appropriate it is to apply Shituf to a theology that recognizes multiplicity within the Godhead. Compromising monotheism, argues Emden, is not the same as Shituf. Shituf should only be applied where hierarchically lower beings are worshipped alongside God, as distinct from recognizing multiplicity within the Godhead. Emden’s question is certainly appropriate in terms of the early rabbinic sources. It is also appropriate in terms of the theoretical justification for Shituf, as provided by Nachmanides’ position. The legitimacy of Christianity, recognized by Emden as a universal position, appeals to the only position that makes validation of Christianity possible—Shituf. Closer scrutiny of the category and its theoretical justification runs the risk of undermining it, a risk that Emden avoids by bracketing his own theological musings and ultimately deferring to broader consensus. 

 The question of theological inadequacy is exacerbated when we turn to Hinduism. In one way, Hinduism may have an easier time dealing with the question posed by Emden. Emden is concerned that Shituf, in the case of Christianity, touches on the very essence of the Godhead, introducing multiplicity within the Christian Godhead. Shituf, argues Emden, cannot justify a complex notion of the Godhead. In this sense, Hinduism may have a satisfactory answer. Brahman provides the notion of the one God, beyond all, while all that is in the realm of 

*[p. 96]* ^pg102

the manifest is in some way distinct from that unity. If, according to Emden, successful application of Shituf assumes a tension between the one God and the multiplicity of other beings, Hinduism may be able to address this need. But Hinduism also challenges the premises that inform a theology of Shituf. The affirmation that God is all-pervading, that everything is divine, and that God can manifest or come in or as anything means that the very tension between God and non-God, that is the basis of Shituf, crumbles before a Hindu regard of reality. Of course, one may refer to the phenomenal world as distinct from the transcendent world and thereby uphold the dual perspectives that could make Shituf meaningful. Nevertheless, what makes Shituf a meaningful category is the fundamental tension between God and non-God. Hinduism undermines the possibility of full-fledged application of this duality, thereby undermining botha certain critique of its worldview and the very possibility or legitimacy of accommodating it through a Shituf strategy. 

 We note, then, that the most fundamental categories that have served the Jewish discussion of other religions run into significant difficulties when applied to Hinduism. We must therefore ask whether the categories we have used to describe, and then evaluate, other religions are adequate for the task. Posing this question is important in several ways. First, it forces us to take some distance and to reflect upon our own process of viewing another religion. Conventional approaches to other religions assume that the different religions speak the same language, but use that language to make different statements. The various understandings of Avoda Zara presented earlier attempt to contain other patterns of faith and worship within a conceptual matrix developed within Judaism. It may be instead helpful to think of the different religions as speaking different languages. Such an a priori recognition would require greater efforts at translation and at understanding the religious vocabulary of another tradition on its own terms. Furthermore, the possibility that differences are informed by fundamental differences in approach makes it possible to ask what we might learn from those who speak another religious language. For both Maimonides and Nachmanides, as well as for proponents of a Shituf view, there is nothing possible that we might learn from worshippers of other religions, classified as Avoda Zara. For Maimonides, they are engaged in error and lies; for Nachmanides, as well as for a Shituf view, their approach to spiritual reality is at best a partial approach. If it is recognized that the categories with which we approach the subject do not adequately describe the religious reality of the other and that there exist multiple religious languages, new horizons for understanding open up. Talk of understanding would no longer be limited to understanding the other religions as part of assessing their validity and their status as Avoda Zara, but would extend to the possibility of spiritual sharing and learning from those who speak another religious language. 

 Our discussion has come to an important moment. Having first applied the category formed in relation to Christianity to Hinduism, we have now cast some doubt on the very suitability of this category to Hinduism, as well as to Christianity. This should not be considered backtracking, leading to an impasse. Each stage in the unfolding of the argument is significant as we struggle to make 

*[p. 97]* ^pg103

sense of an unfamiliar religious tradition. Applying precedents established in relation to Christianity to Hinduism is a strategy of working within the tradition, using its recognized language. Questioning the suitability of traditional categories to the case of Hinduism is intended to stretch our thought further. This constitutes an invitation to accommodate more complex theological patterns, to consider the intention of worshippers more seriously, and to reflect on possible lessons that we may derive from the religious insight of a tradition commonly considered to be Avoda Zara. 

 I would like to conclude these critical reflections on Shituf by contrasting what the category makes possible and how it operates with Nachmanides’ view of other religions. As suggested by Emden’s conflation of the two views, there is significant overlap between the positions, leading to their possible identification. There are, however, significant differences between the two positions as well. One difference concerns the focus of attention. Shituf is conceived from the perspective of the worship of God at the center, a worship that is augmented by the worship of another being alongside God, either as part of the divinity, as later authorities have applied the notion to Christianity, or completely outside the divine realm, as the original source in the Tosafot suggests. By contrast, Nachmanides does not assume that non-Jews have to primarily worship God, while they may augment their worship of Him by the worship of other beings. Rather, the primary being that is worshipped is the other being, the ministering angels or planets. The emphasis is different. Affirming the permissibility of Shituf for non-Jews assumes that both God and the other being are objects of worship. In Nachmanides’ presentation, however, we hear nothing of the worship of the Supreme God by other nations. Only Israel worships God. Other nations worship their ministers. The Rashba is explicit concerning what makes this worship permissible: “Whoever worships the particular star that guides a particular place is not like someone who engages in Avoda Zara , provided he knows and recognizes that the power of the star and its rulership are only due to God, who made it a ruler in that particular land.” Knowledge is the crucial factor that determines whether a certain form of worship is or is not considered Avoda Zara. One is not asked to worship God in the highest, only to recognize His existence, thereby framing the worship of another being and providing it with a broader context. 

 The difference between the Shituf position and my reading of Nachmanides is particularly relevant to a discussion of Hinduism. Here, Nachmanides may be more helpful than the Shituf construct, inasmuch as that construct is based on sharing of worship. Nachmanides thus challenges us to think what it is that matters most in our view of another religion—its rituals, practices, and worship or the theological understanding it brings to bear. Shituf focuses on worship and requires its direction to God. By contrast, Nachmanides offers us a much more accommodating model that is more readily adapted to the reality of other religions. While both Nachmanides and the Shituf model might be critiqued with regard to Hinduism, Nachmanides’ model emerges as more adequate for a description of Hinduism and successfully avoids some of the pitfalls that inevitably befall Shituf. That Nachmanides’ views were developed as an intentional theory of religions, while Shituf developed by force of contemporary needs, 

*[p. 98]* ^pg104

irrespective of its original parameters, might well account for the respective strengths and weaknesses of both models. 

 Following these critical reflections on Shituf, I would now like to offer some constructive reflection on the category, in what might be termed a theology of Shituf. Adequate or not, appropriate or not, the fact is that Shituf is one of the most important tools we have for developing a positive appreciation for other religions. Complementing the critical reflections on its adequacy is an attempt to think through what permissibility of Shituf really means and what its implications are for any Jewish view of or encounter with other religions. All too often Shituf is simply cited as a legal category. Even where this legal category has evolved into a principled appreciation of other religions, this development is accompanied by little theological reflection. 

 Why is Shituf permitted for non-Jews? Other than Nachmanides’ theory of the allocation of stars and celestial ministers to oversee the nations, regardless of the difference in emphasis compared with the Shituf construct, we have no theory that could account for why non-Jews may worship other beings alongside God. <sup>7</sup> The following reflections seek to address this challenge. 

 As I trace the evolution of Shituf, the notion underwent a double transformation: from reference to ways of honoring God by not sharing his honor to a proper theological category and, concomitantly, from reference to humans (or Scripture) that are honored alongside God, to reference to God proper, that is to the particular Christian view of God. While on one level there is something accidental in the emergence of the category, on another level its development not only serves needs but articulates and gives expression to a profound spiritual vision. In order to make the point, let us revisit the dual sense of Avoda Zara—alien god and foreign worship. This dual meaning reminds us that familiarity and strangeness pertain both to the goal and to the path. Having established the identity of God who is the ultimate recipient of worship and the ultimate goal of the spiritual quest still leaves open the question of the means of approaching Him. Usually one thinks of the means in terms of worship and ritual. What one finds particularly problematic is the use of images. I would like to suggest that in relation to Christianity, the religion in sight when Shituf was formulated, there is a more fundamental dimension of means to an end—the figure of Jesus Christ. The means are collapsed into the end, when one thinks of Jesus as part of the Godhead. Jesus is approached as God, as part of the Christian understating of the Godhead. But Jesus is also the path toward the Father. It seems superfluous to quote relevant scriptural passages or to engage in an analysis of the rich and varied ways in which Jesus functions as an intermediary, an interlocutor, a path, and various other ways in which Christian theology has expressed his role as instrumental in reaching God. <sup>8</sup> 

 This leads to the Jewish shunning of Jesus’ function on the theological plane, as the instrument is worshipped and identified with the Divine. However, once we accept the normativity of Shituf for non-Jews, we may also reflect on the theological possibilities that are thereby realized. The category of Shituf provides the means for recognizing the access that humanity needs for mediating divine presence. God is brought close to humanity through the agency of those who not 

*[p. 99]* ^pg105

only speak of Him but also represent Him and are recognized as in some way divine, as a part of Him. One could simply dismiss this as a basic or lower need of the spiritual life that ought to be overcome by higher teaching and clearer spiritual vision. The view that considers Shituf to be forbidden might be understood this way. If, however, we are willing to accept Shituf as a valid spiritual option, it testifies to the need for representation and for making God accessible. This need is recognized and there is room for fulfilling it. The evolution of Shituf as a legal category must find correspondence in its unfolding as a theological category. Shituf allows us to recognize that when humans turn to God through other humans, or through understandings of God that are different than our own, they are not turning away from God. Rather, they approach the same God we approach, but through a different means, a different path. 

 Proponents of the permissibility of Shituf are in a sense willing to go along with practitioners of the faith. When a particular spiritual path leads to worshipping the one who represents the path, this is still considered a path, a means, rather than a different goal, a different god. Difference in path is tolerated as a permissible means of approaching the same God, even if it impacts how the Divine is understood. 

 It is clear that Shituf is an educational strategy that is only permissible for non-Jews. Israel has a special spiritual charge and a special spiritual path that demands direct approach to God, without the confusion of instrument and goal, of ends and means, of method of worship and recipient of worship. The covenant is twofold. It includes the forming of a special and exclusive relationship between God and Israel. But it also includes the acceptance of covenantal laws and stipulations, which we commonly refer to as commandments. Now, the covenant has these two dimensions built into it—a relational goal and a path that includes a detailed way of life, designed to establish Israel in God’s presence. The demand that Israel worship in one way and not in another is thus an expression of covenantal exclusivity. The exclusivity of the covenant goes farther than the insistence that only Israel’s God be worshipped and no other god. It also includes the demand that the method of worship be exclusive to the covenant and that Israel worship God in a way particular to it, setting it apart from all nations. If other nations may worship God through the instrumentality of other beings, Jews are commanded to have a direct relationship with God alone. Herein is found the deep rationale for the fundamental difference that pertains to method of worship, and the prohibition on Jews worshipping God through other beings. In this view, Israel’s God may not be exclusive, but is known to all. However, the path He has chosen for Israel is. 

 For a Jew to practice worship through Shituf would be a form of Avoda Zara. But let me account what might be meant by this, beyond the obvious halachic sense, by appealing to an interesting paraphrase of Avoda Zara, found in the Talmud. <sup>9</sup> The Talmud states that “one should always rent oneself out to Avoda Zara , rather than become economically dependent on human beings.” <sup>10</sup> This is brought as an aggadic, that is nonlegal, saying to justify the fact that one of Moses’ descendants is said to have become a priest to Avoda Zara. However, the editor hastens to add, the saying that passed from generation to generation in 

*[p. 100]* ^pg106

Moses’ family was misunderstood. What was meant by Avoda Zara was not the literal service of other gods, preferable as it were to financial dependency, but the willingness to undertake an *avoda* , that is a labor, an occupation, that is foreign to oneself, in other words, beneath one’s dignity. <sup>11</sup> Now, this passage plays on the meaning of *avoda* , but it also highlights the range of possible meanings one can associate with *zara* . As we already know, *zara* can describe foreign, strange, wrong, repulsive, repugnant, abhorrent. All these dimensions have become associated with Avoda Zara. The present text allows us to bring Avoda Zara back to its most fundamental sense—something that is unfamiliar, unaccustomed, inappropriate, for who we are. It is a statement about us and what is suitable for us, rather than a statement about the other and how abhorrent their religious life is. 

 What is unique about the Jewish path to God is the demand that we serve Him exclusively and approach Him through the path He has given us, itself an exclusive path, that does not allow us to approach Him through other beings as divine. For Jews to worship a human person, or some being, as an expression of God, or as a means to God, is foreign to the very essence of the spiritual path of Judaism, to the foundations of covenantal exclusivity. The sense of *zara* is not simply a cultural strangeness or something that goes against an age-old collective habit of mind, though it certainly is that as well. Worship of other beings, even when incorporated into our worship of God and serving it, is foreign to the spirit of the people as formed by its highest spiritual vision, its covenant with God. Thus, we may consider the foreignness of this aspect of Avoda Zara not so much a matter of wrong and right, as much as what is right and appropriate for *us* on *our* unique spiritual path. 

 This covenantal exclusiveness is identity forming. While its purpose should not be limited to the formation of collective identity, as distinct from that of other religious groups, the demand for exclusive worship certainly does become a strong identity-forming factor. The most obvious indication for this is that Jews have given their lives for this very principle for generations. <sup>12</sup> The halachic basis is, of course, the talmudic requirement to die rather than commit one of three transgressions, including Avoda Zara. <sup>13</sup> However, the ethos of martyrdom and the broad history of the phenomenon suggest that this has been a profound factor in shaping identity, beyond being a formal obligation. 

 The concern for identity and the question of different methods of worship suitable for Jews and non-Jews are the subject of the following anecdote that allowed me to confront the problem and the pain it causes head on. Several years ago I was invited to address a course of yoga teachers. The course was given by an Israeli branch of an international religious movement, Yoga Sivananda (not to be confused with the Sivananda Ashram mentioned elsewhere, though owing its name to the same inspiring spiritual figure). I have been friendly with an Israel swami of that group for over 30 years. <sup>14</sup> Based on our long-standing relationship, I was invited to address a course of yoga teachers in training. Walking into the hall, I noticed the various images of Hindu deities that often adorn Hindu temples and other establishments. It was clear that these were used not only for decoration but also for purposes of worship, as part of the training of these future Israeli, and obviously Jewish, yoga teachers. This made me feel uncomfortable. 

*[p. 101]* ^pg107

I was comfortable enough with my hosts and with my audience that when I was asked to share my spiritual experiences in Judaism and in Hinduism with the audience I made a point of referring to the worship of Hindu idols. I stated my own boundaries, why I felt they should be upheld and why I thought a way could be found to advance in the practice of yoga without recourse to the worship of images. <sup>15</sup> My friend the swami later stated that he had hoped I would be articulate on these very issues. 

 Following the lecture I was approached by a throng of students, who shared with me their pain at being forced to worship Hindu images. The philosophy was given, it was made clear to them that one is worshipping the absolute, that these are only means, that ultimately one is approaching the same God. In short, the finest vedantic theology was put in the service of accounting for the use of images in worship during an Israeli yoga teacher training course. But for many students this was not sufficient. They were suffering from being forced to partake of worship of images, either actively or passively, under penalty of being thrown out of the course were they to not fulfill all of its requirements. Some chose to leave the course; most stayed on; many suffered. I attempted to mediate between the students and the course leaders. The latter, however, were simultaneously understanding and dismissive, considering the suffering created by this situation as part of the growth process of the students, a stage they would outgrow, in accordance with the epistemology and educational processes spelled out by their vedantic worldview. There was no resolution, in practical terms, to the tension and suffering. 

 Looking at this incident in light of our discussion, we have here the very point where the suggestion concerning the meaning of Shituf and how it might function differently for Jews and non-Jews comes to play. We may be able to extend the notion of Shituf to the Hindu worship of the various deities that were placed on the makeshift altar. I have no doubt that not a single person in that room thought they were “God” in the full sense we ascribe to the term, or that they conflicted with the sense of God in the absolute. The spiritual understanding informing the worship of these images could have been fully compatible with the views of those who permit Shituf, and possibly with those of Nachmanides as well. However, what might be justified and understood when the worshipper is a native Hindu looks completely different when this spiritual path is exported to an Israeli kibbutz and applied, in fact forced, upon Jewish practitioners. It is here that the question of the method of worship unique to Jews, in keeping with the exclusiveness of the covenant, plays out so clearly. <sup>16</sup> 

 Hindu religious life offers various challenges, when applying the permissibility of Shituf. One of these was just mentioned: the worship of gods and deities, all of whom are understood to represent the Divine in Hinduism. But Hinduism also offers another challenge, which is in some ways more complex and even more problematic. I refer to the worship of human beings, saints, great sages, teachers. One might argue that this is not fundamentally different than the worship of Jesus as God in the Christian context. Theologically, this is true. If a theory of Shituf can accommodate the worship of Jesus as part of the Christian understanding of God, it should also be able to accommodate the worship of gurus and 

*[p. 102]* ^pg108

holy men in the Indian religious landscape. There is, however, one major distinction. Even if Jesus was a human person, he was not, to the best of our knowledge, ever worshipped while alive. <sup>17</sup> While in the case of Jesus one could argue that one is worshipping a human person, in fact it is the *idea* or memory of the person that is worshipped. <sup>18</sup> Even if for believers that person continues to be present, in terms of ordinary day-to-day social relations he no longer is. In that sense, the worship of a human person remains symbolic or theoretical. <sup>19</sup> Even if Jesus’ humanity is affirmed, alongside his divinity, there are no social and political consequences to such worship that stem from Jesus’ earthly personality. The only operative factor is the faith of believers. 

 The situation in India is very different. Saints are worshipped while alive and often treated as divinities, even as they go about their daily business. We may recognize the theoretical basis that makes this possible. It is founded upon a combination of the recognition of God’s omnipresence and its realization to an outstanding degree in the life and consciousness of an individual. However, this is still an individual of flesh and blood, with bodily needs and personality, who is being treated in some way as a god. The potential for abuse is obvious. Recent memory leads us to recall Rajneesh, also known as Osho, who called himself bhagwan , Lord, and who adopted a sumptuous lifestyle and preached a libertine gospel, under the authority of his divine self. Abuses need not be as extreme. It is sufficient to recognize that the inevitable human propensity for error, not to mention sin, is at odds with the view of a person as god, and even more so with the offering of worship and adoration to such a person. I can testify that some of the problems (though not necessarily moral problems) that I myself have seen with certain Indian spiritual teachers could be traced to their own self-understanding as a living god and to the pressures and abnormalities this creates. If someone can never be criticized or queried, is the source of divine inspiration without fail, and cannot even practice ordinary bodily activities such as eating and drinking in the presence of others, this has the potential for either corrupting that person’s spiritual senses or those of his or her followers. 

 Having recognized the dangers, we are also called to appreciate the depth and beauty of devotion toward spiritual teachers and leaders, the profound reverence they receive and the utmost centrality that the spiritual life and its representatives receive in India. There is something inspiring in the reverence shown to spiritual teachers and when, as does happen occasionally, those teachers are truly great spiritual beings, there is a coherence in the system between its interior logic and its outward manifestations. 

 What does this say to us in terms of Avoda Zara? Clearly, there has been a long-standing Jewish refusal to ascribe divinity and to worship human beings. The story of Esther, celebrated on Purim, is understood as one biblical narrative to that effect. <sup>20</sup> The rabbis portray various figures, not least of which is Pharaoh, presenting themselves as divine. <sup>21</sup> And we should not forget rabbinic opposition to emperor worship, itself a strong dimension of rabbinic opposition to Avoda Zara. Coming out of this tradition, one would have a hard time accepting the worship of humans. One might consider the distinction between worship of figures who are spiritual as opposed to political figures. While the distinction is helpful, 

*[p. 103]* ^pg109

it also has its limitations. Regardless of the motive, the worship of any human being touches a deep Jewish nerve. Moreover, the boundary between religious and political can easily be crossed and India’s history is replete with rich and at times complicated intersections. Furthermore, religion is also a source of power and holy men have been great power mongers throughout the generations. 

 Does this mean that we must declare every instance of guru worship as Avoda Zara? I do not wish to jump to that conclusion. It may be sufficient to state how deep Jewish discomfort is with the worship of human beings. As much as avoidance of the worship of humans is constitutive of and deeply ingrained in the Jewish psyche, the respect due to and the worship of great spiritual beings is equally deeply ingrained in the Hindu psyche. Hinduism would not be what it is without the full and worshipful appreciation of its spiritual giants. If we apply a Shituf theology to Hinduism, this means we seek to simultaneously affirm the difficulties Jews would have with such practices, as well as an understanding of what Hindus seek to express through them. A Shituf theology is pluralistic in the sense of recognizing different spiritual paths. The Jewish path is one that has no room for the worship of human beings, not even teachers and great spiritual figures. <sup>22</sup> But this does not automatically mean that Hindus who practice veneration of their teachers are engaged in Avoda Zara. Their actions have to be interpreted within the broader religious system within which they operate. The worship of teachers is part of the quest for God. The teacher is not God, even if he represents Him, and in certain cases is claimed to be His manifestation. Whatever that means, it does not fully identify, nor exhaust what God is within that person. Even if the teacher is recognized as God, and therefore as part of the goal, the object of worship, he or she are also instruments, means of attaining a goal that is surely beyond themselves. Thus, in the figure of the living teacher means and ends are in some way collapsed and identified. Following the suggestion above, this can be recognized as a valid and important expression of Shituf theology. Non-Jews may approach God through the guru, the saint, the teacher. That is their path and that path passes through a person, who is thereby in some way incorporated into the understanding of God. Ultimately we look to the same God. <sup>23</sup> But the path is different, leading to significant differences along the way, including the worship of teachers. <sup>24</sup> 

 If Judaism’s primary attitude to other religions is affirmation of their status as Avoda Zara, this means Judaism has no message for others, at least not one to which they can listen. It patiently and passively waits for a messianic world in which all will come to know the true God and throw away their idols of stone and gold. But it has nothing meaningful to say to other religions on the common stage of world religions. Invalidation is not an effective strategy for garnering adherents to a worldview. Relations, under this model, would be kept to the human, social, and humanitarian level. Judaism has no meaningful theological message to share, let alone receive. A Shituf theology, such that I have tried to develop, places Judaism in a position of having something meaningful to say, because it is in a position to have a meaningful spiritual dialogue with other religions. The position that affirms covenantal exclusivity also affirms a plurality of paths and ways to the one common God. This allows a meaningful dialogue to ensue. Within 

*[p. 104]* ^pg110

this dialogue there is room to listen. And indeed, there is what to listen for. If we recognize in India a great spiritual culture, a beacon of spirituality, and a source of saints, we cannot simply dismiss its forms of worship as Avoda Zara. And if we take them seriously, we must listen to the spiritual testimony they offer and to the understanding of God they convey. Within this dialogue Judaism too has a message. Surely its refusal to worship God through another being is more than just a means of affirming its uniqueness. Surely this is a theological vision, rather than simply an identitarian strategy. If so, what is this vision and what is its message? It is the message and the testimony of what it means to worship the one God, exclusively, to approach Him without any intermediary, to look to Him alone. 

 But if there are different spiritual paths for Jews and non-Jews, there is little to talk about. What would be the purpose of dialogue, if the paths are only suitable for Jews and non-Jews respectively? Each should follow their path. As an initial response I would say that I find it hard to think of Judaism as a club, intended only for its members, with no message or meaning for those beyond it. The dialogue has not yet begun. It is hard to anticipate where a serious dialogue on God and the spiritual life could take us, were it carried from the dual platform of covenantal exclusivity and a Shituf *-* based pluralism. But even without knowing what its outcomes may be, it seems to me clear that the fruits would be spiritually beneficial to both sides. Crafting our message in dialogue with the other seems like the most appropriate way of advancing in our own spiritual quest, remaining true to our identity, and answering to the contemporary challenges of dialogue and peaceful coexistence among the world’s religions. 

 One significant fruit of a future dialogue has already emerged in the preceding paragraphs. My attempt to articulate a Shituf *-* based theology of religions is itself a contribution to a dialogue between Judaism and Hinduism, and as such constitutes an alternative to prevailing Hindu theologies of religions. The prevailing presentation of Hindu views of other religions presents all religions as doing the same thing. All are paths up a mountain top. It matters little what path you take, as long as the path leads you up the mountain. This theology of religions washes away differences and highlights the ultimate commonality of the goal. In view of the common goal all paths become equivalent. Now, the proposed Shituf theology ultimately agrees that the goal is one, when it recognizes that different religions worship the same God. However, the differences in the path are taken to be much more significant. Not all paths are equivalent, or at least the path of Judaism and the path of other religions are fundamentally different. The path for Jews requires exclusive focus on the goal, and does not recognize the collapse of goals and means, which leads others to worship God through the instruments that make His presence known and available. Non-Jews worship God in another way. This way could deflect from exclusive attention to God, or it could enhance the realization of divine presence in all. Various expressions of Christianity have become so christocentric as to detract attention from the God recognized as the common God of Judaism and Christianity. And many Hindu faithful so fix their devotion on one or another incarnation or representation of God as to lose sight of some of the broader cognitive dimensions, by means of which we could identify the same God in Judaism and Hinduism. On the other hand, other Hindu 

*[p. 105]* ^pg111

worshippers could, ideally, experience an enhanced awareness of God, by not limiting Him to His transcendent being, and by finding Him in all things present and visible. Whether the Hindu approach detracts or enhances is secondary to the point I wish to make at present. The important thing is that the difference between Judaism and Hinduism, seen through the eyes of a Shituf theology, is real and should not be minimized. 

 Taking a Shituf theology to the next level of reflection, we are led to think of the meaningful differences between all religions, and even between the different manifestations of Hinduism itself. Even if the ultimate goal, God, is the same, the process is colored in very different ways according to who is being placed alongside Him and who is the means through whom God is known and approached. Approaching through Jesus, Krishna, or Sai Baba may follow similar principles and may be accounted for through one governing logic, but the actual experience of approaching God will be different, considering who provides access, and how that figure’s access-providing is collapsed into the understanding of the Divine. And all these differences stand out further when contrasted with the refusal to worship God through any instrument and to collapse means and spiritual end, as affirmed in Judaism’s practice. Thus, even if we affirm the ultimate commonality of the spiritual quest of all religions, the differences in paths and their details are significant and should not be minimized. A Shituf-based theology provides a mechanism for cultivating a vision of how Judaism might accommodate other religions, while at the same time affirming the fundamental differences that characterize Judaism in relation to Hinduism and other major world religions. 

 These reflections consider Shituf to be a fundamental divide between Judaism and other religions. Such a fundamental difference invites dialogue and calls for some degree of mutual appreciation. There is, however, another way of considering Shituf, as an interim strategy, as a step toward a higher goal. According to such a view, Shituf should be viewed not as an alternative spiritual path but as an educational and evolutionary path, emphasizing a patient pedagogy, rather than an alternative path. <sup>25</sup> From the perspective of human spiritual evolution and psychology, worship through Shituf may aid spiritual growth. The various gods and in particular the teachers and great ones who offer religious instruction are in fact pointing to God and leading the way to Him. They are thus instrumental to religious growth. <sup>26</sup> Rather than competing with the true God, they aid their followers and disciples in attaining proper teaching and proper living that will lead them to the reality of God. Shituf is accordingly viewed as an educational theory that takes into account human and social nature. Given there is a high standard of cognition that is ultimately sought in relation to God, a serious challenge is posed in relation to how to attain this standard. How much patience is there for the process that leads to such perfect understanding and how much can lower views and understandings be accommodated within a worldview that highlights proper understanding of God as its ideal? Shituf may be presented as a patient pedagogy that accommodates the needs of those who are not members of Israel’s covenant, as they advance on their spiritual path toward the one and same God. 

 To a large extent, Judaism has long agreed to postpone the real spiritual challenge of making God known to all till the future messianic era. If proper 

*[p. 106]* ^pg112

knowledge of God, in its purity and exclusivity, had been Judaism’s most urgent concern, and if Judaism indeed felt its calling to share this knowledge with all, we would have seen a much more missionary and outgoing Judaism than what Judaism has become. That historical circumstances may have shaped this more introvert Judaism only accounts for how this came about. The end result is the same: by withdrawing from active missionary work, Judaism has agreed to fulfilling its mission in terms of the knowledge of God and to seeing its worldview and spiritual vision triumph only in the distant future. Present-day reality is thus a time of waiting, a process, leading up to a future fulfillment. This attitude of patient waiting conditions Judaism’s attitude to the spiritual well-being of humanity. Humanity will only attain its spiritual perfection in the future. If indeed one is willing to accommodate present-day reality, in light of a fuller messianic realization, then the position that Shituf is permissible for non-Jews makes perfect sense. Shituf is a strategy for accommodation. It suggests that perfect knowledge is not presently available. For the time being one may settle for imperfect knowledge of the Divine. The standard that Judaism expects of non-Jews, according to this view, is not one of perfect knowledge of God but one that is suitable for a process. In this process there is room for other beings to aid in bringing believers to God. Non-Jews can enjoy a meaningful relationship with the true God, proclaimed and recognized in part, and reached through the beings that are associated with Him. <sup>27</sup> This degree of recognition and relationship is sufficient for what is possible today, in an unredeemed world. It is sufficient for non-Jews, who have not undertaken the fullness of relationship with God and who are therefore not expected to accept the theological stringencies of perfect teaching that Jews are. It is sufficient for a world in which Judaism has an impact, but a very partial one. To recognize the legitimacy of worshipping God through Shituf is to offer a judgment on today’s reality and on the meaning of spiritual process. It is a statement of historical vision, a recognition of Judaism’s limitations and an expression of a view of God as a teacher who is patient and compassionate, awaiting greater spiritual realization of both Jews and non-Jews at some future point in time. <sup>28</sup> 

*[p. 107]* ^pg113

### 10 Thinking of Hinduism in Light of Meiri

 The Middle Ages have bequeathed us one additional major resource for developing a theology of other religions. In many ways it is the most promising 

and most helpful resource, because it deals directly with the question of the status of contemporary religions, rather than constructing a history of Avoda Zara, as did Maimonides and Nachmanides, or resolving the practical challenges posed by living in a Christian society, as did the Tosafists’ Shituf construct. This resource is the teaching of Rabbi Menachem Hameiri (henceforth: Meiri; 1249–1310). Meiri is, in my opinion, the single most important classical resource to be harnessed for a contemporary Jewish theology of religions, and the full import of his theology of religions has yet to be fully appreciated. 

 Some pointers to Meiri’s thought have been interspersed in our discussion earlier. One particularly noteworthy mention was in Steinsaltz’s reference to Meiri as the foundation for his own theory of tolerance. If so, Steinsaltz would be one contemporary application of Meiri, and would in fact be lending his own voice to a growing chorus of halachists who are willing to adopt Meiri’s position in contemporary interreligious discourse and in the framework of developing contemporary attitudes to other faiths. <sup>1</sup> However, as noted before, Steinsaltz’s arguments actually reflect the perspective of the Tosafists. By identifying Meiri with the view that non-Jews have a less stringent, more patient, theological yardstick by which they are expected to act, Steinsaltz is in fact missing out on what is most important in Meiri. <sup>2</sup> The following section constitutes my own attempt to apply the teachings of Meiri beyond their original historical context and to make them speak to the contemporary challenge of a Jewish view of Hinduism. In so doing, I will first present Meiri’s views in and of themselves and then reflect on the degree to which they may be applicable to a Jewish view of Hinduism. 

 Meiri is a treasurehouse of thought and talmudic mastery. However, his talmudic commentary *Bet Habechira* remained in manuscript for many centuries and was printed only in the twentieth century. Consequently, his views have been outside the mainstream of the Jewish learning tradition. <sup>3</sup> It may well be the case that Meiri’s views on the religious other are what he is most famous for. The 

*[p. 108]* ^pg114

degree to which his views on non-Jews and other religions have been integrated by halachists and thinkers during the last century is itself a function of their own predisposition to Meiri’s views. That his views have not been engaged for hundreds of years means that he remains a resource whose significance is yet to be fully unpacked. Even within the circles that uphold the kind of open or tolerant view represented by Meiri, his thought has not yet received the full attention it deserves. Its implications for Judaism’s views of world religions, especially in the contemporary context, have not been fully fathomed. The present attempt to reflect on the significance of Meiri to a Jewish view of Hinduism accordingly provides an opportunity to explore further what Meiri might mean to a contemporary Jewish theology of religions. 

 As described before, the biblical references to other gods and the talmudic references to Avoda Zara create a culture of distance and disdain in relation to other religions and to a large extent in relation to non-Jews as well. <sup>4</sup> This attitude, formulated in response to ancient religions and the religions of late antiquity, was, on the whole, transferred into the Middle Ages and conditioned Jewish attitudes toward Christianity. However, Christianity, as well as Islam, presented challenges that are unique in the history of Jewish dealings with other religions, both theologically and sociologically. Theologically, no longer was one dealing with other gods, but with other religions that claimed to worship the same God. For the modern observer who encounters Jewish attitudes to other religions, it is often difficult to understand on what grounds Jews could reject either religion, given that they basically believe in the same God. Carrying over the charge of Avoda Zara from classical literature to the dealings with Christianity, and even with Islam, is part of the heritage of Judaism, as it confronts new religious forms while upholding the categories and attitudes of old. That such continuity should exist is in part an expression of Jewish faithfulness and fidelity. But it cannot be divorced from the historical and sociological reality of Jews living as a minority, often a persecuted minority, in Christian and Muslim lands. Such historical conditions do not easily lend themselves to profound theological rethinking of one’s attitude to the oppressive majority society. However, as we have already seen, a series of practical issues, stemming from the need to maintain commercial relations within such an environment, arises and necessitates finding a way of relating to earlier prohibitions on contact with Gentiles and their religion. Meiri is to be appreciated within this context and in light of this brief historical summary. 

 It is worth noting that Meiri never refers to Christianity specifically, nor to Islam. <sup>5</sup> Rather, he refers to religions as “religions” and to their adherents as people who are bound by the ways of religion. Accordingly, Meiri develops a series of expressions such as “the boundaries of religion,” “nations restricted by the ways of religion,” and the “masters or ones possessed of religion” that are unique to him. These categories serve Meiri to develop contemporary attitudes to the religions of his time. Scholarly discussion of Meiri has concentrated mainly on his attitude to Christianity. <sup>6</sup> The present discussion explores the implications of Meiri’s views on Christianity to a view of Hinduism. Quite apart from the contribution to a Jewish view of Hinduism, this also allows us to consider in greater 

*[p. 109]* ^pg115

depth Meiri’s thought and how it might serve as a resource to a contemporary Jewish consideration of other religions. 

 Meiri’s great novelty lies in adopting a systematic and principled approach to other religions and in resolving many of the practical and moral challenges that were and are encountered in the attempt to apply talmudic legislation to other religions and their members. Over and against various halachic authorities who resolved difficulties in relation to Christians and Christianity on an ad hoc basis, largely through appeal to the Shituf construct, Meiri developed a systematic view of other religions, thereby providing us with one of the earliest attempts to formulate a broad Jewish theology of contemporary religions. Meiri’s views are summed up by two main claims. The first concerns the nature of idolatry of old and its relation to contemporary religions. According to Meiri, idolatry of old no longer exists, except in remote regions. <sup>7</sup> Accordingly, all that appears in talmudic sources concerning pagans and idol worshippers is not relevant to contemporary religions. As Moshe Halbertal has suggested, Meiri relies on a theory of religious progress. <sup>8</sup> Avoda Zara is really a matter of the past as humanity and its religions have progressed from an earlier primitive (fetishist) understanding to a higher understanding of God. Such a theory of progress draws from the philosophical literature of the Middle Ages, but it seems to me it draws no less on the fundamental distinction between the religions of old and the religions that Meiri encounters. <sup>9</sup> The latter are monotheistic religions, or proclaim themselves as such, worshipping the same God as Jews do. The move from polytheism to monotheism, or from fetishism to the recognition of a transcendent God, can legitimately be described in terms of progress. To the theoretical recognition may be added the intuitive understanding that one’s neighbors or conversation partners are not the base idolaters described in the Talmud. Meiri shares a common universe of discourse with Christian scholars. We know of intellectual exchanges between himself and Christian clergy, and his entire spiritual environment is one in which the intellectuals and thinkers of the different religions form bonds of common community and purpose. <sup>10</sup> Such a climate of sharing provides a counterpoint to prevailing attitudes of Jewish minorities in other European centers and is surely one of the factors behind Meiri’s views. Within this context, one can readily consider the view of progress in the history of religions, according to which the religions seen today—notably Christianity and Islam in Meiri’s case—are fundamentally different from the religions spoken of in the Talmud. As a consequence, many of the talmudic rulings concerning Avoda Zara no longer apply. 

 The other key component in Meiri’s view is the recognition that the nations of today are bound by moral codes and practice moral living. Meiri uses the moral argument to distinguish between the religions of old and contemporary religions. Today’s nations are restricted by the ways of religion. They possess a moral code. The negative and immoral representations of non-Jews found in earlier sources therefore do not apply to them. The significance of the argument for morality of contemporary religions and their adherents can be variously understood. In classical Jewish terms, this may allow us to see contemporary religions as fulfilling the requirements of the seven noachide commandments, understood as 

*[p. 110]* ^pg116

Judaism’s religious vision for humanity. <sup>11</sup> A more reasonable reading of Meiri is that morality establishes these religions within the very sphere of “religion,” thereby affording them legitimacy. I shall discuss this possibility at greater length further ahead in the chapter. 

 A key challenge in understanding Meiri, in cultivating his thought as a contemporary resource, and in applying his thought to new encounters, such as the encounter with Hinduism, is to understand the relation between the two components of Meiri’s thought. What is the relation between the claim that contemporary religions are not idolatrous and their description as moral and law abiding? There are two ways of constructing the relationship between the two statements. The first is to consider them as interdependent. Accordingly, proof of morality is proof that a religion is not idolatrous, either because morality and religion are fully identified or because morality serves as an index to proper knowledge of God. The other way of considering these two statements is to consider them as complementary but independent of one another. If so, moral living might provide us with *one* component in light of which we might distinguish present-day religions from religions of old. But other criteria might equally apply, and the entire issue needs to be conceived in terms that are broader than whether a religion does or does not teach moral living. Let me begin by exploring the more common view that identifies morality with true religion. 

 On first consideration, Meiri shifts the ground of discussion from theology—the ground upon which most considerations of Christianity’s status as Avoda Zara took place—to morality. Rather than focus upon the nature of the understanding of the Divine espoused by a given religion, Meiri claims that the relevant consideration is the moral life enabled by the religion. Religions that have a moral code and that espouse moral living should not be considered along the lines the Talmud prescribes for idolatrous religions. At first sight it would seem that Meiri sidesteps theology and locates the heart of the religious life, and hence a view of the religious other, elsewhere. However, upon closer examination, we are led to understand that Meiri’s position should not be understood as an avoidance of the theological dimension, but as establishing a different method by means of which the nature of the God worshipped by a religion may be known. Accordingly, the life of the believers, rather than the theological statements of the religion, is the ultimate proof of the nature of the religion, and hence of the nature of the God it worships. A religion that teaches and upholds a moral way of living is proof of the God who is worshipped through that religion. 

 Such a view has much to commend it. We must consider what in the first place is wrong with Avoda Zara. If Avoda Zara is considered from the perspective of covenantal exclusiveness, it makes perfect sense. The covenant demands perfect loyalty to one God and to no others. <sup>12</sup> A covenantal view of Avoda Zara may make sense, but is not problem-free. For covenantal exclusivity to make sense as a theological demand, one must assume some reality to other gods, making the demand for allegiance to Israel’s God meaningful. Historically, the covenant ceased to be the guiding conceptual framework for postbiblical Judaism, some time after Israel’s faith consolidated the claim that in reality there is no god besides God. If one considers God in terms of perfection, as many thinkers in 

*[p. 111]* ^pg117

the Middle Ages did, one must revisit the question of what is wrong with Avoda Zara. Surely the perfect God cannot be impacted by misguided worship. One answer is that something is morally wrong with idolatry. God objects to the worship of other gods because it has harmful moral consequences. Such an understanding is well supported by the biblical portrayal of ancient idolatry as morally corrupt and hence repugnant. <sup>13</sup> To locate the problem with Avoda Zara in the moral field is thus both scripturally and philosophically appropriate. <sup>14</sup> If so, the following equation may be suggested: just as Avoda Zara involves immorality, so too morality can be used as proof of the identity of the God who is worshipped through a religion that teaches and enforces moral living. 

 Most readers of Meiri are concerned with the practical solutions offered by him. <sup>15</sup> I am not aware of any attempt to make theological sense of the position that living by moral codes exempts a religion from the status of Avoda Zara. Pointing back from moral living to the God who commands and who is known through moral living assumes that morality is the sole privilege of a true religion and a true God. One could not rely on moral living as a sign of true religion if morality were recognized among people who do not know God, or who worship idols. Such, indeed, is the position of Meiri, as he describes idol worshippers and as he spells out what distinguishes valid and invalid forms of religious life. In several places we find not only a description of contemporary nations as bound by the boundaries of religion, but also a description of the nature of idol worship and its concomitant immoral lifestyle. The most succinct formulation of the relationship between religion and morality is the following quote:

 Because they worship idols, and do not believe in divinity, they are not bound by the ways of religion. And every transgression and all that is ugly is beautiful in their eyes. And they have no concern regarding anything. <sup>16</sup> 

 Idols are the opposite of true divinity and the worship of idols is causally related to lack of any moral boundary. Thus, worship of idols is equivalent to lack of any moral standard and a complete reversal of the proper moral order. Or in another quote:

 For since they do not possess any religion in the world, and do not submit themselves to the fear of divinity, but rather offer incense to the hosts of the heavens and worship the idols, they do not refrain from any transgression and they harm \[others\]. <sup>17</sup> 

 The fear of divinity is contrasted with the worship of powers that are non-divine. Meiri does not deny the efficacy of such worship, nor its attractiveness. <sup>18</sup> The sign of true religion is not the effectiveness or ability to attain powers and benefits through the worship of various forces, but the moral life that points to God and is possible only through contact with God. This reciprocal relationship between divinity and morality allows Meiri to claim that worshippers of the true God are moral and conversely that moral nations are ipso facto not idolatrous. True religion is not about rituals and the worship of various powers but about submission to God, the ultimate guarantor of moral living. <sup>19</sup> 

*[p. 112]* ^pg118

 Meiri takes his cue from biblical sources. <sup>20</sup> Already the Torah presents idol worshippers as immoral:

 It has already been explained that these things \[i.e. references to idol worshippers\] were stated concerning those times, when those nations belonged to the worshippers of idols. And they were filthy in their deeds and ugly in their moral qualities, as it is partially mentioned (Lev. 18,3): “You shall not copy the practices of the Land of Egypt where you dwelt, or of the Land of Canaan, to which I am taking you.” But the other nations, that are bound by the ways of religions and that are clear of the ugliness of these qualities, moreover they punish such transgressions, there is no doubt that these matters do not pertain to them at all. <sup>21</sup> 

 It is worth noting that Meiri does not simply refer to the fact that certain practices are avoided by the religions of today, but that there are legal codes that enforce these laws, thereby ensuring the moral character of society. <sup>22</sup> The public character of how religion impacts society seems central to Meiri’s views. The moral yardstick by means of which religions are measured may be judged in relation to individuals or to societies. Meiri here indicates that religion infuses society, guaranteeing its overall moral rectitude. 

 To conclude this small collection of texts, pointing to the nexus of faith in a true God and moral life, I would like to note two additional texts, each of which offers further nuance to Meiri’s thought structure. In one place Meiri writes:

 What I mean is that of idolaters it is said that they were not restricted by the ways of religion. On the contrary, every sin and everything repulsive is fit in their eyes. As the foremost of the philosophers has already said, “Put to death one who has no religion.” However, anyone who worships God, even though he does not belong to a religion, is not included in this ruling. <sup>23</sup> 

 The opposite to being bound by ways of religion is complete lack of morality. Godlessness is equated with repulsive ways. Quoting an unidentified philosopher, <sup>24</sup> Meiri teaches that lack of religion is deserving of death, lack of religion here understood as lack of morality. That morality is at stake, rather than other aspects of religion, becomes clear from the continuation of the passage where the worship of God places one in a separate category, despite not belonging to a religion. Thus, God is the guarantor of morality, above and beyond the ways He is known through religions. Moral living is mediated by religion, but can also be attained through direct knowledge and worship of God, independently of religion. God and morality are fully identified, as are immorality and godlessness, with idolatry. This passage is significant because it is the one and only reference to the possibility of worshipping God outside religion. It both proves the broader thesis of the interrelatedness of religion and morality and transcends it, by positing the possibility of worshipping God beyond a specific religion. Thus, while religion is Meiri’s primary category, at its heart is the knowledge and worship of God. These provide the guarantee for moral living. 

 The other text that makes a surprising application of the religion-morality equation applies these principles to a Jew, rather than to Gentiles. In this, Meiri 

*[p. 113]* ^pg119

shows how principled his position is. It is much more than a solution to practical problems that arise from living in a Christian society. It is a total view of religion that cuts across religions, Judaism included, without discrimination or preference for one religion over the other. <sup>25</sup> Meiri writes:

 An *Am Ha’aretz* (i.e. an ignoramus who does not follow the ways of the Torah) may, at times, be killed even on a Yom Kippur that comes out on Shabbat. <sup>26</sup> For he does not know the nature of the commandments and the ways of religions, and he is drawn after his \[physical\] nature, like an animal. And sometimes we might encounter him chasing a male \[for purposes of intercourse\] or after a betrothed maid. And it would be permitted to kill him even on Shabbat. <sup>27</sup> 

 The same criteria that had been applied in relation to idol worshippers are here applied to a Jew who does not know and does not fulfill the commandments. Religion and morality are thus a matter of religion, all religions. <sup>28</sup> Consequently, the same criteria that apply outward apply also within. 

 Meiri’s identification of religion, the knowledge of God, and moral living allows us then to sustain the argument that moral living is an indicator of the Divine within a religion. Meiri does not simply replace the theological demands that have led tradition to view other religions as Avoda Zara by moral demands. Rather, he uses morality as the yardstick through which something may be learned concerning the nature of the God that is worshipped, or more correctly: whether it is God or other beings—idols, astral forces etc.—that are worshipped. 

 According to the first reading of Meiri, then, we may posit the following understanding of the relationship between Meiri’s two statements regarding contemporary religions. The second statement, according to which the nations of today are bound by the boundaries of religion, holds the key to the first that they are no longer idolatrous. It is not simply that they have ceased to worship idols, but rather that the means by which we are able to make this statement is through an examination of the moral—and I add: also spiritual <sup>29</sup> —quality of their lives. Morality points to the God who is known through the religion, and moral living suggests a notion of God that is real, in terms of His impact upon the lives of believers. 

 Let us contrast this with the second reading of Meiri. According to this reading, moral living is not the sole and absolute means of establishing the identity of the God who is worshipped. Rather, as some of the quotes from Meiri already indicate, several criteria apply, all of which point not simply to God, but to the character of a religion as a valid one. This reading of Meiri highlights the very notion of “religion” as the core concept, in light of which other religions may be recognized as legitimate. <sup>30</sup> Accordingly, it is more than a strategy for resolving the difficulties arising from the challenge of how to deal with Avoda Zara in the framework of living alongside members of another religious tradition. It is a systematic view of religion as a phenomenon, of its working, structure, components, and goals. As we have begun to see, this systematic view relates to all religions, Judaism included. 

 It is significant that Meiri does not develop his views simply in response to the challenges of Avoda Zara. A thorough examination of Meiri’s writings 

*[p. 114]* ^pg120

reveals hundreds of references to the terms “religion” and “religions,” quite apart from the various references already encountered to nations bound by the ways of religion. Moreover, such reference to “religion” includes Meiri’s reference to Judaism, on a regular basis, in contexts in which other authors might simply have said “Torah” or used some other term that is specific to the Jewish religion. The centrality of the category “religion” to Meiri’s thought is the key to how he applies it to other religions, ancient and contemporary. 

 Let us consider then what a religion is, according to Meiri. We already know of one important component, namely the moral teaching that religion offers. Another component of religion is ritual. Here Meiri surprises, especially when contrasted with Maimonides, certainly a model and a figure who left a deep impact on Meiri’s philosophical views. Gerald Blidstein has shown that Meiri introduces a significant novelty in relation to the permissibility of development of non-Jewish forms of worship. <sup>31</sup> According to Maimonides, non-Jews may not develop their own religion. Religion is closely related to revelation and therefore only one of two revelatory tracks are available: the full revelation, available to Israel and open to potential converts, and the more limited revelation of the seven noachide commandments, designed for the rest of humanity. By contrast, Meiri fails to place any limitation on novel forms of worship, and therefore on the emergence of other forms of religious life. It seems likely that we have here an important distinction between two views of what constitutes the heart of religion. For Maimonides, religion must be based on revelation, and therefore there is no basis for inventing new rituals or religions, as these would be devoid of the required foundation in divine revelation. <sup>32</sup> It seems that for Meiri intentionality plays a more important role. Therefore, religions may be successful in achieving their goals, given the spiritual intention that drives their founders and practitioners. As I will shortly suggest, this seems to be part of a broader view of religion and its purpose, a view that is much more anthropocentric, highlighting transformation in human nature through the practice of religion, rather than the theocentric emphasis on revelation, found in Maimonides. We may thus identify a second component of religion, any religion—worship. Some of the quotes brought above point to the ritual dimension of religion and take this dimension for granted. The flaw in ancient idolatries was not the very fact of worship, but that it was misdirected. It was offered to beings other than the one God, source of moral living. 

 But what of faith in God and correct teaching concerning His nature? Here too Meiri surprises. It is common to think of the differences between religions as focusing upon their respective teaching about God, or in other words the truth that the diverse religions teach and concerning which they differ from one another. In fact, according to the common view, it is likely that differences in theology form the heart of the differences between religions. Rather than highlighting such differences, Meiri plays them down. This is not to suggest that Meiri considers that all religions offer the same teaching as Judaism. Judaism is probably unique in the full correctness of its views and teachings, whereas other religions do offer erroneous teachings. However, for Meiri theological error is not so grave. At least as far as other religions go, one can overlook theological error, 

*[p. 115]* ^pg121

as long as fundamental teachings—theological as well as moral—are intact. <sup>33</sup> If we raised the possibility earlier that Meiri overlooks theology preferring instead moral teaching, we now come to a more correct way of stating Meiri’s position—Meiri is willing to overlook errors in the specifics of theological teaching, provided the fundamentals of the religious teaching are intact. <sup>34</sup> 

 Based on the texts I shall presently analyze, I would like to suggest that Meiri’s core concern is that one reaches God and has a relationship with Him. Such a relationship is measured by the quality of moral life and by the overall impact that religion has upon the lives of its believers. It is only God, as opposed to idols, astral forces etc., who can transform lives and effect moral living. What is important is not how one understands God and whether or not one’s understanding is full and correct. What is important is that one’s relationship and the relationships mediated through a given religion be directed to God and therefore be effective in bringing about personal transformation and appropriate moral ordering of the life of the individual and society. In a sense, all that is crucial theologically is to know whether one has it *basically* right, that is, whether one is worshipping God or other forces. Once it has been established—and moral living is the major instrument in establishing this—that one is indeed worshipping God, the theological details of how God is understood, and for that matter even the details and practices of ritual, become secondary, maybe even negligible, certainly something to be overlooked in case of error. There are of course some minimal requirements that are required as part of identifying faith in God, rather than in idols. But these minimal requirements seem to be met readily by those religions with which Meiri was familiar, making the area of theological difference of secondary importance. 

 Let us look at some of the texts from which this unique theory of religion emerges. Concerning the obligation to return lost objects and its possible limitation to objects lost by Jews, Meiri writes:

 Whoever is from the nations that are bound by the ways of religion and that serve divinity in some way, even though their faith is far removed from our own faith, they are not included in this \[exemption from duty to return lost objects\], but they are fully as Israel for these matters, even in case of lost objects, or of error committed during financial transactions, and in all other matters, without any distinction. <sup>35</sup> 

 What counts is serving divinity in some way, in other words having some kind of recognition of and relationship with God. As long as one is in touch with God, regardless of the fact that the actual contents of faith seem strange and far removed, one is in fact within the bounds of legitimate religion. The rules that would apply between members of the community of true believers would therefore apply to such faiths as well. Theological correctness takes a very secondary place. What Meiri is concerned about is the recognition of the other religion as a “religion.” Minimal knowledge of God is sufficient to establish such a recognition, as the category of “religion,” consists of other dimensions that are more significant than a correct description of God, primarily the moral dimension. 

*[p. 116]* ^pg122

Thus, Meiri neither limits what is important to the moral field, nor tries to come up with a definition of what is the minimally needed knowledge of God. Rather, both components are appreciated as part of the broader assessment of another religion as “religion,” a member of the broader class of “religions,” and hence as valid, legitimate, and leading to various practical consequences. 

 The same emerges from another passage. <sup>36</sup> The passage is telling in terms of both how Meiri views idolatry and his tolerance for theological error:

 You know already that idol worshipping would exercise great attraction upon the heart, due to the benefits that could be attained through its worship, as it says, “But since we gave up offering incense to the Queen of Heaven and pouring libations in her honor, we have been destitute and have perished either by sword or by famine,” <sup>37</sup> and similarly in the final chapter of the \[Talmud\] tractate of [[Sanhedrin 102#102b|Sanhedrin 102b]], with reference to Menashe, \[accounting for why he could not refrain from worshipping idols, he replies to the sage:\] Had you been there at that time, you would have raised the edges of your gown and followed it \[the idol\]. . . . Nevertheless, nations that are bound by the ways of religion and believe in His (blessed be He) existence, His Unity and His power, <sup>38</sup> even though they are in error concerning some matters, according to our faith, the rules discussed above do not apply to them. <sup>39</sup> 

 The passage again appeals to biblical testimony. Here we hear the actual voices of idol worshippers who testify to the efficacy of such worship and to its benefits in their lives. Far from dismissing such testimony, as Maimonides would, Meiri upholds it, making it a characteristic of idol worship. <sup>40</sup> It is as efficacious as it is attractive. To understand this passage, one would have to adopt the view that such idol worship uses energies and powers to accomplish personal ends. Such energetic manipulation and the ensuing benefits to the lives of practitioners of rituals directed at beings other than God do not provide proof of authenticity of the religious life. These rituals are instrumental and are used to attain personal gains and to fulfill personal desires. God is only known through the quality of life associated with Him, not by the physical boons, apparently bestowed by lower beings. 

 Complementing this clear description of what idolatry is, Meiri again offers a definition of acceptable alternative understandings of God. Meiri seems to offer us some minimal requirements for a valid notion of God, probably because such a notion provides the basis for grounding a moral life in God. God exists, is one, and is capable, perhaps better rendered omnipotent. My understanding is that because He is omnipotent He controls life, can punish sinners, and reward good doers, and generally ensure the continuing practice and application of a moral life. Interestingly, Meiri emphasizes God’s unity as one of the fundamental characteristics. It seems that only the single God can be the source of a moral life, whereas lower deities might compete for the attention of followers, by bestowing favors upon them, regardless of their moral quality. Now, Meiri hastens to add that once the basics of proper religious faith are established, we need not be unduly concerned with details. To the extent that this passage does refer to Christianity, and it would seem it does, this means that the Trinity is one such detail. Meiri would be willing to recognize Christians as monotheists and to disregard the theological error that he would ascribe to the Trinity as a detail that 

*[p. 117]* ^pg123

one can forgive or overlook, as part of the overall assessment of Christianity. Thus, it is not simply that Christianity is a religion that has a moral teaching and whose institutions guarantee a moral society. <sup>41</sup> Rather, it is a valid religion, because all of the necessary components are present in it. The degree of correct theological understanding needed to recognize the God of Christianity as “God,” “divinity” in Meiri’s language, is found in Christianity. <sup>42</sup> 

 According to my reading of Meiri, theology is important not because it constitutes the heart of the religion and its message, but because it provides the needed conceptual foundation for the entire edifice of the religion. The problem with Avoda Zara as a wrong understanding of God then is that it lacks the foundation needed for the construction of a proper and healthy edifice of “religion.” In an amazing passage, Meiri applies this very understanding to Judaism itself and to issues related to fundamental spiritual teachings that constitute the foundation for the edifice of the entire religion. The context for the following discussion is the biblical prohibition on a prophet to order the worship of idols. Meiri extends the biblical and rabbinic discussions limiting the prophet’s power to order the worship of Avoda Zara to other fundamental religious teachings:

 A prophet who has been recognized, who commanded to add or to refrain from observance of a commandment, even temporarily, if the matter is the permission to worship Avoda Zara, one does not listen to him at all . . . And included in this is anything he (the prophet) would do with the intention of destroying a foundation from the foundations of religion, such as His existence, His unity (blessed be He) and the removal of corporeality from Him, and matters such as these. <sup>43</sup> 

 Meiri extends the notion of Avoda Zara to anything that might touch upon the cornerstones of the religion. We encounter once again God’s existence and unity. Here Meiri also mentions God’s incorporeality. This does not seem to exhaust the list of fundamental theological foundations, as the previous text shows us, and as Meiri himself suggests. The point is clear, however. Avoda Zara is not simply a problem in and of itself, but it is problematic because it undermines the foundations of the entire structure of the religion. Any similar undermining is treated in the same way. What counts, then, at the end of the day is not simply theological correctness, but the integrity and maintenance of the edifice of religion as a viable edifice that can continue to guide people and to help them in their lives and in their processes of transformation. 

 Transformation is a key word in understanding the very function of religion and therefore the importance that Meiri attaches to the moral strictures and disciplines that religions ensure. In several places Meiri juxtaposes human nature, with its physicality and animal tendencies, and religion. Religion and its guidelines for living are the medicine with which to treat the weaknesses of human nature. We saw this already in the quote above, where Meiri explains the talmudic statement rhetorically permitting the killing of an *Am Ha’aretz* . The reason is that he “does not know the nature of the commandments and the ways of religions, and he is drawn after his \[physical\] nature, like an animal.” We find the same juxtaposition in various places throughout Meiri’s writings. Meiri explains 

*[p. 118]* ^pg124

the talmudic saying that Israel do not come under astrological influences, “there is no astrological sign for Israel,” <sup>44</sup> in light of this juxtaposition. It is not, as the plain sense of the Talmud could be read, that Israel is above astrological influences by its very being. Rather, by virtue of religion it has the means to rise above any disadvantageous tendencies of body and personality that may be the result of astrological influences. 

 The ways of religion will make them turn from what would have been necessitated by their preparation or their birth, for religion is a potion prepared for whoever is sick from head to toe. And that potion is effective for all kinds of illnesses. <sup>45</sup> 

 Religion is thus a panacea for all illnesses stemming from human nature. Significantly, it is not the Torah but religion that is the panacea. The healing power of the weaknesses of human nature is what makes religion as important as it is in Meiri’s thought. True religion has the power to heal and transform the weaknesses of human nature. As this is a major aspect of the workings of religion, any religion that offers a method for control of the weaknesses of human nature and offers a method for their transformation is recognized by Meiri as belonging to the category of “religion,” and is therefore valid. Clearly, within such an understanding, worship and its various accoutrements are of secondary significance and will not be the primary consideration in assessing the quality and legitimacy of another religious system. <sup>46</sup> One of the consequences that Meiri draws from such a broad understanding of the workings of religion is that the talmudic adage describing Israel as being beyond astrological control should be applied to all those who possess true religion, and not to Israel alone. <sup>47</sup> Other occurrences that juxtapose human nature and the beneficial effects of the teachings and bounds of religion confirm the centrality of this understanding in Meiri’s thought. <sup>48</sup> 

 We may now return to the distinction between Avoda Zara and true and valid religion. It is not simply that Avoda Zara is the worship of trees and stones, idols and stars. Rather, such worship is phenomenologically of a different quality than true religion. <sup>49</sup> While it may be effective, it satisfies purely human needs and aids in achieving material goals. Hence the power of its attraction. In satisfying material human needs, it fails to bring about any real transformation. Rather than transform one’s animal nature it only feeds it, by satisfying physical needs and pleasures. True religion, by contrast, is transformative. It helps raise one above the animal nature, the weaknesses of the human body and temperament, even when these are grounded in astrological reality. And it does so by teaching discipline and providing boundaries that help curb the appetites and desires of the body. Avoda Zara is therefore to be faulted not simply as a cognitive error or an error in worship, <sup>50</sup> but as it amounts to a total phenomenology of the religious life, or of life itself. It is not simply that faulty moral behavior is associated with Avoda Zara. Rather, Avoda Zara is an entire construct of what false religion is and how it fails to achieve what religion ought to achieve. In this reading, the ultimate problem with Avoda Zara is considered in anthropocentric terms, the failure to transform the human person, rather than in theocentric terms, an offensive and wrong approach to God. The centrality afforded in Meiri’s thought to the 

*[p. 119]* ^pg125

boundaries and ways of religion and how these determine his judgment of other religions are rooted in a broader view of what religion is and how it operates. <sup>51</sup> In light of this philosophically oriented understanding of what religion is, Meiri is able to revolutionize halachic tradition and to reframe the entire approach to the subject of Avoda Zara. <sup>52</sup> 

 Meiri’s views on religion and on Avoda Zara are a function of the religions that he encountered as well as of the positive relations he entertained with Christians in his own context. In effect, by making “religion” the central category, rather than Avoda Zara, Meiri has eliminated Avoda Zara as a major concern for daily living in contemporary societies, both his and our own. But just how far-reaching is Meiri’s understanding? Has he completely done away with Avoda Zara, relegating it to the past? The theory of progress, upon which Meiri relies, allows him to draw clear demarcations between then and now and to suggest that major shifts have occurred in human societies, making Avoda Zara all but obsolete. It would seem that Meiri could accept a worldview and a reality that no longer considers Avoda Zara an actual threat. In the same way that various issues that were of concern to the Torah, such as slavery, may be obsolete, so too something as major as Avoda Zara could in theory be obsolete, due to the progress of human society. <sup>53</sup> Meiri himself, however, adds to the temporal distinction also a spatial one. Meiri recognizes that Avoda Zara continues to exist in some remote locations, on the periphery. Drawing on the geographic understanding current in the Middle Ages, civilized society is at the center and barbaric society on the periphery. Meiri has dealt adequately with any challenges that might be posed by his immediate religious environment of Christianity and Islam. He therefore has no problem conceding that on the edge of the civilized world Avoda Zara continues to exist. <sup>54</sup> It is interesting to consider one discussion in which the endurance of Avoda Zara may be more than a remote and theoretical manner. In discussing the talmudic prohibition of selling weapons to non-Jews, which, as always, is limited by Meiri to pagan religions of old, Meiri makes the following statement:

 In the days of our \[talmudic\] masters they allowed to sell all these \[even\] to the Persians, because they dwelt amidst them and they (the Persians) protected them (the Jews). And whenever a person is protected in the shadow of another, it is appropriate to aid him, as possible. And we too, in these and matters like these, and in all matters, we rule permissively for ourselves for the same reason, even in the extremities where idolatry has remained, because all of Israel’s work is done through them, and whenever we benefit by the things we sell them, it is permissible. <sup>55</sup> 

 This passage makes it clear that Avoda Zara does remain of some concern and we are given one example of what might count as Avoda Zara—the religion of the Persians. In such cases another basis is found for permitting the sale of weapons, but what is important for our discussion is the very recognition that idol worshippers still exist. Of course, reference to the Persians is taken from the Talmud and does not constitute an independent evaluation of Zoroastrianism or Manichaeism. <sup>56</sup> It would therefore make little sense to speculate on whether either of those religions had a moral code and whether, when judged in and of 

*[p. 120]* ^pg126

themselves, they should be judged as compatible with Meiri’s criteria for true religion. But Meiri seems to point to his own contemporary ruling on similar matters that involve religions that he considers idolatrous. In other words, the notion of extremities where Avoda Zara persists is not merely a theoretical notion, but is part of the sphere of contact with which Meiri has to cope in his capacity as halachic decisor. From this passage it would seem that Meiri himself ruled concerning certain religions that they were still idolatrous and that therefore talmudic legislation should apply to them. Meiri’s broadening of the discussion from a specific instance to all matters further suggests that he is reporting an actual practice, and not just a hypothetical or theoretical possibility. <sup>57</sup> 

 To what religions is Meiri referring? Anything we would say would be speculative, but in all likelihood these rulings would point eastward. Given the image and knowledge of India that was current in the Middle Ages, one could of course consider India as one such extremity. However, it is extremely unlikely that Meiri would issue a ruling for an Indian religious context. To the best of my knowledge, India, its sages, and its religious reality do not appear in Meiri’s works. <sup>58</sup> And it is highly unlikely that Meiri would be called upon to issue a ruling that concerns Hindu religion. <sup>59</sup> 

 Let us now move from the presentation of the possible readings of Meiri’s teaching to an attempt to apply those teachings to Hinduism. What could be a Meiri-based approach to Hinduism? The answer will unfold in stages, as we consider the different dimensions and possible readings of Meiri. Let us begin with the moral dimension, understood independently of specific theological claims. If Meiri posits the legitimacy of a religion as a function of its upholding moral living, as opposed to idolatry that encourages all forms of ugliness and sin, then surely Hinduism too should come under the rubric of “nations bound by the ways of religion.” <sup>60</sup> But let us ponder this further. Judging a religion by its moral fruits places us before an interesting challenge. In the history of Hinduism (perhaps of all religions) we encounter both the highest and the lowest, in terms of moral and spiritual values. On the one hand, it is clear that Hinduism upholds a highly disciplined life that is anything but the free pandering to desire and sinfulness that Meiri associates with idolatry. Basic moral precepts govern Hindu life, and these may even match requirements of the seven noachide commandments. <sup>61</sup> On the other hand, there are many expressions in Hindu religious life that could be construed as contrary to the sense of morality espoused by Judaism. Some of these are issues that are no longer relevant and some endure within Hinduism to this very day. Temple prostitution was a part of Hindu religious life, at various points in history. <sup>62</sup> This would certainly be a practice that is explicitly abhorred by the Torah and one that has close potential associations with idolatry, when we consider ancient Israel’s immediate neighbors. <sup>63</sup> Some customs that were common at various points in history may also be queried in relation to our sense of morality, for example *sati,* the burning of widows on their husbands’ funeral pyre. In a more fundamental way, we may find fault with the social teachings and practices associated with the caste system and its oppressions. 

 This raises the difficult question of when disagreement concerning practices and moral teachings is just that, and constitutes an important dimension of the 

*[p. 121]* ^pg127

identity and teaching of Judaism in relation to other religions, and when such differences are so egregious as to force one to consider another religion as not bound by the ways of religion, according to the principles formulated by Meiri. The question is, of course, not specific to Hinduism. David Berger recently queried the impact of suicide bombing on a view of Islam, in light of Meiri’s principles. <sup>64</sup> Could the god who tolerates the bombing of women and children, or the god who tolerates temple prostitution, be the same God we proclaim? <sup>65</sup> Does either wrong teaching, if it is indeed a teaching of the religion, or wrong practice, if it is the fruit of misunderstanding or misinterpretation of the tradition, render a religion that might otherwise be recognized as legitimate an expression of Avoda Zara? 

 The problem is twofold. On the one hand, if we find moral fault in another religion and consequently declare its god to be another god, we are in fact assuming perfection is possible and that any religion can attain a reasonable standard of moral living that would never be called into question. While in theory this is an attractive proposition and might in some way be implied by the ethos of the noachide commandments, in reality it is a standard we may never achieve. Upon closer scrutiny we are bound to find moral fault with any religion, without exception. Indeed, the other side of the problem is that Judaism too might not emerge blame-free from such moral scrutiny. Whether it be slavery of old or present-day oppression of other peoples, not to mention a host of other spiritual ailments with which we have been plagued over millennia, it is impossible to imagine a Judaism that is above moral criticism. It is therefore perhaps easier to use the negative pole of the definition, as indeed Meiri does. Avoda Zara is identified with wanton libertinism. Whatever jihadistic Islam is and however strongly we may disagree with its practices, it is not Avoda Zara in the moral sense portrayed by Meiri. And the same will probably be true for many Hindu practices that we may condemn on moral grounds. That acts that are immoral are committed in a religious framework does not mean the religion lacks morality. It only means that corruption has entered the religion and that a morally corrupt sense has been at work. But this need not affect the basic definition of the religion and its ability to point to God as its ultimate referent. Misapplication of moral, legal, or ritual principles is not wanton libertinism. 

 To this we might add that Hinduism itself has been steadily undergoing changes. It is part of the fabric of the Hindu view of history that *dharma* , the teaching and practice of moral and religious duty and obligation, steadily declines. In this sense, Hinduism accommodates its own weaknesses and imperfections within a conceptual structure that accounts for how such imperfections come about. The flip side of this recognition is that such moral and spiritual decline is the occasion for the coming of great teachers, who come in order to correct the upset balance and to restore the teaching. One classical expression of this view is found in the most popular of all Hindu religious works, the Bhagavad Gita. <sup>66</sup> The understanding that the present-day teacher has come to restore a lost balance is quite common in relation to various teachers and groups. For example, Swaminarayan’s identity, mission, and authenticity are understood against the backdrop of prevailing immorality in the eighteenth century. <sup>67</sup> The point can be made in relation to Guru Nanak, the founder of Sikhism, <sup>68</sup> and even in relation 

*[p. 122]* ^pg128

to contemporary teachers. I recall Sri Sri Ravi Shankar addressing a stadium full of devotees, alluding to his divine mission in precisely these terms. If so, it is possible within a Hindu framework to recognize that faults have crept into the system, while pointing to the purified tradition as the authentic or true expression of tradition. 

 Loss of teaching and its reestablishment is not only a purely internal matter for tradition. It is also fed by encounter with external civilizations. For several hundred years India was in contact with and under the control of the British empire, a fact that has had profound impact on some of its practices. The British did much to uproot those dimensions of religion that they considered immoral, notably the practice of sati . Encounter with the British also spawned various Hindu reform movements that have contributed to the present articulation of Hinduism. It is fair to say that Hinduism as encountered by early British and Portugese colonialists is not identical with present-day Hinduism. If Meiri uhpolds a theory of progress in religion, one could apply this theory not only to the contrast between present-day religions and the religions of old but also to different historical expressions of the same religion, in our case of Hinduism. <sup>69</sup> There is little doubt that when dealing with Hinduism today we are no longer dealing with some of the moral challenges that we might have had to deal with 500 years ago. For a view that espouses a notion of progress in religions, the latter-day forms of a religion are its most important expressions, and the religion should be assessed in light of those. 

 All this having been said, I would like to point to one important expression of Hindu religious life, though it would be dangerous to equate it with Hinduism in its entirety. In fact, it is likely that contemporary Western attention to the phenomenon—both academic and popular—causes this phenomenon to be viewed as far more central and pervasive than it is within its indigenous Hindu context. I am referring to the practices of Tantra. <sup>70</sup> These are based on a conscious reversal of the prevalent moral order in an attempt to transcend the duality of good and evil by intentionally committing moral and ritual transgressions. <sup>71</sup> Still, one notes that the logic upon which Tantra is based is a logic of reversal of the existing moral order, which in fact provides testimony to its centrality. Were there not a powerful moral and ritual order of right and wrong, there would not be anything worth reversing, as part of a mystical drive for higher union. <sup>72</sup> Tantra thus reinforces, rather than undermines, the view of Hinduism as an instrument of moral standards. 

 Having explored possible objections to the view that Hinduism espouses and offers a moral teaching, we are led back to the intuitive starting point of this discussion, namely that one cannot ignore Hinduism’s moral foundations and that these would be commensurate with Meiri’s moral criteria for other religions. Whatever we may say about Hinduism, past or present, and for that matter about Islam, does not change the fundamental fabric of the religion as espousing moral teaching and living. If Judaism has a moral vision and model to offer other religions, it may continue to do so as part of its work of *Tikkun* (global healing and repair). However, differences in moral teaching should not render the God of those other religions an other god, and those religions a form of Avoda Zara. 

*[p. 123]* ^pg129

 Thus far, I have reflected upon Hinduism in light of Meiri’s core notion of a nation bound by religion. Let us now move the discussion to the next level and tie explicit theological awareness to moral teaching. As we have seen, in some places Meiri does stipulate the knowledge of God as part of his positive view of other religions. The formulations vary. One formulation we encountered was serving “divinity in some way, even though their faith is far removed from our own faith.” In other words, the other culture must have a notion of divinity, even if different, indeed far removed, from our own. That sense of divinity would be more encompassing and total than what the worship of isolated trees, stones, stars, and idols would provide. It would be a sense of divinity in its totality, grounding the moral life in such broader recognition. I cannot see how anyone would exclude Hinduism from such a formulation. Despite the fact that in theory all is subject to worship, the governing notion is one of divinity in the broader sense posited by Meiri. One could, in theory, suggest that the distinction between a broader notion of divinity and the limiting of attention exclusively to some specific manifestations of power, trees, stars, etc., would be the criterion that distinguishes legitimate from illegitimate religion. Accordingly, it may be that some forms of religious life, some practices of individuals or communities, might be seen as *merely* the worship of isolated forces, removed from the broader notion of an all-encompassing divinity. In other words, it is conceivable that within the religious life of India we will find expressions of Avoda Zara, if this particular criterion is applied. However, it seems fair to say that the governing notion of the religious culture is that there is a “divinity,” and not only notions of isolated powers. This is certainly the case for the higher philosophies, the various schools of Vedanta, and other schools. But it is also the case in popular understanding that there is a notion of divinity, even if its practical expressions translate back to the worship of stones and idols. Thus, according to this basic definition of what is theologically required, Hinduism would again meet with Meiri’s approval. 

 Let us look at Meiri’s other theological formulation. As we saw, in another context, Meiri speaks of “nations that are bound by the ways of religion and believe in His (blessed be He) existence, His Unity and His power, even though they are in error concerning some matters, according to our faith.” Again, error is permissible or acceptable, but here Meiri does seem to offer some minimal theological requirements. These are existence, unity, and power. <sup>73</sup> At this point the consideration of Hinduism becomes far more complex. To what extent can the understanding of divine unity itself be part of the error that Judaism recognizes in another religion without compromising its validity? For the philosophical Hindu, there is no doubt concerning God’s unity. Whether one speaks of the absolute Brahman or of the preferred manifestations of the Divine that are worshipped, most Hindus would be able to subscribe to Meiri’s criteria, if we were to take them as a theological minimal baseline. While it is also true that many worshippers might not be able to affirm unity as one of the primary attributes of God, the more general question of who speaks for Hinduism could lead us to privilege the voice of the learned who do recognize the ultimate unity of God as sufficient for complying with Meiri’s criteria. In any event, I am not sure that the theological gap between Jewish and Hindu views of divine unity is significantly 

*[p. 124]* ^pg130

greater than the gap between Jewish and Christian views, and the latter, as we know, were deemed adequate by Meiri. 

 Philosophically, it is worth considering the relationship between theological premises and moral behavior and how these are related differently in the cases of Judaism and Hinduism. The minimal requirements of faith may be needed as premises for establishing a moral life. It may be that belief in God is needed not simply as a correlate of a religious system, but because without basic beliefs concerning God it would not be possible to uphold a moral order. While this makes sense in relation to God’s existence and power, it makes less sense in relation to His unity, and even less sense in relation to His incorporeality, posited in one passage as a minimal theological requirement. <sup>74</sup> The question is to what extent morality and theology are tied together as two components needed to construct the category of “religion,” or whether theology provides the foundations for morality. If the answer is the former, then we need not account for how theological faith motivates moral behavior. We need only recognize that a religious system is a valid “religion” possessing all basic traits of what a “religion” ought to possess. Meiri, in fact, does not present theological belief as the foundation for moral behavior, and we need not force this understanding upon him. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that for Meiri at least some of the foundational theological beliefs are also prerequisites for a moral life, inasmuch as they ground moral behavior in the operative notion of revelation. The one God, who exists and has power, reveals Himself, demanding moral behavior, that is guaranteed by His power. 

 This structure of thought can be applied in only a limited way to the Hindu context. Hinduism’s scriptures are not revealed by a personal God. <sup>75</sup> If we recognize the unity of God in Brahman, we must also recognize that Brahman is beyond revelation and beyond the active moral management of this world, including considerations of recompense. The Hindu worldview is highly aware of considerations of recompense and of the moral value of all actions. These, however, are not tied directly to the same set of theological beliefs that operates in Meiri’s Abrahamic milieu. While it is important to be aware of this distinction, not too much should be made of it. If the validity of a religion is determined by the conjunction of moral behavior and a coherent set of theological premises that condition such moral behavior, Hinduism provides us with an alternative way of configuring religious belief and moral duty. While many of the fundamental details of belief—God, unity, power—may be recognizable, they appear in different combinations, carrying different weight within the overall system and operating in different ways as they interact with the moral order. But whichever way we tackle the problem, we would seem to still be able to recognize Hinduism as a valid religion. If we look for the presence of the diverse elements as constitutive elements of a religion, they are there. And if we look for the theological grounding of moral behavior, it is there. The requirement that moral behavior be grounded in a particular configuration of theological beliefs, as indeed is the case in the religions that Meiri knew personally, seems to me to go both beyond Meiri’s broader spirit and beyond the specifics of how he describes the terms under which other religions may be recognized. 

*[p. 125]* ^pg131

 As is obvious from my presentation, I consider the correct way to read Meiri is not simply to highlight a governing notion, such as “nations bound by religion,” but to appreciate the entirety of his thought structure. Accordingly, the key question is not whether a given religion is idolatrous, or even whether it is moral, but rather whether it is “religion” in the full sense that Meiri attaches to the term. It would seem that Hinduism is very much a “religion.” In fact, Meiri might provide a way of helping us identify what a religion is and to consider Hinduism in this light. The particular combination of constitutive beliefs about the Divine, a comprehensive worldview, fundamental moral teachings, and ritual expression, these broad characterizations apply to all forms of Hinduism. But more significant in this context is the transformative power of religion. What gives religion as such its weight, in Meiri’s view, is its power to transform human nature and to help guide and intervene in the ongoing battle against the weaknesses of human nature. Judged from this anthropocentric and spiritual perspective, not only is Hinduism as much a “religion” as those religions with which Meiri himself was familiar, but in many ways it can serve as a model worthy of emulation by other religions. Much of its self-understanding focuses upon structuring life toward an ultimate telos that is in conformity with this description of the workings of religion. I refer here to the famous fourfold division of the life cycle, leading to the final goal of liberation, itself an expression of how religion intervenes in the battle against physical nature. The battle against nature has produced expressions of asceticism, sacrifice, and religious ways of life that make India a model for how religion operates in this way. Thus, if a religion is measured by its transformative power, in accordance with the core components that make religion a “religion,” it is clear that Hinduism must be acknowledged as a full “religion,” and hence beyond the specter of classical Jewish charges of Avoda Zara. 

 The transformative workings of religion are founded upon morality but extend beyond the moral realm to the higher regions of the spiritual life. The battle against the animal nature produces not only decent human beings, but also individuals who enter the spiritual life so deeply that they are considered as having made significant contact with and even entered the divine realm. This is a major draw of Hinduism. Indeed, Hinduism has produced and continues to produce holy men and women, as much and perhaps more than any other religious culture. The wanton libertine, who defines Avoda Zara for Meiri, is to be placed at the very opposite extreme of the spectrum, contrasted with the Hindu saint. While saints are few and they may be far more apart than popular Hindu imagination would like to admit, the fact that Hinduism can and has produced great spiritual saints, whose sanctity should be beyond dispute, suggests that God can be known and recognized within Hinduism through the traces He leaves in the lives of those who have come close to Him, who have known Him, and who have reached union with Him. In this lies the ultimate justification for considering it a “religion” in the fullest possible sense that Meiri could have imagined. 

 While realizing that Avoda Zara has on the whole been transcended, Meiri does recognize there are still pockets of Avoda Zara on the periphery. This notion may be reworked in order to accommodate the complexity of religious realities 

*[p. 126]* ^pg132

within the broader framework of Hinduism. While, in light of Meiri’s teachings, we may conclude that Hinduism, broadly constructed, belongs in the same category as Christianity and Islam, recognized by him, there may still be pockets of Avoda Zara within Hinduism. That is, there may be people or groups whose religious life is *phenomenologically* that described by Meiri as Avoda Zara. Rather than focus on the forms their religious life takes, we should consider the overall structure and value of their religious and spiritual life. The use of ritual, the appropriation of power, and the attempt to fulfill one’s own desires may be characteristics of a religious phenomenology of Avoda Zara. Meiri’s insight, when examined against reality, may be valid. People who pursue religion with such narrow aims may indeed lead immoral lives. But if so, Meiri’s phenomenology is of far greater importance than his contribution to the neutralization of the specter of Avoda Zara from Christianity. Avoda Zara emerges as a self-serving, nontransformative means of using religion or worship for personal ends, coupled with unbridled license and abandon of strict moral standards. For Meiri this is related to the object of worship. If one worships idols, stones, and stars, this is their moral correlate. But if Meiri is describing a phenomenology of religion, such phenomenology is neither limited to those who worship stones, nor is it found exclusively in religions beyond Israel. We note time and again that Meiri is willing to apply the same standards to Jews and to non-Jews, based on his view of religious and moral reality. If so, an Avoda Zara–like approach to religion may be found even within Judaism. Someone leading a life of moral abandon and taking up Jewish magical practices—a phenomenon that one encounters nowadays with the spread of some forms of Kabbalah within and without—would, according to this understanding, be lacking in what Meiri considers true “religion.” We are thus led away from the broad generalizations through which we seek to classify, categorize, and make pronouncements about huge and complex phenomena such as Judaism and Hinduism toward a more nuanced view that distinguishes between basic structures of a religion and the spiritual lives of individuals or groups within them. What is true for Hinduism will be true for Judaism. When Avoda Zara is seen as a phenomenology of religion rather than a description of the objects of worship, a different sense of the meaning of religion and how it challenges us to transform arises. 

 I shall explore the implications of shifting the discussion from broad pronouncements on religions to more specific and individual configurations of religion in the final chapter of this book. But before doing so, I would like to revisit all that we have discussed so far by reframing the discussion in a way that could help us get beyond the challenge of the worship of images in Hinduism.
