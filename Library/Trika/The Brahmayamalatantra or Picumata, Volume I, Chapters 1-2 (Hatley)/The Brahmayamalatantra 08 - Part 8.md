5.2.3 Vocative epithets The feminine vocative epithets occurring in Sivas speech to the Goddess in Devīpurāna 119.14—-73b are typical of the BraYa. These function rhetorically, reminding readers of the narrative frame and serving as verse-fillers. In addition to the generic devi/mahādevi, the epithets by which Śiva addresses the Goddess are as follows (here listed in the nominative): varavarnini (“of fine complexion;” 14b, 19d, 59d), bhāvinī (“passionate;” 15d), varānanā (“of lovely visage;” 16b), priyā (“beloved;” 17d, 36d), maheśvarī (23b), pārvatī (51d, 55d), sundarī (“beautiful;” 72d), and bhavānī (49b). Only the latter is not used in the Bra Ya at all, and it occurs in a section of Devipurāna 119 which departs more than usual from the text of Bra Ya 83. It is possible that zśvarī (“mistress/goddess”), vīramātr (“mother of heroes”), and akhileśvarī or perhaps kaleśvarī also occur, but in these cases the text is suspect.?? Most of these epithets are somewhat generic, but varavarninī and varānanā, in particular, seem untypical of the Devīpurāna but characteristic of the Bra Ya. The epithet bhavānī, on the other hand, occurring in a passage where the Devīpurāna departs from the BraYa, seems typical of the Devīpurāna. For its part, chapter 83 of the BraYā has no incongruent vocative epithets that might point towards redaction from another source. While relatively weak evidence, the vocative epithets of the Goddess thus suggest that the Devipurāna is rather more likely to draw upon the Bra Ya than the reverse.

5.2.4 Narratological clues

The structure of Bra Yā 83, summarized earlier in §4.1.1, is relatively transparent. After an exposition of the skull-rite (kapālasādhana), the text consists of a dialogue between Bhairava and Bhairavi alternating between narrative and didactic modes. It begins with the creation of the universe, proceeding to tell the origins and ritual meanings of Bhairavas bone accoutrements—paraphernalia of the Great Vow (mahāvrata). In contrast, chapter 119 of the Devipurāna exhibits less clarity of purpose and a more complex structure, linking up with events of other chapters and possessing multiple narrative layers. One particular layer, the innermost, has close parallels in the Bra Yā and BraYāSāra. As no trace of the Devipurāna’s outer narrative layers or links with other chapters surface in the BraYaā, the latter is, prima facie, unlikely to have been the borrower.

Spanning chapters 111-16 and 119, Khatvāsura’s story cycle in the Devṛ-purāṇais convoluted, being presented in a non-linear sequence interwoven with episodes such as the birth of the elephant-headed Vināyaka. The asura appears to be slain thrice—twice at Sivas hands, once at Vināyakas—and the deaths are presented out of sequence. Chapters 111-15 offer a reasonably coherent narrative: iva, roaming Mount Mālavya as an elephant, quarrels with Viṣṇu and an asura arises, evidently from Sivas (or their combined) anger. The

5.4 Implications

That the Devīpurāna adapted a chapter of the BraYa, and was in turn drawn upon by the BraYāSāra, has significant ramifications. On one level, the Devz-purāna, like the BraYāSāra, provides valuable testimonia for constituting the text of BraYa 83. This must of course be treated with caution, and not only because of the problematic state of the Devīpurāna: the redactors rarely adhered to the precise language of the BraYa, freely adapting their source within a purāṇic idiom. Nonetheless, in several cases the Devipurāna has proven valuable for resolving difficulties in BraYā 83, as noted in the annotation to the edition.4 On another level, the Devīpurāna’s use of the BraYā intimates how widely the latter may have circulated, surely beyond its inner cultic circle and indeed beyond the circles of tantric practitioners. 6.3 Situating the Brahmayāmala’s khatvāùga in material culture

As comparison with Vajrayāna khatvāṁgas and the Trika trident illustrates, the iconography of Bhairava’s trident-khatvāńga merits broader consideration visà-vis the visual record. While a thorough history of the khatvānga is beyond the scope of this chapter, a number of observations may be made that help situate the BraYa’s khatvānga historically. In the development of Śaiva iconography, the khatvānga emerges comparatively late, long after bone ornaments and skull-bowls.?5 In fact, khatvāngas appear infrequently in statuary prior to the seventh century and become common only from the eighth. Sivas association with the skull-staff must be older, however: in the Sauptikaparvan of the Mahābhārata, Aśvatthāman praises the god as bearer of the khatvānga (X, 7.4), an epithet also appearing in the old Skandapurāna (14.9a). Images of Siva as a khatvānga-bearer are nonetheless difficult to trace before the seventh century, and it never becomes a characteristic emblem: by and large, Śiva in most of his forms remains bearer of the trident. It is only with Bhairava and the goddess Cāmundā that the skull-staff becomes ubiquitous, though not invariable, alternating with the trident. Other sometime-bearers of the khatvāńga include Mahākāla and Kālī, Yama, and a plethora of Śaiva and Buddhist esoteric deities who appear in the visual record primarily from the ninth century onwards—Heruka, yogints, and so forth.

Surveying Indic statuary reveals numerous variations in skull-staffs. Early khatvāngas are typically stout, short, unembellished clubs with a skull affixed or embossed onto the thick head. This type is exemplified by a circa seventh-century image of Mahākāla in the role of door-guardian housed in the Vidisha State Museum (figure 6.4). One of the earliest bearers of the khatvānga, Mahākāla retains a version of this emblem even in his later Buddhist guises (figure 6.7). Short khatvāngas often have less taper than the Vidisha Mahākāla's, being more like rods than clubs, and may have decorative rings and other embellishments, such as a serpent entwined around the shaft and/or skull. Although my reconstruction of the BraYa’s khatvānga and of the object's history are somewhat speculative, these highlight the importance of material culture for the study of tantric traditions. While doctrine and ritual are no doubt critical to understanding Śaivism’s history, objects—whether images, manuscripts, ritual paraphernalia, or textual accounts of these—have much to tell us as well. With its detailed and often unique descriptions of materalia, the BraYa has much to contribute to this subject, especially in chapter 4. Analysis of the visual record suggests that Mantramārga Śaivas may have fundamentally altered the khatvāńga, transforming it from the club-like object utilized by Atimārga votaries of the Great Vow into the ornate, staff-like trident described in the BraYa. In both construction and symbolism, the Bra Ya’s khatvāùga has notable parallels in the skull-staffs of Indo-Tibetan Vajrayāna Buddhism, and draws on a common stock of Mantramārga trident symbolism. It would seem that, much as Śaiva groups continually innovated in ritual and doctrine, for instance by extending the cosmos upwards, positing new cosmological levels unreachable by their rivals,’ they also continually refashioned and re-envisioned their cult objects.
