## About

The oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the oldest scriptures of Hinduism, composed in Vedic Sanskrit. Hindus class them as śruti ("what is heard") and regard them as authorless. There are four: the Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda and Atharvaveda.

- **Layers:** each Veda has a Samhita (hymns and formulas), Brahmanas (ritual commentary), Aranyakas and Upanishads. The vault's document covers the four Samhitas.
- **The Samhitas:** the Rigveda has 1,028 hymns, about 10,600 verses, in ten books (mandalas). The Yajurveda is chiefly prose formulas for ritual offerings. The Samaveda has 1,549 stanzas, almost all from the Rigveda. The Atharvaveda has about 760 hymns, some 160 shared with the Rigveda.
- **Dates:** Wikipedia puts most of the Rigveda at c. 1500–1200 BCE (Oberlies allows 1700–1100), the other Samhitas at c. 1200–900 BCE and the Atharvaveda around 900 BCE; Witzel spans the Vedic corpus from c. 1500 to c. 500–400 BCE. Britannica dates the Rigveda to about 1500 BCE, in the Punjab.
- **Transmission:** passed down orally for centuries, with mnemonic techniques that stressed exact sound over meaning. Wikipedia says the Vedas were written down only after 500 BCE; Britannica says the Rigveda was written down about 300 BCE. Only one recension of the Rigveda survives, from the Śākalya school. Manuscripts are rarely more than a few centuries old; some in Nepal date from the 11th century.
- **English:** Ralph T. H. Griffith translated all four Samhitas between 1889 and 1899 (Wikipedia). Sacred-texts lists his Sama-Veda (1895), Atharvaveda (1895–96), Rig-Veda (1896) and White Yajurveda (1899); its Black Yajurveda is A. B. Keith's (1914).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls them a primary source for Vedic culture and ritual, and the Upanishads the foundation of Hindu philosophical thought. UNESCO added Rigveda manuscripts to its Memory of the World Register in 2007.
