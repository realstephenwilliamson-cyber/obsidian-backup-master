## The Unity of Research and Teaching in the Social Conditions of the Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries

*8 November 1961*

\[Peter Schneider:\]<sup>1</sup> Fellow scholars, ladies and gentlemen – today I have the pleasure of declaring our Mainz University Colloquy open for the seventh time. If we are to believe that time is the mother of institutions, we might conclude that our Mainz University Colloquies are at least on the point of becoming an institution. It may be pleasing that something begun under the sign of experimentation, and as an experiment, has become an established event. But every silver lining has a cloud, and in this case the cloud can be brought to mind by the saying that time is not only the mother of institutions but also the mother of boredom. And this is not the least of the reasons why I am so very delighted to be able to welcome you, dear Herr Adorno, to our group this evening; for, whatever anyone might reproach you with, there is one thing which your thinking cannot be reproached with, that is, with following the well-trodden paths of boredom. Yours is a body of thought in which sociology and philosophy are combined in the most felicitous way, and which is able to register precisely and sensitively the turns taken by time, most surprising as they often are. We are grateful that you are to introduce for us the topic which we have set as a guiding thread for our University Colloquy: the problem of teaching and research as a constitutive principle of our university in the conditions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. I now ask you, therefore, to speak to us on this topic.

*Adorno*: Honorary president,<sup>2</sup> dear fellow scholars, ladies and gentlemen, and dear fellow students,

*[p. 118]* ^pg125

This task, which I have accepted at short notice, which did not give me time to prepare a script – something which many of you, however, may perhaps consider fortunate – is not such that I think of myself as having been asked to give, say, an intellectual-historical outline of the relation between research and teaching over the last one hundred and fifty years, itself something which can hardly be decently undertaken while standing on one leg.<sup>3</sup> This is all the more the case as, in any event – as I was able to verify even in the few days I have had to prepare for this talk – even in the works of Wilhelm von Humboldt himself, who is the first person one thinks of in this connection, there does not seem to be any explicitly formulated theory of the unity of teaching and research, unless it is simply that in my haste the presence of such a theory has escaped me in my perusal of Humboldt’s text.<sup>4</sup> I think that instead I ought to take hold of the problem of this notion of the unity of research and teaching, and the situation today, in a substantive way, that is to say, by saying something to you about the possibilities and difficulties which were already present within these categories in the period of so-called classical German philosophy; that I should then try to bring out, in a sort of ideal-typical abridgement, what has changed about the situation; and that I should then at least say something cursory, since this is in any case unavoidable, about what might be done, in the present situation, about the dissatisfaction which must indeed be assumed silently to affect us all. Let me say by way of outlining my case that the unity of research and teaching is really the same thing as the concept of a formative education \[*Bildung*\], that is, that only in the ether of education can anything like this unity be imagined, and that, on the other hand, wherever such a unity is really achieved, one may speak of something like an achieved and realized education. I will therefore start by saying that, for the sake of this inner connection, I will not limit myself to the verbal definition of ‘the unity of research and teaching’ but will always treat this set of problems, the specific matter under consideration, within the broader horizon of the problem of education, and that at the same time I will at all events be looking into what is certainly a series of quite specific and concrete questions concerning this unity.

One might say, then, that without the unity of research and teaching, that is, without this substantial element of cultural formation – which I mention at first in this rather dogmatic way, but which I may be able to make a bit more detailed as I go on – that, without this unity of research and teaching, a university is simply unimaginable. Without such a unity, the university is inevitably split into a specialist training school on one hand and a research institute on the 

*[p. 119]* ^pg126

other. That is one aspect of the question, and such a case is certainly very plausible. There is another aspect opposed to the first, however – and I cannot help treating this complex, too, dialectically. This is that this unity itself is, today, extraordinarily problematic in terms of society as it really is, and, what is more, this is not to be seen as some sort of social failing on the part of our educational institutions, but instead this unity has become problematic from a theoretical point of view too, just as the concept of education finds itself in an extraordinarily acute state of crisis. I would like first of all to set out the conditions which make this unity of research and teaching possible and then sketch out a rough contrast with the situation today, as well as discussing a few ways of looking at the matter. When I said to you that this problem is not in any case dealt with explicitly by Humboldt, the explanation for this may lie – and this is also the impression that one gets when one reads the relevant writings of Fichte’s, such as the ‘Reasoned Plan’ relating to the University of Berlin,<sup>5</sup> or the extraordinarily important and significant lectures of Schelling forming an introduction to academic study<sup>6</sup> – in the fact that the concept of the university is already grasped emphatically in Humboldt’s writings, that so much more is meant by it than someone who is coming into a university today understands by the word, and that, in this emphatic concept of the university as a community and as a site of education, the idea of this unity’s being a problematic phenomenon is simply not yet considered. People usually start to think about objects of this sort only when they no longer exist, when they are beginning to disappear, when they are seized by the ‘fury of disappearance’, as the *Phenomenology of Spirit* has it,<sup>7</sup> or at least when the inner justification for their existence is no longer unquestioned.

The concept of cultural formation, as it \[was presupposed\] in Humboldt’s time, or, as I might put it, during the great period of German idealism, that is, roughly in the period which stretches from the appearance of the first edition of the *Wissenschaftslehre* \[Science of knowledge\]<sup>8</sup> – in 1794, if I am not mistaken – until the death of Hegel in 1831, this great springtime of the German university was indeed astonishingly brief. We always forget that this preternatural blossoming was really just a moment that lasted hardly any longer than the period which separates us from the early thirties. This whole sphere of German idealism presupposed the idea of formative development in a quite specific sense which was originally achieved in Kant’s philosophy, and which continues to play a decisive role even when, as in Hegel’s work, it does not really constitute the explicit theme any longer: namely the idea of the autonomous person, that is, the person who has come of age and undertakes to 

*[p. 120]* ^pg127

act independently and decide upon their own inner and outer destiny. This idea of autonomy as ‘maturity’ is inseparably bound up with German idealism, and anyone who has a feeling for the change of intellectual climate which took place after the first generation, that is, after Kant, and finds clear expression in Fichte will immediately detect that the concept of autonomy, which in Kant’s work is still relatively separate \[*chōris*\]<sup>9</sup> from the reality of society,<sup>10</sup> is in Fichte’s work already concretely related, from the very beginning, to the context of its social realization, so that here the autonomous subject is no longer merely understood as the transcendental subject, but rather as a society which gives itself its own laws.<sup>11</sup> Now, education, or the unity of research and teaching, depends on autonomous beings who are cultivated in this sense, and, on the other hand, it would like to produce educated people, in which respect this remarkably circular conception of reality is completely in accord with the climate of this sort of thinking: that just what is supposed to be produced as culture \[*Bildung*\] is supposed always already to be present as a substantial element, so that people may experience what there is to be experienced here. The fundamental change which was brought to completion with the unity of research and teaching may have been that, on one hand, this ideal of education, which was at that time imagined in a way which was both extremely drastic and extremely socially real, has collapsed and that, on the other hand, the society in which we live, to a great extent, precisely denies people the conditions which make such an autonomy possible – that autonomy which, as I have already pointed out, is the precondition for such cultural formation.

You must remember that the educated class, at around the year 1800 – that is, at around the time when German intellectual life was concentrated at the University of Jena – that at this date the German educated class was relatively homogeneous and that co-option to this educated class therefore did not cause any great difficulties, since even those sons of very poor and needy families who had to struggle along as private tutors, or in some other way, in order to enter this cultural class nevertheless already had an essential relation to education, as the sons of pastors or of teachers or in whatever other way, despite their depressed material situation. This is no accident. On the contrary, the fact that, at this most productive period of the German university’s history, the concept of education took such a strong grip of people that there was something like a consensus in education has to be connected to the fact that all those people of whom I am speaking here were, precisely, swept along by the wave of bourgeois emancipation, of the rise of bourgeois society, 

*[p. 121]* ^pg128

and bourgeois society’s attainment of an independence which it had been denied for much longer in Germany, where its development in economic terms at least had far outstripped its political and institutional development, than in Western countries. I do not think that we should think of this as a primitively materialist view of history might do, as if these people were participating in the rise of the bourgeoisie as a result of their evident material interests. You all know that the living conditions of these bearers of the German spirit were at this time thoroughly modest, with few exceptions, and that some of the greatest productive forces of this period, from Lenz<sup>12</sup> through Hölderlin<sup>13</sup> to Georg Büchner,<sup>14</sup> died in extreme poverty. It is rather that the feeling of participating in a total movement through which the emancipation of one’s own class is being brought to completion, the feeling that one is, if I may put it like this, swimming with the world-spirit, a feeling which is so very strongly expressed in Hegel’s philosophy, and above all in the *Phenomenology*, that this feeling inspired people and that it has something essential to do with the concept of education, which was thus binding for them, because for them education was – and I shall have more to say about this later – essentially identical with this attainment of independence. Now there is one thing which is quite decisive for this age of German classicism and of German idealism, an age which one might also call the age of German universities, and that is that, despite this moment of bourgeois emancipation – and all you need do to discover this is to read a text of Fichte’s, of the younger, earlier, Fichte’s – education is still moving (as corresponds to the actual historical situation in the Germany of that day, wherever no revolution had taken place) within the element of an enlightened absolutism. I think that the importance of this aspect in general in this context is, however, underestimated. The situation is that at this time the consequences of a fully emancipated education in the democratic sense, as they are reflected in our examination system, in the meritocratic principle, and in the principle of a bureaucracy selected on purely rational grounds – as these have been portrayed in an extraordinarily penetrating way by Max Weber in the chapter on the sociology of domination in his *Economy and Society*<sup>15</sup> – that, despite this already self-emancipating bourgeois ideal of education, this has not been carried through but, instead, if I may say so, the models or the schemata of education and, with that, of the unity of theoretical and practical cultural formation, that is, of research and teaching, were still drawn from a tiered and hierarchical society, and that for this reason all those problems which have now been thrown up by full social emancipation had not yet been posed at that time. There was thus at that time, above all, no fully unleashed 

*[p. 122]* ^pg129

meritocratic principle. And I should like to assume that the extraordinary, preferential, privileged situation, the intellectual and spiritual situation of German universities at that time, is precisely connected with the fact that it represents a moment, that is, the moment of the liberation of the bourgeois spirit, but in a world \[which,\] as Hegel would have said, still to some extent had at its disposal a substantial culture \[*Bildung*\] – that is, in which the ideas of what an educated person really was were adopted in a relatively unproblematized way from the aristocratic society of that time. I think that if one reads *Wilhelm Meister* from this perspective, especially *Wilhelm Meisters Wanderjahre* \[Wilhelm Meister’s journeyman years\],<sup>16</sup> one will on the whole find this confirmed. This is what has to be said first of all about the social preconditions for us in Germany.

To this must be added, if I may make a couple of sociological comments, the extraordinary openness within German universities, that is, the quantitative moment of the relatively small number of students. I don’t want to offer you lots of figures here but will content myself with telling you that, even in Hegel’s great course on logic in Berlin, a course which probably exerted the greatest attraction in the purely philosophical field, there were, if I am rightly informed, only around thirty students, and at a seminar of Hegel’s – since he certainly selected somewhat from among these thirty – there would not have been more than twelve people present.<sup>17</sup> An absolutely idyllic set of circumstances, a paradise, when one thinks of what a university teacher has to face today. There was at that time something like a *face-to-face*<sup>18</sup> relationship between students and professors, that is, there was the possibility of immediate contact, and the unity of research and teaching is indeed quite essentially connected to this immediacy of contact. I myself can always observe, *si parva licet componere magnis* \[if it is permissible to compare small things with great\], that when I am able to talk about my own work, my own theoretical work, to my students, instead of merely immersing myself with them in texts in a seminar or in lectures, that a different order of impetus comes out of this research, into which I seek to draw them, than is otherwise communicated to them.

Homogeneity, moreover, is fostered by the restricted number of those who belong to the educated class. Even today, the smaller a class is, the more homogeneous the cultural forms it is accustomed to produce, while we otherwise find the phenomenon of a social vacuum appearing. Please do not treat the rather crudely quantitative remarks which I am making with too much contempt, but I think that here the quantitative relations, the openness of such a society – in which the monstrous forms of the large corporation, for example, had not yet 

*[p. 123]* ^pg130

come into existence, and in which society itself in its structure was to some extent transparent to every educated person – that this openness made an essential contribution to education, because it is much easier to become educated in a society which one can understand – for this understanding is, indeed, education \[*Bildung*\] itself – than in a society in which this potential for transparency is limited. But if I speak here of the transformation of quantity into quality, I am at the same time, equally, thinking of a truly qualitative, a spiritual, unity. And I think that this is really an aspect which we are all inclined to underestimate, and even we so-called professional philosophers, since we are probably, in this respect, all under the spell of the intellectual habits of the nineteenth century, that we see just this movement, precisely, too much through the lens of the category of great thinkers, whereas in reality what was happening here was a *symphilosophein* \[a shared process of philosophizing\], and one which reached, in my view, far beyond the academic specialism of philosophy, something which we can hardly any longer imagine. If it was originally uncertain, before later philology clarified the situation, who actually authored the essay on the *Differenz der Fichteschen und Schellingschen Systems* \[Difference between the Fichtean and Schellingian systems\] – which was Hegel’s first published work<sup>19</sup> – that fact is highly symptomatic of this process of mutual interaction. I should like here to voice the suspicion – and here perhaps I can stimulate, a little, those of you who have interests in intellectual history – that German idealism, in the thirty or thirty-five years of which I am speaking, was not just a philosophy but the general movement of the whole spiritually and intellectually emancipated class in Germany to comprehend everything real from the standpoint of spirit. And only if we bear in mind this drive, this elan, which is in a very emphatic sense philosophical – that is, only if we bear in mind that all these people, from the great theoretical thinkers to the youngest and most innocent adepts, believed that the world is open in the sense that it permits itself to be comprehended through and through from the standpoint of spirit, because it is itself only spirit – only then can we understand how education \[*Bildung*\] and, with it, the unity of research and teaching had such force at that time. I merely remind you that at that time there was a sort of common philosophical language, of the sort that we can in our day hardly even imagine, which extended even to physics – I am thinking, for example, of Ritter’s *Fragmente einen jungen Physikers* \[Fragments of a young physicist\]<sup>20</sup> – and that this shared language, this medium or, as Hegel always calls it, this *Äther* \[aether\],<sup>21</sup> by which everything is penetrated, and which extended at that time even into the realm of the natural sciences, in which 

*[p. 124]* ^pg131

connection it may have been an entirely symbolic occurrence that in Jena one of Hegel’s followers – it may have been in the late nineties, I do not remember the exact date – who was an adept of Schelling’s *Naturphilosophie* provided medical treatment according to the method of that philosophy to a young girl with tuberculosis, upon which the poor girl promptly died.<sup>22</sup> I mention this death because it seems to me symbolic of a number of things, one of which is just this phenomenon of the coming apart of philosophical education and the natural sciences, which became glaringly evident for the first time with the philosophy of nature in Hegel’s *Encyclopedia*,<sup>23</sup> but also \[of the facts\] – and this is a much larger point – that the fate that befell that age, to which we so readily look back with romantic longing, was not entirely undeserved, otherwise the girl would not have died. And I should like to see this incident as symbolic of this, so that you can already see here, to a certain extent, the dark side of this unity of philosophy and education and, above all, of this unity of philosophical doctrine and concrete scientific research, two things which even at that time did not any longer fit together especially well. Perhaps I can permit myself for a second to digress by telling you that the phenomenon which I am alerting you to here, this peculiar collective unity which characterizes the consciousness of the epoch of German idealism, might in part explain to us the indeed prohibitive difficulty of the writings of many of the great philosophical authors of the period, especially of those who come after Fichte. They had so much in common as to their language that, if I can put it like this, as a result of this shared medium they were able to understand, within a certain horizon of haziness, even things which were vague and which were not formulated in a completely unambiguous way; and a good deal of what later showed itself to be questionable in this idealism, and what positivists of every stamp reproached as fuzzy thinking, as dogmatism, and as nothing but a kind of ‘conceptual poetry’, or as anything you can think of, is in reality connected to the fact that it is possible to understand much more in such a homogeneous linguistic medium, which is at the same time a homogeneous spiritual and intellectual medium, than can immediately be understood from the rational content of the individual sentences. Once the bubble of this unity then bursts, a great many such propositions and texts stand there, as it were, exposed in their nakedness; they are no longer housed in that medium, and it is from then on that they begin to present their real difficulties.

What I have told you about homogeneity is not merely to be thought of, say, as some sort of atmosphere, as a more or less vague mood surrounding this thinking, but it is in fact connected 

*[p. 125]* ^pg132

to the content of this philosophy, or to the content of this spirit as such. And what is indeed central to this content is the idea of the unity of theory and practice as it was first radically formulated by Fichte, when he inferred, from Kant’s idea of original creation, the idea of a free ‘*Tathandlung*’<sup>24</sup> \[‘primordial act’; literally, ‘deed-act’; in apposition to *Tatsache*, ‘fact’\], and thus extended the primacy of practical reason to epistemology. The unity of research and teaching is nothing other than the institutionalized unity of theory and practice, and the expression which it \[acquires\] in that period is connected to this. Wherever research consists in the freedom of thought, namely in production, that is, in not appropriating something given in advance but in original creation – and this idea of original creation is not merely suspended in the clouds as the deed of a transcendental subject but is instead in a certain sense demanded of every individual active consciousness – there philosophy or science also needs not to communicate cut and dried doctrines but to draw its adepts into a living process of thought. It is then no fiction that we think together with our students (while we imagine, without reason, that we knew better anyway), but instead this shared thinking goes back to the philosophical concept of original creation, and it is thus, first of all, a reference to antiquity, to the Socratic ideal, and the Platonic ideal, of *symphilosophein*, which was ubiquitous in that period;<sup>25</sup> \[it\] goes together with just this substantive moment of the conception of German idealism.

To this must be added – and I cannot go into the philosophical justifications for this at this point, but only formulate it as a thesis – that in this period spirit and world were imagined as being commensurable with each other, if not in the end, in however mediated a way, as identical with each other. The world is not merely to be recorded by consciousness and science, but it is to be comprehended \[*begriffen*\]; and it can be comprehended precisely because it is itself concept, that is, it can be comprehended from the inside out. Kant very strongly objected to this comprehension from the inside out in the chapter on amphibolies of his *Critique of Pure Reason*;<sup>26</sup> but if the development of post-Kantian thought agreed on one objection to Kant, it was on this point, and what consciousness has now actually lost – and, I would say, has lost to its detriment yet at the same time necessarily so – is just this idea of being able to comprehend the world, for it is precisely the view that the world as such is meaningful, a view whose truth was then self-evident, that has been so cruelly belied by the course of history.

Now, the activity which is thus posited, with this comprehension of the world as meaningful, is imagined not as that of the individual 

*[p. 126]* ^pg133

consciousness but as that of a universal subject and hence, if one translates this into the language of reality, as something brought about by a real society of human beings, that is, by thinkers, researchers, and intellectuals; and the idea of the subject as a universal subject, which first has to learn that it is universal, as developed in the opening chapters of Hegel’s *Phenomenology*,<sup>27</sup> this idea has this sense throughout that the universal must also at the same time be thought as the comprehensive community of spirits.

Research, then, effectively meant having spirit, and teaching means the same thing as putting spirit into practice. This is at its strongest in Fichte, whereas later, in the work of Hegel’s last period, at any rate, we can already see something of what we would today describe as a ‘neutralization’ of education.<sup>28</sup>

Education was not yet, at that time, education ‘for something’. The specialisms had not yet been consolidated. Science was to be identical with philosophy and was thus necessarily to include the speculative self-reflection of all the disciplines, that is, to include all the disciplines’ spiritual and intellectual consciousness of their spiritual and intellectual content. Philosophies were then described by using the expression *Wissenschaftslehre* (‘science of knowledge’, ‘theory of science’) or, as in the original title of the *Phenomenology* and the final title of the *Logic*, simply the word *Wissenschaft* (‘science’).<sup>29</sup> Today, by contrast – and this is a very telling sign of the difference – the philosophies which call themselves ‘sciences’ are really only those which are actually opposed to philosophy and are indeed anti-philosophical, such as those of extreme positivism, if one interprets such philosophies according to their own self-understanding. At that time, not everyone who reflected on substantive scientific fields as Ritter did, or as Carus<sup>30</sup> did, or as Goethe, for example, did, was branded a dilettante. A situation which, living as we do under the reign of terror of accountability to the individual disciplines, we can no longer even imagine, while everything that we marvel at in that age for its verve, its profundity, its grandeur of intuition, is inseparably connected to a freedom which, in general, every professor would today prohibit his students from exercising in the very first semester. I think that we must be aware of this opposition in the sharp form in which I have given it here, so as not to fall into a merely aestheticizing and contemplative relationship to those happy times. It is no accident that free reflection came to be discredited, but such a discrediting was necessary, as a critique of conceptual mythology, that is, of the idolizing of the concept, a critique which has now in fact been carried through in great philosophy. What is bad, however, is that the idolization of the concept was refuted because something which is 

*[p. 127]* ^pg134

actually socially produced, the division of labour among the sciences, was hypostasized. If you would permit me to moralize, ladies and gentlemen, I would say that we ought neither heedlessly to praise the past state of affairs nor heedlessly to regard the state of affairs from which we suffer today as a positive and progressive one. A moment of truth sticks in idealism, a moment which we should not romanticize but should recognize as a truth, the critique of this mere division of labour; and this moment is present, in particular, in the theory of that original creation which is supposed to precede any determinate or particular content in the sciences. Here I may perhaps remark that, if we manage to penetrate through to the experiential content of idealism – through, that is, to what underpins it as the ‘attitude of this consciousness towards objectivity<sup>31</sup> rather than merely to the individual and to some extent very problematic philosophemes – by tracing idealism back to its experiential content in this way, I say, to that which is condensed within idealism as its attitude towards reality, that idealism would appear in a much more favourable light than it does today under the reign of terror exercised by existential philosophy, which really treats idealism as if it could just be put on the scrap heap. I cannot show this in detail here<sup>32</sup> but touch on it only in passing.

Now, the unity of research and teaching, which has its substance in the concept of education \[*Bildungsbegriff*\], was already problematic even then. It is not permissible to take the spirit of German idealism, some of whose features I have touched on for you, to be the immediate reality of that period, nor perhaps even for its reality in any way at all, let alone ‘immediate reality’. I have already spoken of the inter-subjective, communicative nature of the subject which underpinned this spirit then. In other words, the unity of research and teaching, as its substance resides in the concept of education, was imagined as a community, and this community was, in truth, already retrospective even then, which is perhaps precisely connected to the aristocratic models of an elite that underpinned that idea. The collegial fellowship which is intended here is a sort of island amid a bourgeois society that is in truth already atomized, and the eccentric features, in part very peculiar features, which you can find precisely in the fact that the most radical of the German idealists, namely Fichte, was expelled from the university<sup>33</sup> may be connected to the very fact that what was devised here was a sort of Platonic and half-retrospective utopia, and one which has all the features that constructions of such a sort assume when they are radically distanced from their social basis. One might say that Goethe’s idea of the ‘pedagogical province’<sup>34</sup> lets the cat out of the bag, if you will let this misleading metaphor pass; that is, one 

*[p. 128]* ^pg135

might say that in this ‘pedagogical province’ there come to light the particular, insular, isolated features of this world of education which I have been discussing in general. And that is precisely a sign of the fact that this narrow and homogeneous educated stratum, about which I spoke earlier and which awakens in us the idea of a golden age of the spirit, in truth posited its very concept of education from above, from the concept, one might say, and that that concept certainly took on a substantive reality in that stratum, but that it was by no means a substantive reality in bourgeois society as a whole, in that society to which the musings of these thinkers, these products of bourgeois emancipation, necessarily had to refer.

Think, for example – to mention only something which already appears in the writings of these thinkers – of the fact that the difference (later portrayed as a difference of role) between what falls to the single individual in bourgeois society and the vocation of such an individual as such, that is, his ‘vocation as a human being’, as Fichte would have called it,<sup>35</sup> was already present then. In Hegel’s *Phenomenology* there is a whole section in which he illustrates the point that what the world demands of us and what it makes of us is in contrast with what we know ourselves to be.<sup>36</sup> And Hegel then in a way tried to get over this conflict, which he had already noticed, affirmatively, an attempt which has the features of impotent consolation and empty affirmation all too clearly written on its brow. Now, one could say that, as early as this, the university was already an attempt to close up this difference between what we are as human beings – as it would then have been put, having recourse to conceptions drawn from natural law – and our social function, that the university stuck with the situation faced by young people who were not yet fixed in a profession, a situation which gives them something like a pause for breath between their existence and their social role and which, moreover, coincides with a phase of their psychological and intellectual development in which they are not yet defined by their role, a phase in which they are not yet specialists themselves. But by virtue of the fact that by being limited to young people – something which recurred in a much starker way in the youth movement \[*Jugendbewegung*\] – this unity became a sort of exceptional state beyond the real production process of bourgeois society, this state of exception thus also has an element of romantic caprice and futility about it. This is the reason why, in the project of Fichte’s which I mentioned to you, universities are separated from society and are transposed into ideas of universities as a sort of elite sect or special order, ideas which do not appear in the work of Humboldt<sup>37</sup> or in Schleiermacher<sup>38</sup> but which are all the more emphatically 

*[p. 129]* ^pg136

present in that of Fichte – just as, a little later, the conception of science as the dominant power in life, analogously, appeared in the work of Auguste Comte.<sup>39</sup>

Thus, I repeat, in reality the difference between then and now was not by any means so radical as it seems to us, and I think that what also needs to be said is that Fichte, Schelling, Hölderlin and the young Hegel (I say nothing here of Schopenhauer, who already falls within a later period) lamented the rise of the over-specialized person \[*Fachmenschen*\], as that figure is seen today, and has been seen since Ibsen’s *Hedda Gabler*, in which the expression itself was coined.<sup>40</sup> Hegel and Goethe, the mature Hegel and the mature Goethe, who, so far as the central motifs of their position are concerned, are in agreement on all these questions, attempted – Hegel under the title of a necessary alienation – to rescue the *Fachmenschen* for spirit. In doing so, they expressed the truth that, without this moment of alienation, without the moment of self-limitation or, as Goethe calls it in his late writings, of ‘renunciation’ \[*Entsagung*\],<sup>41</sup> it is impossible – if, indeed, it is in any way still possible at all – to keep anything like a homogeneous community going within a society which is losing its form ever more acutely. The element of falsity, however, in the splendid ideas of both Hegel and Goethe, and the element in both of them to which they owed the title of ‘realists’, lies in their having assumed that this reconciliation between society and human vocation, a reconciliation by means of alienation and renunciation, was a positive reality, whereas in reality no such reconciliation ever took place in most people’s cases. The unity of the whole, whether that of society or that of the university as a site of mind and spirit, did not exist. Fichte’s and Schelling’s invectives against mindless academic professionalism could have been written today.

I shall only remind you once again of the fact that, as befits the origins of the educated stratum at that time, it was in fact, on the whole, quite without influence in real terms. The bureaucracy was indeed to a certain degree recruited from it, and it was at that time indeed to some extent required of an officer or of a senior civil servant that he should understand or be familiar with Hegel’s philosophy. But those who had been educated in speculative metaphysics in this sense probably had their hands on the actual levers of power just as little as they do today. And I think it is only if we are clear on this point that we can correctly see the necessity of the change which has taken place in this respect. If this element of real powerlessness had not accompanied the idealist spirit like a shadow, it would be hard to understand, by the way, why this whole movement should so abruptly have sunk like a stone and should only have achieved a sort of late 

*[p. 130]* ^pg137

flowering of a very artificial and forced and ephemeral kind with Schelling’s late call to Berlin.<sup>42</sup> The motif of the objective untruth of idealism, in other words, the point that what is actual cannot be held to be identical with spirit – and you will forgive me for putting this so dogmatically, since you will understand that I cannot go into these matters in any even approximately philosophical way here – and, on the other hand, the socially unrealized nature of idealism, that is, the fact that the world in which idealism was conceived – not Greece, not Hölderlin’s Greece, but a world of prose – was objectified and, even in Germany, existed before the industrial revolution, these are two sides of the same coin. And it would follow from this that the classical conception of the unity of research and teaching is already ideology in a very striking sense, in the sense that what we think of when we think of ideology – if we are not improperly to over-extend that concept – is really something that would be good if it existed, and whose untruth lies in the fact that this good thing is ascribed to something which actually exists, but which, in contrast to the claim that is made by the concept which forms the ideology, is not actually realized. Its untrue element is therefore this non-existence, or what then appears in Humboldt’s work or in that of the later Hegel as a sort of separated education and culture split off from social reality as a whole.

Now I shall mention once again the positive moments which would result if this ideal were actually realized, since we must indeed hold on to this positivity in the concept. By being united with its presentation and communication, research would no longer be encapsulated in different branches and fetishized, that is, would no longer be blind to society. So, for example, one might well consider that the one-sidedness of technological progress in the direction of a mere increase in production – rather than in the direction of setting up of a production process worthy of humanity, or mechanisms of redistribution, or of a relationship to nature worthy of humanity – came about precisely through the very fact that research has insulated itself from social problems and fetishized itself as something separate from them. A recent example of this would be the rigid dogmatism into which research has got itself by using a problematic conception of science with respect to psychoanalysis, so that psychoanalysis is kept outside, as something which supposedly cannot be realized by means of research in any strict sense, whereas many incomparably less substantial and valid forms of inquiry are treated much more leniently because they do not touch on taboos in the way that psychoanalysis does.

Teaching, on the other hand, would no longer be, as it is today, dogmatic, dead, cut and dried, result-like, but would instead stand 

*[p. 131]* ^pg138

in a living relationship to its audience and would itself be a living thing. It would lose that pejoratively authoritarian element which university teaching has in our present academic life, as I think you, colleagues, will admit it still does, whether we want it to or not. One need only think of the difficulties that one has even in getting students to speak in discussions to realize how serious this problem of the bad authority of academic teaching has become. I hope that, when we come to discussion, you will not allow yourselves to be tyrannized over by this anxiety and these structures of authority. The less teaching is separated from research, the more it will become self-criticism and also, thereby, a critique both of reality and of the consciousness which that reality oppresses.

The separation of research and teaching – and this must also be said with emphasis here – has its reasons not only in social organization, in, say, the rise of rational bourgeois administration and other things of this kind, but it also has a substantive reason for existing, that is, it is simply grounded in the immanent needs of research. The more developed and differentiated a discipline, the further research and teaching are to this day distanced from each other, and here research is usually ahead of teaching, which then in turn has its own very dangerous consequences for teaching, because teaching thus always seems to be a little bit overshadowed by research, and the students always have the feeling that what is being told them is not what they are really hoping for, is nothing to do with the reason why they came to university. This is one of the reasons why so many students are rightly disappointed with university. I should like to say once again here with great emphasis that I regard the disappointment of so many students with university as being completely legitimate, and that I also believe that it is only rarely the case that the individuals themselves are to blame, but that it is instead a question of structural matters.

Philosophy is a little better off. In philosophy, then, research and teaching – I hope I can go into this briefly – more readily go together, but this good fortune is a little bit precarious, and things are not really so good for us since this good fortune comes our way only because there is no such thing as unequivocal, definite progress in philosophy as there is in the positive sciences. Because we philosophers, that is, are not a whit cleverer than Plato or Aristotle were, it is therefore also possible for us to begin anew over and over again with our students on the same so-called original problems, while the individual sciences justifiably smile a little to themselves over this and say, ‘Ah, they’re starting to ask all over again *ti en* \[*einai*\], what there really is, or was, or what being really is.’

*[p. 132]* ^pg139

Now, the ideological aspect of Humboldt’s ideal, on the other hand – if I can briefly make this a bit more specific to the question of research and teaching – that the unity of research and teaching is supposed to be brought about immediately, in the nature of education, rather than being mediated by society as a whole, a society which is taken by him, as by the other thinkers of his age, as being immediately emancipated and which, apart from those famous paragraphs in Hegel’s *Philosophy of Right*, is not thought of as something which points beyond itself.<sup>43</sup> Education and culture is for Humboldt, quite as it is for Hegel, a department of spirit, and even though there are many passages which imply the opposite – nor shall I be so bold as to go into Humboldt’s philosophy in detail here – it is nevertheless at the same time an end in itself, which is separate from the realization of the Idea. A deep contradiction comes to light here. On one side there is the Hegelian and Goethean idea of alienation, that is, of the particular realization of culture and education and of the self-sufficient education of the individual for himself as an end in itself, while renouncing realization; while, on the other side, Humboldt nevertheless still upholds the common concept of culture and education in the sense of universal humanity; and his thinking was unable, for objective reasons, to overcome this contradiction. It could probably be shown – although I don’t wish to stick my oar into the business of an esteemed specialist in pedagogy<sup>44</sup> – but it could probably be shown how in Humboldt’s work these two motifs, more or less unhappily, toil against each other. The neutralization of education, so that it is represented as a state of mind of the individual’s, is a problem occasioned by an understanding that education is not fully realizable under existing circumstances, an understanding which necessitates this retreat; but, by accepting this situation as if it were something positive, the whole idea takes on a falsely resigned quality, although even here, ladies and gentlemen, one must distinguish very carefully, for without this withdrawal of culture and education into itself, this exit from and return to social reality, as education or science understand this matter in Anglo-Saxon countries, it is probable that neither the great achievements not only of metaphysics but also even of music, say, would ever have been made. Despite this, it must be said that the concept of education is itself affected by being split off and hypostasized. It cannot simply vegetate there in all innocence for itself alone but has to pay for all this in the way it is put together. There has always been talk of ‘educated obscurity’ \[*Bildungsdünkel*\] and of the ‘arrogance of elites’, talk which has represented matters as if there were some sort of distortion here which was simply the result of particular forms of organization within the university. I think this 

*[p. 133]* ^pg140

view is too superficial. For at the moment when education becomes an end in itself, by becoming an asset and a possession which no longer aspires to be realized in society, it absorbs that element of vanity, of thinking oneself better and of excluding others, into its own concept, and the distortion of culture and education into a vanity of this sort, and, in the end, into the bad class-consciousness of the academic group is, if I may say so, already tele\[ologically implicit\] in German idealism’s grandest conceptions of culture.

Now, ladies and gentlemen, I should like to limit myself at this point – since I see that I have not made nearly as much progress with what I had planned to say as, in my innocence, I had imagined I would do – to some observations about the present state of affairs. It is perhaps not wholly illegitimate for me to do so, since if I have not begun too blunderingly with my exposition of the concept of culture and education, and of the idea of unity in German idealism, I have already given you some indications of an outline of the way in which the present situation is different. If I can put this in a slightly reductive form, what has happened today to the unity of research and teaching, a unity which is inseparable from the idea of education itself, is something twofold. On one hand, the idea of culture and education has been presented with \[the bill\] for its ideological elements, that is, for its having separated itself from any idea of its actual realization.<sup>45</sup> Insofar as education and culture has collapsed, one might say, it is education itself that bears the blame for this. On the other side, meanwhile, the core of truth which lies in the concept of culture and education, their own truth-content, has been suppressed. The baby has, if you like, been thrown out with the bathwater, and it is believed that through a sort of despairing identification with the aggressor, under the name, now, of ‘the concrete’, of ‘the task’, or whatever else it might be, that precisely the limitations resulting from the collapse of the concept of education and culture can be affirmed as something positive and, indeed, that under these circumstances these limitations can be justified as something metaphysically higher. It is certainly the case that, in comparison with the social need for cultured educated people as it may have existed in the year 1810 – and one would need in the first place to investigate very carefully how things really appeared to stand at that time – society today demands technicians and the well-informed. You hear this incessantly, and I am almost embarrassed to repeat it to you. For this reason I would rather put an idea into your head and say that it remains uncertain whether this demand for the well-informed and for technicians is really required by the current state of the technical forces of production, that is, by what would be possible for human beings, or whether it is merely 

*[p. 134]* ^pg141

demanded by social conditions, for it ought already to be accepted that, because of the progress of technology and the consequent progress in quantification, every person is qualified to carry out any task whatever, and that consequently this specialization is not so very necessary after all. Whenever something like the necessity of specialization is bleatingly repeated by everyone, then (if I may speak as a moralist once again) it is appropriate to put a neat question mark against it and to consider whether the difficulty does not lie precisely in the points upon which everyone is unanimously agreed.<sup>46</sup>

What corresponds to this need – about which, therefore, there may be different opinions – is academic teaching as the merely undialectical communication of items of knowledge, without any active participation of the students. The participation of students in the university becomes a practice of replication; it carries on roughly as practising the piano does in relation to a musical composition, in the sense that, even if I have practised the ‘Waldstein’ sonata twenty-five times so that I can play it fluently, this still does not mean that I have composed the ‘Waldstein’ sonata. And there is something extraordinarily dishonest when replication is confused with productive activity. This activity, then, which students are to a large extent compelled to undertake as matters are currently organized, is no longer the production of something which has not been codified or foreseen in advance but is really a process of mere reproduction. And, at bottom, what this type of instruction delivers would be better done by means of reading. I shall only touch here on the question of the extent to which our form of academic instruction, which dates back to a time when texts were not universally accessible by means of printing, has archaic features, and whether we ought not to reflect on just this fact very energetically if we wish to bring academic instruction up to date.

Added to this is the equally familiar point that, once it is a matter, in universities, of communicating positive knowledge rather than of developing the capacity for autonomous thought, there is immediately so much to communicate – and not just these days – that on the whole there is no time left for thinking or for autonomous research, from which there results the contradiction that empirical observation in the sociology of education always comes up against, that the practical abilities and knowledge which have been acquired in this way are not very highly regarded by the actual practitioners, in business for example, but instead their insufficiencies are loudly lamented, while in universities, in the meantime, we are rushing about all over the place trying to be as practical as we can be, whereas what is delivered by universities by way of practical training is in fact particularly useless in the world. I am simply drawing your 

*[p. 135]* ^pg142

attention to this contradiction here, without wanting at this point fully to enter into its dialectic. Society can no longer count on the need for education. You have all heard something of the crisis of the individual, and I cannot yet discuss this crisis either. In any event, though, this crisis nevertheless has on the one hand this aspect, that anyone who no longer believes that he can determine his own real destiny as an individual in the world comes to think of education as a sort of ornament of which he no longer has any need. The generally perceptible demand for educated young people in reality relates – and we were able to ascertain this through a consultation with what I may perhaps call our ‘clientele’ on the subject of the results of university education in business and administration – mostly to the virtues of a salesman.<sup>47</sup> Whenever someone says that students should not merely have specialist knowledge but that they should also be educated people, what is really meant by that, when you get to the bottom of the matter, is that in order to sell cars better they ought to be able not just to talk about cars but also to talk about Rilke’s *Duino Elegies*, and, while I suppose this might do the cars some good, I’m quite sure it doesn’t do anything for the *Duino Elegies*.<sup>48</sup>

For the unity of teaching and research presupposes autonomous listeners. Yet those we have are hardly that, either in respect of their self-awareness or in respect of the development of their intellectual capacities. What is lacking, above all, is the element of spontaneity, that is, the element of an activity which is only possible and which is only substantive when one is able to believe that one is achieving something decisive oneself by means of this activity. The imagination, too, as Günther Anders, for example, has shown, is atrophying;<sup>49</sup> and what is also atrophying, as may be seen especially in America, is the virtue of memory and, in short, all the virtues which are associated with the individual as an independent being asserting themselves and endowed with foresight, instead of which the virtues which are in demand today are those of agility, adaptability and pliability. Just what is expected first of all from students, that is, the capacity to think for themselves, retreats behind technical capacities, which are being rationalized with the scientific need for absolute certainty. The ideal of security, which, today, is absolutely typical, has extended itself to spirit too. People still retain, on the whole, a sense only for such intellectual content as they can describe as absolutely certain; they wish, even intellectually, not to take any risks, and they then rationalize this anxiety with a false superiority towards those who look … all too clever<sup>50</sup> or a bit dubious. German idealism is certainly spoken of with great respect, but if anyone were in earnest to display the intellectual qualities which Fichte or 

*[p. 136]* ^pg143

Hegel or any of the rest of them had, then he would probably be chased away or, more likely, be broken by means of our pedagogical institutions in such a way that nothing at all would remain of this, of his human vocation. I always have exactly the same remarkable experience in this context: that when I have anything to do with students, that what they actually find most difficult is not what the traditional idea of youth regards as the most difficult thing, that is, to achieve self-discipline, to alienate their own work, to set strict standards for themselves – they can already do that, and they come to university already being able to do it, so to speak – but to let themselves think of something, to see something in things, to let something come up for them, something which was not already there in the first place, that is to say, the very thing that the traditional idea precisely \[proscribes\] to the young person who is not yet fully formed, that is the most difficult thing of all. And I actually had to have seminars, and now I am describing the situation quite literally, aimed at developing these supposedly primary capacities, that is, the ability to think of something, to see something in the matter being studied, and it always seems to me as though it is the old man, I myself, who am raving, while the young people take up an attitude of sedateness which sometimes terrifies me. You really do find yourself in a situation like that faced by a friend of mine who once had to teach his dog how to bark.<sup>51</sup> I don’t mean that students are like dogs, but that the natural and spontaneous impulse can be achieved only as a result of cultural formation, if at all, and that it is precisely here that the greatest difficulties arise.

Ladies and gentlemen, I should like, here, to be very open about the most serious question of all, that is, about the change in the purpose of science and learning \[*Wissenschaft*\] themselves, and above all in that of the human sciences, for which I myself am to speak. Science once meant the emancipation of mind and spirit, and with more or less unconditional certainty it opposed to heteronomous dogma the idea that we should only accept what we were able to understand immediately as something necessary. Today, this ideal has been hypostasized, this ideal of doubt-free certainty, in such a way as to oppose thinking. In idealism – and I already pointed to this when I was telling you about the titles of Fichte’s works – the idea of science possessed an enormous pathos. This goes back to the Enlightenment, to which, indeed, Kant and Fichte, and even Hegel, retained a thoroughly positive attitude, and which was only really deplored by Schelling and everything which followed from his work, in such a way that this negative attitude then, unhappily enough, became the common property of German thought in the later nineteenth 

*[p. 137]* ^pg144

century. If you read \[Condorcet’s *Esquisse*\], for example,<sup>52</sup> which I have been back to in the last few days, then you will see the pathos of science in a way which is almost moving for us. Science, there, is the epitome of those aptitudes by means of which human beings raise themselves above animals.<sup>53</sup> It is thus the instrument of progress. In the meantime, however, the course of history has belied this equivalence of progress and science. I need only give you the keyword ‘atom bomb’, and you know everything I mean by that. The progress of science has been connected with drastic regresses not only in society but also of the free and unfettered spirit. But the pathos of science, which dates only from the idea of this equivalence of science and progress in the eighteenth century, has remained with science, and it has become literally fetishized. Once this unity between progress and science was broken, infinitely many sciences, especially among the human sciences, have become an end in themselves and have therefore atrophied. And the only ones which are now flourishing are those which have command of the world-spirit, that is, the natural sciences, those which yield technological results. Many of the human sciences have no other \[*raison d’être*\] in mind – and I say this, ladies and gentlemen, without any reservations – than that of being science. In their hearts they know that this is not enough, and they draw from this a resentment which kills genuine productivity in advance. In order to satisfy this concept of science, which to a great extent derives from the history of the universities and, on the other side, from the natural sciences, even within the human sciences, everything which might give science any meaning is prohibited, because it infringes upon those rules of the game which were once intended to protect against superstition, but which have long since become a superstition themselves.

Here I cannot resist telling you a little story about a committee in the humanities in which a particular study was criticized for its complete lack of spirit and its stolid worship of facts, and when it came to passing judgement on it a gentleman whose name is well known stood up and said: ‘Well, if our colleague lacks the element of mediation by the mind and spirit, in this study, then that mediation is, nevertheless, present *eo ipso* by virtue of the fact that this study was indeed undertaken within the domain of the science to which it belongs.’ Now, if that is not precisely a \[case\] of fetishism, that the presence of the spirit which is called for in this science should be demonstrated by the fact that a study belongs to this science, then I simply don’t know what fetishism is, and I would think that if the human sciences, which are the disciplines closest to my interests, do not rethink all these matters in a radical 

*[p. 138]* ^pg145

way, and instead remain content with simply infiltrating certain philosophemes from outside to represent spirit, as happens in so many ways in Heidegger’s work, then the prognosis for our human sciences does not look good.

I will not attempt to tell you much more about the reasons for this phenomenon. The sociological reasons are just as well known as those which lie within the organization of the university itself. Since I do not wish to keep you here for too much longer, I may perhaps refer you here to my text on the ‘Theory of Semi-Education’, which appeared in *Der Monat* \[The month\] and which will shortly appear in the volume of essays which my friend Horkheimer and I are preparing at the moment.<sup>54</sup>

Just a few more words, ladies and gentlemen, and I really do mean a few, on the question of what ought to be done in this situation. First of all, I think it is a mistake always immediately to ask ‘What can we do, then?’ The spirit which immediately wants to do something is the same spirit which is no longer spirit at all. And I would think that it is important, at this point, to pause first. I think it is more productive to reflect upon the moments which I have set out for you, to become conscious of them, instead of too quickly to provide nostrums for how things might be made other than they are. The possibilities for changing the things of which I have spoken are socially limited, for we must clearly understand that the phenomena of crisis which, here in the university, we observe in our students and also in ourselves, that these do indeed appear concretely to us in the university, but that they are only epiphenomena of structural changes which are taking place within society itself. No sociologically naive university reforms will be of any use here, and this, it should be noted in passing, is one of the reasons why in our institute we have applied ourselves with so much energy to the research field of the sociology of education.<sup>55</sup> But on the other hand, because, today, everything that exists is underpinned by this social set of problems, and because it is impossible to intervene in society as a whole, one may not remain content with this. Otherwise the reference to society becomes an empty one and sociology a sort of universal panacea to which one puts off everything that can no longer be sorted out in particular and concrete scientific contexts. The problem of reform, then, the problem of particular change, is posed in a new way by the preponderance of totality.

I will restrict myself to a few words about disciplines in which I feel myself to have some sort of competence, that is, about the relationship between research and teaching in philosophy and in sociology. Here I should like to draw on what seems to me to be a 

*[p. 139]* ^pg146

very productive distinction which my colleague Dahrendorf drew in a lecture or in a contribution to discussion a few weeks ago at the Tübingen sociology conference – that is, the distinction between closed sciences, in which a given positive instructional content is simply to be passed on by means of lectures and open sciences.<sup>56</sup> In closed sciences, the separation between research and teaching cannot immediately be done away with but only reflected upon and, perhaps, in this way altered – that is, to distinguish more consciously between things as they are set out in textbooks and those which necessarily refer to living ideas. I could also say something here about a series of particular human sciences, for example about the discipline of musicology, which is very close to my heart, and I think that I could say some quite drastic things about the way in which the problems have been formulated, but I would prefer, in the end, to declare myself too to be a specialist, a *Fachmensch*, and I will stick here to philosophy and sociology, even though I am quite clear in my own mind that these disciplines are defined precisely by the fact that they are not disciplines in this sense.

As far as philosophy is concerned, I have the following suggestions, which are themselves only really headings. Philosophical lectures, if they are to have any meaning at all today, must be examples of reflection. As a matter of principle, they ought not merely to rehash what could more rationally be got from books. If Kant says somewhere that one ought not to learn philosophy but that one can only learn to philosophize,<sup>57</sup> then philosophical teaching needs really to understand the consequences of that. That is, the only form of philosophical teaching which really seems worthy of humanity to me is that of thinking spontaneously oneself, if one is able to, and thus to allow the students to participate in the process of thinking, whereas the congealed form of thinking, objectified ideas, finds its proper place in a book. When, then, an element of experimentality arises in the lecture form, when one has to correct oneself, or when one gets into a contradiction, then as long as one does not try to hush it up but instead follows it through, that seems to me to be the lesser evil. I think that Georg Simmel had the right instinct about this when, if I am rightly informed on this point, he used to lecture, in principle, without a script<sup>58</sup> and really did nothing other than speaking in words, just as one does when one speaks silently in order to write down something one is listening to. I think that this by itself would constitute a demand on the teaching of philosophy which, for all the risks that I can certainly see in it, can make that teaching productive in a way which it would otherwise not be, and my own efforts in this direction, however problematic they might be, 

*[p. 140]* ^pg147

have nevertheless strengthened this sense of mine by virtue of their effect on the students.

In the philosophical seminar, where we are working through texts, it is not first of all a matter of philology. Of course, there will be certain difficult texts, difficult texts by Plato or Aristotle, in working on which philology will be indispensable if one does not wish to be speaking dilettantish nonsense. But it is a matter of interpreting these texts and of their truth-content. I should like to propose here that the interpretation of philosophical texts is not at all possible without subjecting them to criticism. One can only understand what one understands in its own truth, and one can only understand the truth of an idea if this truth is at the same time an index of the false,<sup>59</sup> that is, when this truth is distinguished from what is untrue. And this is an idea, moreover, which was not so far from Plato’s way of thinking. Otherwise there is absolutely no way of understanding ambitious texts. When, therefore, I discuss the second book of Hegel’s greater *Logic* in a seminar,<sup>60</sup> and, in particular, when I come to the crucial point of the transition from ‘the ground’, that is, to the existence of the pure determinations of essence, the pure determinations of reflection,<sup>61</sup> just the point where the greatest substantive difficulty, in my view, where the \[subreption\] of Hegel’s philosophy can be found, then it is not enough just to set out what Hegel himself thought, or rather, it is not enough to set out what is in the texts – as to what Hegel thought, we do not in any case know – but we can only understand it when we take the substantive questions which the passage throws up with complete seriousness, and not as if they had all already been decided on.

As far as doctoral theses and *Habilitationsschriften* \[writings submitted for examination in order to acquire conferring the notional right to teach in a university\] are concerned, in philosophy such documents ought to be developed out of the substantive questions that emerge in seminars. Whenever anyone intending to complete a doctoral thesis or even a *Habilitationsschrift* is looking for a topic which, as is so sweetly said, has not been worked on yet, then this is a hopeless business from the start, and I make it a rule in any case in philosophy politely to remind people who come to me with suggestions of this kind that this sort of idea is not in accordance with the concept of philosophy itself. And these studies should not only be developed out of seminar papers, although the best seminar papers often provide a very good starting point, but they should above all be developed out of the motifs which emerge through living discussion, from which, on the whole, the most fruitful ideas emerge. The discussion ought to be looked at from the start from 

*[p. 141]* ^pg148

the standpoint of the fruitfulness of the original idea. I am thus also against insulating and locking up senior seminars with a sort of ritual; instead, everyone ought to have the chance to think as freely as actually works, and if it comes to light in the course of this that someone is blathering – and it is well known that there is one sort of student who can let no topic pass without offering a word on it and having something to say about it – then the director of the seminar has to be man enough, in God’s name, gently but firmly to get him to shut up. Without this nothing works.

I should like particularly to recommend, in this context, as a medium for this unity of research and teaching – and I hope you will not be cross with me if I speak so forthrightly right at the end of my talk and shoot somewhat from the hip with a few quite concrete suggestions – that a particular tool which has shown itself to be useful is to make a record of the discussion, which of course is a pedagogical tool. One learns to understand discussions if one concentrates on them in such a way that a record is made of them. Then this has the significance of communicating to the seminar in a concentrated fashion just which essential motifs have emerged from discussion. And then on the whole this record is also not just of pedagogical significance but also a research tool, insofar as someone who is skilled at making such a record can now really ascertain which new motifs are emerging, motifs from which further research can commence.

In sociology, so as also to say a word about that, research and teaching can be connected with each other in a simpler and happier manner, since here there is the possibility of structuring seminars as practical exercises, and also the further possibility of enlisting students at a relatively early stage in research projects, in specific investigations. And it is then always shown to be the case that when one does not simply lecture at students but, rather, actively involves them in relatively straightforward research tasks, that through such active participation their spontaneity and independence is developed extraordinarily well. After all, this is presupposed by the existence of institutes which are connected with seminars, in which research tasks can be tackled which for their part meet really scientific criteria and which do not, like so much small-scale sociological activity, have something amateur about them.

That is roughly what I wanted to say to you today about the problem of the unity of research and teaching and about the suggestions I \[would have\] for how it is to be shaped. I don’t think I can do better than to conclude with a dictum of Arnold Schoenberg’s at the beginning of his *Harmonielehre* \[Theory of harmony\], because this

dictum really does express what sort of ideal the unity of research and teaching is for any academic teacher who takes his vocation as a teacher seriously and who is not completely fossilized or sclerotic.
