## Authorship and Date

This Gospel was titled “According to Mark” in the earliest manuscripts. It is possible that the author is the John Mark of [[Acts 12#^12-12|Acts 12.12]]; [[Acts 15#^15-37|15.37]], who is called Mark in [[Colossians 04#^4-10|Col 4.10]]; [[2 Timothy 04#^4-11|2 Tim 4.11]]; Philem 24; and [[1 Peter 05#^5-13|1 Pet 5.13]]. Papias, an early second-century Christian bishop in Asia Minor (Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* 3.39.15–16), believed that Mark had accompanied Peter to Rome and recorded what Peter had said. Papias also considered Mark less dependable as an author than Matthew, since Matthew was presumed (based on [[Matthew 09#^9-9|Mt 9.9]]) to be one of Jesus’ original followers. This traditional view of the relative authority of the first two Gospels continued until the nineteenth century. Today, many scholars theorize that the Gospels were originally anonymous; the names were attached later to provide them legitimacy.

The shortest of the Gospels, Mark was likely one of the sources for Matthew and Luke; the Gospel of John may reflect an indirect knowledge of Mark, but is also possibly independent (see Introduction to John’s Gospel, p. 67). Although good literary reasons support this source-critical view of Marcan priority, it has religious implications: the claim that Mark’s Gospel is the earliest Gospel, a claim that developed in the nineteenth and early twentieth century especially in Germany, made it easier to downplay the Jewish practices of Jesus which are more prominent in Matthew.

The Gospel of Mark was likely written between 64 and 72 CE, the time of the First Jewish Revolt against Rome when the Jerusalem Temple was destroyed, several decades after Jesus was crucified. References to the Temple’s destruction (13.2) and to wars (13.7) as well as the depiction of refugees (13.14–17) could apply to the events of that period, although they are too general to secure a precise date. Cogent arguments have been made for the Gospel’s origins in Rome, where Papias places Mark, but also for southern Galilee, northern Galilee, or Antioch in southern Syria (see map, p. 70).

The evangelist (henceforth, Mark) probably utilized a number of sources for the Gospel including a Passion tradition (that is, the account of the days leading up to the crucifixion of Jesus), stories of healings, accounts of conflict with Pharisees, and parables. Mark’s Gospel also reflects a rich use of the Septuagint (LXX). A few texts—Daniel, Isaiah, Deuteronomy, Zechariah, and some of the Psalms—are quoted often. But while Mark quotes Jewish Scriptures often and generally places Jesus’ and John the Baptist’s preaching within the range of Jewish religious concepts, Mark expresses ambivalence or even antagonism to the Jerusalem Temple (see 11.12–25nn.) as did other groups within Judaism, such as those associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Although Mark presents an earthly Jesus and not the heavenly mediator emphasized in Paul’s letters (e.g., [[Philippians 02#^2-6|Phil 2.6-11]]), Mark and Paul share several important themes: the centrality of the followers’ faith, the emphasis on Jesus’ death rather than his resurrection, and reservations about Peter’s role. Both also assert that all foods are clean ([[Mark 07#^7-19|Mk 7.19]]; [[Romans 14#^14-20|Rom 14.20]]). Paul defines himself as the “apostle to the Gentiles” ([[Romans 11#^11-13|Rom 11.13]]), and Mark does highlight aspects of a Gentile mission: Jesus goes to Gentile areas such as the region of Tyre where the Gentile Syrophoenician woman engages him (7.24–30). He also says (13.10) that the good news must be announced to all nations (but it should be noted that Jewish sources also speak of Gentiles coming to worship the God of Israel, e.g., Jonah; [[Zechariah 08#^8-22|Zech 8.22–23]]).

However, the evidence often adduced that Mark was a Gentile or advocated a law-free mission is ambiguous. In its favor are Mark’s apparent inaccuracy on Jewish practices and his statement that Jesus “declared all foods clean” (7.19); the phrase is absent in Matthew’s parallel story ([[Matthew 15#^15-1|Mt 15.1–20]]). Mark’s counting of days from sunrise instead of sundown (14.1,12) is unusual, but it is known in ancient Judaism (Josephus, *Ant.* 6.248,336; *m. Pesah.* 5.1). Just as it is not clear where Mark was written, it is also not clear whether Mark was Jewish or Gentile, whether the Gospel’s first audience was predominantly Jewish or Gentile, or whether Mark advocated the abrogation of the laws of Torah to facilitate a Gentile mission. What we do know is that at some point the Gospel became associated with Rome and with both Pauline views of a Gentile mission and the abrogation of Jewish practice.

## Key Themes

Each Gospel offers a distinct picture of Jesus; for Mark, Jesus is a divinely favored if not divine figure who recollects Isaiah’s suffering servant (Isa 52–53), the psalms of lament (e.g., Ps 22), and the persecuted righteous person (Wis 2–5). Mark recounts how Jesus is rejected by his family (3.19b–21), townspeople (6.1–6), Gentiles who witness his healing power (5.17), Peter (8.32; 14.71), other disciples (14.50), chief priests and Sanhedrin, and the “crowd” influenced by them (ch 14; 15.8,11,15), those who “passed by” his cross (15.29), the two men crucified with him (15.32), and finally, even—apparently—by God (see 15.34n., and “Jesus’, ’Cry of Dereliction,‘ ” p. 103).

*[p. 68]* ^pg99

It is often assumed that Mark presents a very human Jesus (called “low Christology”) because the idea of a fully divine Christ had not yet been articulated at the time of the Gospel’s composition. But “high Christol-ogies” of a divine or pre-existent Christ existed at a very early stage (e.g., [[Philippians 02#^2-6|Phil 2.6–11]]). Further, not only does Mark three times have Jesus predict his vindication (8.31; 9.31; 10.34), by depicting Jesus as walking on water (6.48) and then as transfigured (9.2), Mark sometimes suggests that Jesus is divine. Yet elsewhere, divine claims for Jesus are minimized (see 16.5n.). At times Jesus is even said to lack power (6.5; see also 14.36; 15.34). Mark emphasizes instead Jesus’ suffering and death as a ransom (10.45). It is probably for this reason that Mark ends not with a triumphant resurrection scene, but with frightened women fleeing from Jesus’ empty tomb (16.8). Mark’s audience knows, however, that their silence is not the end of the story.

This silence also speaks, ironically, to another Marcan theme: the messianic secret. Numerous times Jesus commands people to keep silent about his miracles or his identity. These commands could be a form of dramatic irony used to increase awe of Jesus (esp. because the audience knows the true meaning of the secret). They may suggest that keeping a low profile is the best policy in a setting where governments distrust charismatic leaders (as seen not only in Jesus’ death by crucifixion but also by the death of John the Baptist at the hands of Herod Antipas). The motif may be part of Mark’s Christology: the Gospel insists that Jesus’ messianic identity necessarily includes suffering and that Jesus dies “as a ransom” (10.45); his role thus cannot be fully understood until after his crucifixion. The German scholar William Wrede (1901) attributed these commands for secrecy not to Jesus but to the evangelist: Wrede suggested that the author sought to explain why more people did not embrace Jesus as messiah during his lifetime. Although the secrecy motif is adopted by Matthew and Luke, it is almost totally lacking in John. How the motif functions in the narrative, as well as the question of its origin (from Jesus? the oral tradition? Mark?), remain debated issues.

The texts of the New Testament reflect a variety of views about whether Gentiles will be entering the community in large numbers, and an equally broad array of views concerning to what extent Jewish law must be maintained as before. One of the most interesting aspects of the Gospel of Mark is its ambiguity on these points. Long considered a sure case of a Gentile-oriented, post-law text, each passage in Mark is now being questioned in this regard. The reason for the ambiguity may simply be that the author is writing before the clearer terms of the debate emerged. Mark may have been much more typical of the debates within the Judaism of the first century than has been generally assumed.
