Tambiah’s main argument goes a significant step further, as he sees the two basic orientations as being equally “inherent” in human beings. They are part of man’s anthropological nature (which does not rule out of course that their actual expression and evaluation is socially constructed) and constantly run parallel. Both orientations coexist simultaneously in one and the same culture, in one and the same person — even if their position in the formulation of collective identities varies between societies, as we would continue Tambiah’s argument. Here the degree of differentiation in a society probably plays a lesser role than is often supposed. Very complex and territorially larger societies such as those of the god-kingdoms of Ancient Egypt or of the Incas, can have a central myth as their motivating idea, through which they formulate their collective identity, like the myth of the god-king. Europeans, on the other hand, trace their history back to small city-states like the Athenian polis, which did not have that type of identity (such as identifying with its city hero Theseus). Its identity developed much more pragmatically via the exemplary Athenian civic sense, the quality of its educational institutions and very generally via the historical successes of the city of Athens, such as fighting off the Persians. Let us look first of all at two ‘thought worlds’ occurring in India, whose polarity still corresponds totally with Tambiah’s axioms in the most extreme form: the Vedic god Rudra, as characterized in the \$Íata-rudriya litany, and the atom (anu) of the Vaiśesika philosophers. What is a Rudra, and what is an atom? 

Rudra is a person, he has bows and arrows, yellow hair, and a blue throat. He is extremely dangerous to livestock and human beings, but he is also the physician who heals. Other statements assert that Rudra is the flood, the storm and the beaten drum. Yet other passages give the impression that Rudra is everything that has anything to do with battles and war. The confusion is complete when there is suddenly talk not only of one Rudra, but of Rudras in the plural, then even of a thousand, and finally again only one is addressed. There is one thing that Rudra definitely is not: a single individual, since even the number of Rudras is obviously not clearly defined. But what is absolutely defined is the mythical substance: Rudra is always the wild and catastrophic one, sometimes even the murderous one. But in a remarkable dialectic shift, he is also the precise opposite of what is lethal and wicked: he is what heals and rescues. In all his aspects, Rudra represents what is liminal and “exceptional” — which cannot be localized in either good or evil. Apparently there are no defining limits to what can be called Rudra - perhaps it would be better not to speak of Rudra at all, but of “that which is Rudric.” But then everything that is “Rudric” would in some way be Rudra himself. Such crossing of boundaries can only be explained by the “law of participation.” Entities such as Rudra do not exist at all except through participation. Rudra ultimately only exists in the things which he inhabits. How it is possible that there are a thousand Rudras, but that these thousand are themselves only one, becomes comprehensible only via the explanatory model of participation. 

Like Democritus’ atom, the atom of the philosophical Vaiśeṣikas is the precise opposite of this. It is a totally independent, separate entity. Atoms can only exist divided from one another. Atoms have no consubstantiality. We can make earth wet with water, but an atom of earth

*[p. 303]* ^pg327

that is surrounded by atoms of water does not get wet, but instead remains what it is, an atom of earth. Atoms have nothing at all to do with perception via the senses. In the first place, they are conceived of as absolute individual existence. In the case of atomistic thought systems one can speak of total emancipation. This is particularly true of the Vaiśeṣikas, because they also imagine the soul to be an atom devoid of all properties, while our Western ideas of the spirit or psyche still retain much from fluid participatory thinking. This means that we Westerners are not at all at the zenith of causalistic thought, since rigid Cartesian thinking has separated the spirit completely from any causation — which does not mean that there were no attempts to causalize the spirit, too, as in dialectic materialism or Foucault’ s political theory of discourse. 

Just like Rudra, atoms are also ultimately only ideas. Just as no-one has ever seen a real Rudra, no-one has ever seen a real atom. Of course matter exists, which one can explain as being built up of atoms, but there are also wars and diseases, which one can similarly explain very effectively as the presence of a destructive spirit or god such as Rudra, even though we would hardly ever do so in the West today. Both phenomena — matter and wars — are just as real as all other phenomena. The ideas that we associate with them are something quite different. Explanatory models do not always have to do with reality, but always have a great deal to do with socialization and habits. However, what makes the difference is doubtless how we explain the world to ourselves. In the Rudra model there is no reducible individual existence at all; there is only Rudra’s existence, while in the atom model every particle really exists in itself and quite alone. First of all one thinks the atom, and then the relations that the atom has. So the relatum must come before the relations. With Rudra, the situation is reversed: here the relations come before the relatum, i.e. if there was no war, flooding or disease, there would be no Rudra either. 

Therefore, everything that Tambiah says about causality can be better explained ontologically in terms of individualization, or the idea of an existence as an individual. Atomic individualization is central to Tambiah, too, but he regards it as a hyponym of causality. From a philosophical and cross-cultural standpoint, this is problematic, since there are civilizations that cannot always be forced to fit Tambiah’s scheme of participation and causality. 

Even the Indian linguistic philosopher Bhartrhari, with his highly abstract reflections, his question of where meaning comes from, and his thesis of the linguistic constitution of reality, does not fit into Tambiah’s model at all, since for Bhartrhari, causal patterns of explanation are not of the slightest importance. Ultimately Bhartrhari does not even need causality, since all individual existence arises through the transmutation

of an original One to a plurality. He also applies this model to time. All divisions into individual pieces and into sequences are a function of empirical consciousness and not a function of existence. Only the whole is real. An ethnographer may put up the counter-argument that such elitist speculations have nothing to do with him, and that primarily he is only interested in the man in the street. But in fact the form of thought outlined by Bhartrhari is found even in the simple village Tantrist: the whole Tantric system requires (at least implicitly) precisely that form of thought explicitly articulated by Bhartrhari. 

So, there are some highly abstract systems that are not causalistic. We will therefore use the term “emancipation” instead of the term “causalism.”\*\* For not only Bhartrhari’s philosophy shows that there are reflective systems that do not lead into the total emancipation of things from one another (as in modern science), but instead develop quite different abstractions. At a basic level we are quite familiar with philosophical theorems such as those of Bhartrhari. One only has to think of Parmenides’ (0 on (“the existence”) and Plotinus’ hen (“the one”). But for us Westerners they are less important in the intellectual history of mankind and remain rather “strange birds,” who apparently were seldom able to leave the elite circle and who had much less influence on the development of cultural self-awareness than Aristotle or Descartes. Although a fundamental change is taking place today in New-Age-type spirituality, which has a relatively large following (cf. our chap. 6), this did still not become a majority culture. 

There are not only highly reflective systems that are not causalistic, but there are also participatory ‘thought worlds’ building upon causal relations, such as magic. If magic has been labeled “irrational” and “illogical,” then this only shows primarily how unreflectedly the terms “rationality” and “logic” are generally used. The problem is that these terms have a primary meaning and a secondary meaning, which are often confounded. Logic can firstly be understood as being derived from logos, referring to a form of thought based on terminological, dialectic and abstract aspects. If magic is understood in this way then it is not logical, because it forms no terms, does not argue dialectically and knows no abstractions. But on the other hand if, following a more modern use of language, logic is understood as consistent conclusions and comprehensibility, magic is not at all illogical, but is strictly consistent and comprehensible. Here again in the theoretical field, it is the key difference between participation and emancipation that makes it possible to examine the sensory aesthetic and affective dimensions of the way one handles media, different reality domains, abstractions, objectivization, formal rationality, purposeful action, etc., and therefore to describe acting with experience, and a practice that does not have its meaning removed by scientific representation. The key dichotomy of participation and emancipation allows more subtle analytical procedures for intercultural and intracultural continuities and variances than the dichotomy of orality and literacy, and can do more justice to the interplay between media, spirit and culture than the literacy thesis and its one-sided focus on logocentricity can. It helps shedding light on the dynamics of historical shifts and transfers in the past and modern globalized culture. 

Admittedly, participation and emancipation are terms founded in cultural philosophy and are not empirical entities like orality and literacy. 

They require a certain amount of explanation and are better thought of in narrative and intuitive terms than by using just terminology. Similar to the term “sonality,” the binary pair of “participatory/emancipatory” is an abductive construction inasfar as the formation of the pair aims to capture the mental draft of a new, meaning-generating rule.
