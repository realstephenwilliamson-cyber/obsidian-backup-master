> [!quote] Me
> What do we know historically about the community that created and preserved the [[Hub - Gospel of John|Gospel of John]] in its earliest forms?

Honestly less than the confident summaries suggest, and the gap between what's attested and what's reconstructed is very wide.

**The hard floor.** External evidence gives us almost nothing about a community and only a little about the text. 𝔓52 puts John in Egypt sometime in the second century — the traditional c. 125 date rests on palaeography that Nongbri showed permits a range stretching toward 200. 𝔓66 and 𝔓75 around 200. Patristic attribution begins with Irenaeus placing "John the disciple" at Ephesus, backed by Polycrates' letter to Victor and by Papias' obscure reference to a John the elder — but Ephesus could as easily be second-century inference from the Apocalypse as independent memory. The earliest *use* is telling: Heracleon writes the first known commentary in the 170s and he's a Valentinian, with Ptolemy already glossing the prologue. Charles Hill has argued against the old "orthodox Johannophobia" thesis, but the reception record still shows the Gospel circulating first among readers proto-orthodoxy would disown.

**What the text itself implies.** The strongest evidence for a group is not the Gospel but the Epistles, and it's evidence of a network rather than a community: [[1 John 02#^2-19|1 John 2:19]] records a schism over Christology, 3 John shows an "elder" whose authority Diotrephes can refuse across some distance, 2 John writes to a congregation not his own. That's a cluster of house churches with contested leadership, held together by letters. Add 21:24's οἴδαμεν — a "we" vouching for a written testimony, immediately followed by 21:25's singular οἶμαι — and the seams (14:31 into 18:1, the two endings), and you have real evidence of a text edited and transmitted by more than one hand over time. That much is secure. It's much less than "a community whose history the narrative encodes."

**The reconstruction and its erosion.** Martyn's two-level drama and Brown's phased history built an entire social biography out of mirror-reading — the ἀποσυνάγωγος verses as expulsion, chapter 4 as a Samaritan influx, 12:42 as crypto-Christians. Kimelman took out the load-bearing beam: no first-century universal *birkat ha-minim* functioning as excommunication, the *notzrim* Geniza text late and local. Then Bauckham and Klink attacked the method itself — the assumption that a Gospel mirrors one sectarian group is an assumption, not a finding, and the inference from narrative detail to community event is circular. What has aged best is Meeks' 1972 essay, precisely because it claims less: not what happened to the group, but what the Gospel's idiom *does* to a reader, reinforcing boundaries by rendering itself opaque to outsiders. That's a claim about function, and it survives the collapse of the history.

**Where that leaves the question.** Location undetermined (Ephesus, Syria, Transjordan all defended). Date conventionally 90–110, but that range was calibrated to the expulsion argument and should widen now that the argument has. The Jerusalem topography — Bethesda's five porticoes, Siloam — is good and archaeologically confirmed, which argues for reliable tradition regardless of composition date. And Boyarin's work means the group need not be outside Judaism at all.

For your purposes the erosion is useful rather than costly. Your reading of 9:22 as boundary maintenance competently executed doesn't require Martyn's dating to be right — it works whether the agreement is 30s, 90s, or literary. Which is worth saying explicitly somewhere, since a reviewer will assume you've inherited Martyn's framework along with his verse.

> [!quote] Me
> I'm not sure any of that constitutes enough to really say anything. 
> The consensus is that the Epistles and the Gospel really are linked? Does anyone hold the authorship is the same pen or redactor? To me, it is much easier to read as what we'd now call Gnostic than Orthodox.

Your skepticism is right, and I'd sharpen it: we can't do social history here, but we can do reception history, and that's a different kind of knowledge rather than an absence of it. The question "who were they" has no answer; "who read it first and how" does.

**On the link.** It's about as secure as anything in the field — shared idiom (μένω, light/darkness, ἀλήθεια, born of God), and 1 John's opening deliberately playing the prologue back. What's disputed is the nature of the link, and the differences are not small: 1 John has futurist eschatology and an antichrist, ἱλασμός at 2:2 and 4:10, no παράκλητος-as-Spirit but a χρῖσμα, and zero Scripture citation in a corpus whose Gospel is saturated with it.

Same-pen is a live minority position — Westcott and Brooke held it, Marshall defends it, and Judith Lieu has done the most interesting work arguing the Epistles should be read on their own terms rather than as appendices, which cuts against dependence in either direction. Grayston argued they come *first*. The majority follows Brown: different writer, same school, Epistles later.

Redactor identification is also a real position, and probably the one you'd find most useful. Bultmann posited an "ecclesiastical redactor" who added the futurist and sacramental material (5:28–29, 6:51–58) — and those are precisely the elements 1 John shares with the Gospel against the Gospel's own tendency. Thyen, if I have him right, argued the hand behind chapter 21 is the hand behind the Epistles; verify that attribution before printing it. The οἴδαμεν at 21:24 and the ὃ ἠκούσαμεν of [[1 John 01#^1-1|1 John 1:1]] are the same rhetorical move by a collective vouching for something it did not itself compose.

**On the Gnostic reading.** Your instinct has a distinguished pedigree — it's essentially Bultmann's, that John draws on a pre-Christian redeemer myth. That specific thesis collapsed: the Mandaean evidence is seventh-century and later, and Colpe showed the "redeemer myth" had been partly reconstructed out of John, so the argument was circular. Then Qumran supplied a Jewish home for the dualism — 1QS III–IV gives you sons of light, a spirit of truth against a spirit of deceit, in a text nobody calls Gnostic.

The structural disqualifier is creation. Every developed Gnostic system needs a botched cosmos and a creator distinct from the true God. John gives you πάντα δι' αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο and a God who loves the κόσμος. σὰρξ ἐγένετο is the sentence Valentinians had to work around, and [[1 John 04#^4-2|1 John 4:2]] turns it into a shibboleth.

But — and this is where I think you're onto something real — the *susceptibility* is a fact about the text, not just its readers. The first commentary on John is by a Valentinian. Ptolemy glosses the prologue before any proto-orthodox does. Brown's whole schism thesis is that the secessionists were reading the Gospel correctly enough that it took an epistle to close the reading down. And "orthodox" is anachronistic anyway; the category was partly *formed* in the fight over this book. Williams and King have made the reciprocal case that "Gnosticism" is largely a heresiological construct. So the question may be less "which was it" than "what made it available to both."

Two data points for your file. John uses γινώσκω constantly and the noun γνῶσις never — not once. Same with πιστεύω, ~98 times, against πίστις zero. The Gospel has no nouns for these states, only verbs for the acts. Whatever else that does, it means the text refuses to let recognition be a possession, which is exactly the thing a gnostic system needs it to be.

> [!quote] Me
> Do we have the Valentinian commentary and or the Ptolemy gloss?

Not as texts, no. Both survive only inside their opponents, and the shape of what survives is determined by the opponents' own transmission accidents.

**Heracleon.** Around fifty fragments, conventionally numbered from Brooke's 1891 edition, nearly all embedded in Origen's *Commentary on John* — Origen quotes him at length precisely in order to answer him. That's better than the usual heresiological practice; Origen sometimes concedes a point before disagreeing. But Origen's own commentary survives only in pieces (books 1, 2, 6, 10, 13, 19, 20, 28, 32), so Heracleon's coverage is visible only where Origen's happens to be. Clement preserves one further fragment in *Stromateis* IV, on confession before men, not from the John work.

One caveat worth having: Berglund's recent study of Origen's citation practice argues we can't reliably distinguish verbatim quotation from paraphrase or hostile summary in these fragments — Brooke's edition front-loads more confidence than the evidence carries. Check that before you cite Heracleon's *wording* for anything.

**What's actually in them** is the part relevant to you. The densest concentration is on [[John 04#^4-1|John 4]] — Origen's Book XIII, on the Samaritan woman — and Heracleon reads it as a recognition scene about the pneumatic recognizing her own nature: the six husbands as her entanglement with matter, the true husband as her consort in the Pleroma, the abandoned waterpot as what she no longer needs. So the earliest surviving sustained interpretation of John is a reading of the chapter you're already treating as the Gospel's longest recognition scene, and it takes the recognition to be self-recognition of an innate nature. That's the version of your thesis you have to distinguish yourself from, and it's on record before anyone else's. He also has a substantial reading of 8:44 on the three natures — the position that makes the Jews diabolic *by constitution*, which is the earliest form of the reading your non-supersessionist argument exists to refuse.

**Ptolemy.** *Adversus Haereses* 1.8.5 is a continuous block, one of Irenaeus' longer direct quotations, mapping the prologue onto the Ogdoad: Arche, Logos, Zoe, Anthropos read as aeon-names rather than predicates. Irenaeus attributes it to Ptolemy's circle, so even the ascription is his. Separately, the *Letter to Flora* survives essentially complete in Epiphanius, *Panarion* 33 — the fullest Valentinian text preserved by a heresiologist, but it's on the tripartite division of the Mosaic Law, not on John, though it cites 1:3 in passing.

**The control case.** Nag Hammadi gives you Valentinian writing unmediated, and the *Gospel of Truth* is the one to sit with: Johannine idiom throughout, essentially no explicit citation. If you want to see what John sounded like to a second-century reader outside the polemical frame, that's the closest available, and it shows how thoroughly the Gospel had been absorbed before anyone was fighting over it.

> [!quote] Me
> The Gnostic point I was making is really what you said about it being available to both but a step further. Without the prologue it aligns with the Gnostic reading just as easily as it does any other.
> The Ogdoad mapping might actually be very helpful, especially if we look at it through the lens of cosmic function instead of personalities of the godhead.

Your first point is stronger than a concession — it's testable, and it mostly holds. Run the Gospel without 1:1–18 and the residue that actively blocks a demiurgical reading is thin:

- **4:22**, ἡ σωτηρία ἐκ τῶν Ἰουδαίων ἐστίν. The hardest verse for any system where the Jewish God is the demiurge — and Heracleon felt it, since he has to gloss it rather than pass over it.
- **5:17**, ὁ πατήρ μου ἕως ἄρτι ἐργάζεται. This is a sabbath argument, so it presupposes that the Father is the one who rested on the seventh day. The Genesis creator is being identified with Jesus' Father, outside the prologue, in a load-bearing dispute.
- **The flesh material**: 6:51–58 with τρώγω doing crude chewing work, 19:34, 20:27.
- Moses and Abraham as **witnesses** rather than dupes (5:46, 8:56), and Isaiah seeing his glory at 12:41.

Now notice what happens to that list under source criticism. Bultmann assigned 6:51–58 to the ecclesiastical redactor. The prologue is widely taken as a prior hymn adapted, and the John-the-Baptist verses inside it as intrusions. So the material that immunizes John against gnosticizing is disproportionately the material independently flagged as added. Which is close to your point in its strongest form: the Gospel does not determine its own reading, and the frame that determines it may be the latest layer. That's not "John is Gnostic." It's that the prologue functions as a hermeneutical key prefixed to a text that would otherwise underdetermine — and given what you're doing with translation and interpretation, a key attached later to fix a reading is very much your material.

The residue that resists hardest is 4:22 and 5:17. I'd build on those two rather than the incarnation, since the incarnation argument routes back through the prologue you've bracketed.

**On the Ogdoad functionally read.** This works, and it works better than the personified version even on Valentinian terms. Bythos/Sige, Nous/Aletheia, Logos/Zoe, Anthropos/Ekklesia stop being a family tree and become a sequence of moments in one act of self-manifestation: unmanifest ground, self-awareness arising in it, articulation, and the manifest form with its plurality. Ptolemy's move in 1.8.5 is to read Ἀρχή, Λόγος, Ζωή, Ἄνθρωπος as *stages* where the received tradition reads *predicates* — and Irenaeus' objection is essentially that he's turned adjectives into people. Read functionally, Ptolemy isn't doing that at all.

You'll see the parallel immediately: it's the same shape as the tattva sequence from Śiva through Śuddhavidyā, and Abhinavagupta already gives you the warning against entifying the levels. Prakāśa/Vimarśa maps onto Bythos/Nous-Aletheia with almost no strain.

The place it breaks, and where I'd hold the line so this doesn't go eclectic: the Valentinian sequence is **declensional**. Each stage is a further remove, and the series terminates in Sophia's error and a cosmos that shouldn't exist. Trika's is not — ābhāsa is not loss, and the concealment is svātantrya rather than a fall. So converting the Ogdoad into a functional cosmology gives you something structurally Śaiva, and the Valentinian would say you've removed the point. That objection is worth stating in the footnote yourself rather than letting a reader find it.

> [!quote] Me
> My reply to the Valentinian would be that they're only seeing half the picture. Each phase is ascensional when seen from the other direction (and it seems the true God must have allowed the demiurge to do its thing in the first place)

Both halves of that need separating, because one is a strong argument and the other concedes more than you want.

**The bidirectional move is real, and it has form.** Kṣemarāja's is the cleanest: the same sequence read down is *sṛṣṭi*, read up is *saṃhāra*, and neither direction is privileged — which is exactly the *spanda* point you've already placed in the introduction as methodological frame. The Ishbitzer has his own version in *yeridah tzorech aliyah*, the descent for the sake of ascent, and Lurianic *ḥistalkut* / *hitpashtut* gives you the same alternation cosmologically.

But note what the Valentinian will say: he *has* the return. The whole system is an ascent narrative — the pneumatic recovers, Sophia is restored, the Pleroma is repaired. So "you're only seeing half" isn't quite the objection. His answer is that the return is a **repair of something that went wrong**, not the other reading of a single motion. The Śaiva claim is stronger and structurally different: there is no error to repair, and the concealment is the same act as the disclosure rather than its failure. That's the disagreement worth naming, and it's sharper than "you missed the ascent."

**The permission argument, though, is the one to be careful with.** It's the theodicy move: if the true God permitted it, the demiurge's work is within providence, so the cosmos isn't a mistake. Fine as far as it goes — but permission preserves the two-tier structure. It leaves you with a real subordinate agent whose work is real and merely tolerated, which is closer to Origen or to a Christian felix culpa than to anything in your system. Kashmir Śaivism doesn't permit the concealment; it *is* the concealment. Śiva's self-limitation isn't allowed by Śiva, it's Śiva's own act, which is why āṇavamala is a contraction of the same light and not a foreign residue.

You already have the vocabulary that gets this right and it isn't *anujñā*. It's **svātantrya** — and this is precisely where *krīḍā* and *vilāsa* earn the distinction you've been insisting on, since permission implies a second party and play doesn't. The Valentinian demiurge is ignorant of what's above him; Śiva's forgetting is deliberate and is the game. Same apparent structure, opposite internal logic.

So the reply I'd actually make: not "you have half," but *you have a fall you must undo, and I have a motion whose two directions are one act.* Which also, incidentally, is why your epistemological framing does work the ontological one can't — for you nothing has to be repaired, only recognized, and that's the whole difference between *pratyabhijñā* and a restored Pleroma.
