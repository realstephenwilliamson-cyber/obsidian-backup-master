## About

A late dialogue of Plato on whether pleasure or knowledge and intelligence is the good for human life. It concludes that neither is the highest good, and that the good human life is a mixture in the right proportion.

- **Characters and setting:** Socrates, who leads; Protarchus, son of Callias, who defends pleasure; Philebus, a young hedonist who says little and may be fictional; and a silent group of young listeners. It opens abruptly, with no stated place, time or occasion; Athens is the only possible setting. No dramatic date is given.
- **Date of composition:** scholars almost unanimously place it in the late period; Wikipedia reports one proposal of 360–347 BC, while some prefer the end of the middle period. The IEP puts it in the late group, c. 355–347 BC; the SEP groups it on stylistic evidence with the *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Timaeus*, *Critias* and *Laws*. No source consulted questions its authenticity.
- **Main subject:** a fourfold division of what exists: the unlimited, limit, their mixture, and the cause of the mixture. Pleasure belongs to the unlimited, and some pleasures are false. Goods are ranked: measure, beauty, reason, sciences and true opinions, pure pleasures. It also treats the one and the many and the method of collection and division.
- **Place among the dialogues:** one of the few late dialogues with Socrates as main speaker (Wikipedia, IEP, SEP). Thrasyllus put it in his third tetralogy, subtitled "On Pleasure", with the *Parmenides*, *Symposium* and *Phaedrus* (Diogenes Laertius 3.58). Stephanus pages 11a–67b.
- **Context:** Eudoxus of Cnidus, a hedonist, may be the target of its critique (Hackforth, 1958); Aristotle engaged closely with its account of pleasure.
- **Transmission:** the oldest surviving manuscript was made for Arethas of Caesarea in 895. Marsilio Ficino's Latin translation appeared in 1484 and the first Greek edition (Aldus, Venice) in 1513. Schleiermacher (1809) counted it among Plato's most important and most difficult works.
