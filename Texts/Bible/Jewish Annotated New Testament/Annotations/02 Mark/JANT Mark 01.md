### 1.1–15: Introduction

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-1|Text]], p. 69* · Mark’s overture strikes several themes: repentance and forgiveness (v 4), the Spirit (vv 8,10), Jesus the Son (v 11), and the kingdom of God (v 15), but unlike Matthew and Luke, it does not include an account of Jesus’ birth. ^1-1

### 1.1–11: John’s baptism

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-1|Text]], p. 69* · ([[Matthew 03#^3-1|Mt 3.1–17]]; [[Luke 03#^3-1|Lk 3.1–22]]; [[John 01#^1-6|Jn 1.6–34]]). ^1-1-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-1|1]]:** *Good news* (Gk “euangelion”), or gospel, was not used in the first century to refer to a book; its Hebrew equivalent ([[Isaiah 52#^52-7|Isa 52.7]] \[Heb “mebaser,” “bringing {good} tidings”; Gk {LXX} “euangelizomenos,” “announcing good news”\]) suggests the good news of God’s deliverance, and in the Roman world “euangelion” refers to the good news of the peace brought by the emperor. Mark gives the common term a new connotation: the good news proclaimed by, and about, Jesus. The opening clause identifies Jesus as messiah (“anointed one”; Gk “christos,” Heb “mashiach”—the Gk term is the source of the title “Christ”), a title already applied to kings of Israel, the high priest, prophets, and even King Cyrus of Persia when he enacted God’s plan ([[Isaiah 45#^45-1|Isa 45.1]]). The Tanakh never uses the term “messiah” for the ideal future Davidic king, although the promise that the Davidic line will endure in [[Psalms 089#^89-20|Ps 89.19–37]] (see also 2 Sam 7) implies an eternal anointing. Mark’s readers thus need to determine what sort of “messiah” Jesus is: royal, priestly, prophetic, salvific, etc., or some combination of these. *Son of God* (the title is absent in some ancient manuscripts; see translators’ note *b* and 1.9–11n.), also could be a royal title ([[Psalms 002#^2-7|Ps 2.7]]; [[Psalms 089#^89-27|89.26–27]]). ^1-1-3

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-2|2–8]]:** *Isaiah*, one of the biblical books most quoted by Jews, including Jesus’ followers, but Mark’s citation actually begins with [[Malachi 3#^3-1|Mal 3.1]]; Matthew (3.3) and Luke (3.4–6) correct this misattribution by removing *“See . . . your way. ”* Mark may have known these texts from “testimonia,” collections of verses on a common theme: in this case, on God’s *way*. “Way” is a technical term in Greek, Jewish, and Christian ethical discourse on choosing the good, albeit difficult, path as opposed to the immoral, easy path. God’s ways are emphasized in biblical passages such as [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^5-30|Deut 5.33]] and [[Jeremiah 07#^7-23|Jer 7.23]]. The word “way” (Gk “hodos”) is comparable to Jewish notions of “halakhah,” how one walks, and “derekh eretz,” the way of the land (cf. *Jub.* 12.21, *4 Ezra* \[2 Esd\] 7.12–13; *Derek Eretz Rabbah*). *1 En.* 71.17 speaks of the “upright way” of the Son of Man. According to [[Acts 09#^9-2|Acts 9.2]], Jesus’ first followers called themselves not Christians but “belong\[ing\] to the way.” Thus Mark’s readers might understand the Baptist to be proclaiming not simply the right “way” but the “way” of Jesus. In its original context, [[Isaiah 40#^40-3|Isa 40.3]] prophesied a road in the desert for the exiles to return directly from Babylon to Jerusalem. ^1-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-4|4–5]]:** *John the baptizer*, began a movement similar to that of Jesus in preaching repentance and forgiveness (1.15). There is an Israelite and Jewish tradition of coordinated pairs of significant leaders—Moses and Aaron, David and Jonathan, Elijah and Elisha, Ezra and Nehemiah, Jeshua and Zerubbabel, Hillel and Shammai, and cf. Peter and Paul in Acts. Jesus and John are related in all four Gospels, but in different ways. In Mark, Jesus begins with John, but moves beyond him; still, the death of John is significant for Jesus’ followers and foreshadows Jesus’ death (6.29; 15.45–46). ^1-4

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-4|4]]:** The Tanakh mentions water purifications (e.g., [[Torah 27 - Tazria#^13-6|Lev 13.6]]; [[Torah 28 - Metzora#^15-5|15.5–10]]); the Second Temple period witnessed an increased interest in such rites: [[LXX Judith 12#^12-7|Jdt 12.7–9]] (ca. G A L I L E E 36° 35° The geography of the Gospel of Mark. S Y R I A Sidon Caesarea Philippi 33° Gennesaret *River Jordan* D E C A P O Gerasa L I S 32° 0 Miles 10 20 31° 0 10 20 Kilometers 100 BCE) mentions bathing as purification, and pools for ritual immersion (Heb “mikvaot”) begin to appear in various locations, especially lower Galilee. See also Josephus, *J.W.* 2.130–32; *b. Ber.* 53b. At Qumran extensive cisterns and water channels reflect the ritual bathing mentioned at 1QS 5–6. *Baptism of repentance*, a ritual bathing that was understood to effect the *forgiveness of sins.* John gives a common practice new symbolic meaning: no longer (just) a ritual designed to cleanse impurity (e.g., from menstruation or coming into contact with a corpse), it now serves as public testimony of repentance and preparation for the in-breaking of God’s kingdom. ^1-4-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-6|6]]:** John the Baptist is presented as a new Elijah (9.13; [[II Kings 01#^1-8|2 Kings 1.8]]). ^1-6

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-8|8]]:** *Baptize you with the Holy Spirit*, perhaps an anticipation of early Christian practices ([[Acts 02#^2-1|Acts 2.1–4]]; [[Acts 08#^8-14|8.14–17]]). ^1-8

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-9|9–11]]:** The rabbis referred to words from heaven as “bat qol,” “the daughter of the voice” (*t. Sot.* 13.2; *b. Eruv.* 13b); here the words are influenced by [[Psalms 002#^2-7|Ps 2.7]] and [[Isaiah 42#^42-1|Isa 42.1–2]]. The psalm depicts a royal adoption: when anointed, the Davidic king becomes a son of God. Jesus is never called “Son of God” by the disciples, but he receives this title from God, from unclean spirits (5.7), from Jewish authorities (14.61, in a question) and from a Roman soldier (15.39). In some Christian circles the title Son of God included attributes of pre-existence ([[John 01#^1-1|Jn 1.1–14]]) and equality with God ([[John 05#^5-18|Jn 5.18]]). In Mark, “Son of God” was more likely understood as the raising of a human being to a special status with God; counterparts include both the Davidic king and the Roman emperor (Livy, *Hist*. 1.16). God “adopted” Jesus as God’s son, as the king was adopted by God (as in [[Psalms 002#^2-7|Ps 2.7]]). ^1-9

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-10|10]]:** *Torn apart,* see 15.38n. *Dove*, perhaps symbolizing gentleness ([[Psalms 074#^74-19|Ps 74.19]]). ^1-10

### 1.12–13: Jesus’ temptation

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-12|Text]], p. 71* · ([[Matthew 04#^4-1|Mt 4.1–11]]; [[Luke 04#^4-1|Lk 4.1–13]]). Mark’s temptation account lacks the drama found in Matthew and Luke’s versions. *Satan,* the “adversary” or “accuser,” appears in post-exilic Jewish sources when, initially under Persian influence (538–333 BCE), this figure becomes prominent ([[Zechariah 03#^3-1|Zech 3.1–2]]; [[Job 01#^1-6|Job 1.6]]; cf. *Gen. Rab.* 57.4). In *Jub.* 17.18 Satan is called Mastema, a name that also occurs at Qumran (1QS 3.23; 1QM 13.11). Christian sources typically regard Satan as a demonic force; rabbinic tradition alternates between depicting Satan as evil and depicting him still as (the) satan or “accuser” who serves to test the righteous (as in Job). According to the Babylonian Talmud (*B. Bat.* 16a), Resh Laqish states, “Satan, the evil prompter, and the Angel of Death are all one.” *Wilderness* and *forty days* suggest both the wanderings during the Exodus ([[Torah 16 - Beshalach#^15-22|Ex 15.22ff.]]) and the fasts of Moses and Elijah ([[Torah 46 - Eikev#^9-18|Deut 9.18]]; [[I Kings 19#^19-8|1 Kings 19.8]]). ^1-12

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-14|1.14–15]]:** **Proclaiming the kingdom of God** ([[Matthew 04#^4-12|Mt 4.12–17]]; [[Luke 04#^4-14|Lk 4.14–15]]). Jesus’ message, like that of John, emphasizes the kingdom of God and the urgency of the times. ^1-14

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-14|14]]:** *Galilee*, the region north of Judea and Samaria, west of the Sea of Galilee, where most of the narrative is set until Jesus comes to Jerusalem. It had indistinct boundaries, but it often included parts or all of the tribes of Issachar, Zebulun, Naphtali, and Asher (Josh 18–19). More rural and less Hellenized in the first century ce than Judea, lower Galilee reveals, through archaeology, a strong interest in Jewish identity markers such as ritual baths, stone vessels that do not convey impurity, and coinage (minted by Herod Antipas) without human images. ^1-14-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-15|15]]:** *Kingdom of God*, the concept that God is the true king appears in the earlier Jewish sources ([[Psalms 005#^5-3|Ps 5.2]]; 10.16; 103.19; [[Psalms 145#^145-11|145.11]],[[Psalms 145#^145-13|13]], etc.) but the expression “kingdom of God” is rare in Second Temple and Rabbinic literature ([[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 10#^10-10|Wis 10.10]]; *m. Ber*. 2.2,5), where it indicates a promise of an ideal state, as in [[Zechariah 14#^14-9|Zech 14.9]]. *Repent*, Gk “metanoia,” “change of mind,” where “mind” connotes the whole inner being; usually the LXX translation of Heb “n-ḥ-m,” “regret, repent.” In [[Jeremiah 31#^31-19|Jer 31.19]], “niḥam” is paired with “shuv,” “turn, turn back”; repentance is not in regard to individual sins, but rather “returning” to God. *Believe in the good news*, the noun “faith” and the verb “believe” (from the same Gk root) are strongly emphasized in the NT (e.g., [[Matthew 08#^8-10|Mt 8.10]]; [[Matthew 24#^24-23|24.23]]; [[Mark 13#^13-11|Mk 13.11]]; [[John 11#^11-40|Jn 11.40]]; [[1 Corinthians 11#^11-18|1 Cor 11.18]]). In ancient Israel and in Mark as well, “faith” and “believe” (Heb “ ʾaman” root; cf. “amen,” see 3.28n.) often connoted faithfulness or trustworthiness regarding both humans (e.g., [[Isaiah 38#^38-3|Isa 38.3]]) and God (e.g., [[Psalms 071#^71-22|Ps 71.22]]); cf. also the “amanah” or faith-covenant in [[Nehemiah 10#^10-1|Neh 9.38]]. In the Second Temple period, faith/believe took on a new emphasis: conviction, confession, even conversion ([[LXX Judith 14#^14-10|Jdt 14.10]]; [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 01#^1-2|Wis 1.2]]; [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 16#^16-26|16.26]]; [[LXX Sirach 01#^1-14|Sir 1.14]]; [[LXX Sirach 02#^2-6|2.6]]). In rabbinic Judaism, those who tithe carefully are called “neʾemanim” or faithful ones (*m. Demai* 4.6). Mark often discusses faith and Torah but never contrasts faith and law as Paul does (but see 2.1–12). ^1-15

### 1.16–20: Call of the first disciples

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-16|Text]], p. 72* · ([[Matthew 04#^4-18|Mt 4.18–22]]; [[Luke 05#^5-1|Lk 5.1–11]]; [[John 01#^1-35|Jn 1.35–42]]). ^1-16-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-17|17]]:** *Fish for people*, fishing is used both positively and negatively in biblical texts ([[Jeremiah 16#^16-16|Jer 16.16]]; Am 4.2); some rabbinic texts speak of fish in relation to new disciples (*Avot de R. Natan* A40). Greek and Roman philosophical circles and rabbinic Judaism describe students as seeking teachers rather than being called by them (as in [[John 01#^1-35|Jn 1.35–40]]; cf. *b. Eruv.* 30a; *b. Ketub.* 66b). However, Jesus’ method of calling here is likely based on Elijah’s call of Elisha ([[I Kings 19#^19-19|1 Kings 19.19–21]]). Jesus and Elijah both call followers; the disciples and Elisha both respond immediately, and they explicitly leave their parents in order to follow. ^1-17

### 1.21–45: A series of healings

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-21|Text]], p. 72* · Disease was generally associated with demonic possession (Ps 91; Josephus, *J.W.* 7.185; *b. Ketub.* 61b). In Mark, healings enact the eschatological promises of [[Isaiah 35#^35-5|Isa 35.5–6]], establish Jesus’ power over Satan’s forces, and are evidence that the kingdom of God has drawn near. Unclean spirits were sometimes connected to fallen angels (*1 En.* 15.8,34; *Jub.* 7.21; 10.1; 1 QM 13.5; 11QPsa 19.15). The modern tendency to distinguish Jesus’ healings from those of contemporary Jews such as Hanina ben Dosa and pagan miracle workers such as Apollonius of Tyana (see “Jewish Miracle Workers,” p. 680) is perhaps theologically motivated. The Gospel miracles contain the same procedures, healing formulae (e.g., “be muzzled,” “rebuked” v 25), and narrative structures found in pagan and Jewish sources. ^1-21

### 1:21–28: Exorcising and teaching with authority

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-21|Text]], p. 72* · ([[Matthew 07#^7-28|Mt 7.28–29]]; [[Luke 04#^4-31|Lk 4.31–37]]). ^1-21-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-21|21]]:** On *synagogue*\[s\] see “The Synagogue,” p. 662. That people were gathered in the *synagogue* on the *sabbath* suggests some type of worship service (see 6.2). ^1-21-3

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-22|22–24]]:** Jesus’ *teaching* includes not just his words, but also his *authority* to rebuke *spirit*\[s\]*.* Mark uses the term *unclean spirit* more often than the more typical “demons.” *Holy One of God*, applied to Elisha ([[II Kings 04#^4-9|2 Kings 4.9]]); as counter to the *unclean spirit*, such a prophet would restore the boundary between the demonic realm of death and the world of life created by God. ^1-22

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-25|25]]:** *Rebuked*, a common word in Jewish exorcisms (Gk “epitimaō*,*” Heb “gaʿar”; 1QapGen 20.28–29; cf. [[Psalms 106#^106-9|Ps 106.9]]). See also 1QM 14.9–11 in reference to God defeating foes in battle or overcoming Satan, and cf. later rabbinic sources (*Num. Rab.* 19.8; *Pesiq. Rab.* 36.1). *Be silent*, lit., “be muzzled,” also refers to the control of unclean spirits. ^1-25

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-27|27]]:** *Authority* (Gk “exousia”), the freedom to express one’s powers by teaching without referring to other teachers or scribes while successfully commanding *unclean spirits*. ^1-27

### 1.29–34: Short statements about many healings

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-29|Text]], p. 72* · ([[Matthew 08#^8-14|Mt 8.14–17]]; [[Luke 04#^4-38|Lk 4.38–41]]). Summaries describing people thronging to Jesus will later be ironically contrasted with Jesus’ commands to silence (2.1–2; 3.7–8; 6.53–56). ^1-29

**[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-30|30–31]]:** *Simon’s* \[Peter’s\] *mother-in-law*, appears only in this scene; Peter’s wife is never mentioned, but see [[1 Corinthians 09#^9-5|1 Cor 9.5]]. The term for her *serv*\[ice\] (Gk “diakoneo,” “deacon”) anticipates Jesus’ commending of service to others (10.45) as well as the other women who minister to Jesus (15.41). ^1-30

### 1.35–39: Jesus’ mission

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-35|Text]], p. 73* · ([[Matthew 04#^4-23|Mt 4.23–25]]; [[Luke 04#^4-42|Lk 4.42–44]]). The disciples will continually misunderstand Jesus’ identity and mission, and indeed Jesus is often indirect. Mark’s audience, however, was told his significance in 1.1. *Deserted place . . . he prayed*, Jesus regularly prayed alone, which adds to the sense that Jesus remains separate from those around him (e.g., 1.1; 14.35; cf. [[Matthew 04#^4-1|Mt 4.1–11]], and see Introduction). ^1-35

### 1.40–45: Healing skin disease

*[[NRSVue Mark 01#^1-40|Text]], p. 73* · ([[Matthew 08#^8-2|Mt 8.2–4]]; [[Luke 05#^5-12|Lk 5.12–16]]). The term *leprosy* (see translators’ note *a*) today refers to Hansen’s disease, a serious, disfiguring, contagious illness, but Heb “tsaraʾat” (Gk “lepros,” “scaly, rough”) is more likely psoriasis, eczema, or a fungal infection (see Lev 13–14); minor skin diseases that were considered forms of ritual impurity. By *touch*\[ing\] the unclean man (v 41), Jesus violates no commandment, and the Bible does not explicitly state that the impurity from this disease was contagious. Four times Mark emphasizes that Jesus makes the man *clean* of his disease, suggesting that this is the focus of the healing. This cleansing does not abrogate the purity laws any more than does the cleansing of Naaman in [[II Kings 05#^5-10|2 Kings 5.10]]. Jesus commands the man to present the offering prescribed by Torah. Mark characterizes the spirit world in terms of a distinction between the “holy” spirit and “unclean” spirits (cf. [[Zechariah 13#^13-1|Zech 13.1–2]]; *1 En.* 10.17–11.2; 38.2; 1QS 4.20–21; 9.3). Rabbinic Judaism speaks of a purification occurring near the Temple altar (*m. Zeb.* 9.1); some apocalyptic texts assume that this cleansing could affect the saved community away from the Temple as well (1QM 13.2–6; 1QS 4.18–23). In Mark, Jesus is not erasing the Jewish distinctions of impurity; he is defeating impure spirits through the actions of the Holy Spirit. This removal of impurity is a sign of the regeneration of the community at the end of this age, visible in the exorcisms. ^1-40
