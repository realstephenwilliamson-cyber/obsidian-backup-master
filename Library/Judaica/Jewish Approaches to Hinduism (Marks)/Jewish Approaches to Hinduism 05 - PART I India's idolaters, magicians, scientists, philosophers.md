Another group, the *H. ashishim*, a ferocious people living in the mountains of Lebanon and Persia, “do not believe in the *dat* of the Ishmaelites” (Muslims), but instead follow their own prophet and his commands. They call him the Sheikh of *al-H. ashishim* and obey his commands even when he orders them to take actions that will bring them certain death. They are loyal and faithful, *ba\`alei ‘emunah*, to his words.<sup>67</sup>

Unlike the Druze and *Balakhzin*, Benjamin never rejects the possibility of considering the rules and beliefs of the *Kofar al-Turak* and *H. ashishim* a form of *dat*. Clearly, these peoples live on a higher moral and social level than the depraved Druze, murderous *Balakhzin*, and mindless *Benei H. am*. Their behavior and beliefs reflect ritual and social regulation, even though Benjamin would consider them faulty and incomplete and lacking a divine source.

Considering, then, all the religions that appear in *Book of Travels*, we discover, behind the book’s style of neutral observation, a theory of religions that compares and evaluates them from lowest to highest. As we saw with the smaller nations of the world, the main distinction is between those who may possess *dat* (as social regulation) and those who certainly do not. Lowest are the *Benei H. am*, the Druze, and the *Balakhzin*, who follow no *dat* and so live violently, eat whatever comes their way, and behave with sexual abandon. But many peoples of the world obey authority and follow laws that guide at least some of their behavior. They comprise a middle category of *dat*. The *H. ashishim* follow the commands of their prophet, showing loyalty to him. The *Kofar al-Turak* venerate the wind although they lack dietary laws. The highest religions are Christianity, Islam, and Judaism, which are all systems of *dat*. Christianity seems to be the lowest of these because its worship takes place at *bamot*, a word connoting idolatrous sacrifices, rather than in *batei tefilah*, houses of prayer. Its pilgrims are *to\`im*, people who wander about in error. Islam, however, exhibits actions and institutions similar to Judaism: Muslims expound a scripture explained as “their *torah*,” have sages who study their scripture, offer an annual sacrifice similar to the paschal lamb, and pray in “houses of prayer” and “places of assembly.” But Islam’s prophet can still sometimes be called a “madman” and its pilgrims *to\`im*. Judaism, as pictured in *Book of Travels*, differs mainly in being comprised of the spiritual virtues of “holiness,” observance of law, studying the holy books, and generosity to other Jews, and has its own system of inherited authority.<sup>68</sup>

Where do the religions of Qulam and Ibrig fit in this comparative scheme? At first, they seem to belong to the middle category. Like the *Kofar al-Turak*, who worship the wind, the Qulamese and Ibrigans worship powers of nature (fire and the sun) rather than the one revealed God of the nations of *dat*. *Book of Travels* openly declares the Asian beliefs erroneous: worship of the sun in Qulam “is folly for them,” and the Ibrigans’ faith in 

*[p. 96]* ^pg109

their fraudulent priests is hardened error. These priests minister in a “house of idolatry” (*beit \`avodah zarah*).

In other ways, however, the South Asian religions differ significantly enough from the religions of the *H. ashishim* and *Kofar al-Turak* that they cannot easily fit the middle category of religions. Unlike those religions, the religious ritual of Qulam and Ibrig is complex, detailed, and organized, and Ibrig possesses religious hierarchy. Moreover, these two kingdoms follow complex religious and social laws. The Qulamese obey their king and treat foreign merchants with honesty and respect; they work hard, even when their weather is too hot for day-time labor, and they grow, process, and sell pepper, and do not steal their neighbors’ pepper trees. They engage in elaborate procedures for preserving their dead, and they design machinery to reveal the exact moment of sunrise. Every morning, regularly, they venerate the sun by lighting incense. Similarly, the people of Ibrig obey rules for worshiping fire and follow the exact procedure leading up to their self-immolations; and after someone’s death, they divide property and pay and collect debts. They pray in a house of prayer (*beit tefilah*) next to their *bamah*. The complexity and morality of these regulations make Qulam and Ibrig more like the Muslim and Jewish communities of *Book of Travels* than like the other non-monotheistic nations of the book. If *dat* in *Book of Travels* implies social and religious regulations and a level of morality, then Qulam and Ibrig are nations of *dat*. What they sadly lack, however, is correct belief – that is, revealed doctrine.

Benjamin’s view of South Asian religions

In conclusion, comparison with Arabic travel accounts shows that the author(s) of *Book of Travels* intended the book’s portrayal of the kingdoms of South Asia as realistic reports rather than fantasy. The book’s portrayal reflects similar themes that appeared in Muslim images of India. But the author exhibits a flair for vivid storytelling which the Arabic accounts generally lack. One area where *Sefer Masa\`ot* diverges from the Arabic accounts, the minor motif of child-sacrifice, reflects instead the book’s tendency to blur south Asian religion with ancient biblical idolatry, through associative images (fire, valleys, *bamot*).

Comparison with the other religions of *Sefer Masa\`ot* additionally suggests the book’s evaluation of Qulam and Ibrig. These kingdoms seem to combine elements of the monotheistic forms of *dat* with elements of tribal beliefs and rituals. Qulam and Ibrig possess religious and social laws, and train their followers to obey rules of worship and society. They exhibit a clear ethical component, especially Qulam, where people practice the virtues of honesty and hard work.

Benjamin nevertheless calls their beliefs “folly” and “error” – because south Asians worship the sun and fire as divinities and because the people of Ibrig believe their deceptive priests. Furthermore, compared with Islam 

*[p. 97]* ^pg110

and Judaism, the people of South Asia possess no *torah*, nor sages to study it, nor concept of holiness. But like the Jews, they are honest among each other and hospitable to travelers. Thus, the Asian religions are certainly not monotheism and correct belief, but just as certainly not non-religion or the simple beliefs and limited laws found among the tribal nations. The south Asian religions are *dat* but defective, and south Asian societies are moral and multifaceted.

In a sense, and seen from a distance, Benjamin’s picture of south Asian religions combines Ha-Levi’s “good” and “bad” images of India: good citizens plus pagan worship of natural forces. Like some of the ancient nations described by Ha-Levi, Qulam and Ibrig seek the most powerful force in the world to worship, and erroneously find it in nature and worship it instead of God. The earlier motif of magic reappears in *Book of Travels* but of a different kind from the astral magic in *Kuzari*. Two new motifs enter the history of Jewish ideas about India: deceptive priests and, as a minor implication, the idea that Indian worship and biblical idolatry share common features.

Glorious wisdom: Jacob ben 

### 4. Translations and transformations: Glorious wisdom: Jacob ben El\`azar and India’s Panchatantra

We turn now to two Hebrew translations composed in the13th and 14th centuries. The first one reflects, rather distantly, its source in a classic Sanskrit book, *Panchatantra*, and the second reworks Hellenistic accounts of Indian philosophers who debated with Alexander the Great. We’ll meet talking jackals and the problem of fate, and naked sages who quote the Bible and Talmud and denounce Western religion. These Indians look more like Ibn Ezra’s image of them than Ha-Levi’s. They do not worship nature or venerate images but believe in an abstract God of morality.

The India of Ben El\`azar’s *Kelilah and Dimnah*

For Jacob ben El\`azar (first half of 13th c.), it was exactly the lack of revelation that had spurred the creation of India’s “glorious” moral literature with its universal human appeal and practical benefit, useful even to Jews with their revealed Torah. Indians lacked revelation, but Jews could still learn practical morality from them.

Ben El\`azar was referring to an ancient Indian book that had been translated into Arabic with the title *Kalila wa-Dimna*. This is the same book that Abraham ibn Ezra had praised in the introduction to his translation of *Ibn al-Muthanna’s Commentary* (1160) when he told the story of how Indian science came to the Arab world. In his view, *Kalila wa-Dimna* was the first Indian book that had reached the Arabs.

A great king of the Arabs whose name was Al-Saffah. … was told by certain people that in India there was a very esteemed book concerning counsels for kingship (*melukhah*) which took the form of parables (*meshalim*) from the mouths of dumb creatures and contained many illustrations to make the book esteemed in the eye of the reader. The name of the book is *Kalilah and Dimnah*, which refers to the lion and the bull about whom the book speaks in the first section …. When he \[Al-Saffah.\] saw how wonderful (*mufla’*) the book was in its argument (*ta\`am*), for so it truly is, he yearned for more knowledge of the wisdom (or “sciences,” *h. okhamot*) of India.<sup>1</sup>

*[p. 103]* ^pg116

Ibn Ezra presents this book as advice to kings on how to rule; the advice comes in the form of animal fables, a method of presentation that he, agreeing withal-Saffah. , considers “wonderful.” He categorizes the book as a form of *h. okhmah*, a word that includes both “wisdom” and “science”– essentially knowledge based on human thought and observation. The content of *Kalilah and Dimnah* is thus, for Ibn Ezra, compatible with the knowledge of math, astronomy, and astrology that also come from India. He erred, however, in explaining the title of the book, as we will soon see.

Jacob ben El\`azar, on the other hand, saw the book not as political advice but as ethical teachings about human relationships. This author lived several generations after Ibn Ezra, in Toledo, among Muslims, Christians, and Jews ruled by Christian kings. The city supported a multilingual culture and served as a major center of learning and translation. We don’t know much about Ben El\`azar’s life. Inside the walls of the city’s Jewish quarter, he prayed at one of its many synagogues, perhaps the one built by Samuel Ha-Levi in Mudejar style, with splendid arches, high graceful windows, and walls adorned with biblical verses. He had a cosmopolitan education, he read books written in Arabic, Hebrew, and Latin. In Arabic he wrote a long book on Hebrew grammar, and in Hebrew he composed two books of philosophy, both in rhymed prose (a style called *maqama*). His *Sefer Meshalim* (Book of Parables), written in 1233, contained a number of romantic stories among its narratives. Ben El\`azar’s connection with India came through a request made by a patron named Benveniste ben H. iyya Aldian, who asked him to translate the Arabic *Kalila wa-Dimna* into Hebrew. Ben El\`azar decided to compose his translation in the same style of rhymed prose as his other works, imbedded with frequent biblical phrases throughout. Like Ibn Ezra and other Jews of the time, he believed that the Hebrew language, including phrases from the Bible, were just as capable as Arabic, if not more so, of expressing philosophy, literature, and science.<sup>2</sup> Ben El\`azar’s introduction to *Kalilah and Dimnah* begins by informing his Jewish readers that the book comes from India and contains much of value for them. One of his opening poems, with the lines divided according to verse meters, summarizes the introduction’s argument:

This book Jacob ben El\`azar translated into the Hebrew tongue.

Composed it in ancient days did the sages of India from their precious moral teachings (*musarim*)

And promoted it in place of righteous and upright laws.

It is their instruction (*torah*), for they have no seers and prophets to warn them. Its words appear in the mouths of fish of the sea, and eagles of the beasts, mountains.

It seems astute to the old, but laughable to the young.

. . . But when they \[the young\] “grow cunning”<sup>3</sup> They will learn intelligence from it until “the heart of the rash learns to know”<sup>4</sup> Its glories because of its “goodly sayings” “polished like sapphires.”<sup>5</sup>

*[p. 104]* ^pg117

In this poem, Ben El\`azar praises the Indian book for its valuable moral teachings that even the young who notice only its entertaining fables will eventually discover to be astutely intelligent. When Ben El\`azar speaks of the old and young, he means all human beings, not just Indians; he means his Jewish readers.

He then expands in prose on the book’s origin and composition:

Jacob ben El\`azar declares, When the ancient sages of India saw, and the wise men of the early nations, that they walked dark and confused in the land, because they had no prophets or seers to teach them, for there was no strong law or “frequent vision” \[[[I Samuel 03#^3-1|1 Sam. 3:1]]\], and the hearts of the sages of India were sharpened swords \[cf. [[Ezekiel 21#^21-14|Ezek 21:14]]\] in all wisdom, and “each man said to his neighbor,” and they were grieved, “why do we sit here?” \[[[II Kings 07#^7-3|2 Kings 7:3]]\]. They hastened their minds along the road of wisdom, and cast their thoughts and understanding outdoors,<sup>6</sup> to find light for their darkness and a straight path through twisted ways. They saw the heavens and the clouds, the sun and the moon and the stars, and they observed the greatness of the world and its wonders, and the earth and all its minions, and that nothing dwelling there was missing its sustenance, “and the men marveled one at another” \[[[Torah 10 - Miketz#^43-33|Gen. 43:33]]\], and said, who created all this and who sustains it, who stood each and every person upon his foundation? Who will show us the creator of the heavens, who will give us knowledge of the one who founded the earth upon the water, and who will send us one of his messengers, and who will “teach us his ways” \[[[Micah 4#^4-2|Micah 4:2]]\]? And when they saw that finding an entrance was impossible, they yearned and longed until they were almost lost and ruined, “and the men were grieved and very angry” \[[[Torah 08 - Vayishlach#^34-7|Gen. 34:7]]\]. And they said, Let us make ourselves a book of choice parables (*meshalim*), and of all kinds of moral teachings (*musarim*), and it will be for us and all the community “for instruction (*torah*) and for testimony” \[[[Isaiah 08#^8-20|Isa. 8:20]]\], and they made this book, providing “goodly sayings” \[[[Torah 12 - Vayechi#^49-21|Gen. 49:21]]\], and they called its name Kalilah and Dimnah, composed of matters of “moral guidance and discernment” \[*musar u-vinah*, [[Proverbs 23#^23-23|Prov. 23:23]]\].<sup>7</sup>

This story of the origin of *Kalilah and Dimnah* could be considered Ben El\`azar’s history of the origin of Indian theology and ethics, since he sees this book as the only guidance Indians have in life. This history involved a certain sequence of mental processes. It begins at a time when all ancient peoples, including the people of India, grieve over their state of dark ignorance and confusion because they lack divine revelation to provide them with guidance. From this grief, the intellectuals of India apply their minds to searching out knowledge and guidance. They begin by observing the world around them, especially its wondrous magnificence and sustenance, which leads them to the rational deduction that the world has a creator. But 

*[p. 105]* ^pg118

since they can learn nothing more through human thought, they yearn for a messenger who would reveal the Creator’s character and will. This yearning, worsened by the impossibility of making further progress through mere human reasoning, leads to despair. But this time, the intellectuals of India decide to solve part of the problem with their own minds by composing a book of moral teachings to guide their community in proper behavior and “discernment” – that is, discerning the ways of the world.

Three times Ben El\`azar uses the word *torah* to characterize *Kalilah and Dimnah*; the word means instruction but has the same sound as the revealed scripture of the Jews, their holy *Torah*. In this paragraph, he has the Indian sages say that *Kalilah and Dimnah* “will be for us and all the community ‘for instruction (*torah*) and for testimony’ ([[Isaiah 08#^8-20|Isa. 8:20]]). In the following paragraph, he writes, “Know that the sages of India placed parables in this book, as their laws (*h. uqotam*) and instructions (*torotam*).”<sup>8</sup> And in his prefatory poem, he had written, “They promoted it \[*Kalilah and Dimnah*\] in place of righteous and upright laws. It is their instruction (*toratam*).”

What is more, when describing how *Kalilah and Dimnah* should affect people’s minds, Ben El\`azar draws on three biblical phrases that Jewish readers would associate with their holy Torah. Two of the phrases appear in a sentence in which Indian sages command everyone to read the book and to place its tales “between your eyes in the most precious of your desires, lest they depart from your hearts all the days of your life.” The phrase “between your eyes” appears three times in Exodus and Deuteronomy in reference to *totafot*, usually translated as frontlets, to be placed “between your eyes” for the purpose of remembering and obeying the commandments revealed at Sinai. Second, the phrase “and lest they depart from your hearts all the days of your life” (Deut. 4:9) refers to these revealed laws and regulations. The third phrase appears in a sentence about children learning the lessons of *Kalilah and Dimnah*: “And on his heart will it be written,” which echoes a line in the Book of Jeremiah referring explicitly to the instruction, *torah*, from God: “I will put my instruction within them and write it on their hearts” (31:32).<sup>9</sup> What did Ben El\`azar imply about this Indian book through his repeated use of vocabulary associated with the Torah? Did he want his readers, who would know the context of the biblical phrases, to draw further meanings from his choice of them? If not, these biblical phrases would function as mere ornament; that is, they would refer in high language to common human attitudes and actions rather than specifically to the Torah revered by Jews. Ross Brann (1991), writing about the Hebrew poetry of Muslim Spain in his book, *The Compunctious Poet*, asserts that biblical references in this poetry functioned in three basic ways: ornamental, for the sake of elegance; dynamic, as intertextual devices to confer biblical meanings upon the poetry; and fundamental, in which the poem’s basic meaning depended on knowledge of a biblical source.<sup>10</sup>

I would not argue that all of Ben El\`azar’s biblical quotations add intentional meanings to his text, but in his introductory paragraphs about the 

*[p. 106]* ^pg119

role of *Kalilah and Dimnah* in Indian society, his choice of quotations associated with the Torah fits what he claims about the book. He could have found quotations about teaching, remembering, and obeying from elsewhere in the Bible, such as the first chapter of Proverbs. But his choice of biblical quotations that his Jewish readers would associate with their holy Torah conveyed the way he conceptualized what *Kalilah and Dimnah* meant to Indians. The book, he implied, functions for Indians in a way parallel to how the laws of the revealed *Torah* function for Jews.

Yet in Ben El\`azar’s view, this Indian book could not provide knowledge of God that transcends what is obvious in nature. Ben El\`azar conceived of the book as rational morality framed by a rational, and hence abstract, concept of God – all of which is available to the thoughtful human mind. This idea of Indian philosophy is not very different from Ibn Ezra’s idea of the scientific concept of God held by the world’s intellectuals, or from Ha-Levi’s concept of the god of philosophers. But Ben El\`azar’s story portrays Indian thought much differently from Ha-Levi’s view of it, which pictured Indians rejecting prophecy out of irrational scoffing. Ha-Levi’s Indians were quarrelsome and weak-minded, and Maimonides’ Indians were simply lost in the darkness of Sabian delusions. In contrast, the Indian sages of Ben El\`azar’s history are intelligent leaders who care about their communities and possess a deep inner sense of moral rightness. They do not reject prophecy but sincerely yearn for it. Like Ibn Ezra’s Indians, they are rational monotheists who differ from Jews mainly in their lack of revelation and reliance solely on reason.

Ben El\`azar’s theology of Indian ethics

Immediately after recounting the history of the composition of *Kalilah and Dimnah*, Ben El\`azar begins to discuss the general content of its wisdom and this wisdom’s embodiment in moral behavior: “For wisdom is a tree and the deed is its fruit, and a man is the form and the mind is its beauty, and doing the good and the upright is ‘the beauty of his ornament’ \[Ezek. 7.20\], whereas folly is a rotting tree that dies suddenly.” But then, to explain why wise men nevertheless act foolishly and behave badly, he introduces the great problem of human life, uncontrolled desire: “The evil inclination (*yetzer ha-ra\`*) that is within them will slay them and they will walk after the stubbornness of their hearts \[[[Jeremiah 13#^13-10|Jer. 13:10]], [[Jeremiah 23#^23-17|23:17]]\], when their desires overcome their minds and corrupt their actions.” Not desire in itself, writes Ben El\`azar, but inordinate desire in the form of greed for wealth and power, corrupts wise men, tempting and ensnaring them. They take advantage of a friend’s loss, they lie, betray, and speak high-sounding words while acting the scoundrel. They are hypocrites, pretending to help others or show compassion for the poor while doing nothing.<sup>11</sup>

However, the sage who behaves this way – “his sin is heavy, and his end is to perish eternally.” He will meet his “time of judgment.” Meanwhile, his 

*[p. 107]* ^pg120

attempt to fulfill his endless desires will not even bring him satisfaction or peace of mind but only trouble. The world and its desires, says Ben El\`azar, “can be compared to a fire that consumes a tree for food … and ‘will not be satiated’ \[[[Ecclesiastes 01#^1-8|Eccl. 1:8]], [[Ecclesiastes 04#^4-8|4:8]], [[Ecclesiastes 06#^6-3|6:3]]\].” The truly wise person will therefore learn that “all that decreases his desire in the world decreases his trouble and increases his spirit and his joy,” for the only way to “decrease his groaning” is to “separate himself above the world.” Controlling desire is the only rational course of action. The truly wise person should, therefore “cleanse his heart of all dross and defect” caused by immoral desires. “He must set a limit to his desires and a goal to his arrows,” and rejoice in his portion and trust in God to provide for his needs – who “gives bread to all flesh” and “will accompany him in his hard work” \[[[Psalms 136#^136-25|Ps. 136:25]], [[Ecclesiastes 08#^8-15|Eccl. 8:15]]\].<sup>12</sup>

Ben El\`azar thus conceives of a universe that, in addition to its wondrous magnificence, enforces limits upon selfish desire.

And the creator of all things placed a limit to each thing from which it does not depart, an eternal law that it cannot transgress \[[[Jeremiah 05#^5-22|Jer. 5:22]]\], and he who transgresses this law, for the law confounds, will suffer evil. Upon it there is nothing to add and nothing to remove \[[[Ecclesiastes 03#^3-14|Eccl. 3:14]]\].<sup>13</sup>

This is a divine law of balance and right conduct which a person would be wise to understand and conform to.

Therefore, it is upon every man to place a limit upon his seeking, so that law and judgment be upon each thing that he wants, for he who finds this law rejoices in his portion, and greater things he will not seek from it.<sup>14</sup>

The moral life is lived in harmony with cosmic law, embodied in daily human behavior. Ben El\`azar concludes his discussion of Indian wisdom by connecting it with a famous moral directive from the Torah: “And it is right for a wise person to love his neighbor as himself \[[[Torah 30 - Kedoshim#^19-18|Lev. 19.18]]\], and in that, he will obtain the good will of his Holy One.”<sup>15</sup>

This, then, is the theology of Indian ethics constructed by Ben El\`azar. Indians discovered God’s natural moral law embedded in the structure of the universe. If one loves one’s neighbor as oneself, one lives in accord with this divine law. Wise people observe human behavior and discover the bad consequences of living in opposition to natural moral law, and then teach principles of wise conduct – which is simultaneously moral conduct – to the masses in the form of animal fables. This morality, in Ben El\`azar’s view, is mostly about loyal friendship, kindness, and honesty, which are always threatened by the chaos of inordinate desire. Ben El\`azar’s introduction says nothing explicit about political strategy.

His writing style, as we have seen, combines his own poetry with copious biblical quotations. Since he employed these biblical quotations to convey Indian ideas and values, did he think that Indian philosophy could be 

*[p. 108]* ^pg121

accurately expressed through these biblical phrases, such as loving one’s neighbor as oneself? Could he even have thought that *Kalilah and Dimnah*, as an Indian book, teaches the same message as his quotations from the Bible? These questions are hard to answer because Ben El\`azar never explicitly compares the content of *Kalilah and Dimnah* with the Bible but claims only that the Indian teachings function like biblical laws.

But even this claim leads to a theological problem: why do we need the Torah if we already have “precious moral teachings” and social guidance discovered by human reason? Ibn Ezra answered this question, at least implicitly, by asserting a fundamental difference between the God of reason known by all nations and the God of Judaism, “the Glorious Name,” known only through particular revelation to Jews. This divine revelation offered Jews eternal life and a personal relationship with the God of history. The same question plagued certain Jewish philosophers of the time, for if Aristotle’s science and metaphysics led the rational mind logically to all necessary truths about God and human ethics, what was the role of revelation? Ben El\`azar’s Indians are philosophers whose metaphysical and moral conclusions he praises, even though they have received no divine revelation. Ben El\`azar, however, never identifies deficiencies in those conclusions. Nor does he ever compare Jewish and Indian theology. Indeed, his constant use of biblical quotations in translating *Kalilah and Dimnah* sharpens the problem by seeming to equate its teachings with those of the Bible.

Muslim and Jewish transformations of the *Panchatantra* Judah Halevi and Moses Maimonides based their knowledge of India on the *Book of Nabatean Agriculture*, a text with no relation to Indian culture, and on a few ethnological items in Muslim reports about India. Ibn Ezra knew of “a book on agriculture” but connected it with Egypt.<sup>16</sup> He based his idea of Indian thought on Indian sciences, including astrology, and on ethnological reports about diet and civic behavior. Ben El\`azar, in contrast, had read and translated an entire book that originated in India. *Kelila wa-Dimna*, the Arabic book he translated, was based on a text close to the one we know today as the *Panchatantra*, written probably around 300 CE in Sanskrit. We might reasonably think, then, that Ben El\`azar’s understanding of Indian thought was deeper and more accurate than the authors we studied earlier. But this conclusion would be questionable, as you may already have realized.

Indian tradition regards the *Panchatantra*, probably meaning “the Five Discourses,” as a treatise on government, a *nitishastra*.<sup>17</sup> But this is a treatise composed of stories interspersed with poems and proverbs teaching political strategies and prudent behavior. In the frame story, a king asks a great Brahmin sage named Vishnusharman to teach his dullard sons the principles of successful government, and the sage does this by telling five sets of stories dedicated to five main topics: “causing dissension among 

*[p. 109]* ^pg122

allies,” “securing allies,” “war and peace,” “losing what you have gained,” and “hasty actions.” Most of the characters in the stories are animals trying to solve various problems of life, and they make poetic speeches and often support their contentions by telling further stories, and then stories within those stories.

The book’s central message, in the words of Patrick Olivelle, is that “craft and deception constitute the major art of government.” Not only should one use deception but one should also guard against its use by others.<sup>18</sup> A prime example of this advice and the book’s narrative style appears in a story in Chapter 3 about a guileful crow. After owls attack and kill many of the crows living in a banyan tree, the king of the crows seeks advice from his ministers about how to respond. Of all his ministers, the oldest one offers the best advice, which is, in light of the superior strength of the owls, to employ deceit. This old minister sends away the other crows and then, when the owls return for a second attack, he feigns injury, claiming his king turned against him. Through guile he gains the trust and protection of the owls and begins residing in their cave. Eventually he finds an opportunity to call in the other crows, who set fire to the cave, killing all the unsuspecting owls. When the old minister again meets his king, he gives a long discourse on how a wise man must sometimes pretend friendship, hide his feelings, and bide his time; on the carelessness and arrogance of enemies who bring about their own ruin; and on how a good man, to succeed in his plans, must focus his mind on timing, location, fortune, and the nature of his enemies and allies. Swords alone cannot defeat enemies; “they are really killed when they’re killed by wit; then they’ll not rise again.”<sup>19</sup> In the course of this story, various characters tell sub-stories to illustrate points in their arguments, and in these sub-stories we meet an old merchant with a young wife who detests him, a Brahmin priest who turns a mouse into a human girl, a cat pretending to be a pious hermit, a hare who tricks an elephant into thinking the Moon God wants him to leave the lake where the hares live, a cobra who invites frogs to ride on his back, an unfaithful wife and her gullible husband, and several more characters.

The *Panchatantra* became very popular throughout India and was soon translated into non-Indian languages to the east and to the west. Indeed, as Olivelle writes, it “spread at an earlier time and more extensively in the world than any other piece of Indian literature, including such well-known texts as the Bhagavadgita.”<sup>20</sup> The Jews of medieval Spain encountered the book in its Arabic form with the title, *Kalila wa-Dimna*. Before reaching them, it had been translated into Middle Persian (Pahlavi) in the 6th century, and then, from this language, into Arabic by the famous 8th c. master of early Arabic literature, Ibn al-Mukaffa\` (d. 756), himself born in Persia. Unfortunately, no manuscript survives of his original translation but only later rewritings of it.<sup>21</sup> I have relied on Thomas Ballantine Irving’s English translation of two 13th-century manuscripts, one Arabic and the other a Castilian translation of an earlier Arabic manuscript.<sup>22</sup>

*[p. 110]* ^pg123

In the Arabic *Kalila wa-Dimna*, the short fables of the *Panchatantra* have stayed the same, but entire chapters have been added and many of the speeches and poems have vanished or appeared in abbreviated form, often with their main ideas and Indian context dulled or absent. All references to Hindu gods, Brahmins, yoga, sacrifices, and pilgrimages have been removed. The frame-story of the *Panchatantra* has changed, becoming a dialogue between the king of India and his chief philosopher, named Bidpai, who tells him stories illustrating basic principles of statecraft and friendship. The speeches of the guileful crow, for example, with their advice about employing deceit and patience to conquer an enemy, appear also in the Arabic text, but a moralizing element enters when the crow minister praises his king for compassion toward the poor, from which the minister learned the useful art of humbling oneself. Brief formulaic references to God have also entered the dialogue, as in “God has bestowed a mighty favor upon us” and “I pray God to maintain you in power.”

As an extended example of the way *Kalila wa-Dimna* modified the *Panchatantra*, let us compare the two versions, Sanskrit and Arabic, of the most famous and longest frame-story of the *Panchatantra*, that of Chapter 1, about two jackals named Karataka and Damanaka (who became Kalila and Dimna in the Arabic versions), who are ministers to a lion king. Damanaka decides to ingratiate himself with the king by helping him deal with a frightening bull who has found his way into the lion’s territory. To impress the king, Damanaka recites wise proverbs and tells animal fables illustrating useful political lessons. But Karataka warns Damanaka against seeking status and power through guile. Eventually, the bull himself becomes a close friend to the king, and this then arouses Damanaka’s envy. He proceeds to lie to the lion about the bull and to the bull about the lion, inciting in them distrust and anger against each other – until they attack and a long fight ends in the death of the bull, which the good king soon regrets. Karataka, now very angry with Damanaka, rebukes him for causing someone’s death, lying to the king, and acting out of envy and desire for power. In the *Panchatantra* the story ends here, but the Arabic version adds an entire chapter in which the guileful Dimna (Damanaka) receives his just punishment after other animals discover what he has done and accuse him before the king, who puts him on trial and then executes him. In the *Panchatantra*, the story of Damanaka illustrates the chapter’s topic, “causing dissension among allies,” while the next chapter, about various animals who become friends by helping each other, illustrates the opposite principle, “securing allies,” thus creating a balanced view of destroying and forming political alliances. But in *Kalila wa-Dimna* the chapter about Dimna’s punishment assumes that Dimna’s deceit has caused such a grave injustice that it must first be rectified in a newly composed chapter before moving to the subject of alliances and friendship.

So now the story as a whole has become a lesson in the penalty for causing conflict among friends. When Ben El\`azar translated this story from *Kalila wa-Dimna*, he did not have to make major changes in the plot or 

*[p. 111]* ^pg124

characterizations. His introduction to his translation had discussed the hypocritical, desire-driven sage, and this typology fit Dimnah perfectly, a smart animal whose behavior Ben El\`azar interpreted primarily as betrayal of friends. Dimnah’s death as just punishment for killing another creature, also fit Ben El\`azar’s ideas. But Ben El\`azar nevertheless made some important changes to the Arabic version.

In an article published in 2011, Alan Verskin compared the speeches of Kalila and Dimna in an Arabic version with Ben El\`azar’s translation and rewriting of them. One of the main differences, he shows, is Ben El\`azar’s greater emphasis on theology and moral conduct.<sup>23</sup> The Arabic Kalila’s advice to Dimna consists almost entirely of pragmatic warnings against seeking higher office through lies and betrayal. Dimna, he declares, acted without considering consequences, ruined the king through smooth deceitful talk, has become vain and acted stupidly, and now everyone shuns him to avoid sharing his bad reputation and influence. Only near the end of the first chapter does Kalila briefly express moral condemnation, berating Dimna for “vices” with “evil consequences.”<sup>24</sup>

In contrast, Ben El\`azar’s Kalilah, from the very beginning of his responses to Dimnah’s plans, adds moral rebuke to his pragmatic advice: not only should Dimnah accept his current position in court but should also “purge your heart of evil thoughts.”<sup>25</sup> Most importantly, Ben El\`azar also adds a pronounced theological component. For example, Kalilah asserts that “every person dwells in the shade of the mercy of God … and in the hand of our good creator.” The creator formed his creatures according to the plan of his righteous laws, and “no man can obtain what is not the portion of his cup or rise in station unless it is his lot.”<sup>26</sup> “Hollow men will not rise to the position of the great and strong, for ‘the rod of the wicked will not rest on the lot of the righteous’” \[[[Psalms 125#^125-3|Psalms 125:3]]\]. Kalilah warns Dimnah that he will fail to change his station in life through immoral means because God decrees each person’s place in the world and frustrates the plans of the wicked. He should be content with his portion in life.

But Ben El\`azar has Dimnah refuse to hear this and at one point Dimnah says that a clever man is naturally drawn to higher station and power, and anyway, “everything depends on the constellations (*mazal*).”<sup>27</sup> With this phrase, Dimnah is asserting that astrological forces will determine the outcome, rather than God or law.<sup>28</sup> However, Dimnah’s eventual downfall confirms Kalilah’s advice, for at the end of this chapter in Ben El\`azar’s translation, the king’s philosopher summarizes the theological lesson in this way:

It is proper for men of understanding … to guard their souls from seeking their own advantage \[in rising\] from the place that He created for a human being in the world, whether with guile or enmity or deceit, for a scheming man, his end will be destruction, “one law to put him to death” \[[[Esther 04#^4-11|Esther 4:11]]\], “returning his deeds upon his head” \[[[Psalms 007#^7-17|Ps. 7:17]]\], “and if it tarries, wait for it” \[[[Habakkuk 2#^2-3|Hab. 2:3]]\].<sup>29</sup>

*[p. 112]* ^pg125

A wicked man has been brought down by the just laws of the universe. The Arabic text also identifies the outcome as “the punishment for covetousness and what happens to envious and lying people,” but lacks the theological dimension of Ben El\`azar’s retelling.<sup>30</sup>

The problem of fate

At this point in the discussion, we would have to say that Ben El\`azar’s summary of Indian philosophy and his translation of stories deriving from the Panchatantra show little connection with that book and its teachings.

But another way to approach this question is through a concept that challenged the central ideas in Ben El\`azar’s interpretation of Indian philosophy: fate. To see how the Panchatantra’s concept of fate made its way through the Arabic *Kalila wa-Dimna* and then into Ben El\`azar’s revision, let us follow the linguistic and cultural translations of a conversation between a dove and mouse that appears in the second chapter of the Panchatantra. The story begins in the Sanskrit book with a hunter who traps a flock of doves in his net. The doves save themselves by all flying off in unity, together raising the net. Still entangled in it, they fly to the home of a friendly mouse so that he can gnaw the net’s strands and free them. This mouse, however, expresses surprise that the doves would allow themselves to be caught. In reply, the king of the doves blames the unpredictable power of “fate.” Olivelle uses the English word “fate” to translate three different Sanskrit words in the stanzas of this conversation: *kritanta*, causing, or bringing to, an end; *vidhi*, what is ordained or laid out, destiny; and *kala*, time, which always overcomes us, doom.<sup>31</sup> The dove then gives examples of animals (elephants, snakes, birds, fish), the sun and moon, and wise men, all brought down by fate. He continues:

Bad behavior, good conduct – do they mean anything in this world? For Fate (*kala*) extends its hand of misfortune and grabs you from afar.<sup>32</sup>

Fate, in this sentence, is a blind power that negates any sense of fairness in the world, attacking the good and the wicked equally.<sup>33</sup>

Comparing the same conversation in the Arabic *Kalila wa-Dimna*, we find similar imagery, and Thomas Irving also uses the English word “fate” for his translation. The leader of the doves (female in this version) tells the mouse that “fate” threw her into her predicament by blinding her eyes to the net. Beings more powerful than she cannot thwart this power, such as the sun and moon, fish, and birds. “There isn’t anything good or bad but it is fated (*muqaddar*) to strike someone upon whom fate strikes (*al-maqadir*).”<sup>34</sup> Here the word translated as fate is *qadar* with its primary sense of measuring, fixing a limit, and appointing, and used here in the sense of fixing a destiny.<sup>35</sup> In the *Arabian Nights*, the word is usually connected with an inescapable personal fate decided by God, and the classical 

*[p. 113]* ^pg126

Islamic theological phrase *al-qada wa ‘l-qadar* refers to God’s decision and decree.<sup>36</sup> But the context of the word in the dove’s speech in *Kalilah wa-Dimna* implies no explicit theological dimension, and indeed, the book contains very little theology. We find the same concept of *qadar* in the first chapter of the book when Dimna tells the ox that the lion hates him. Paralleling the ox’s speech in the *Panchatantra*, the Arabic ox says, “What is it but fate that has brought me to stay with the lion?” Through examples of strong, smart, and industrious animals brought down unexpectedly, the ox portrays *qadar* as a power that overturns all effort and is always unfair, but he resigns himself to it because “fate is never averted.”<sup>37</sup>

Recall, however, that Ben El\`azar has identified the central theology of *Kalilah and Dimnah* as God’s moral law embedded in the structure of the universe, which supports wise and moral behavior but brings down actions driven by inordinate desire. How, then, will Ben El\`azar deal with the dove’s speech about unfair fate?<sup>38</sup> First, we notice that he places in her mouth the same list of strong and smart creatures that appear in the *Panchatantra* and *Kelilah wa-Dimna*, all encountering unexpected misfortune.

And do not be amazed at us \[doves\], for are not the sun and moon darkened, and the fish in the heart of the sea caught by a hook and net, and the bird of the heaven snared by a trap, and do not the feet of the swift stumble, and the lazy one succeeds?

Ben El\`azar’s version differs from the others, however, in identifying the agent who overcomes these creatures, overturning human plans, as God rather than Fate, and Ben El\`azar adduces numerous biblical phrases to illustrate this. The dove says, “‘The way of man is not in his control’ \[[[Jeremiah 10#^10-23|Jer. 10:23]]\] … for ‘it happened to us from the Lord’” \[cf. [[I Kings 02#^2-15|1 Kings 2:15]]\]. Nor will one necessarily understand why one has suffered.

The Creator of everything sometimes reveals the web of a creature and sometimes conceals it, “and the Lord, He will do what seems good in His eyes\[[[I Samuel 03#^3-18|1 Samuel 3:18]]\].”

And human beings cannot resist God.

The human being, what does he have to avail him that would benefit him … and vanity is all his plans, and whatever arises in his mind is naught, for his Maker will raise him up, and his Owner will annul his existence … for all his wealth and all his honor “the Lord will not leave in his hand \[[[Psalms 037#^37-33|Ps. 37:33]]\].”<sup>39</sup>

Ben El\`azar’s response to *Kalilah wa-Dimna*’s concept of Fate, therefore, is first, to accept the reality that people do indeed seem to suffer unfairly. 

*[p. 114]* ^pg127

But unlike the *Panchatantra* and *Kalila wa-Dimna*, Ben El\`azar views God as the cause. Like Fate, God sometimes ignores our needs and efforts. Like Fate, God is far more powerful than we are. In using certain phrases from the Bible, Ben El\`azar even magnifies human weakness and frailty. So what happens to Ben El\`azar’s concept of a just universe that topples the wicked and upholds moral behavior? Of Dimnah, Ben El\`azar had Kalilah say, “For a scheming man, his end will be destruction.”

Like Alan Verskin, I do not see in Ben El\`azar’s book an answer to the problem of unfair suffering or a serious attempt to reconcile the notion of a just rational God with that of a powerful unfathomable God. Did Ben El\`azar even see a moral problem in God’s unfathomable ways? Instead, the main speeches of the Hebrew *Kalilah and Dimnah* insist that moral action is wise because it leads to happier consequences and that, as is clear to all who observe the world about them, God always helps the righteous.

Ben El\`azar’s encounter with the *Panchatantra*

More than any previous Jewish author, Jacob ben El\`azar praised Indian thought. Not only was he translating an Indian book that, in his mind, could teach morality in a new form to his Jewish readers, but he thought this book functioned for Indians in a way parallel to the revealed Torah, even as this Indian book was inferior for not having been revealed by a prophet. In Ben El\`azar’s picture of Indian thought, Indians were not idolaters rejecting prophecy but were rational monotheists who, having yearned for prophecy, were compelled to rely on human reason, through which they had discovered universal moral principles in the universe, and they had written clever animal fables to teach these principles to their people. They conceived of a natural law of morality that placed limits on human desires and upheld a life of harmony and contentment. Unlike Ibn Ezra, who commended *Kalila wa-Dimna* as excellent political advice for rulers, Ben El\`azar saw the book as instruction on interpersonal relations. For this he praised it generously.

But whether he knew more about Indian thought than previous Jewish authors is another matter. His version of Indian thought looks little like the Sanskrit book of *nitishastra* from which his translation ultimately originated. Ben El\`azar received the plot-lines deriving from the *Panchatantra*’s many stories, maintaining their basic narrative, which gave his version a similar appearance. But Ben El\`azar’s Hebrew speeches interpreting those stories diverge significantly from the *Panchatantra*. The particular religious, philosophical, and cultural elements of the Panchatantra did not survive the book’s migration through Persian to Arabic and then to Hebrew. Moreover, with Ben El\`azar’s interest in interpersonal morality as the book’s theme, his translation adds a strong tone of moral judgment absent in the Indian text. And with his expression of theology through biblical phrases, a biblically conceived God repeatedly enters the speeches of the animals and sages who explain the action. We see this theology in 

*[p. 115]* ^pg128

Kalilah’s warnings to Dimnah and the dove’s explanation of her suffering – none of which is found in the Arabic text that Ben El\`azar translated, much less the Panchatantra. Ben El\`azar also explains Dimnah’s downfall as the judgment of divine law. All of this looks quite different from the text of the *Panchatantra* and impels us to conclude that Ben El\`azar had learned nothing about actual Indian thought.

Yet, it may be that Ben El\`azar encountered an element of Indian thought through the *Panchatantra*’s concept of Fate, which reached him in a culturally recast form through *Kalila wa-Dimna*. Ben El\`azar could not ignore the speeches of the ox and the dove of *Kalila wa-Dimna* who explained their predicaments as blind Fate in the form of *qadar*. These speeches forced Ben El\`azar to expand his concept of God to explain undeserved suffering as God’s unfathomable will and power. Of course, Fate is not a concept limited to Indian thought (where it takes a culturally particular form), and the problem of theodicy already occurs prominently in Scripture and is discussed extensively in medieval Jewish philosophy. But judging only by the prominence in Ben El\`azar’s book of the counter-view of a just universe supporting wise behavior and bringing down villains who ignore moral limits, I doubt that the problem of unfair suffering would have entered Ben El\`azar’s book if it had not presented itself in his Arabic source, which was echoing, through its own cultural particularity, the problem of blind Fate and Time in the *Panchatantra*. Perhaps this was Ben El\`azar’s one true encounter with Indian thought.

### 5. Translations and transformations: Immanuel Bonfils, the Brahmans, and Alexander the Great

None of the Jewish ideas about India that we have studied imagined Indians as philosophizing ascetics living in the forest and debating Alexander the Great. That, however, was the image of Indian thinkers that Jews of medieval Italian, French, and German lands encountered. They also read about Indian sages yearning for death – reminiscent of the men of Ibrig in Benjamin’s *Book of Travels* who end their lives by joyously throwing themselves into roaring fire.

Indian philosophers in the writings of Philo of Alexandria 

and Josephus Flavius

Reports about Indian ascetics who courageously threw themselves onto funeral pyres to end their lives, circulated in the Hellenistic world, appearing in the writings of Strabo, Plutarch, and Arrian. Two Jewish authors, Philo of Alexandria (approx. 20 BCE–49 CE) and Josephus Flavius (37– 100 CE), took up these reports for their own writings. These authors could be considered the originators of a Greek tradition in Jewish images of Hinduism, although none of the original Greek works of either author was read by medieval Jews, and Jews rediscovered them only in the 16th century. We now turn briefly to Hellenistic Alexandria and early imperial Rome to examine how Philo and Josephus portrayed Indian philosophers.

Philo drew upon the image of self-immolating Indians in three of his late treatises.<sup>1</sup> In his treatise concerning the biblical Abraham, Philo commends him for his willingness to sacrifice his son Isaac, a unique act of piety in his time. Philo then raises a hypothetical counter-argument pointing out that other nations and individuals were equally willing to sacrifice themselves and their children for a greater good. Indian gymnosophists (“naked philosophers”) appear here as an example of selfless courage when they throw themselves onto burning pyres as soon as “the long incurable disease of old age begins” even though they could have lived many years longer. Philo adds that one could with good reason praise their wives, too, for courage and contempt for death when they “hasten rejoicing to share their pyre,” burning themselves alive with their husbands.<sup>2</sup> Philo replies to this 

*[p. 119]* ^pg132

counter-argument, however, that all the Greek and foreign examples of sacrificing oneself or one’s children were performed out of custom or fear or the pursuit of honor, whereas only Abraham offered his son out of pure obedience to God and piety (*eusebeia*) and against his strong love for his only son.<sup>3</sup> Indian philosophers and their wives thus function in this treatise as examples of courageous self-sacrifice but of a lower order than the biblical courage motivated by pure obedience and piety.

Philo’s treatise on dreams refers just briefly to “naked philosophers” sleeping outdoors on the ground,<sup>4</sup> but they appear prominently in a philosophical treatise titled “Every Good Man Is Free,” which argues an apparently straight Stoic philosophy, perhaps for a non-Jewish audience. The essay distinguishes sharply between body (*soma*) and mind (*psyche*, *dianoia)*, arguing that freedom of the mind is the only true freedom (*eleutheria*) and is attainable only through indifference to bodily needs and passions such as the fear of death.<sup>5</sup> Philo points to the Essenes living in Palestinian Syria as proof that a whole community, in attaining virtue, also attains freedom, and then to Indian philosophers as examples of individuals whose virtuous and courageous lives demonstrate the actual existence of human freedom in this world, even among barbarians. These gymnosophists, claims Philo, study moral and natural philosophy and make their lives models of virtue (*arete*).<sup>6</sup> Philo then points to one gymnosophist named Calanus as a model of true courage which cares nothing for the body.<sup>7</sup> When Alexander commands Calanus to follow him to Greece, Calanus refuses:

Souls you will not compel …. Fire causes the greatest trouble and ruin to living bodies, but we are superior to this. We burn ourselves alive. There is no king, no ruler, who will compel us to do what we do not freely wish to do.<sup>8</sup>

Here, Indian thinkers appear as perfect examples of Stoic indifference, courage, and spiritual freedom. Philo’s opinion of them in this treatise, however, seems to differ from his view of them in his treatise on Abraham.

A related image of Indian courage would later enter medieval Jewish literature, but not directly from Philo and the Hellenistic accounts that he learned from. Rather, those accounts served as a background for a new picture of Indians that appeared in Josephus’ *The War of the Jews*, written around 75 CE, which provided the foundation of the medieval book titled *Sefer Yosippon*. The picture in Josephus’ book had gained an otherworldly ideology, replacing the Stoic courage of Indian philosophers with a Platonic longing for spiritual immortality. In *The War of the Jews* “Indians who profess to practice philosophy” appear as a rhetorical example in the now-famous speech of El\`azar ben Ya’ir at Masada. Urging his followers to commit suicide rather than allow Roman soldiers to conquer them and their families, he points to “the testimony of foreigners,” Indians of “true courage” who, out of great longing for an “immortal state,” hasten to throw themselves 

*[p. 120]* ^pg133

onto funeral pyres even though they suffer no illness or misfortune. They live their lives in reluctant servitude to their bodies, all the while yearning for release. When they do decide to free their souls through the purification of fire, all their friends and family rejoice because they all believe firmly in an afterlife of immortal souls where they will again meet their loved ones. Their friends send them off to their deaths with rejoicing and hymns.<sup>9</sup>

Just before speaking about Indians, El\`azar had already portrayed the situation of the human soul in a form similar to their theology. He had told his followers that the soul (*psyche*) is imprisoned in the body, and only death provides true freedom from all suffering, so that bodily life is the real death. It is only bodily death that restores the soul to its “proper sphere” and provides energy and god-like power. Hence, argues El\`azar, Jews should not fear death or allow the “poorer spirit” of Indians, who lack Torah (law, *nomos*), to shame them and bring the Torah into contempt when people see the Jews of Masada cowering before their own suicide.<sup>10</sup>

Menachem Stern, Menachem Luz (1983), and others have argued that these ideas about the soul and the afterlife derive from a popular Hellenistic genre of consolation literature circulating in Josephus’ time. These works, by joining a strong sense of suffering in life to Plato’s arguments in *Phaedo* for the soul’s immortality, intended to demonstrate that, in Luz’s summary, “one should not grieve over death because it is a great good and better than life itself.”<sup>11</sup> These are the ideas that Josephus has added, as a new justification, to the earlier reports about Indians immolating themselves.

Josephus himself, however, despite the moving discourse he composed for El\`azar, seems to have disagreed with him and his Indian philosophers. As Tessa Rejak points out, Josephus undermines El\`azar’s arguments through an earlier speech voiced by his own persona.<sup>12</sup> There Josephus, while still a Jewish commander, tries to persuade his followers that suicide (*autocheiria*) is wrong because it is unnatural and represents disobedience to God and impiety (*asebeia*). Rather, Jews should surrender to Rome because God has sent it to conquer Judea as punishment for the sins of the Jews. Josephus expresses a belief in the soul’s survival with reward or punishment after death, and then – in line with his definition of Pharisaic doctrine – after it has enjoyed a holy place in Heaven, it will be reborn on earth into a pure body. Life is not imprisonment for the soul to endure; rather, the soul is a precious gift from God, a loan not to be returned until the time that only God determines.<sup>13</sup> Holding these ideas, Josephus must have disagreed with the Indian philosophers of El\`azar’s speech who yearned for death, seeing it as a liberation of the soul from the body’s painful prison, a liberation that one had the right to enact by one’s own decision.

Indian ascetics in “*The Book of Josephus*”

Indian philosophers later appear in two different forms in the popular medieval book called *Sefer Yosippon*, “The Book of Josephu*s*,” composed 

*[p. 121]* ^pg134

in Italy in the 10th century in a biblical style of Hebrew. Based mainly on a 4th-century Latin version of the historical writings of Josephus Flavius, it came to circulate widely among Jews in medieval Muslim and Christian Europe. Biblical and Talmudic commentators quoted it, scribes rewrote, modified, and edited it several times, and it was later printed in many editions and translated into Yiddish and other languages.

The first image of Indian philosophers in the *Yosippon* derived ultimately from Josephus’ *The War of the Jews*, based on the life-denying Indian ascetics of El\`azar’s speech. As determined by the investigations of David Flusser, the original manuscripts of the book contained this version of the speech:

And he \[El\`azar ben \`Annani\] told them further about the sages of India, who if they would die, they died, and refrained from crying or lamenting over him, for they say: has not the soul gone to the place of its life, which is a place of eternal life. And the other nations listen to them and believe that there is hope at the end – and we \[Jews\] who know the mercy of *‘Adonai* our God, is it not proper for us to believe that there is hope after our end?<sup>14</sup>

The *Yosippon* omits the motif of self-immolation, replacing it with the Indians’ firm faith in an eternal afterlife and their detachment from life in this world. El\`azar’s purpose is to shame his Jewish followers into showing at least as much faith in the afterlife as do the Indians, who lack the close relationship with God given to Jews.

Later printings of the *Yosippon* modified much of this story. The printings based on the Mantua edition of 1476–1479 introduced the Indian sages with two biblical verses bemoaning the sorrows of life:

And about this Solomon said, Good is the day of death \[from [[Ecclesiastes 07#^7-1|Eccl. 7:1]]\]. And further he said, Man (‘*adam*) is born to trouble \[[[Job 05#^5-7|Job 5:7]]\]. And also the sages of India do likewise because man (‘*adam*), his sorrows are before him all kinds of song \[*sic*\], and see, they have no Torah of God. They believe that there is hope after their end and moreover that we are his slaves knowing his reward.”<sup>15</sup>

We gain a glimpse of world-weary Indians seeking the afterlife who agree with the sad sentiments of Solomon, but because of the faulty text, we do not understand why they sing. Despite their lack of revelation, these Indians believe in the same God as Jews do, a God who rewards obedient servants after death.

However, this account of Indians longing for death disappeared altogether in the more popular and longer version of the *Yosippon* printed in Constantinople in 1510, upon which most later printings were based.<sup>16</sup> Such, then, was the fate of the Hellenistic image of Indians adopted by Philo, which portrayed them courageously throwing themselves onto 

*[p. 122]* ^pg135

burning pyres, and such too Josephus’ image of Indians yearning for death followed by the survival of their immortal souls.

The second image of Indians, picturing them as ascetic philosophers who debated with Alexander the Great, was inserted into the *Yosippon* two centuries after its original composition.<sup>17</sup> Jews who read this revision of the book encountered Indian “wisdom” through the words of “sages” described as “exceedingly wise and naked without clothes.”

These Indians live in booths and dusty caves. When Alexander approaches with his army, they tell him they will not fight him, “for unto you fighting is proper while unto us wisdom is fitting.”<sup>18</sup> Then Alexander asks them a series of riddles. To the question, “Don’t you have graves?” they reply, “My dwelling (*mishkani*) is my grave.” Question: “What is kingship?” Reply: “Extortion and robbery.” Alexander asks which is better: animals or human beings, night or day, the right side or the left. The gymnosophists answer each riddle with terse explanations.

But in the *Yosippon* these explanations differ from the early Greek texts upon which this episode was based. The most pronounced differences show a concern for the poor and a Jewish concept of God. In reply to the question, “Who number more, the living or the dead,” the Indian sages of the *Yosippon* say, “The dead are more numerous, for the poor and the dead are more numerous than the rich and the living.” The original Greek text, however, lacks any mention of the poor. Most tellingly, the *Yosippon* adds a question-and-reply entirely absent in the Greek: “To whom can a man not lie?” “To the God of truth (*‘El ‘emet*) who knows truth.” The phrase “God of truth” echoes [[Psalms 031#^31-6|Psalms 31:6]] and is used in the Talmud and Jewish liturgy. These changes to the original debate bring the gymnosophists closer to traditional rabbinic thinking.

Then the gymnosophists ask Alexander why he wages war “to pillage and loot and subdue the world,” for he cannot bring his conquests with him when he dies. Alexander admits his desire for a quiet life like that of the Indians, but he claims he must acquiesce to the will of God (*reshut shamayim*), “the lord of all,” and conquer the world.<sup>19</sup>

The Mantua printing (1476–1479) of the *Yosippon* preserved this story fairly well, but the Constantinople printing of 1510 and its later editions in Venice, Amsterdam, Warsaw, and elsewhere, dropped the legends about Alexander’s life, including his encounter with the naked sages of India, just as it had dropped the Indians of El\`azar’s speech.<sup>20</sup>

The Alexander Romance in Christian Europe

It was this second image in the *Yosippon*, that of philosophizing ascetics who debated with Alexander, which formed an episode in a fictionalized retelling of the life of Alexander the Great that circulated widely in Europe in several forms as “The Alexander Romance,” a legendary version of his life distinct from more historical accounts such as that of Arrian of Nicomedia. It was through the Alexander Romance written in Latin, 

*[p. 123]* ^pg136

expanding on and rewriting the original Greek version, that medieval Christian Europe formed its image of India and its philosophy.<sup>21</sup> Beginning with a Latin translation by Leo the Arch-Priest in the 10th century, “the Romance would be translated more frequently than any other work except the Gospels,” wrote Richard Stoneman (2008).

The figure of Alexander would be incorporated into Arthurian legend and into sacred scripture; he would become an example for moralists and theologians and a vehicle of scientific knowledge of the age; a model for kings and emperors and an emblem of the life of man equal, sometimes, even to Christ.<sup>22</sup>

For example, Alexandre de Paris in his 12th-century book, *Roman d’Alex* *andre*, rewrote the Latin text as a poem portraying Alexander as “a chivalric hero of a feudal society who swears by Jupiter but also prays to God and introduces cults of saints.”<sup>23</sup> Rudolf von Ems, in the 13th century, shaped Alexander as a humble knight of the cross empowered by God to win battles.<sup>24</sup> There were many more rewritings of his character.

The Latin translations of the Romance usually included scenes of Alexander’s meeting with India’s philosophers, called *Bragmani* (Brahmans), and the lengthy letters that their king, Dindimus, wrote setting forth their beliefs. The letters had been incorporated into the medieval romances from a short Latin work called *The Correspondence of Alexander and Dindimus*, written before the 8th century.<sup>25</sup> This expanded Latin text of the Romance, rewritten a number of times, began to portray the *Bragmani* as righteous ascetics not unlike Christian hermits.

In 1978, Thomas Hahn, in an article on “The Indian Tradition in Western Medieval Intellectual History,” identified statements about Indian sages found in Christian theological literature written from the 3rd through the 14th centuries. He demonstrated that the image of Brahmans changed during this period from one of misguided ascetics, as in Augustine’s *City of God*, to a widespread medieval view of them as philosophers who exhibited admirable wisdom and virtue without need of Christian revelation. Hahn argued that this final image recurred so widely in medieval Christian writings that it could be considered “a unified tradition of thought about these virtuous Indians.”<sup>26</sup> George Cary (1956), in *The Medieval Alexander*, provides some examples of this Christian image of India’s Brahmans. The 12th-century historians Godfrey of Viterbo (in Germany or Italy) and Jacque de Vitry (France) portrayed Dindimus as a Christian preacher who berated Alexander for arrogantly calling himself the son of Jupiter.<sup>27</sup> In the words of Hahn,

Geoffrey \[Godfrey\] repeatedly has the Indian philosopher employ paraphrases and quotations from the Bible in his castigation of the boastful Macedonian king, and in this way the poet demonstrates the less-than-veiled understanding of the Brahmans and their adherence to divine truths.<sup>28</sup>

*[p. 124]* ^pg137

Hahn quotes passages from other Christian thinkers who praised the Brahmans. Peter Abelard in the same century applauded the devotions and abstinence of the Brahman people and their “sacred faith” in a universal God above the nations; Brahmans lived a virtuous life without Christ or revelation.<sup>29</sup> Hugh of St. Victor (12th century, Saxony) saw Dindimus as a model of virtue and truthfulness.<sup>30</sup> Thomas of Cantimpre (13th century) wrote of the “wondrous worship, innocence, morality, and life” of the Brahmans, their “holy life and morals” and “their worship of one God” and belief in “the coeternity of the Son with the Father.”<sup>31</sup>

The translation by Immanuel Bonfils: 

Similarities with his Latin source

Jewish authors, too, translated and rewrote the Alexander Romance. Three medieval translations have survived. These are all based on 12th-century versions of the Romance that included the entire *Correspondence of Alexander and Dindimus*. Each of these Hebrew translations has its own particular traits, but I shall limit my examination to the one that claims to be translated directly from a Latin source (probably Recension J<sup>2</sup> of *Historia de preliis*) and whose author is known. This translation is the manuscript found in the Bibliotheque Nationale in Paris as part of Ms. Hebreu 750, which was translated into English and discussed by Israel J. Kazis in 1962.<sup>32</sup> Its Hebrew title is *Sefer Toldot Alexandros Ha-Makdoni*, “The Book of the Events of Alexander the Macedonian,” and it was composed in the middle of the 14th century by Immanuel ben Jacob Bonfils, a mathematician, astronomer, and physician living in Provence. He thought the stories in his translation would offer readers “a source of delight … as honey to their taste.” He added that he did not believe the wondrous phenomena recounted in the stories of Alexander.<sup>33</sup> Bonfils is best known for his book of astronomical tables, composed in 1365, that dealt with the movements of the sun and moon.

In Bonfils’ translation of the Alexander Romance, as in the Latin source, the Brahmans appear in two connected episodes – first, paralleling the story in the *Yosippon*, as naked philosophers who answer Alexander’s riddles, and second and much longer, as *Bargamnim* (Brahmans) whose ethics, asceticism, and criticism of Western values come into view through a long letter written by Dindimus to Alexander. Jews who read Bonfils’ translation encountered a more complex and philosophical image of Indians than had appeared in the *Yosippon*, and it deserves some careful study. To discern the changes that Bonfils made to the Latin version of the Alexander Romance, I shall compare Bonfil’s translation with his Latin source, Recension J<sup>2</sup>, asking how his translation portrays the Brahmans and their philosophy and how it differs from this source. I will then explore what we might learn from Bonfils’ translation about the way he compared Indian and Jewish thought.

*[p. 125]* ^pg138

In translating the Latin letters of Dindimus, Bonfils rewrote nearly every sentence to express ideas in his own way for his Jewish readers. Many of these changes were primarily stylistic. In the Latin text, Dindimus begins his discourse with these words: “We Brahmans live a simple and pure life (*simplicem et puram vitam*). We commit no sins and wish to have no more than the proper share required by our nature. We endure everything and persevere.”<sup>34</sup> Bonfils rewrote this as “the *Bargamnim* lead a life of privation (*tza\`ar*), are pure (*tamimim*), and will not commit a sin or wrong. They do not desire more than suffices for them or than fate (*mazel*) brings about. They will bear everything for righteousness.”<sup>35</sup> Perhaps Bonfils shows more interest in morality than the Latin original, but this is not always the case elsewhere in his translation.

In both the Latin and Hebrew texts, Dindimus proceeds to define the basic principles of Brahman life. They eat only the minimum amount of food needed for survival, do without houses and without fires in the winter, wear only leaves for clothing, neither farm the land nor fish the waters. They live in voluntary poverty. Dindimus identifies the essence of this life as the conquest of desires. Brahmans have no human enemies but only inner enemies, namely, their own desires, which they have succeeded in conquering. Doing so, they feel no need to fight outer enemies, and they live in security and freedom. As equals in their own society, they feel no envy and commit no crimes, need neither laws nor courts, and abhor slavery. They never study rhetoric but speak simply and minimally, valuing silence. For pleasure they observe the beauties of nature: the stars and sun, fish leaping in the purple sea, flowers, and singing birds.

In both the Latin and Hebrew texts, Dindimus then pronounces a long and sophisticated denunciation of Western society and worship. He accuses Alexander and his people of pride and never being satisfied, since their desires rule over them. They bring war and discord to the entire world and spread slavery. They commit many sins and especially adultery and incest. Westerners speak eloquently but without wisdom, and their judges distort the law. Westerners eat and drink to extreme and own an excess of possessions, causing them insecurity. Their worship consists of venerating many gods, such as Jupiter and Proserpina, who engage in sin and force their followers to sin. Westerners worship a different god for each part of their bodies – Minerva for the head, Mercury for the tongue, and so on. And Western worship consists of bloody animal sacrifices, gold statues of the gods, and bejeweled altars in beautiful temples, even though the true God above, creator of the world, demands only good deeds and prayer from us. For their misdeeds, Westerners will suffer punishments after they die.

Such is the picture of Indian thought presented by both the Latin and Hebrew texts. Indians appear in both texts as austere masters over their bodies, adherents of a strict morality, simple dwellers on the earth, and fiery critics of Western values. They resemble Cynics, the Greek thinkers who sought inner freedom and a simple life close to nature, apart from the 

*[p. 126]* ^pg139

vices of society.<sup>36</sup> But these Indians also speak of one righteous God and an afterlife of reward and punishment.

General differences from the Latin source

While Immanuel Bonfils retained these central ideas of the Latin text of Dindimus’ letter, he also made some significant changes. Israel Kazis, in his groundbreaking study of the Bonfils translation, identified the major ways that Bonfils modified the Latin Romance. Foremost among those changes, the Hebrew text “abounds in Biblical expressions and allusions,” and Kazis lists a dozen of them from various parts of the story. For example, after Porus, the king of India, dies, Alexander quotes [[Micah 4#^4-4|Micah 4:4]] in promising peace to the Indian soldiers, saying they would “sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree.” And to recount Alexander’s death, Bonfils paraphrases [[Torah 12 - Vayechi#^49-33|Gen. 49:33]] telling Jacob’s death: Alexander “drew his feet into the bed and, breathing his last, he was gathered to his people.”<sup>37</sup>

In addition, Kazis identified several ways that Bonfils elaborated and expanded on elements of the Latin text. He added minor plot actions such as Candace the queen asking Alexander to “lie with her.” Bonfils filled in details “where the Latin leaves room for imagination.” Kazis gives this example: “The Latin speaks of the deformities of the people. The Hebrew however describes the nature of the deformities: ‘they had but one leg, one arm and one eye …. ’” Bonfils also made “rhetorical embellishments” through similes and hyperbole. An example of hyperbole: instead of the Latin’s “Darius … wept and was very disturbed,” Bonfils wrote, “Darius … wept bitterly, fell to the ground in a state of bewilderment, and his heart grew faint.”

But Bonfils made more substantive changes by, in Kazis’ term, “Judaizing” the Latin text – meaning that he inserted Jewish theology or social values. For example, when Alexander daringly enters the palace of Queen Candace in disguise, he is discovered but narrowly escapes and returns safely to his army. Bonfils then adds an entirely new idea, namely, that “the Lord” saved him in order to “make him ruler over the entire world, thus fulfilling the vision of the prophet Daniel.”<sup>38</sup> I will add two more examples of “Judaizing” that involved Alexander. In the Latin text, Alexander often worships at foreign temples, sacrificing to local gods and to his divine protector, Ammon. Bonfils retains much of Alexander’s sacrificing to “the gods” (*ha-’elohi-im*), but sometimes he has Alexander speak of the one Creator (*ha-bore’*) or use the Tetragrammaton in referring to God, as when Alexander first speaks to the Macedonians after King Phillip’s death: “The Lord (*‘Adonai*) will give me power to save you.”<sup>39</sup> When Alexander later receives a prophecy of his imminent death, he turns not to Ammon but to the Great God (*ha-’el ha-gadol*) who is “righteous”; using words from [[II Samuel 19#^19-38|2 Samuel 19:38]], he then confesses his sins and surrenders himself into God’s hands.<sup>40</sup> Thus, altering the Latin source, Bonfils’ translation “Judaizes” Alexander into a sometime-monotheist and occasionally even a devotee of 

*[p. 127]* ^pg140

*‘Adonai*, the specifically Jewish term for God. In this way Judaization of Alexander paralleled what Bonfils’ Christian counterparts were doing in turning Alexander into a Christian knight or pious Christian king.

Bonfils’ Hebrew revision of Dindimus’ letter

We find the same stylistic and theological tendencies in Bonfils’ translation of Dindimus’ letter to Alexander. Most obvious are the biblical and rabbinic phrases that shape the way Dindimus expresses his views. To convey one of the central concepts of Brahman thought, the mastery of desires, Bonfils uses the language of a famous statement by Ben Zoma in the Mishnah ([[Pirkei Avot 04#^4-1|Avot 4.1]]): “Who is a mighty warrior (*gibbor*)? He who conquers his *yetzer*” (impulse, desire). Bonfils places this metaphor in Dindimus’ letter as “You are a mighty warrior in conquering your impulses” (*gibbor bikvoshkha et yitzrekha*) and “a mighty warrior over his impulses” (*gibbor al yitzro*).<sup>41</sup> This language fits well with the Latin text’s metaphor of waging battle against desire, the inner enemy, but roots the idea in the rabbinic wording of it. Bonfils also inserts a complete biblical verse ([[Torah 02 - Noach#^8-21|Gen. 8:21]]) about human impulses: “the *yetzer* of man’s heart is evil from his youth.” So Dindimus voices words from Scripture as well as Talmud, and in this way, the Latin metaphor of conquest has taken Jewish form. Dindimus sounds like a rabbi also when, to express the idea of simple and minimal speech, he utters another statement from the Mishnah: “A fence for wisdom is silence” ([[Pirkei Avot 03#^3-13|Avot 3.13]]).

Consider as another example of biblical language, Dindimus’ attack on Greek sacrifices. The Latin declares that neither money nor “the blood of a calf or a goat or a ram” gain God’s favor, but only good works and prayer.<sup>42</sup> Perhaps the Latin distantly reflects Isaiah’s rebuke in 1:11–17, but Bonfils’ translation captures more if it. Various citations from the Bible and Talmud come together in his rendition of Dindimus’ diatribe:

Does the Lord take pleasure in the sacrifice of rams (*lizvo-ah. ‘elim*) and in burnt offerings (*\`olot*)? In doing this, has one served his Lord? Is it not rather that one is reconciled with God by acting the mighty warrior over his impulses and pursuing wisdom and dwelling in solitude and seeking his Creator and he does not walk after the stubbornness of his heart.<sup>43</sup>

The first sentence takes up Isaiah’s oracle ([[Isaiah 01#^1-10|Isa. 1:10]]) in which God expresses angry disgust over excessive sacrifices (*rov zivh. eikhem*) and burnt offerings of rams (*\`olot ‘elim*) performed by Judeans while they are also committing bloody crimes. But instead of concluding, like Isaiah’s oracle, with God’s demand for social justice, Bonfils returns to the theme of conquering desire, which he expresses through the rabbinic metaphor of a warrior’s might, but then introduces an idea absent from the Latin: seeking God in solitude. In the process he adds a biblical phrase related to mastering desire: resisting the obduracy of the heart – after an expression appearing three times in 

*[p. 128]* ^pg141

the Book of Jeremiah: “they will no longer walk after the stubbornness of their hearts.”<sup>44</sup>

Dindimus’ letter is filled with such quotations. Three more examples will suffice. First, the Hebrew Dindimus berates the Greeks for imagining they had acquired their “vast riches” by their own power and the might of their hands (a phrase from [[Torah 46 - Eikev#^8-7|Deut. 8:7]]), and for ignoring the fact that at death, they will lose all their gold, silver, and ornaments. Here Bonfils adds words from [[Job 01#^1-21|Job 1:21]] and [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^3-19|Gen. 3:19]]: “You came out of your mother’s womb naked and you will return naked to the earth from which you were taken because you are dust and to the dust you shall return.”<sup>45</sup> Second, when the Hebrew Dindimus claims that Brahmans fight no wars, he adds, “We have beaten our swords and spears and we do not learn war,” a paraphrase of [[Isaiah 02#^2-4|Isa. 2:4]].<sup>46</sup> Finally, in decrying slavery, when the Latin text declares that Nature “gave men to be brothers” (*natura fratem dedit*) and “the one heavenly father” (*unus pater celestis*) created human beings as brothers, Bonfils ignores the reference to Nature (as he does throughout his translation) but expands the reference to God by paraphrasing [[Malachi 2#^2-10|Malachi 2:10]]. He writes, “We all have one Father, and one God created us, and why do we break faith with one another, profaning the covenant of our God?”<sup>47</sup> The context implies that enslaving other human beings profanes this universal covenant.

Not only does Bonfils add Jewish quotations and metaphors; he also reworks the content of the Latin letters of Dindimus. Kazis’ term “Judaizing” fits the following examples. When the Latin Dindimus states that Brahman schools “teach us to live and point out what the scriptures (*scriptura*) say … \[and\] teach us to help others as true justice requires,” the Hebrew Dindimus characterizes Brahman schools as meeting-houses (*ken* *esiyot*) containing books of laws and teachings (*h. uqot, mishpatim, torot*) that Brahmans study as a source of joy, and “we observe our teachings and laws as our sages and ancestors (*h. akhameinu v’kadmoneinu*) taught us.”<sup>48</sup> So the Brahmans now resemble rabbinic students taking joy in studying law and following the teachings of their *h. akhamim*, a word connoting rabbis in a Jewish context.

Another “Judaization” occurs in regard to the reason why the Brahman wives reject jewelry. Whereas the Latin wives wish to be beautiful “through their natural appearance,” since Nature cannot be changed, the Hebrew wives reject jewelry to avoid being “pleasing in the eyes of other men.”<sup>49</sup> Bonfils has again ignored the Latin text’s concept of Nature, substituting the motive of marital fidelity.

The Latin Dindimus delivers some harsh criticisms of Western social values, but Bonfils sometimes interprets Western vices theologically, as forms of idolatry. Thus, the Latin Dindimus reproaches the Greeks for amassing gold and silver, whereas Bonfils turns this into “Gold, silver, and wealth are your gods and you worship them.”<sup>50</sup> The Latin rebukes them for ornamenting their tombs and building luxurious sepulchers, but in Hebrew the Greeks “worship the dust of your graves and the wood of the coffins.”<sup>51</sup>

*[p. 129]* ^pg142

Both the Latin and the Hebrew texts speak of punishment after death, although the Latin text is more explicit. For example, “Surely you will deserve the wages and torments you will receive after death for your sins.” Instead, Bonfils only hints of suffering after death, using vague phrases such as “guilty after death” and “recompense for your deeds after death.” He does include a long description of Gehinom that is absent in the Latin: adulterers will “lie in Gehenna, a place of darkness and shadows, and inherit *She’ol* and *Avadon*” – although the suffering in this Hell is still vague.<sup>52</sup>

But more importantly, Bonfils adds to the Latin his own concept of Two Worlds: a lowly perishable one (*ha-\`olam hazeh ha-kaleh veha-shafal*) and an eternal blissful upper world (*\`olam ha-elyon ha-neh. mad veha-yaqar*). Sinners see only this lower world, but the Brahmans control their desires in this world and care far more about the upper world that they will enjoy after death.<sup>53</sup> Thus, instead of the simple “life of the world to come” (*vita futuri speculi*) in the Latin text,<sup>54</sup> Bonfils has inserted (twice in Dindimus’ discourse) the more detailed structure of Two Worlds.

Another important difference in Bonfils’ translation of the Latin romance is a greater importance given to God. Thus, for Bonfils the main purpose of overcoming desires is to gain the ability to acknowledge one’s Creator at all times.<sup>55</sup> Unlike the Latin, moreover, he has the Brahmans say that “the Lord, the Creator of all” is in their midst so they need no one else.<sup>56</sup> Unlike the Latin, too, Bonfils sees the purpose of Brahman solitude as enabling a Brahman to approach his Creator (*le-hasig et bor’o*).<sup>57</sup> In addition to the rather abstract term “Creator,” the Hebrew text has Dindimus speak of *‘Adonai*, the particular Jewish name of God (abbreviated in Hebrew through the letter *yud* doubled). For example, “Have you not heard,” exclaims Dindimus, “that the Lord, God (*‘Adonai ‘Elohim*), living and true,” will not forgive your iniquities simply because of sacrifices.<sup>58</sup>

And this sentence brings us to Bonfils’ most creative contribution to the Latin concept of God. In the Latin text, God will not forgive sinners merely for offering sacrifices because, as we saw, God wants good works and words of prayer from them. The Latin then inserts a paraphrase of [[John 01#^1-1|John 1:1-2]]: “Only through words does man resemble God because God is the Word (*Deus Verbam est*), and that Word created the world. We worship this Word, we adore it, we love it.”<sup>59</sup> What does Bonfils do with this paraphrase of the New Testament? He simply repeats it, perhaps not knowing its source, and then dilutes it by adding two more metaphors for God and then claiming that all metaphors are inadequate anyway.

For God is the utterance (*ha-’el hu ha-dibbur*) and by utterance was the world created. And this utterance we Brahmans love and to Him we pray because the revered Lord (*‘Adonai ha-nikhbad*) is hope (*toh. elet*), delightful and memorable … He is also called light because he illumines the entire universe.<sup>60</sup>

*[p. 130]* ^pg143

Scripture often associates God with light, especially in the Book of Psalms, and the metaphor appears prominently in the Zohar. God is identified with hope in several Psalms, but using the more common word, *tikvah*. In the end, however, argues the Hebrew Dindimus, “No one can apprehend Him as to likeness, comparison, beauty, and splendor, and he cannot be told or written about.”<sup>61</sup> So although God can be called utterance, hope, and light, he is really beyond all resemblance to anything we know – a concept expressed by medieval philosophers such as Maimonides and commentators such as Malbim. And so God is not really the Word after all! The Latin text contains nothing like Bonfils’ philosophical concept of God’s ineffability.

With these changes to his Latin source, Bonfils has rephrased Brahman speech and reshaped Brahman beliefs. His Brahmans still speak of asceticism and reclusion, which are unusual values for rabbinic Judaism, but now they do so in the more moderate language of the Bible and Talmud. They condemn Greeks with the denunciations of biblical prophets. The main actions of Bonfils’ Brahmans are to commune with God in solitude and to attend study houses where they learn to follow the laws and teachings of their sages and forefathers. Their wives speak of marital fidelity rather than the ways of Nature. Western excesses look to Bonfils’ Brahmans like biblical idolatry. They refer vaguely to Hell but define Heaven precisely as the higher of Two Worlds. These Brahmans speak often of the one Creator, even using particular Jewish terminology (*‘Adonai*), and they conceive of God through an abstract concept of divine ineffability.

Translation as comparison

Bonfil’s Brahmans are still the married ascetics of the Latin text who live in the wild, conquer their desires, and embrace poverty, simplicity, and absolute purity. But they also quote the Bible like Jews, study laws like rabbis, and believe in an ineffable God whom they call by Jewish names. This is the image of Indian beliefs that Jews encountered who read Bonfils’ translation. Can we infer from this translation, however, anything about Bonfils’ response to those beliefs? Can we infer a comparison between these Brahman beliefs and Bonfils’ version of Jewish beliefs?

That depends on what Bonfils’ revisions meant to him. Israel Kazis thought these changes were merely matters of style, to elaborate, fill out, dramatize, or Judaize the Latin text. Bonfils’ role, in Kazis’ view, was to turn the Latin sentences into a language that his Jewish readers could understand easily and enjoy. In other words, he made the foreign, familiar; the dull, dramatic. He was entertaining his readers rather than educating them.

How much of his revisions did he actually believe to be true? Did he really think Alexander prayed to *‘Adonai* as well as Greek gods? Did he really think the Brahmans quoted the Bible and Mishnah and studied laws in study houses? Surely he did not, but he could certainly have thought that some Brahman ideas found in the Latin text could be expressed 

*[p. 131]* ^pg144

through verses from the Bible and Mishnah, and that the Brahman study of wisdom resembled the Jewish study of Torah. He could have thought that the Brahman value of self-control was the same as that of Ben Zoma’s dictum in the Mishnah about conquering one’s *yetzer* like a warrior. On the other hand, Bonfils did not “Judaize” certain other ideas of the Brahmans – their extreme poverty, living in the wild, their concept of living in accord with Nature. He would have had trouble phrasing these concepts in traditional Jewish sentences. The Brahmans could be imagined as similar to Jews in a number of ways but differed in other ways that could not be bridged.

In a sense, then, Bonfils was engaging in an act of comparison by Judaizing certain elements of the Brahman character and doctrine, and leaving other elements unchanged. In a sense, he had to ask himself: to which of these elements can I find parallels in biblical and rabbinic language and concept, and which elements must I omit or transmit as is?

He finds points of similarity between Brahman and Jewish values, such as self-control, material contentment, morality, simple speech, rejecting idolatry, a righteous deity, the superiority of prayer and meditation over sacrifices, world peace, the study of books, awareness of death and of reward and punishment after death. He modifies extreme asceticism into the more moderate idea of self-control. He translates Brahman statements about God into Jewish terminology and metaphors and then expands them. These are the ideas of Dindimus that Bonfils could value and turn into Jewish form and language. But he also leaves unchanged other characteristics that are not traditionally Jewish, including, as I said, living in the wild, radical voluntary poverty, and the doctrine of *Natura*, which are expressions of Greek Cynicism found in the Latin text. He could not “Judaize” these elements; they are the irreducible differences.

Bonfil’s picture of the Brahmans bears some similarity to Ibn Ezra’s image of Indian sages: Dindimus espouses a simple monotheism and a doctrine of multiple worlds and, using astronomical terminology absent from the Latin text, refers to “the motion of the sun, the revolution of the spheres, and the motion of the moving stars \[planets\].”<sup>62</sup> Indeed, Bonfils’ Indians resemble Ibn Ezra’s Indians much more than the idolaters and magicians told by Ha-Levi and Benjamin of Tudela. Bonfils’ Brahmans exhibit rationality, high morality, and monotheistic belief. They are not, however, astrologers or any other kind of scientist, and, like good Cynics, they embrace ascetic life and reject government, so they have no king. They are, in this way, rather unusual in the history of Jewish ideas about Indian beliefs.

Jacob ben El\`azar’s Indians look even more like Ibn Ezra’s image of them. The Indians of Ben El\`azar’s introduction to his translation are also rational, highly moral, and monotheistic, believing in a God who guides the world through a cosmic law of moral justice. They, too, lack divine revelation.
