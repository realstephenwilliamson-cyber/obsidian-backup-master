## Chapter 6

## The Summoned Subject

*The Call and the Capable Self*

In chapter 5 we saw how Ricoeur’s hermeneutics shifted away from the interpersonal, I-Thou encounter with his detour through structuralism. The distanciation of the text means that it acquires a semantic autonomy, taking on a life of its own beyond the author. In the context of biblical hermeneutics, this shift leads to questions about how the Bible is the Word of God—that is, how God addresses us in and through the biblical texts.

We see something of a return to the dialogical in Ricoeur’s work from the late 1980s and 1990s, when he starts to frame the hermeneutics of religion in terms of the call and response. This chapter will explore this call and response structure in Ricoeur’s work, showing how the believing self is constituted by the divine call, and how the response to this call presupposes certain capacities like reason, conscience, and desire. This is a continuation of Ricoeur’s efforts to maintain both alterity and ipseity in his hermeneutics of religion. However, this also creates a tension regarding the idea of justification by faith, which is central to Ricoeur’s Protestant framework. Luther’s reading of Paul is the basis for an ontology that informs Kierkegaard, Barth, Heidegger, and the post-Heideggerian Lutheran theologians who are so formative for Ricoeur. This is an ontology in which eschatological possibility is prior to any human act, activity, or actuality. It is unclear how exactly Ricoeur’s philosophical approximation of the theology of the Word and its ontology of justification by faith fits with his ontology of the capable self.

### A Capacity to Hear the Call of the Other?

One of the central debates in Ricoeur’s later work on religion is the relation between self and other, ipseity and alterity. Ricoeur offers a model of 

*[p. 128]* ^pg145

intersubjectivity as a relation of mutuality, reciprocity, giving and receiving. The mutuality between self and other is always a fragile balance, Ricoeur admits (OA 188), but he still insists this fragile relation is a genuine good that does take place.

This position sets Ricoeur in opposition to views that intersubjectivity is founded unilaterally. In that case, there is no genuine relation, no symmetry, only asymmetry or even dissymmetry. Ricoeur sees this one-sided *non* relation in Husserl’s *Cartesian Meditations*, which locates the origin of intersubjectivity in the constituting act of the subject, who understands the other by analogy to itself. In this model, alterity is derived from the ego. Ricoeur also cites the philosophy of Emmanuel Levinas as an instance of asymmetry—though Levinas inverts the Husserlian account so that intersubjectivity originates entirely with the initiative of the other. For Levinas, “the word of the other comes to be placed at the origin of my acts” (OA 336). However, the other is so separate and exterior to the self that “this initiative establishes no relation at all” (OA 188). For Levinas, this encounter with the Other is not modeled on the I-Thou encounter in which there is a dialogical exchange. Instead, there is a sort of *non-*encounter in which I am summoned to responsibility, prior to my own initiative or ability. I receive this summons in the face of the other person, which pronounces a prohibition: Thou shalt not kill. For Levinas this asymmetrical (non)relation is the origin of ethics and ultimately of peace, law and justice.

What kind of self is capable of responding to the call of the other? For Levinas, the self has no pre-existing capacity for the other. In Ricoeur’s words, “the other constitutes me as responsible, that is, capable of responding” (OA 336). Levinas offers something like a phenomenological approximation of the Reformed principle *finitum noncapax infiniti*, which holds that the finite creature has no intrinsic capacity for the infinite, and no natural point of contact to receive divine revelation.<sup>1</sup> Levinas argues that we only encounter the trace of the infinite in the face of the other. Levinas also seems to parallel radical Protestant theologies that insist that there is no continuity—only discontinuity—between nature and grace. Nature has no capacity for grace, and only an election that is entirely prevenient and irresistible can overcome the depravity of the sinful self. Levinas uses rhetoric that is just as severe, maybe even more so. The prohibition against murder runs directly against being—whether in Heidegger’s sense of Dasein’s care for its own being, or Spinoza’s sense of the *conatus essendi*. The infinite Other effects a reversal, even extirpation, of the conatus (OBBE 72, 75). For Levinas, the conatus is not only corrupt, a good thing gone bad. In its very essence, the *conatus essendi* is the *libido dominandi*. Levinas depicts being as a kind of Hobbesian state of nature, a war of all against all. 

*[p. 129]* ^pg146

If being is as hellish as all that, how does the self ever assume responsibility for peace? Reflexive philosophy wants to return into its own consciousness and discover the moment in which the self posits *itself* as responsible, calls itself and makes itself attentive to the other. This is the desire to be autonomous, giving the law to oneself. Yet according to Levinas, this call is thoroughly heteronomous. I do not constitute myself as responsible for the other; I find myself already constituted, from time immemorial, by the election of the other. The responsible self is therefore no longer the transcendental origin of meaning and value—no longer the active “hunter of images,” but now the passive “prey,” wounded, unprotected, restless, persecuted, martyr, “torn from oneself for another,” hostage to the other (OBBE 75, 78–79, 11). 

Ricoeur agrees with Levinas that self-positing consciousness is an illusion, and he also agrees that the word of the other is prior to the act of consciousness.<sup>2</sup> The problem is that Levinas is so single-minded in his efforts to challenge the violence of ontology that he ends up proposing a model of heteronomy and transcendence that is violent in its own regard. As Ricoeur observes, at its most vehement, Levinasian rhetoric is a kind of “*verbal terrorism*.”<sup>3</sup>

Ricoeur’s alternative is not to wax sentimental and suggest that life is beautiful and people are really not so bad if you just give them a chance. Dialogical philosophies that are based on a sentimental view of human being risk overlooking the reality of radical evil. Likewise, there is a danger in philosophies that move too quickly from mutual exchange and reciprocity to some kind of “fusional union, whether in love, friendship, or fraternity on a communal or cosmopolitan scale.” All sorts of vulnerable others can be overlooked or excluded from these fraternities. This is Ricoeur’s point in *The Course of Recognition,* where he insists on maintaining our “attention to the irreplaceable character of each of the partners in the exchange” (CR 263). With these caveats in mind, we can appreciate Ricoeur’s claim that intersubjectivity does not arise unilaterally from the self or the other. Instead, the movements from the self to the other and the other to the self are “dialectically complementary” (OA 340). Although the self is not the origin of its own responsibility, its response to the other’s call does presuppose some receptive capacities (FS 126). Ricoeur therefore proposes a picture of the self with some degree of openness and a “capacity for discovery”—more specifically, capacities for reception, discernment, and mutual recognition (OA 339). This is a non-egoistic self, but a self nonetheless. For Ricoeur, the being of the self is not just solitude, but solicitude—a care for the other—and even a “*benevolent spontaneity*” (OA 190).

On Ricoeur’s account, the self must become capable of hearing the call of the other. We can highlight three capacities for this reception: reason, conscience, and desire. 

The first capacity is reason—particularly, practical wisdom, or prudence, to discern between the different calls that resound. If the other is “the master 

*[p. 130]* ^pg147

who teaches,” it is also necessary to recognize that the other can be an offender. “And what are we to say of the Other when he is the executioner? And who will be able to distinguish the master from the executioner, the master who calls for a disciple from the master who requires a slave?” (OA 339). The self requires a capacity for hermeneutical discernment to recognize the calls of these very different others.

The second receptive capacity is the voice of conscience. In *Oneself as Another* Ricoeur offers a phenomenology of the enigmatic character of conscience, which is an inner voice that is also in some sense *other*. In the call of conscience, the self both affects itself and is affected by the voice of another (OA 342, 355). Whereas Enlightenment concepts of conscience point toward rational autonomy, Ricoeur argues that conscience is “tempered by the confession of nonmastery over oneself” (FS 272). Following Heidegger, Ricoeur treats conscience (*Gewissen*) as a principle of individuation, in which the self becomes capable of authentic existence, free from the inauthenticity of *das Man*. Conscience is a testimony, an attestation of the self and its ownmost power to be. Conscience is the self calling itself (OA 348), and yet this is not a merely monological capacity. Conscience is dialogical, insofar as it unites the attestation of the self with “the injunction coming from the other” (OA 355), such that ipseity and alterity, attestation and injunction, are “cooriginary” (FS 126).

Ethical responsibility also presupposes a third condition: desire. Ricoeur first makes this point in his critique of Kant, who opposes obligation to desire and separates the formal orientation of the will from the material or empirical element of desire. According to Ricoeur, this separation of form and matter has had “disastrous consequences.” From this view, moral obligation excludes the “effort to achieve happiness,” and is therefore “isolated from the process of action” (CI 341, 453). In other words, a pure formalist ethics ultimately cannot understand action and its place in the ontology of the self. Ricoeur is not dismissing the need for ethics to confront the formal, universal point of view, but normative questions of obligation, prohibition, and justice are not most fundamental. Obligation is secondary; *desire* is primary.

Of course, desire is also easily distorted, misdirected, and alienated. Ricoeur therefore follows Spinoza in conceiving ethics as the reappropriation and reorientation of desire. Everything depends on desiring the right things, in the right ways, at the right times. This is why the ancients understood virtue as well-ordered desire. Ricoeur never develops a full-blown virtue ethics,<sup>4</sup> but he does appeal to Aristotle in developing what he calls the “little ethics” of *Oneself as Another*. Ricoeur argues that deontology presupposes a more fundamental teleological orientation to the good life—what he calls “the ethical aim,” which is “*the desire to live well with and for others in just institutions.*” The ethical aim is inseparable from desire—indeed, aiming 

*[p. 131]* ^pg148

at an end is a particular species of desire that Aristotle calls *boulēsis*, the rational wish for what appears good (OA 173).<sup>5</sup>

Ricoeur finds a similar insight in Book IX of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, where Aristotle confronts the question of self-love, or *philautia*. Should we love ourselves? For Aristotle, it depends on what you mean by self-love. The many love themselves selfishly, desiring what merely seems advantageous: more money, honor, or pleasure for themselves. That is the way of vice, and the vicious end up harming themselves and others. By contrast, the virtuous love themselves in a different way, desiring what is truly finest and best for themselves.<sup>6</sup> The virtuous should love themselves in this way so they can benefit themselves as well as others. According to Ricoeur, the ethical aim of living well depends on this desire and effort to be: “The profound meaning of philautia is *desire*: the good man’s own being is desirable to him.” This desire is likewise essential to friendship, as “the being of his friend is equally desirable to him” (OA 186). For Ricoeur, then, Aristotle’s account of virtuous self-love is a proper expression of the *conatus essendi*. The desire for one’s own being therefore lies at the root of ethical formation. While Ricoeur does not work out a complete account of the virtues, the implication for ethics is clear: The desire to be cannot simply be identified with egocentricity or narcissism.

Ricoeur recognizes that desire often goes wrong. This is why ethics must be critically challenged by formal questions of moral norms, imperatives, injunctions, and procedural questions of justice (OA 236, 239). These formal questions are a necessary critical challenge to the desire for the good life, which can easily be corrupted by evil. “Because there is evil,” Ricoeur writes, “the aim of the ‘good life’ has to be submitted to the test of moral obligation” (OA 218). The same is true of the desire to be at home and enjoy our “good life.” Levinas is right that we often need the call of the other to jar us out of our egoistic enjoyment. Nevertheless, these formal questions still presuppose a more fundamental teleological orientation toward the good life. The ethical aim, the desire or rational wish for the good life, is the horizon within which we can hear and respond to the other.

\*

How do these ethical reflections relate to the religious? Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion follows the same pattern that we have seen in his ethics. Both ethics and religion involve the self responding to the call of the other. Who is this other who summons the self? Who calls? The neighbor? God? Both? Ricoeur addresses this question at the end of *Oneself as Another*, writing:

Perhaps the philosopher as philosopher has to admit that one does not know and cannot say whether this Other, the source of the injunction, is another person whom I can look in the face or who can stare at me, or my ancestors for whom 

*[p. 132]* ^pg149

there is no representation, to so great an extent does my debt to them constitute my very self, or God—living God, absent God—or an empty place. With this aporia of the Other, philosophical discourse comes to an end. (OA 355)

Ricoeur does, however, go on to consider what it looks like when this call comes from God. He develops a hermeneutics of the divine call—not in *Oneself as Another*, but in two lectures that were excluded from the published version of the text.

### The Case of the Missing Gifford Lectures

*Oneself as Another* originated in Ricoeur’s Gifford Lectures, which he delivered at the University of Edinburgh in 1986. The series bore the title “On Selfhood: The Question of Personal Identity,” and the lectures allowed Ricoeur to draw together the diverse lines of his research, all of which arise from and converge in philosophical anthropology. What is the human being? Or in a more specifically Ricoeurian key: What is the self? Ricoeur reworked the lectures and published them in 1990, with an English translation following in 1992. In this final published form, the lectures became a series of ten studies on some of the fundamental questions of selfhood.

There were, however, two additional lectures that Ricoeur excluded from *Oneself as Another*. These lectures focused on the self who is constituted in biblical faith. In “The Self in the Mirror of the Scriptures,” Ricoeur explores the symbolic schema by which self-understanding is constituted in Judaism and Christianity. The other lecture is published in English as “The Summoned Subject in the School of the Narratives of the Prophetic Vocation,” and it articulates a model of the self who responds to the call issued by God in the text. On Ricoeur’s account, he sought to avoid the appearance of “cryptotheology” (OA 24) in the book’s arguments, so that his philosophical reflections on speech, action, narrative, ethics, and morality would not appear to depend on convictions borrowed from biblical faith. 

Ricoeur’s decision to exclude these lectures is in a certain sense understandable, since readers with no interest in Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion would be likely to misunderstand the place of the final two lectures in the work as a whole. Still, this decision to exclude the lectures was not strictly necessary, since they remain within the boundaries of Ricoeur’s philosophical hermeneutics and are neither theological nor “crypto-theological.” The lectures work through biblical texts to understand the faithful self, but these readings never switch genres into preaching or proclamation. Similarly, Ricoeur engages with thinkers like Augustine and Ebeling, 

*[p. 133]* ^pg150

discussing theological ideas like the inner teacher and justification by faith, but he is giving philosophical approximations of theological ideas rather than engaging in theology itself. Given the consistently philosophical nature of these two essays, there is no reason to exclude them to protect the boundary between philosophy and theology. In these essays, that boundary is never crossed.

Some readers might wish for an alternate universe in which the two missing essays appeared in another version of the book: *Oneself as Another: The Extended Cut*.<sup>7</sup> There is, there is good reason to read these missing lectures alongside the published text of *Oneself as Another*, since they make a genuine addition to the published text. First, there is an important continuity between *Oneself as Another* and these lectures, insofar as both are concerned with human capability. Second, contrary to expectations, these lectures on religious faith might actually reinforce (rather than transgress) the limits of the hermeneutics of the self. In his introduction to the book, Ricoeur states his preference for a faith that proceeds “without guarantee, following the interpretation given by the Lutheran theologian Eberhard Jüngel in *God as the Mystery of the World*.” Just as Ricoeur rejects the accusation of “cryptotheology,” so he rejects accusations of “cryptophilosophy,” in which biblical faith attempts to provide an ultimate foundation for the self. A “faith that knows itself to be without guarantee” can thus offer a valuable service by helping “philosophical hermeneutics to protect itself from the hubris that would set it up as the heir to the philosophies of the cogito and as continuing their self-foundational claim” (OA 25). Ricoeur’s last two Gifford lectures make exactly this point, showing how the self of biblical faith is neither self-founding nor a finished product. That is the radical implication of a faith that arises in response to the call of God. And that, it seems to me, is a good reason to read these last two lectures along with *Oneself as Another*. Rather than crossing the boundaries into theology and using God as guarantor of absolute self-knowledge, these lectures show how the call of God constitutes a self who is capable of responding, yet is also broken open rather than closed, incomplete rather than absolute.

### The Summoned Subject and the Mirror of the Scriptures

Ricoeur’s decision to exclude these two final lectures is especially noteworthy considering that the Gifford lectures have traditionally been devoted to natural theology. That is what Lord Adam Gifford stipulated in his will when he designated funds for a lectureship or chair for “‘Promoting, Advancing, 

*[p. 134]* ^pg151

Teaching, and Diffusing the study of Natural Theology.’” Gifford defines natural theology in the “widest sense of that term.” In his words:

The Knowledge of God, the Infinite, the All, the First and Only Cause, the One and the Sole Substance, the Sole Being, the Sole Reality, and the Sole Existence, the Knowledge of His Nature and Attributes, the Knowledge of the Relations which men and the whole universe bear to Him, the Knowledge of the Nature and Foundation of Ethics or Morals, and of all Obligations and Duties thence arising.<sup>8</sup> 

At first glance this does not sound very much like the lectures that became *Oneself as Another*. Ricoeur does not engage in natural theology as an attempt to give rational arguments for the existence and attributes of God. In fact, he explicitly distances himself from that sort of project.<sup>9</sup> But it is worth noting the second half of Lord Gifford’s outline for the lectureship—in his words: “the Knowledge of the Nature and Foundation of Ethics or Morals, and of all Obligations and Duties thence arising.” If we transpose the talk of knowledge into a hermeneutical key, we can see how Ricoeur’s studies of the self and the ethical aim, the self and the moral norm, and the discussion of practical wisdom fit these parameters quite well. 

What is most pertinent, however, is the clause that indicates “The Knowledge of the relations which men . . . bear” to God. If we also transpose that clause into a hermeneutical key, we end up with something very much like Ricoeur’s project in the two essays on religion: to understand the relation between God and the self, which is among the most basic concerns of the phenomenology of religion. 

This divine-human relation has been formulated in a variety of ways, and Ricoeur cites some of the formulations most familiar to him: “a feeling of absolute dependence, in relation to a creation that precedes me; an ultimate concern at the horizon of all my preoccupations; an unconditional trust, which hopes despite .  .  . everything” (SMS 206). What seems to be at the root of all these experiences is the structure of call and response—that is, a human response to a divine call. Thus in an essay written a few years later—“Experience and Language in Religious Discourse”—Ricoeur proposes that if there is a fundamental experience for the phenomenology of religion to consider, the best candidate is the divine call and human response (ELRD 128).

The call and response structure is precisely what Ricoeur examines in “The Self in the Mirror of the Scriptures.” Any phenomenology of the call and response must be hermeneutical in nature, since the experience of call and response is mediated linguistically. The call is a word-event, but as we saw in chapter 5, this call is mediated textually in the diverse genres of Scripture. This textual mediation also presupposes the presence of the religious 

*[p. 135]* ^pg152

community, for whom the Scriptures have the authority of a canon. Ricoeur’s project then is to understand the self who is constituted in this encounter with Scripture.

Ricoeur goes on to discuss the imaginative unity of Scripture, drawing on Northrop Frye’s *The Great Code* to discuss the internal structures of the biblical text. Frye’s approach is helpful for Ricoeur, since it brackets external reference to “real historical events” and focuses on the poetic reference of the text. This approach can help to make sense of the self that corresponds and conforms to Scripture, by showing how the biblical texts “instill in listeners or readers the desire to understand themselves in light of this Great Code.” By referring to nothing beyond itself, the text refers to the reader who responds to it, receiving from the text a transformed self-understanding. The self can see itself anew in the mirror of the text, and in this way the poetic becomes kerygmatic (SMS 209–10).

While noting the value of this structural approach to the text, Ricoeur sees the need to go further and show how the text also refers to external reality. Most importantly, the Scriptures name God, and they do so through the diverse biblical genres. By examining the polysemic namings of God, Ricoeur engages in a literary examination of the text. Ricoeur is not doing theology, but he does present the diverse namings of God as *theologoumena*—that is, tentative opinions or beliefs about God that do not have doctrinal authority. This is the closest Ricoeur gets to doing theology in these lectures, but it is still a preamble to a possible theology rather than theology proper. It is also not *natural* theology, in the sense of offering demonstrations appealing to the natural light of reason (SMS 211), since these theologoumena are derived from Scripture.

Ricoeur argues that these theologoumena are significant for the hermeneutics of the believing self, since they all contain a dialogical structure that implies a human response (SMS 213–14). This structure plays out in the different biblical genres. For example, Scripture narrates “the figure of a God who saves”; the corresponding response is a human confession of praise. Scripture includes “prescriptions addressed to a people tied to the promised and given land”—the law is the Word of God blessing his people, and the corresponding human response is a blessing rooted in gratitude. Scripture includes the prescriptions of the law. Corresponding to these divine commandments and prohibitions is the human response of either obedience or disobedience. In the words of the prophets, God condemns apostasy, which is a *lack* of human response; but these words of judgment and woe call for a human response of repentance, followed by confidence and hope (SMS 214– 15). In short, the diverse biblical genres all involve their own distinctive dialogical structures of divine call and human response.

*[p. 136]* ^pg153

The biblical naming of God is diverse, plural, polysemic, and as such it remains incomplete, since these biblical genres name a reality that eludes fully adequate description. Naming manifests God, but it also entails a withdrawal of the Name beyond language. Thus in the final section of this essay Ricoeur discusses the role of limit expression in Scripture. In Exodus 3, God reveals his name to Moses as “I am Who I am,” or “I will be what I will be.” This is not, however, a name that defines God, nor does it give a basis for a “positive ontology.” Rather, it “protects the secret of the for-itself of God” (SMS 217). Likewise, the parables of Jesus tell stories about what the kingdom of God is like, but they use extravagant, paradoxical, and hyperbolic logic that prevents the hearer from drawing everything together to seize control of the meaning of the kingdom and the place of the self in it (SMS 217). These limit expressions pronounce a call to the hearer, or the reader, who is invited to “identify with the book” (SMS 219). However, just as these limit expressions expose the incompleteness of the act of naming God, they also expose the incompleteness of the self, which is not a fully “coherent project” or “continuous totality” (SMS 217). Just as there is a deferred unity of the names for God, so there is a deferred unity of the self (SMS 219).

The incompleteness of the self is also reinforced in the final Gifford lecture, “The Summoned Subject,” where Ricoeur offers a hermeneutic of diverse figures of the responding self. The responding self stands in marked contrast to the hubris of the self who wants to name itself, since it is constituted by a word that is both external and antecedent. Ricoeur takes care to note that he is not presenting this final lecture as a crown to sit atop the philosophical hermeneutics of the self in the earlier lectures. It would be an easy mistake to see these lectures as analogous to Aquinas’ account of the relation between natural theology and sacred doctrine—as though the earlier lectures are something like a neutral, rational ontology of the self that can then be completed by the specifically religious content of the final two lectures—as though grace were perfecting nature. On the contrary, the lectures from start to finish are works of philosophical hermeneutics. The final lecture is a hermeneutics of diverse figures of the responding self. It draws on biblical texts and theological thinkers, but these are the materials required by a philosophical hermeneutics of religion.

Ricoeur presents these figures of the responding self in a discontinuous series. He begins by examining the prophetic call. The prophet is a figure of crisis—a suffering mediator between God and his people. The relation of call and response involves an asymmetrical confrontation—an immemorial word. In being sent, the prophet is given an identity; he is singularized by the prophetic call and commission. The next figure Ricoeur examines is the Christomorphic self—the self who is formed—or *con*formed—according to the image of Christ. This figure of the responding self had an influence on the 

*[p. 137]* ^pg154

tradition of the *imitatio Christi*—the response of imitating Christ, but Ricoeur also detects its influence in his other two figures of the responding self: the figure of the inner teacher in Augustine, and the testimony of conscience.

Augustine’s dialogue *De Magistro* offers an account of Christ as the inner teacher. For Ricoeur, this is particularly significant because of the way it internalizes the correspondence between the divine call and the human response (FS 268).

The call and response structure is most fully internalized in the testimony of conscience. Here Ricoeur expands the phenomenology of conscience from *Oneself as Another*. He argues that conscience can intensify the relation between call and response, so that “the transcendent figure is internalized by the moment of appropriation that transmutes it into an inner voice” (FS 271). Without this receptive capacity, the kerygma, the proclaimed word, would remain radically extrinsic to the self (FS 271–72). It is properly dialogical: conscience is the “organ of reception” for an external word.

Ricoeur develops this interpretation of conscience from his reading of Heideggerian theologians like Bultmann and Ebeling, noting that a full theology of conscience has yet to be written. He does not, however, engage in such a theology of conscience himself, nor does he cross over into theology itself. Instead, this is another instance of Ricoeur’s philosophical approximation of theological ideas.

### Justification and Capability?

Ricoeur’s concern is to show that the self has a receptive capacity without which the kerygma would remain “radically extrinsic.” Once again we see Ricoeur’s effort to maintain both alterity and ipseity. Bultmann and Ebeling are both operating in the context of a Lutheran theology of justification by faith, so the question arises as to how this justification can be understood in terms of the call and the response, and related to this, how the justifying word relates to human capability.

This tension remains in Ricoeur’s subsequent work on the hermeneutics of religious discourse. Ricoeur wants to unite his ontology of human capability with another ontological feature that is common in the theology of the Word; as Jüngel puts it, “We are defined ontologically as hearers of a word by which alone our being is constituted.”<sup>10</sup> This idea of the word as ontologically constitutive is a central theme in the theology of the Word—from Barth, Bultmann, and Bonhoeffer to Ebeling, Fuchs, and Jüngel. Heidegger is also a key influence here, but Heidegger got it from the same sources as these twentieth-century theologians: Martin Luther, and the apostle Paul before him. The key word is *logizomai*, which Paul uses several times in his epistle to the Romans 

*[p. 138]* ^pg155

to describe how the righteousness of God is given: “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” ([[Romans 04#^4-3|Rom. 4:3]]). The “reckoning” here is often translated as “credited” or “imputed,” but the *logizomai* indicates a kind of speaking—a “wording”—into righteousness.<sup>11</sup> This word is creative, bringing into existence a new being that has no continuity with the old. Paul goes on to write of the God who calls into being the things that are not. God gives life to the dead, makes the barren womb fertile, and makes righteous the sinner who is dead in sin. The Pauline point is that the sinner has no immanent potential to be justified before God. The new being is a gift from outside the self.

This is a unique ontology, and difficult to approximate philosophically—at odds with Aristotle, Spinoza, and Kant alike. The rectifying, justifying Word gives new possibilities that come from outside the ontological continuum of self, society, and world. The “emergence of the new”<sup>12</sup> is an act of divine grace, entirely gratuitous and contingent. In Kierkegaardian terms, it is a qualitative leap forward, not the actualization of an immanent potentiality. Nor is it the novelty of semantic innovation, a new meaning arising from imagination and desire, a previously untapped power of the conatus. Perhaps most significant of all, this possibility is unconditional. Contra Kant, it does not require that we must first become worthy of grace. 

The Pauline text does not present this possibility as a generally available but untapped source within created Being. Ephesians 2 describes us as having been “dead in our trespasses and sin” (v.1), but that God “who is rich in mercy, out of the great love with which he loved us even when we were dead through our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ” (vv.4–5). We have been saved by grace through faith, and this is not our doing but the “gift of God” (v.8). This gift is “not the result of works,” but the ontological basis of the new being: “For we are what he has made us, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand to be our way of life” (vv.9–10).

Ricoeur is well aware of this ontology of justification by faith and describes this point precisely in *The Symbolism of Evil*, where he writes: “The philosopher must recognize from the outset how shocking this symbol \[of justification\] is for a mind educated by the Greeks.” This justification comes from outside the self—“from the future to the present, from the outward to the inward, from the transcendent to the immanent. . . . To be ‘just’ is to be justified by an Other; more precisely, it is to be ‘declared’ just, to be ‘counted as’ just.” Yet Ricoeur also (rightly) insists that this reckoning is not merely a legal fiction because the self is “‘made’ just, really and vitally.” The reckoning is not purely extrinsic, but registers ontologically in the self. The crucial point is that the transcendent, eschatological event is the cause of the immanent, present transformation of the self: “For Paul the first is the cause of the 

*[p. 139]* ^pg156

second, but the second is the full manifestation of the first; the paradox is that the acme of outwardness is the acme of inwardness” (SE 147–48).<sup>13</sup> As Ernst Käsemann writes: “God makes the ungodly person a new creature; he *really makes him righteous*.” The eschatological address is an acquittal that sets us free “*from* the power of sin,” but also “free *for* new creation and *alone makes us capable of it.*”<sup>14</sup>

How does this very Protestant reading of Paul’s epistle to the Romans fit with Ricoeur’s later work on the ontology of the capable self? At times Ricoeur seems to be transposing the justifying word into a more generally available dimension of being, a common grace or power that allows religion (in its many varieties) to regenerate human capability. Is this regeneration an immanent human potentiality that just needs to be actualized by the power of religious discourse? That argument would lead Ricoeur all the way down Kant’s semi-Pelagian path. Perhaps Ricoeur is only arguing that the self has some sort of natural capacity or point of contact for grace. That seems to be the implication of his claim that we are “capable of being modified by word.”<sup>15</sup> Word has the power to transform our self-understanding because it addresses the “active core of our existence”—the conatus—and brings about an imaginative regeneration of the “symbolic structures” and “dynamic schemes that express the way in which we understand our situation and the way in which we project ourselves into this situation” (CI 454). This is the sense in which Ricoeur will later describe the goal of religion as liberating the “core of goodness in human beings,” which is deeper than any radical evil.<sup>16</sup> This mutual relation of transcendence and immanence dates back to Ricoeur’s earliest work. In *Freedom and Nature*, Ricoeur is cautious about objectifying the grace that gives new life and liberation to freedom, since it is still something that happens “to a freedom” (FN 30). This liberating Transcendence delivers the will not in a purely extrinsic way, but instead “becomes immanent to the extent to which the will purifies itself in becoming actively associated with its own liberation” (FN 31). One might read this as a strong claim that the word addresses and awakens a fundamental ontological potency in the self. Crucially, this need not be read as an immanent potential to be made right—rectified, justified—in relation to God. The ontological newness of Pauline *logizomai* is not a generally available power to regenerate human capability, but a contingent act of divine grace. And yet this eschatological word does effect an immanent, ontological transformation in the self insofar as it awakens capacities for speaking, acting, narrating and assuming responsibility. The distinction between theology and philosophy is therefore vital to this reading.

Nevertheless, there are still two difficulties with this reading. First, Ricoeur’s philosophical approximation of the regenerative word is not focused on justification, but on religious discourse in general. As a result, 

*[p. 140]* ^pg157

it is unclear what the specific word of justification actually contributes to the regeneration of the capable self that is not available in other varieties of religious discourse. Ricoeur gives an approximation of the justifying word, but justification itself is not central to his hermeneutics of religion.<sup>17</sup> This has theological consequences, since it could suggest the word of rectification is not unique among the manifold of other religious words. Ricoeur seems to move in this direction in his late work, where he argues that the category of meaning is more salient than justification for understanding religious desire in our time (LD 46). We will return to this point in chapter 9.

Second, Ricoeur’s claim that the self plays this active role also raises questions about how this activity works out at the theological level, since it seems to suggest a kind of synergism or co-operation with divine grace. As Fleming Rutledge has argued, if we think we contribute to our justification, we don’t understand what God is doing. It is easy to conclude that with the human response to the divine call, “the agency shifts from God to the human being.” But this makes faith into a work, the one act—the *conditio sine qua non*—required for justification. Instead, the Word is active in both the call and the response, and so human activity is not a self-moving response but a *participation* in the active work of grace.<sup>18</sup>

From the perspective of Aristotelian *kinesis*, the self can actualize its potential for righteousness. In a Spinozistic monism, there is a regeneration of the conatus, which is an expression of “God, that is, nature” (*deus sive natura*). In Kantian moral religion, the self can make itself worthy of grace. From the perspective of a biblical ontology, however, this regeneration is not an immanent possibility. We see this in Paul’s distinction between flesh and spirit, which are basic orientations of existence and intentionality: the mind or mental direction of the flesh is death (*phronēma tēs sarkos thanatos*) ([[Romans 08#^8-6|Rom. 8:6]]—cf. SE 141–42); it “is hostile to God” and “does not submit to God’s law—indeed, it cannot” (*oude gar dynatai*) ([[Romans 08#^8-7|Rom. 8:7]]). In terms of becoming right before God, there is an utter lack of *dynamis* in the self. According to Paul, the existential orientation of spirit is only possible through Christ being in the self, which is made possible by the indwelling of God’s Spirit, who raised Christ from the dead (vv.10–11). Paul goes on to use the imagery of deliverance from slavery, but the text presents a very un-Kantian image of the self—not in terms of liberation to moral autonomy and maturity, but instead *adoption* as children and heirs of God (vv.15–17).

As Ricoeur observes, Paul also uses the image of *participation* to describe our incorporation into Christ (SE 275). This image appears elsewhere in the New Testament. We were dead in sin, but God made us “alive together” with Christ ([[Colossians 02#^2-13|Col. 2:13]]). This new capability does not become our independent possession, but depends on our continued participation in Christ, who tells his disciples: “Apart from me you can do nothing” ([[John 15#^15-5|John 15:5]]). This is obviously 

*[p. 141]* ^pg158

not to claim that the self is simply incapable of any act of speech, action, narration, or imputation. Ricoeur’s philosophical anthropology is possible because these fundamental capacities are present regardless of one’s existential or spiritual orientation. Rather, the point is that the self is incapable of turning these capacities from the existential orientation of the flesh to that of the spirit. This is the ultimate *I cannot*, the *incapax* at the heart of the *I can*.

For a biblical ontology, then, our being-in-Christ is the basis for our regeneration of our capabilities. As Ricoeur observes in *The Symbolism of Evil*, this regeneration is not merely an event in consciousness, since “the experience of pardon is . . . the psychological trace of that which happens in reality”—an enigmatic reality that can only be expressed in the imagery of incorporation (SE 275). What regenerates is not the psychological experience but a real ontological participation in Christ. 

Ricoeur never again expresses this un-Kantian point as clearly as he does in this passage from *The Symbolism of Evil*. In his subsequent work he stops short of drawing directly on these ideas, which seem to move into the domain of proclamation or theology. Decades later Ricoeur writes in *Memory, History*, *Forgetting* that he is aware of the “many dogmatic modes of thought that have allowed themselves to be caught up in disjunctive modes of logic: grace first of all, even grace alone, or human initiative first” (MHF 491). This “impasse” is solidified when the category of causation enters the discussion and leads us to think about human agency and divine action as if they are phenomenal forces to be known by theoretical reason. Ricoeur proposes instead to “leave the paradox in its stage of emergence,” avoiding the theological speculations about who causes what in the *initium fidei* and focusing in that context on a hermeneutics of the “various figures” of forgiveness as an “unbinding” of the agent from his acts (MHF 491).

Ricoeur makes an excellent point here, since theology often tries to systematize enigmas and paradoxes—like the relation between divine and human agency, faith and works—enigmas with which the biblical texts are much more comfortable, and in which the Bible even seem to delight. Having said that, one cannot simply avoid theology if one raises the question of regeneration. If one takes the Kantian approach and describes the regeneration of capability while excluding the presupposition of grace, or by suggesting that one must become worthy of grace, then one is already taking theological positions about the condition of the human being, the role of divine agency, and what it takes to rectify things between God and humanity. By raising these questions, theology arises whether one wants it or not. And if one draws selectively on biblical and theological ideas and then insists on treating these problems in strictly philosophical terms, one ends up with new problems on both sides. It creates confusion in theology, since it is unclear how this philosophical reflection can make a transition to the theological 

*[p. 142]* ^pg159

level. This selective approach is also a problem for the philosophical hermeneutics of religion, since it does not understand religion on its own terms. Moreover, this approach tries to deal with these religious questions by using philosophical approximations of religious sources, which end up serving as mere half measures, rather than drawing on the full resources with which religious traditions actually address the question in view. In short, it makes for bad theology as well as bad philosophical hermeneutics. We will see a specific example of this in chapter 9 when we consider Ricoeur’s attempt to interpret forgiveness in terms of capability.

\*

To conclude this chapter on a positive note, it is worth noting that Ricoeur is pushing back against a tendency in Continental philosophy of religion to emphasize the radical alterity of the call. This debate has been shaped by thinkers like Kierkegaard, Levinas, Derrida,<sup>19</sup> Marion,<sup>20</sup> and Badiou,<sup>21</sup> each of whom presents a model of transcendence as deeply disruptive and overwhelming the capacities of immanence. Revelation strikes like a bolt of lightning, overwhelming the self and its capacities. Ricoeur appreciates this emphasis on the disruptive event and wants to maintain it, but he also wants to mediate these claims of radical alterity with a proper appreciation of ipseity and its role in the reception of alterity. As Ricoeur notes at one point: “Maybe it is a French discussion which has overemphasized this notion of ‘otherness.’ There is ‘otherness’ everywhere. So in a sense, I am resisting this overemphasis on ‘otherness.’”<sup>22</sup>

This moderating emphasis on ipseity is also part of Ricoeur’s hermeneutical orientation. In being revealed, the Word still becomes a fact of human culture and consciousness. The reading, proclamation, and hearing of the Word is a thoroughly hermeneutical process. There is no unmediated reception of the Word, and that is a good thing. Mediation is not a negative consequence of the fall into sin,<sup>23</sup> but rather a positive feature of what it means to be finite human beings who encounter a surplus of meaning in symbols, narratives, and texts. We are finite beings, and therefore we are interpreting beings. Ricoeur’s contribution to these debates about alterity is to bring a greater recognition of the importance of ipseity, mediation, and the receptive capacities that allow the self to hear and respond to the call of the other.
