## About

A short dialogue by Plato, also called *On Holiness*, in which Socrates asks the diviner Euthyphro what piety is. It gives its name to the Euthyphro dilemma.

- **People:** Socrates, waiting for the preliminary hearing of Meletus's charge of impiety against him; and Euthyphro, a prophet from Prospalta in his mid-forties. Euthyphro is prosecuting his own father, who had a worker bound and left to die on the family's land on Naxos. Wikipedia calls the dead man variously a hireling and a slave.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** the porch (stoa) of the King Archon in Athens, 399 BC, shortly before the trial (Wikipedia; SEP). Wikipedia notes that the *Theaetetus* is set on the same day and the *Sophist* and *Statesman* on the next.
- **Argument:** Euthyphro proposes that piety is what he is doing now; what pleases the gods; what all the gods love; care of the gods; and knowledge of sacrifice and prayer. Socrates finds each wanting. The central question asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved. The dialogue ends in perplexity (aporia).
- **Composition and authenticity:** no precise date. The IEP lists it among the early dialogues, written after Socrates' death and before 387 BC. Neither Wikipedia nor the IEP questions Plato's authorship, though Wikipedia reports that Numenius thought Euthyphro himself a fiction.
- **Place among the dialogues:** Thrasyllus put it first in his first tetralogy, before the *Apology*, *Crito* and *Phaedo*. Diogenes Laërtius reports that some teachers used it as the introduction to Plato.
- **Transmission:** papyrus fragments of the 2nd century; the oldest surviving medieval manuscript dates from 895 (Arethas of Caesarea); first printed in Greek by Aldus at Venice in 1513.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says the dilemma has fed one of the longest-running debates in theology and meta-ethics, revived in the 17th and 18th centuries by Ralph Cudworth and Samuel Clarke.
