#### 5.6 Phonematic Cosmogonies

5.6.1 Energy 

How do mantras work? Since ancient times Tantrists have emphasized the concept of energy, or Śakti. To them the cosmic energy or “power” is a genuinely tangible phenomenon. We have occasionally spoken of Tantra believers. In the eyes of a Tantrist, however, this is completely wrong. He or she would speak of knowledge and experience. The primacy of experience is — more or less — at the root of the Tantra. Theoretical and pragmatic knowledge are called for in equal measure. As a rule Tantrists are not sheep, receiving just any sort of mantras from gurus without thinking about them. Particularly in the case of the Āgama texts and also of many Tantras, a section on philosophy typically belongs to the canon. Philosophy and ritual are regarded as complementary. The philosophical sections often explain why the mantras are effective. In the \$āradā-Tilaka-Tantra the doctrine of final emancipation concludes the work, while the first chapter contains cosmogony, anthropology and philosophy. However, mantra practice even leaves its tracks in rational thought. The Tantric philosophy is one of India’s most opaque philosophies because it does not keep strictly to philosophical terminology, but instead operates with suggestive symbolic forms. A philologist must be prepared to tackle a veritable bestiary of cryptisms, hermetisms, occultisms and esoterisms to disentangle the difficult sense. Sometimes it is not easy to recognize a philosophical content at all, for it tends to be the mythical form of cosmogony and in some cases even of theogony that are chosen for expressing non-mythical concepts. 

Modern Tantrists like to use terms such as “experimental confirmation” and “practical efficiency” when they want to emphasize the primacy of experience in their religious conviction. It is typical that they attempt to link themselves with Western science — and since the time of Max Planck that has worked quite well.

##### 5.6.2 Original Matter and Original Sound

Ever since the beginnings of systematic philosophy in India, i.e. since the 

post-Upaniṣadic 

epoch, cosmogonic speculation within Vedic Brahmanic thought has always been characterized by the two concepts of the Spiritual and the Material (purusa and prakrti). The major system in this field is the Sāṁñkhya. It teaches a particularly strict spirit-matter dualism, but not like the one we know from Descartes. Spirit is understood exclusively as pure, unalterable consciousness. The conceptual —and the emotive — are not understood as an immovable spirit (since they are changeable contents of consciousness), but as matter observed by the spirit. The counterpart to the immovable spirit is the eternally changing ‘original matter’ — the Prakrti. It is the whole visible and conceivable universe in all its aspects: from the material elements to mental and abstract aspects. 

For a long time the Sāṁkhya axiom of the eternal opposites of the Unchangeable and Changeable was the foundation stone of Indian cosmogony and characterized the theologies of Yoga and Vedānta. For example, in Patañjali’s Yoga Sūtras God (Ivara) is completely beyond this world. He is a “special spirit being” (purusa-visesa) completely untouched by the world. ##### 5.6.3 Language Creation and World Creation

Even in the early aiva model of the Pauskara-Āgama, there is already a severe dilution of the strict contrast between the world as changeable and God as unchangeable. The contrast is removed completely in the later works of non-dualist High Tantra, which interlace Māyā and Śakti and regard them ultimately as completely identical. Programmatically, the opening verses of the Spanda-Kārikās (“Stanzas on Vibration”), the earliest work in non-dual Kashmir Śaiva theology (9th cent. AD), proclaim: 

“When he opens his eyes the world is created. When he closes his eyes it is destroyed. We praise the peacemaker \[Siva\], whose omnipresence and omnipotence \[have their origin\] in the wheel of \[his\] Saktis. In him this whole \[world\] has its existence. From him comes everything that exists. Because everything is revealed through his form, he knows no limit.” "4 The world is the outward expression of the internal dynamis of the supreme godhead. The opposition of the changeable and the unchangeable has transformed itself into two aspects of Śiva. In Goď’s internal world, the universe has always existed, and as Goď’s external world it becomes the changeable world. The changeability of things in the universe is not explained by their temporary or momentary existence, but by their virtuality. They exist — more or less — in the way that the individual pictures in a film exist in the cinema. They are visible to the audience for only a brief moment, but still the whole film is made up of nothing but these individual images. This is precisely what is expressed in the idea of a “wheel of Śaktis.” The only difference is that here the projectionist, the film and the screen are identical with one another. Of course, this notion implies a huge boost to the evaluation of the worldly realm.

The Śrīvidyā mantra is not only an integral part of the diagram, but also the actual “root mantra” underlying the japa, the repetition.
