##### 2.8.2 OM as Superstructure (Māndükya-Upanisad)

“aksara” now appears merely as a technical term for “syllable” in the sense of a grammatical or prosodic syllable. 

2.8.2 

OM as Superstructure (Māṇdūkya-Upaniṣad) 

Another group of later Upaniṣads is dedicated to OM in quite a different manner. They once more take up and pursue the triadic interpretation of the three sounds in OM (“A,” “U” and “M”) of the Brāhmaṇas. It is still popular to view OM as an abbreviation standing for the whole Veda. According to the Praśna-Upanisad 5.4-5, OM condenses the three Vedas in their entirety. Here OM stands for both the “lower” and the “higher” Brahman, i.e. equally for worldly and supernatural gain. Just as in earlier times, the three sounds of OM represent the three Vedas (Rk-Yajuh-Sāman), the three worlds (earth, air and heavens) and the relevant “victories” over these worlds. One achieves greatness in the world of men via the Rc hymns, while the Yajus sayings lead into the world of the moon, and finally the Sāman songs, with the combination of all three sounds in OM, lift one into the world of light. But unlike the Brāhmaṇa, this light-world is interpreted as the world of Brahman (brahma-loka), in which the “spiritual being that lives in the city \[of the body\]”?®” is perceived — that is, the immortal soul. 

The Māndūkya-Upaniíisad is dedicated exclusively to OM. Independent of the newly acquired performance of the syllable amongst the ascetics, in twelve short verses it develops an interpretation of the world that is based on OM. The interpretation is strictly epistemological and thus takes a completely new path. This very special OM mysticism became exceptionally powerful. The Upaniṣad begins with the classical statement: 

“OM. This syllable is all this.” 

But in the Māṇdūkya-Upaniṣad OM is neither essence nor an image of the world; instead it is understood as a structural pattern for the cosmos.

The structuring function of OM exerts its effects not only via the numerical analogy of the four “meters” with the four morae, but also via the sound of OM itself. The correlation of the sequence A-U-M with waking, dreaming and dreamless deep sleep is phenomenologically clear. The “a” is turned outward, the “u” is turned inward and the “m” is completely internalized. Together they form a step-by-step acoustic and articulatory involution, which makes the abstract contents of the verses into something that can be experienced via the senses and that can be used both for meditative and for contemplative purposes. At the same time, OM acquires an additional symbolic “phonetic” element. The point at which the barely audible “m” appears in the silence is regarded as the fourth phase of OM and it is precisely this phase that is the most important. 

However, despite the empathic component the text is severely rationalized. Apart from the numerical analogies, there are also etymologies in the Brāhmaṇa style intended to legitimize the content, which is not founded in tradition. But these etymologies are rather stylistic quotations without great relevance in terms of content, since the Upaniṣad already argues in a completely systematic way, unlike the flights of fancy in the Brāhmaṇas. In the Brāhmaṇas etymologies are important sources of information, while in the Upaniṣad they merely confirm the otherwise purely systematically developed sentences. For example, the dream and the u phase of OM are more or less characterized by \[qualitative\] “prominence” (utkarsa) and “two-sidedness” (ubhayatva), inasmuch as the dream is located between the waking state and the dreamless deep sleep (vs. 4 & 10). These etymologies are not very convincing if one compares them with the brilliant (and sometimes also slightly crazy) etymologies of the Brāhmaṇas. The creativity in writing has shifted into the development of a systematic and strict representation.

Only one element is removed from the rationality of the system: the Atman. The Ātman is viewed as OM and is the Whole, but in spite of this it is at the same time identical with the “fourth” state (turiya), which is described solely in negatives. Unlike the three preceding stages, this relationship (Atman = fourth state) is a classical mythical relationship: Ātman is a (“fourth”) part and at the same time the Whole, which also contains the preceding three parts within itself. So the actual noetic goal is located at this stage (v. 7). 

In the Māṇdūkya-Upaniṣad the synthesis of two monomyths occurs. The Atman — which is developed from the Subjective — and the cosmic codeword OM, which until now has been independent of a subject, transfer their characters to one another. The Atman, which transcends the phenomenal and which could only be comprehended in negatives (such as “it is not this, and not that”: neti neti) and in equations with the whole cosmos (“Allaussagen”), now gets “four meters.” And yet this Atman is too comprehensive for it to be reducible to individual elements. It encompasses everything that is, all aspects of phenomenal reality, but also that which is not the phenomenal reality and can only be described in terms of the negation of phenomenal reality. 

The interpretation of OM in the Māndūkya-Upanisad brought about an especially powerful history of reception. It was able to bring together the transcendentalist philosophy of the Vedānta with the Veda text in its esoteric nucleus, OM. In such a reception of the Veda, the sacred text turns into the root element of a culture of memory (“Gedāchtniskultur”). This Veda is no longer regarded as a ritual text at all, and it becomes a primordial revelation to which all refer, and OM becomes its representation. 

The definitely post-Vedic Pranava-Upanisad is exclusively concerned with OM, in an almost encyclopedic manner. In contrast to the early Upaniṣads, it documents a strongly mythologized view of the Veda. ### 3 Composition and Decomposition: Analytical Studies and Popularizations of the Veda - Rationality and Aesthetics of Post-Vedic Literatures and Performing Arts

3.1 

#### 3.1 The Nostalgia of the Bards

The “end of the Veda” was the beginning of the invention of its tradition. The Veda is no longer reality that is lived out, but instead has become a rigid canon of fixed texts.' The new aspect is the technical and academic literatures about the Veda, with the Brahmins turning more and more into scholars. It is probably not by chance that the earliest surviving documents of non-Vedic literature — bearing witness to social and religious changes, the growth of kingdoms, and the rise of theistic traditions — also date from this period (about 500 BC through the first centuries AD). The oldest surviving parallel tradition is that of the sūtas, literally “steerers of carts,” but also meaning bards, since the men steering carts accompanying the princes into battle were excellent candidates for the role of early “historiographers.” This new group of authors invents its very own tradition. In fine mythological fashion they see themselves as descended from a legendary primeval Sūta, and this father of all bards is traced directly back to the “compiler of the Veda” (Vedavyāsa), whom some regard as the incarnation of the Lord God Viṣṇu who was just establishing himself at the time. This new literary group tries to cast the nimbus of the Vedic sacrificial tradition around itself. Consequently, gigantic sacrificial festivities lasting a thousand years are projected into the past as a literary fiction, to serve as a legendary stage for the first recitation of the bardic texts. The two great epics Mahābhārata and Rāmāyana go back to these court singers and were absorbed by itinerant reciters of Brahmin-type lore,? and much of

While their origins lay in non-Brahmanic groups associated with the Kṣatriya aristocracy, the epic stories were quickly appropriated by Sanskritic Brahmins and over-coded with a Brahmanic ideology which emphasized the performance of social duty. The free and unfettered development only ended at around 400 AD, when the (Sanskrit) texts gradually began to be seen as a canon, while in the vernacular languages the traditions have remained fluid up to the present day. In addition to two major Sanskrit recensions (Northern and Southern) of both epics, there are a great number of local forms; the Sanskrit narrative traditions are also acted out and recited orally in vernacular languages. The influence of both epics on Hindu civilization has been immense, and has shaped actual cultural life more than the Veda. In modern times they have been cast in a television series which enjoyed great popularity. Traditionally, both epics and Purāṇas (“stories of the ancient past”) are sometimes classified together as śtihāsa-purāna (embracing the Western categories of ‘history,’ ‘legend’ and ‘myth’) and are even known as the “fifth Veda.” The Purāṇas too had, and continue to have, an immense impact upon Hinduism at all levels. Milton Singer rightly observes: “The very tissue of the culture is made from purāṇic themes. Practically every cultural performance includes one — in song, dance, play recitation, and exposition.” (Milton Singer, When a Great Tradition Modernizes: An Anthropological Approach to Indian Civilization, New York: Praeger, 1972, 76). All castes have access to Purāṇic rites, not only the twice-born as it is the case in the Vedic ritual tradition. The oral transmission channels, with a high degree of freedom, constantly re-casting the stories, improvising, and adding local coloring, have been very much like those of the epics (Thomas Coburn, “The Study of the Purāṇas and the Study of Religion,” Religious Studies 16 (1980): 341-352). The Veda has its own validity; one cannot imagine not having it, even if belief in a Lord God and a new pantheon of personal deities increasingly competes with the Vedic ritual for status. For this reason the undistorted and unbroken transmission of the Veda remains a highly valued goal of the Brahmanical society and an integral element in the preservation of tradition, called “dharma.” The term dharma is derived from the verbal root dhr, “to support, to undergird, to establish” - meaning in a global sense maintaining and continuing everything that is good and right. This may include customs, order, social norms and culture just as much as individual rules of conduct and personal ethical values. As for the term’s collective aspect, dharma and vaidika (“that which is Vedic”) become almost interchangeable terms defining what is “orthodox.” The idea of orthodoxy is always linked to a strongly nostalgic element: anyone who recites the Veda perpetuates the Golden Age when the world was populated by holy priests who carried out sacrifices unceasingly. Ascetic Brahmins live there, existing only on roots and fruits and spreading such a peaceful aura that even the wild animals graze at the edges of the settlement without a hint of fear.
