## 3 Narrative Unity, Autonomy, and Kierkegaard’s Aesthetic-Ethical Distinction

1\. 

### 1 Kierkegaard’s Contribution to Narrative Accounts of Practical Identity

#### 1.1 The Debate since Kierkegaard After MacIntyre

With a realist model of basic narrative identity in place, we can consider the higher levels of narrative structure that are associated with personal autonomy. It is at this point that a recent debate about Søren Kierkegaard becomes more useful to broader discussions of identity. In the last two decades, new interpretations of Kierkegaard’s “aesthetic” and “ethical” modes of existence were developed to rebut Alasdair MacIntyre’s charge that the transition into the “ethical” stage of life takes place by an arbitrary choice to regard ethical virtues and principles as having normative force. In 1995, following points in Edward Mooney’s work, I proposed that Kierkegaard’s distinction is closely related (though not identical) to Harry Frankfurt’s contrast between “wanton” agents who act directly on their strongest desires and “persons” who shape their character by forming a higher-order will concerning the motives on which they act:<sup>1</sup> when persons make commitments that involve such higher-order volitions, they find that ethical norms for character, which they already recognize at least as motivationally external reasons or understand in abstract, now *matter* for their volitional identity (whether they follow these norms or not). Thus the salience of ethical norms, or their relevance to the agent’s life, is explained by his “choice” to exercise his full volitional capacities. Of course, such an account requires a conception of free will that can respond to reasons that presently remain external to (what Bernard Williams calls) the agent’s “motivational set.”<sup>2</sup> It also raises the question of why certain ideals of virtuous motivation have objective normativity and why more “authentic” or deliberate forms of aesthetic life remain unsustainable, both of which I addressed in “Towards an Existential Virtue Ethics” by linking the picture of ethical agency in *Either/* *Or* with MacIntyre’s account of “practices” and social life.<sup>3</sup>

This account followed Anthony Rudd’s arguments that aesthetes lack the sort of narrative coherence over time necessary for a fully meaningful 

*[p. 92]* ^pg111

life,<sup>4</sup> which I reinterpreted in terms of Frankfurtian “wholeheartedness” or unity among higher-order volitions. As Rudd put it, for Kierkegaard, morality becomes personally relevant to human agents when they “make long-term commitments” and “accept social roles, and, by doing so .  .  . accept the standards of evaluation that go with them.”<sup>5</sup> In his contribution to our 2001 collection, Rudd also develops his earlier argument that the aesthete’s “life falls apart into a series of disconnected moments,” rather than being integrated into a “constancy of disposition.”<sup>6</sup> He holds that the young man “*A*” in Kierkegaard’s *Either/Or* is in a kind of despair that results from his life lacking “narrative unity” in MacIntyre’s sense. Particular actions find their meaning within the “wider narrative of the agent’s life,”<sup>7</sup> which has to add up to more than random sequences of pleasures, conquests, and reflections on them if the agent is to avoid a despairing sense of boredom and emptiness.<sup>8</sup> *A* clearly suffers from such existential emptiness and can appreciate the ethical diagnosis that his problem results from his refusal to dedicate himself to ends with eternal value—though he may try to deny the rational superiority of this diagnosis.<sup>9</sup>

I continue to believe that both these explanations of the rational superiority of ethical life over aestheticism are on the right track, and provide parts of a more comprehensive account. Yet both have been seriously challenged in the years following *Kierkegaard After MacIntyre*. First, it may not be obvious that there is any connection between these accounts, because MacIntyre’s account of human lives as personal narratives nested in traditions and connected by shared activities in the practices and by social relationships makes no direct reference to higher-order volition. Second, Michelle Kosch, Ian Duckles, and others have argued that Kierkegaard’s various types of “aesthete” do not all fit the pattern of Frankfurt’s “wanton,” as some (like *A* or Johannes of “The Seducer’s Diary”) are more reflective and even committed to aesthetic “projects.”<sup>10</sup> Similarly, in a series of articles, John Lippitt has argued that narrative unity is either too weak a condition to distinguish aesthetic and ethical agents, or too demanding a requirement that unduly limits worthwhile forms of life.<sup>11</sup>

These problems may be unsurprising since Rudd and I were focused on answering MacIntyre’s charge of irrationalism rather than offering a more detailed survey of aesthetic modes of life or a more complete analysis of different forms of narrative connection and integration. We and several of our co-contributors aimed to bring Kierkegaard closer to MacIntyre’s view, rather than to defend that view against external objections. Although I tried to distinguish between aesthetes who are “awakened” to the existential need for deep identity or autonomous character and those who are not, my initial account only offered a generic picture of the basic difference between of responsibility for character through personal appropriation of ethical norms versus aesthetic failure to appropriate. My first construal of the aesthetic–ethical distinction was close to Kosch’s point that “The 

*[p. 93]* ^pg112

aesthetic view is despair because it is characterized as the denial” of moral responsibility,<sup>12</sup> while “the ethical stance” is “the taking up of responsibility,” which is the only attitude towards one’s agency that can be seriously “chosen.”<sup>13</sup> The main difference is that I distinguish legal competence and generic responsibility for actions from *deep* responsibility for practical identity, and I take Kierkegaard’s focus to be on deep responsibility for character expressed in actions that flow from positive and concrete volitional commitments. Most types of aesthetes have practical identities without this kind of character, and the notion of “wantonness” is just one useful first way to think about this. Although *A* is no ordinary wanton, his project is too negative and abstract to have deep moral worth or unworth; he is trying to forfeit rather than to play the moral game. This generic picture has been nuanced in my later work.

Likewise, Rudd has since developed his account of narrative unity in the life of responsible agents;<sup>14</sup> for example, he has argued that to meet Lippitt’s challenge, we must distinguish between the kind of narrative connection that we find in any “self-conscious rational being who has some narrative sense of his or her own past” and a stronger kind of integration that involves “autonomy” and acceptance of responsibility for one’s concrete personality and dispositions.<sup>15</sup> He rightly says that some of Kierkegaard’s aesthetes seem earnestly committed to aesthetic “ground projects” (Williams’s term) but he notes that they still fail in the essential task of self-authorship because they adopt a final end “either arbitrarily, or on the basis of contingent desires, not because it is recognized as genuinely worthwhile.” Their volitional unity thus depends “not on them” but on mutable contingencies; it is liable to collapse because their commitments are not grounded on any deeper and more stable ethical valuations.<sup>16</sup> 

In this chapter, I will support Rudd’s answers to Lippitt by arguing that familiar proposed volitional conditions of personal autonomy are best understood as involving narrative conditions. The kind of narrative unity associated with autonomy goes beyond, but builds on, the narratival connections that are essential to the practical identity of persons in general (as described in Chapter 2 §4). These points will show that what is missing even in the most reflective and deliberate types of aestheticism must be explained at least partly in narrative terms. Then we can also better understand what Kierkegaard adds to other promising narrative accounts of practical identity—in particular, we can appreciate and assess his claim that persons who take responsibility for their character by shaping the talents and traits that nature, upbringing, and society give them are implicitly committed to the importance of ethical norms that can govern their commitments and priorities. Most of the argument in the rest of this section and in §2–3 will be relevant for any reader interested in practical identity or autonomy in general. I will then focus in more detail on Kierkegaard’s aesthetic–ethical distinction in §4.

#### 1.2 A Summary of the Basic Narrative Realist Model of Practical Identity

Before moving forward, it will help to summarize the narrative realist conception of practical identity developed up to this point in response to Lippitt’s first objections 1–4 (as listed in Chapter 2). The ‘basic model,’ as I will call it, includes the following five theses introduced in the prior chapter.

 (A) *The Analogy thesis*: the truthmaker of a biographical story itself has something *like* a narrative structure of meaning-relations, even though much of it arises as first-order content prior to any interpersonal or intrapersonal logos, explanatory telling, or reflective synthesis (see thesis E for qualification). This structure that constitutes a unique practical identity<sup>17</sup> is called a *narravive* to distinguish it from narratives as artistic artifacts or products of reflective composition.

 (B) *The Level thesis*: the sort of robust narratival sequence required for the practical identity of persons includes unity of apperception and minimally well-functioning semantic and episode memory (unity-0) and a cumulatively developing diachronic weave of meaning-relations built upon the teleological links involved in effective planning agency, such as intentions that extend over long periods of time and coordinate multiple types of actions drawing on different capacities (unity- 1). These constitutive forms of narratival continuity enable planning agents to make various kinds of volitional and cognitive eff orts needed to achieve further integration of ends and activities associated with personal autonomy (unity-2).

 (C) *The Mimetic thesis*: the basic human capacity to make secondary narratives, including nonfictional or broadly historical accounts and fictional stories, is *derived from* our experience in living out primary narravives—both those that constitute individual practical identities, and those that constitute the interpersonal histories of groups to which individual identities are linked.

 (D) *The Incompleteness thesis*: even the best literary depictions of a fictional life, or biographical portrayals of an actual life, or historical works about groups of persons, necessarily fall short of the practically infinite detail of significance in actual lived experience, which involves overlapping layers of resonances and associative connections between possible and actual acts and experiences in the past, present, and anticipated future.

 (E) *The Articulation thesis*: the narravive of one’s practical identity includes, from an early age, conceptions of one’s activities, character, personality, roles, and relationships, etc. which usually begin by following familiar paradigms or social scripts. These range from more or less tacit self-understandings to interpretations explicitly worked out in thorough meditation on oneself. Thus reflexive *logoi* of several 

*[p. 95]* ^pg114

kinds add to and reshape the weave of meanings already acquired and continually enlarged through new prerefl ective experience. At least some reflection on self of these sorts is necessary for planning agency in general, and personal autonomy makes further use of the capacity to consider the thematic coherence of one’s central cares and one’s narravive as a whole.

This restatement is a synthesis of points discussed in explaining each of the basic theses; for example, in this version, parts of the robust conception of narratival sequence (RC) are distributed between theses (B) and (D). Different distinctions are mentioned; in particular, it is important not to confuse the difference between first- and second-*order* meanings, and the closely related difference between primary narravive and secondary narrative, with the distinction between *levels* of narrative unity. For in fact, both fi rst- and second-order nodes of meaning are operative at the levels of narravive unity-1 and again in unity-2.

The division of labor between the basic theses is mainly for heuristic value: each of them could be broken down into subcomponents or combined into a single thesis. In their present division, they clarify a way of understanding practical identities in narrative terms that makes it possible to apply different ideals of narrative unity to distinct aspects of identity. I believe this ‘basic model’ is a faithful extension of MacIntyre’s sketch of a narrative conception in *After Virtue*. While parts of it are anticipated by Kierkegaard and Heidegger, the basic model does not by itself commit one to stronger claims about the narrative form of personal autonomy or to any distinctively Kierkegaardian thesis regarding the connection between autonomy and ethical duty.

#### 1.3 Kierkegaardian Extensions of Narrative Realism

I have suggested that this basic narrative model provides a foundation for a description of higher levels of “narrative unity” that go beyond the unity-1 needed to have a personal identity. The possibility of higher levels of integration is often implicit both in narrative theories and in accounts that ignore narrative form. For example, Korsgaard writes that we typically “have many practical identities” or roles and relationships that we endorse and “so we face the task of uniting them into a coherent whole.” This implies that less-than-fully coherent practical identity is possible—even if the agent has to *intend* to be coherent to meet the constitutive condition of responsible agency on her account.<sup>18</sup> She also says that one can acquire a practical identity whose components result entirely from chance or adoption for little reason other than circumstantial opportunity,<sup>19</sup> just as one acquires a natural language via normal early upbringing without planning or eff ort towards this as an articulated goal. This agrees with my prior arguments that narravive structure with unity-1 is the default result 

*[p. 96]* ^pg115

of normal upbringing; though it is not involuntary, given that a child must exercise powers of agency and eventually reflection for unity-1 to result, this usually happens without unity-1 being an intended goal. The higher level of reflection necessary to think explicitly about “narrative unity” in one’s life (or any similar concept) is no part of the articulation condition for unity-1; but this kind of more holistic reflection becomes necessary in eff orts towards unity-2.

Similarly, when Korsgaard says that the task of constituting ourselves as agents “involves finding some roles and fulfilling them with dedication and integrity,”<sup>20</sup> she is talking about an aspect of unity-2 without making the level distinction. For there are plenty of agents with practical identities featuring planned and somewhat effective patterns of intentional action who still show little dedication, sustained eff ort in the face of adversity, or integrity in the sense of loyalty to principles when stronger appetites can be satisfied by their betrayal. The character of Billy Hicks in *St. Elmo’s Fire* represents a person with a practical identity: he is a part-time musician with little interest in musical excellence, a narcissistic fun-seeker, a playboy without loyalty to his wife, and an unreliable friend with no direction in life—a fairly typical instance of the more unreflective type of aesthete in Kierkegaard’s array. In such cases, it is misleading to say that “whenever I act in accordance with \[my\] roles and identities, I endorse them, I embrace them, I affi rm once again that I am them.”<sup>21</sup> Although Kant and Sartre make similar claims, it is only in a *tacit* sense that aesthetes or wantons endorse or “own” the roles they play or relationships they enter into on the basis of brute preference or unevaluated desires. <sup>22</sup>A person acting from a compulsive disorder or coercive threat does not even endorse her motive in this tacit sense; she recognizes it as “hers” only in the *minimal* sense that it is occurs in her consciousness. But a person surrounded daily by forces whose coerciveness is salient, or for whom compulsive addictions are large themes in his life may still have unity-1 (though such problems can eventually erode the basic capacities needed for planning agency). He may conceive his condition accurately, based on reflective assessment or a diagnosis from psychotherapy, or even fixate too strongly on clinical diagnoses in an eff ort to make narrative sense of his experience. But that is not the same as embracing or willing to act on compulsive, bipolar, or schizoid motives. The distinction between autonomous action and intentional action in general is indispensible, and the narrative level-distinction tracks it.

Aesthetic agents are sometimes described in terms that might make it sound as if they lack even unity-1, but careful interpretation will recognize the difference. Will, the protagonist in the film *About a Boy*, is more honest than Kierkegaard’s young man *A* when he says: “My life was made up of units of time. Buying CDs—two units. Eating lunch—three units. Exercising—two units. All in all, I had a very full life. It’s just that it didn’t mean anything.”<sup>23</sup> But Will does not suffer from amnesia, dissociative fragmentation of personalities, an extreme compulsive disorder, a 

*[p. 97]* ^pg116

cognitive disability that rules out long-term intention, or any of the normally involuntary impairments that interrupt unity-1 (the flip-side of its normally developing unintentionally). Will has a practical identity and can even articulate it better than most (he is “an island”). The continuity between segments of time that he lacks is that provided by deeper purpose or worthy ambition. Caring about what Thomas Hill calls “nonmoral standards” or personal “ideals”<sup>24</sup> is typically related to such higher aspirations as well, so that lacking any aims that can be recognized as noble, Will does not see suffi cient value in his “interests, projects, and plans.”<sup>25</sup> These contrasts offer a rough or provisional sense of the phenomena to be explained, with Kierkegaard’s help, in terms of higher kinds of narrative unity beyond unity-1.

The basic model also answers some objections to narrative theories of identity and lays the basis for answers to the others. Once the triviality objection (1) is answered by the robust conception of narratival sequences (Chapter 2 §4.2), and the artifact objection (2) is answered by the distinctions between primary narravives and secondary narrative accounts, and prerefl exive and reflexive aspects of primary life-narratives, the other main objections become easier to handle. Objection (4) concerning fact-value and the constitutive versus normative distinction, and objection (5) concerning flexibility versus ossification, can be solved by an explanation of personal autonomy, authenticity, and ethical maturity as involving types of narrative unity beyond the constitutive level-1 that includes nonautonomous and inauthentic identities. It is here that Kierkegaard becomes especially interesting for the narrative realist in helping to explain and defend two further theses beyond theses (A–E) about unity-1. The first concerns personal autonomy, which is not limited to the Kantian sense of legislating moral norms (via a free rational agency that necessarily commits itself to the inviolable value of such agency), because it includes the *executive* sense of governing one’s core priorities and overall direction in life (with a conditioned right to do so). It was implicit in my initial account of *Either/Or* that the autonomy lacked by aesthetic agents is the control needed for deep responsibility for one’s whole identity (see Chapter 1 §1). But the basic idea can be stated in a way that does not explicitly mention aestheticism:

 (F) *The Existential coherence* thesis: autonomy, understood as the ability to govern one’s core priorities and the overall direction of one’s life (the control-condition of responsibility for one’s practical identity) requires both (a) that we freely form and maintain cares and commitments to long-term ends, and that (b) we *can* consciously reflect and will as needed to make our cares into an essentially coherent narrative whole in which integrity and wholeheartedness are possible.

This thesis is distinctively Kierkegaardian in tying autonomy to wholeheartedness specifically understood in narrative fashion. But as it stands 

*[p. 98]* ^pg117

(F) invites Lippitt’s worry that a focus on unity may blind us to potential new experiences and possibilities, or prevent us from appreciating a suffi cient diversity of ends that can be valued in a rich life, or restrict our lines of activity so narrowly that all adventure of life is lost.<sup>26</sup> I have defended (F) against this concern in my book on willing as end-setting, where I try to show that it is compatible both with attention to a suitably wide array of values and with the existential importance of openness to novelty.<sup>27</sup> But thesis (F) also faces more radical objections (from Strawson and others) that agent-autonomy can be episodic, requiring no long-term commitments or cares in Frankfurt’s sense.<sup>28</sup> It is vital in considering such objections to remember that thesis (F) does not claim that *all* the conditions of personal autonomy can be derived from, or encapsulated in, the idea of narrative unity among ends, life-goals, and ground projects—narrative integration may only be part of the explanation, though it cannot be a trivial addition. The same applies to the most distinctively Kierkegaardian thesis in this debate:

 (G) *The Ethical thesis*: personal autonomy and the kind of narrative unity it involves cannot be developed without taking seriously (as personally relevant to one’s life) ethical ideals and moral obligations with objective status—some sense of “the good” and “duty” that governs other values and personal affi nities reflected in one’s cares, and that is not simply a function of what satisfies the agent’s brute preferences or given desires.

This thesis can be strengthened by insisting that the requisite conception of ethical norms be a perfectionist one, as in the “Decisive Expression” part of Kierkegaard’s *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (CUP 525–555) or even an agapic one, as in his *Works of Love*. Thus the strength of (G), and of the robust narrative unity-2 involved in bringing our other cares and life-projects under ethical ideals, varies according to more specific conceptions of “the ethical.” For instance, Korsgaard defends a version of thesis (G) when she argues that regarding our practical identities as sources of practical reasons for us to act implicitly commits us to the inviolable value of free rational agency, which amounts to accepting a Kantian moral law.<sup>29</sup> But, in itself, as a metaethical thesis (G) does not claim (as Lippitt sometimes worries<sup>30</sup>) that the entire content of normative ethics can be derived from the idea of unity-2; it is mostly the other way around, as the conditions of unity-2 alter as more conventional, Hegelian, Kantian, or perfectionist moral demands are substituted into thesis (G).

Consider in this light Lippitt’s argument that in *Either/Or II*, Judge William defends several normative claims that seem to involve mores specific to his culture or religion, such as the importance of marriage and useful vocation.<sup>31</sup> I suggest that only some of the Judge’s claims, such as the importance 

*[p. 99]* ^pg118

of honesty with oneself or disclosure, are meant to pertain to “the ethical” in its general sense as an existential “sphere” or basic volitional stance that is contrasted with “aestheticism” in general. Some of the Judge’s examples of specific ethical duties are developed to strengthen his argument that the basic commitment to deep agency that lies at the center of ethical life is not only compatible with, but even requires, a retained appreciation of aesthetic values that now appear in a new light. Some of his ethical claims may be symptoms of his personal biases, cultural traditions, or even what Kierkegaard would regard as the limits of the Judge’s ethical understanding. But the drawbacks of these particulars should be distinguished from the Judge’s potent defense of (G) in its metaethical form which concerns “the ethical” as a basic volitional orientation in life. Agents who satisfy the general condition in (G) can disagree quite a bit about what “the goods” of human life and their moral duties are, while agreeing that their nonmoral pursuits, cares, and personal relationships must be governed by the ethical ideal they are all trying to understand (given their shared rejection of ethical relativism). They will agree that a mature identity involves an *ethical unification* that demarcates them from all types of aesthetes, some of whom have instrumentally sophisticated projects pursued over long periods of time.<sup>32</sup> We must also remember that agents can meet the Judge’s volitional condition for agent-autonomy and thus take to heart the authority of ethical norms without succeeding in living according to them, or shaping what they would regard as a *morally good* (let alone perfect) character.

However, (G) faces two other general objections that have nothing specifically to do with the Judge’s presentation. The first holds that *nonmoral* cares, commitments, or projects are suffi cient for an autonomous life; this claim is found in Bernard Williams and (in my view, ironically) in Harry Frankfurt, but not in Lippitt.<sup>33</sup> I have called this Williams’ argument for the possibility of “authentic aestheticism” and defended (G) against it.<sup>34</sup> But perhaps that label would be more apt for the view that “the aesthete’s life has all the ‘meaning’ that he needs,” as Lippitt puts it<sup>35</sup>—that an aesthetic life can be suffi ciently rich in personal meaning without giving priority or any central place to ethical norms that would regulate our personal projects and cares. On this view, an aesthetic life need not turn out to be hollow or superficial in the way that Will found in *About a Boy*. Obviously, these objections are closely connected, because one concerns autonomy and the other concerns authentic life-meaning, which are related. But I do not say that autonomy entails authenticity in this existential sense; though the converse—that a life full of authentic meaning depends on agent-autonomy—is more plausible. The relation between authentic meaning in life and autonomous control is complex, and so the two objections to (G) should be kept distinct. Before we can answer them, we must first consider a direct argument from conditions of autonomy to important kinds of narrative coherence beyond unity-1.

### 2 Narrative Unity-2 and Personal Autonomy: MacIntyre, Frankfurt, and Kierkegaard

#### 2.1 Four Modifications to Initial 
Hierarchical Models of Formal Autonomy

Due to its close relation to the concept of a fulfilling or happy life, the concept of worthwhile activity authentically pursued has been more often employed by narrative theorists in the eudaimonist tradition. In a narrativist like MacIntyre, the link in thesis (F) between narrative unity and agent-autonomy is not immediately evident, but a version of (G) linking authenticity and ethical consciousness is explicitly defended. In critiquing Sartre’s negative account of “integrity” as refusal of conventional social roles and relations, MacIntyre explains authentic agency in terms of virtues: to possess a virtue requires a strong disposition to act on desire for a noble or *kalon* end in many “different types of situations,” and this unity of virtue across domains of action “is intelligible only as a characteristic of a unitary life.”<sup>36</sup> But as we have seen, MacIntyre’s notion of “the unity of a narrative which links birth to life to death” is not a suffi cient condition for strength of character; it is only the necessary condition of unity- 1. The unity of a virtuous life requires “longest-term intentions” within which short-term action-plans are intelligible,<sup>37</sup> and these are implicit or explicit answers to the question of “how best I might live out that unity” that is my life and “bring it to completion.”<sup>38</sup> In my terms, this question can be restated as: how should I develop my narravive from its constitutive unity-1 to a higher unity-2 through pursuit of various goods? Although MacIntyre does not formally distinguish these types of narrative connection, he implies that the higher kind of unity is found in “a conception of *the* good” through which we can order other goods in our life over time.<sup>39</sup> In other words, it involves a comprehensive good that can regulate the other final ends, activities, and relationships in which we recognize intrinsic value. This is a way of defending thesis (G).

Such an authentic identity clearly involves reflection on what makes for a meaningful life, and a conception of how one’s own life fits into the larger stories of practices (primarily arts and sciences) and traditions.<sup>40</sup> We do not develop our own ground projects solipsistically without any interest in what significant others have lived and died for.<sup>41</sup> MacIntyre does not specifically add second-order volitions to this kind of identity, but says that it requires agents “to learn at various stages how to transcend what have been up to that point the limitations of their motivational set” by internalizing what have been external reasons;<sup>42</sup> as we see with temperance, initiation into virtues in general requires that a child learn to “stand back from her or his desires and evaluate them . . .”<sup>43</sup> Thus a kind of second-order volition based on evaluation of objective goods is implicit in MacIntyre’s account. Authentic identity moves well beyond the prerefl ective order of narravive 

*[p. 101]* ^pg120

because participation in practices and traditions involves considering how their standards and conception of their goals have developed over time, and committing oneself to those goals. While explicitly philosophical reflection is not required for authenticity, it is a natural extension of the ability to reflect on one’s whole life “from conception to death” and one’s need to receive care from others at different stages that characterizes “independent practical reasoners.”<sup>44</sup> Such an authentic identity is then a social and historical identity according to MacIntyre, but he avoids terms like “autonomy” with their individualistic connotations.<sup>45</sup>

Yet showing that personal autonomy involves kinds of narrative unity beyond unity-1 may provide a conception that is better able to calm skeptical worries about the unity of ends in a “whole life.” This argument starts from a “hierarchical” approach to personal autonomy like Gerald Dworkin’s or Harry Frankfurt’s initial models from the early 1970s. According to them, a key condition of autonomy is that an agent acts on first-order motives with which she identifies, where volitional “identification” with a motive *M*1 reduces to desiring to act on *M*1, or endorsing *M*1 through unmanipulated critical reflection about it, or regarding *M*1 as expressive of the character one wishes to exhibit (the opposite of regarding *M*1 as “alien”).<sup>46</sup> There are well-known problems with these models,<sup>47</sup> but they can be mitigated if we reconceive “alienation” from *M*1 in terms of *resisting* it in favor of competing first-order motives with which we identify, and interpret “identification” with a motive as making a sincere eff ort to acquire or sustain it because it fits with our cares and the practical judgments they embody. Higher-order will in this sense is not merely decision but a kind of self-motivation. This *rational striving conception* says that higher-order volitions aimed at shaping our first-order motives are authoritative when they meet three conditions:

a. The eff ort to cultivate certain first-order motives (*M*1) rather than others is actively formed and sustained by the agent’s striving will, e.g. by making clear decisions and setting new ends for himself; it is not a mere means to satisfy prior desires.

b. This volitional eff ort to cultivate *M*1 is based on a positive *strong evaluation* of *M*1 or the ends *E* at which it aims; thus it responds to values that the agent takes to have objective status.

c. These value-apprehensions and the resulting eff orts are freely formed: 

(*i*) they have a suitable genealogy (ruling out autonomy-undermining forms of manipulation and mind-control and presupposing the education of suffi ciently robust reasons-responsive capacities).

In what follows, I will say more about the two revisions in conditions (a) and (b), but I will have to leave largely aside the diffi cult and fascinating problem of spelling out condition (c) through an analysis of different kinds of *sources* of psychic states that are autonomy-undermining 

*[p. 102]* ^pg121

or autonomy-promoting (or perhaps neutral). For arguably the source-problem is distinct from the problem of understanding the psychological structure of volitional identification itself. I also leave aside the question of whether to add another clause (*ii*) to (c) requiring some kind of libertarian freedom for the free formation of cares, as the existential concept of autonomy requires (ADR).<sup>48</sup> A couple of terminological clarifications: I will say that a motive *M*1 or action on it is *formally* autonomous when *M*1 is the object of higher-order volitions that meet conditions (a) and (b), though it is not *substantively* autonomous unless it also meets condition (c). To illustrate, our earlier question about Parfi t’s Russian nobleman was: if his later will to keep his property is formally autonomous, is it also substantially autonomous (see Chapter1 §5)? I favor “formal” over the term “structural,” which can imply time-slice conditions, and over “procedural,” which can connote neutrality towards ethical values. I also favor the term “source-conditions” over “historical-conditions” because formal autonomy also involves historical requirements on several accounts, including the narrative account to be outlined here.<sup>49</sup>

Although such a “rational striving” hierarchical theory of identification does not yet explicitly include guidance by ethical standards, it captures much of what distinguishes the “ethical” existence-sphere from the “aesthetic” in general, according to Kierkegaard. In 1995, I argued that Kierkegaard’s “aesthetes” typically lack specific and positive higher-order volitions through which they would become responsible for a practical identity with substantive content by shaping it through definite commitments to ends of perceived lasting value. I now offer some clarifications and amendments to this initial broad-brush interpretation. The first is that some aesthetes, including the young man *A*, have become aware (to lesser or greater extents) that something in their nature calls them towards deep responsibility or responsibility for an identity that would be authentically their own; yet they employ their volitional capacities in deficient ways to resist this call. As Connell says, they become reflective aesthetes by allowing the “possibility of choice opened up by the coming of reflection” to “pass unused,” making reflection into an instrument of their immediacy or first-order motives.<sup>50</sup> Among several ways of doing this to avoid deep responsibility we find a *negative* highest-order volition3 set against the formation of any positive commitments (involving concrete second-order volitions2) which is cloaked in the self-deceptive guise of a project aiming at aesthetic goods.<sup>51</sup>

By contrast, as Judge William says, the individual enters the “ethical” orientation to life by striving from his “actual self” towards his “ideal self” (EO II 259). His reflexive volitional attitudes express the “exemplary self” or character he wills2 to develop out of the contingent aptitudes, personality traits, and social circumstances that are given to his life from birth and upbringing (the passive beginning of his narravive). “Sovereignty over himself” or personal autonomy then consists in his

*[p. 103]* ^pg122

becoming conscious as this specific individual with these capacities, these inclinations, these drives, these passions, influenced by this specific social milieu, as this specific product of a specific environment. But as he becomes aware of all of this, he takes upon himself responsibility for all of it .  .  . \[and\] chooses himself as a product.  .  .  . An individual thus chooses himself as a complex specific concretion and therefore chooses himself in his *continuity* (EO II 251).

In other words, the narrative flow that results from his volitional work on his own psyche draws from his facticity in setting tasks that he wills to continue over time. Thus, by higher-order will, the individual “can gain a history, . . . can give this history continuity,” not via biographical summary but by imbuing his story with freedom (EO II 250). This repeated choice or continual volitional eff ort makes the resulting practical identity free in the positive sense of autonomy. His narravive now expresses an “ethical life-view” in the sense that it deliberately tries to shape the given elements according to values that he sees as having objective authority for his possible tasks. While he will probably not describe this process to himself in terms of “narrative unification,” he does reflectively recognize the importance of dedication or staying power in service of worthwhile purposes—precisely the idea Hill found essential to a form of self-respect that is neither simple meritocratic judgment nor mere recognition of one’s basic rights. Kierkegaard’s Judge also sees this as a kind of respect for one’s spiritual or volitional capacity: to create a new “self” through concrete tasks, “in the direction of continuity” rather than in mystic isolation or abstraction from temporal succession, the individual must be “transparent to himself” in a way that aesthetes are not. He “knows himself, penetrates his whole concretion with his consciousness” because higher-order volition requires critical reflection on one’s first-order motives and traits (EO II 258). Nor is this merely the view of one of Kierkegaard’s multiple pseudonymous authors with a limited perspective; for a similar explanation of “self” or practical identity as a result of exercising free will in forming concrete commitments that respond responsibly to the unique potentials within one’s given psychological, bodily, and social particulars is spelled out by Kierkegaard’s most authoritative religious pseudonym in *Sickness Unto Death*.<sup>52</sup>

It is clear that on this conception of autonomy, its volitional component directly entails a type of narrative continuity beyond the constitutive level of unity-1, which includes intentional but wanton acts that flow from accidental motives with which the agent has never identified. For example, as Norman Lillegard argues, Kierkegaard thinks that the character Claudine in Fru Gyllembourg’s *Two Ages* fails to take active charge of her character according to a “motivational self-concept” or “life-view” that would involve higher-order volitions guided by ethical norms.<sup>53</sup> She plans and acts, but she has not “ethically chosen and found” herself or taken the formation of her identity as her “objective” or goal (EO II 262). By 

*[p. 104]* ^pg123

contrast, volitional identification involves a thicker narrative conception of who we should be that is guided by ethical values. Higher-order volitional response to the agent’s facticity involves a kind of originality or distinctiveness because that response is not determined by the contingent psychophysical particulars that define her initial identity at the unreflective aesthetic stage. As Brännmark puts it, “To have narrative autonomy, one has to be the kind of person \[who\] has reached a sense of what to achieve in a way that has involved making up one’s own mind,” rather than simply allowing one’s purposes to be determined by the desires and expectations of others.<sup>54</sup> Such Kierkegaardian claims are supported by a fourth revision of the 

hierarchical model (d) which says that volitional identification is typically *part* of “caring” or is intelligible within the context of cares. Frankfurt implies a weaker link between these phenomena. In his analysis, cares constitute “our will” in one distinctive sense of that polyvalent term—namely that they are motives with *inherent* agent-authority: they are formally autonomous without needing any further endorsement by separate psychic states or processes.<sup>55</sup> This contrasts with the “motivational power that the passions mobilize” whose strength gives them “no inherent motivational authority;” whereas care and love are not “elementary psychic dat\[a\]” like appetites that need endorsement by our will, because they are already volitional states.<sup>56</sup> Frankfurt notes that for a person to love someone or care about something in his sense “entails that he has corresponding volitional attitudes towards himself,” identifying with fi rst-order motives that fit his love and alienating those that conflict with it; thus his cares partly define “his ideal self.”<sup>57</sup>

Cares necessarily involve a strong form of narrative continuity over time, as Frankfurt implies in several later essays: “caring,” as distinct from mere desiring, is an extended state of devotion to some goal, ideal, or person for their own sake, which the agent actively sustains by “having and identifying with” higher-order volitions for the appropriate first-order motives.<sup>58</sup> Brännmark agrees that the “striving” involved in caring can make a goal “part of my life-narrative.” Cares and preferences with deliberative priority would constitute “a narrative thread or theme in one’s life.”<sup>59</sup> While caring is outwardly directed, its reflexive component helps maintain our devotion to what or who we care about. As Jeffrey Blustein argues, at least the (usually nonselfi sh) forms of care that involve “commitment” to a thing, institution, person, or cause outside us require giving ongoing “deliberative priority” to considerations relevant to their well-being or promotion, which requires managing our desires. And our most central or “identity-conferring commitments” provide a basis for “integrity.”<sup>60</sup> Blustein here follows Gabrielle Taylor, who argues that a person of integrity cannot be a “wanton,” and that his volitional identification with fi rst-order motives must not alter on a whim; instead his higher-order will must express evaluations and have “some consistency” over time.<sup>61</sup> She describes the “shallowly sincere” person as one who is not wanton but 

*[p. 105]* ^pg124

who does not see his choices and higher-order volitions as narratively connected: he “capriciously and irresponsibly identifies with diff erent desires . . . as if what he says and does were isolated events with no implications for the future, and equally no roots in the past.”<sup>62</sup> His higher-order volitions are episodic. By contrast, the person of integrity must value consistency in, and critical control, over the evaluations on which the commitments central to her identity are based; they cannot alter to suit each group to which she belongs or social contexts in which she acts. The necessary stability in these evaluations requires that they are not based on contingent desires or brute preferences.<sup>63</sup> They are not “wholly superficial and short lived,” and so such evaluations cannot be changed without some “upheaval in a person’s life;” such revisions cannot be taken lightly. In sum, integrity requires a type of assessment of first-order motives that Charles Taylor has described as “strong evaluation.”<sup>64</sup>

While this does not guarantee that a person of integrity will have enlightened values, it does suggest that the narrative process of caring has epistemic conditions. Contra Frankfurt’s claims in *The Reasons of Love* and other late essays, caring and higher-order willing are naturally based on perceptions or beliefs about objective values; such a basis partially explains their nonderivative agent-authority. Within the “rational striving” conception, caring (like identification) requires the agent’s conviction that (*i*) the people or goals to which he is committed are inherently *worth caring about*, or that (*ii*) the *process* of caring about these ends and pursuing projects related to them is inherently valuable (or both), where (*iii*) such inherent value is independent of her prior desires or preferences, though not of her history. This is a brief summary of an objectivist account of caring that I have defended in detail elsewhere.<sup>65</sup> As Blustein argues, following Lomasky, at least “deep-seated” cares that have a central place in “the substance of one’s life” cannot regard their object’s value as purely subjective: “one must suppose it to have a kind of value that does not simply derive from one’s own individual caring about it.”<sup>66</sup> As a result, integrity is a second-order virtue; it is important mainly because there are first-order values that transcend our will and our desires that are worthy of our devotion in themselves. When one exhibits integrity, her “primary allegiance is to the objects of \[her\] core commitments, not to some ideal of self” that operates independently of them—though the value of integrity can be a supporting reason not to compromise one’s principles or violate one’s cares.<sup>67</sup>

This completes the deduction from autonomy to narrative unity-2: formal autonomy entails volitional identifications, which (for the most part) occur as nested within cares, which in turn involve a strong type of narrative connection in the form of a purpose actively sustained by the agent’s authoritative eff ort—a theme whose continuation is not accidental, and which has an important place in the agent’s life, giving it personal meaning—just as Williams had argued in defending the need for ground projects (an especially strong type of care).

*[p. 106]* ^pg125

These revisions to Frankfurt also support the Kierkegaardian theses. The connection between autonomy and caring made in condition (d) supports the Existential Coherence thesis (F), while conditions (b) and (*i*–*iii*) in the definition of cares support the Ethical thesis (G), and a related conception of what Frankfurt calls “caring about caring.” On the rational striving conception, the kind of caring1 that is existentially central—that gives life most of its first-personal meaning—necessarily involves caring2 that the values that ground our cares1 or personal commitments, ideals, life-goals, and central relationships are rightly discerned, not mere sentimental wishes or illusions of ideology. We cannot sustain the motivation required for pursuit of complex and challenging ends, shared activities, or rich interpersonal communion while believing that in themselves, such goals and relationships are pointless, or that all their value is imposed by our accidental desires, or (even worse) that our interest in them derives solely from manipulation by others. Our second-order concern that our cares1 are well-grounded requires that the apparent values to which they are loyal or which they express are not false or intrinsically meaningless. As Kierkegaard recognizes, it is a central fact of human agency that we do not have the power to invent meanings or posit values by willing them; we cannot retain a stable sense of the significance of our ends despite believing that ultimately, meaning and value are just shadows of brute preferences or attitudes we have without any practical justification.<sup>68</sup>

#### 2.2 How A and the Seducer fail the Integrity Requirement for Unity-2

If these modifications of Frankfurt’s model are convincing, then the *constancy* that is essential to earnest caring, as opposed to its fickle simulacra, is a response to what we take to be enduring practical realities outside of the self, many of which involve some sort of social relationship (e.g. common goods). Thus the kind of caring that is central to a meaningful life has a narrative structure that depends on a larger, shared (though continually debated and refined) story concerning what is worth caring about in life. The development of cares is thus partly dependent on developing social understandings of values worthy of our devotion. About this, Taylor and MacIntyre are right: to move into the “ethical” life-view is not only to start caring1 positively about something or someone, but also to care2 about the objective value of our cares1. That is why Kierkegaard’s Judge describes the primordial choice out of the aesthetic into the ethical stage as choice of oneself in one’s “eternal validity” because it “links him to an eternal power for an eternity” (EO II 206), i.e. it establishes volitional continuity on the basis of universal objective values.<sup>69</sup> As Connell says, “An essential part of choosing oneself in one’s eternal validity is accepting one’s reality,” including one’s contingent properties and one’s past, including one’s flaws. Thus,

*[p. 107]* ^pg126

Through the central aspects of ethical existence—repentance and duty—the self is constituted as historical. Its whole past is gathered into its present as the real that must be chosen or appropriated. In the process, the self’s future becomes present in the form of the \[ethical\] ideals that are projected in this ethical reflection on the given. Thus, all the moments of the ethical self’s life stand in internal relation to each other, co-inhering and involving each other.<sup>70</sup>

Kierkegaard’s portraits of more sophisticated aesthetes like *A* and the Johannes the Seducer are meant to show this by way of contrast. As Rudd argues, both *A* and the Seducer fail to achieve the kind of narrative unity associated with taking responsibility for one’s self through autonomy or deep control, even if their lives have the intelligibility provided by unity- 1.<sup>71</sup> Rudd details the ambiguities in *A*’s different writings, and insightfully suggests that by “The Rotation of Crops” in *Either/Or* I, “*A* has become fully conscious of his previously implicit commitment to the ultimate value of freedom and living for ‘the interesting,’ and has deliberately chosen to embrace it.”<sup>72</sup> Even more clearly, the Seducer is committed to his similar “ground project with ‘aesthetic earnestness’ and thus may seem to have a considerable measure of unity/narrative coherence in his life,” or seem to be formally autonomous.<sup>73</sup>

Yet neither *A* nor the Seducer meet the conditions for unity-2 that I have described; for their projects are mere means that they would set aside if they ceased to please. Thus *A* says, “No part of life ought to have so much meaning for a person that he cannot forget it any moment he wants to . . .” (EO I 293), and this includes anything he presently finds interesting. By contrast, the unity-2 of a care involves a commitment that cannot be dropped at will. Such a commitment must also identify with motives on the basis of some kind of value—for *A*, aesthetic values of interest and beauty conceived as a stimulus to passions that are enjoyable, engrossing, or fascinating to experience. But neither *A* nor the Seducer are willing to accept such values as having *normative authority* for their cares; for normative significance could hardly be restricted to the mostly vague aesthetic values that they celebrate in nebulous terms. If they appreciated the objective status of any values, they would have to recognize the intrinsic importance of other clearer and/or higher aesthetic values, and of the wider range of non-aesthetic values that are the proper objects of many of the emotions they instrumentalize or try to enjoy solely for their interest-value.

This sentimentalist perversion of the emotions shows that *A* and the Seducer are concerned with value-appreciation only as a means to a kind of enjoyment and connoisseurship.<sup>74</sup> Whatever they may *say* about their “pacts” or “ideals,” neither is really devoted even to aesthetic values *for their own sake* in the way that an artist with integrity must be. A real artist, as Mooney puts it, recovers the forgotten “plenitudes” in human 

*[p. 108]* ^pg127

“errors and sorrows”—in ordinary artifacts, in “living things, or animated things like great storms, or things resonant with serenity, like great reclining rocks,” acting as a loving cultivator, husbanding meaning, reenchanting the world.<sup>75</sup> In other words, a real artist allows herself to be governed by values with a significance that exists prior to her will, though her creativity may also reveal new values. Neither *A* or the Seducer love hidden dimensions of meaning in this way. For example, it is impossible to imagine either of them refusing to censor their prose or poetry under threat from the King because they were bringing a new kind of beauty to humanity. On the contrary, aesthetic values are “important” to *A* and the Seducer only because a focus on such values is an interesting way to live in the present moment. Thus Rudd is exactly right when he says of the Seducer:

. . . he is not constrained in the choice of such a project by the consideration that the project is of overriding worth in itself. For the Judge such a project still lacks autonomy, because the project has been chosen either arbitrarily or on the basis of contingent desires . . . hence this sort of “commitment” is somewhat unreal . . . The Seducer has nothing that he accepts as having a claim on him.<sup>76</sup>

Even the Seducer’s (ever fluctuating) aesthetic standards of erotic beauty have no authority for him apart from his current interest in them, and the same goes for *A* (at least in his later writings). In other words, neither the Seducer nor *A* really *cares* about aesthetic goods because neither takes the intrinsic value of such goods as a final end based on their intersubjective importance. The thematic continuity in their lives that is apparently brought about by their will is superficial, lacking the basis needed for resilience and the repetition of daily renewal. Their apparent dedication to an aesthetic ideal involves no true loyalty to anything wondrous outside their own psychic states, and this serves their ulterior concern with avoiding deep responsibility. Lippitt, Kosch, and Duckles all miss this point because they measure the success of a hierarchical account of aestheticism solely according to whether aesthetes lack higher-order volitions *as Frankfurt* initially defined them. Instead, I have stressed that only an account recast in terms of rationally based caring fits with Kierkegaard’s conception of deep responsibility for character and explains the sense in which his aesthetes tend to lack volitional autonomy.<sup>77</sup>

These modifications agree with Gabrielle Taylor’s critical analysis of Frankfurt and integrity-phenomena, as we saw. They also largely agree with Korsgaard’s suggestion that, “instead of thinking of personal projects as arising from . . . \[agent-relative\] or personal values, we should think of them as arising from a desire to stand in a special relationship to something that we regard as having intersubjective or universal value.”<sup>78</sup> The only difference is that I explain the agent’s personal relation to the relevant value as established by a volitional eff ort in caring, rather than as based on a desire. 

*[p. 109]* ^pg128

Similar alterations in Frankfurt’s initial definition of volitional identification have been defended by Gary Watson, Susan Wolf, Eleonore Stump, and Michael Bratman among others.<sup>79</sup> Charles Taylor has presented the most detailed arguments that motives with inherent authority for human agents involve “strong evaluations” that distinguish the quality of different ends and motives rather than merely ranking them in a preference-order according to desire-strength.<sup>80</sup> Following Stephen Darwall, Blustein has likewise argued that we cannot think that the objects of our central commitments are trivial, or important to us only because of our desires.<sup>81</sup> And in response to Williams, Blustein, and others, Cheshire Calhoun agrees that “fidelity to our core values” constitutes part of our integrity.<sup>82</sup> She adds that, as a social virtue, integrity also requires that we be willing to stand up publicly for, and defend before others, our own best judgment of what matters and how to live.<sup>83</sup> This publicity condition for integrity fits with unity-2 in one’s practical identity if caring requires loyalty to values that we think should be recognizable by anyone with enough information, experience, or insight, though it does not involve claiming infallibility.<sup>84</sup>

This point about integrity is an extension of the (d) condition for formal autonomy: the shared goal of reaching collective evaluative truth is implicit in the practice of standing up for the ideals, principles and values behind one’s cares. As Calhoun rightly emphasizes, this is not only incompatible with selling out, conforming too quickly to authority or popular opinion, and hypocrisy (all of which weaken caring); it is also incompatible with “ .  .  . defensiveness, incivility, close-mindedness, \[and\] deafness to criticism (traits particularly connected with fanaticism) . . .”<sup>85</sup> A self-righteous ideologue insulates his cares from critique by a form of self-deception that betrays a lack of earnestness; he is more concerned about his self-image than about the true worth of that which he supposedly cares about. Thus the strong evaluative conception explains why earnest caring cannot be fanatical: if our understanding of the practical realities that justify our cares is challenged by new evidence, we may have to reconsider them. Our second-order concern that our cares are well-grounded requires openness to this possibility. As Blustein says, “exceptional resoluteness is an ideal species of integrity,” but such an agent can still change her mind without *akrasia*. People sometimes do adapt, conform, or modify their commitments out of weakness, but “it is not weakness of will to be open to reason and persuasive argument.”<sup>86</sup>

Thus on the rational striving model of formal autonomy, unity-2 requires a kind of commitment that exhibits strength of will while being open to revision in light of new evidence and new experience—which is part of the solution to Lippitt’s objection (5) about excess rigidity. The social conditions of integrity that Calhoun outlines also show one of the ways in which the Articulation condition (E) become stronger in narrative unity- 2: the evaluative burden of caring helps explain why one cannot achieve autonomy without accepting the burden of being a “codeliberator” with 

*[p. 110]* ^pg129

others about values worthy of care. Although these include a wide range of nonmoral values, and Calhoun does not insist that they include ethical considerations, the communicative ends required by her conditions clearly have moral implications, as Habermas’s work on discourse ethics has shown. We cannot cooperate with others in articulating practical truth or warrant for beliefs without respecting their status as reasoners with practical experience that may differ from ours. As Gabrielle Taylor puts it, the person of integrity must “give some recognition to others as persons who have views and interests” and justifications of their own, and so she “cannot be a moral solipsist.”<sup>87</sup> Thus this social extension of the Articulation condition for unity-2 begins to support the Ethical thesis (G) as well.

### 3 From Unity-2 (Caring) to Unity-3 (Wholeheartedness)

#### 3.1 Wholehearted Caring versus Essential and Instrumental Conflict

We can now define unity-2 more precisely as the *volitional continuity* that emerges from the self-sustaining diachronic form of the cares through which our practical identity governs more short-term plans, courses of action, and emotional responses to circumstances affecting what we care about, thereby giving these stronger teleological connections than are found in wanton courses of action. A person who forms lasting cares (including “ground projects” for which she would be willing to die) brings dramatic unity to many aspects of her life and finds deeply rooted meaning in the activities involved in each identity-defining commitment: each is a ‘subplot’ within her life that she pursues in an especially active way (rather than wantonly following along). Thus she has a type of narrative continuity that is lacking in wantons and in the simpler kinds of aesthetes. Imagine a rich Don Juan who pursues one-night stands without any reflection on whether this pursuit has any lasting meaning, or an artist who enjoys her natural talent for piano and lives off this skill without any devotion to artistic excellence for its own sake. Their lives may appear outwardly simpler than the lives of agents who risk diffi culties for their cares, but that is because the most important level of practical identity is empty in them, because they do not strive for anything noble. Agents with volitional character for which they are deeply responsible manifest autonomy over time along each of the parallel lines of care that play central roles in their lives because these cares depend on commitments that the agent strives to sustain on evaluative grounds that meet at least the minimal source-conditions for autonomous agency. To the extent that the agent’s eff ort to care succeeds, we can think of each of these lines as exhibiting unity-2: each care is a line of thematic unity of a special kind that is 

*[p. 111]* ^pg130

lacking in some personal narravives with unity-1. Kierkegaard’s imagined Don Juan, for example, has a story, but it is all surface-play.

We now need to consider the relations between the agent’s central cares. The kind of commitment to ends beyond oneself that requires serious devotion opens up the possibility of at least two new sorts of tension, which also add ‘drama’ to such lives. Caring agents face *instrumental* conflicts between cares that cannot be simultaneously pursued, or that compete for scarce resources (either external or psychological), given their particular circumstances or because of the general facts of human life. These conflicts require what I will call “pragmatic” resolution by trying to prioritize, to find ways to pursue one’s projects together, to discern which is more likely to succeed, etc. The best we can do is to balance them in phronetic judgment. Lippitt sometimes seems to imply that narrativists owe us a developed account of how diversity of interests and values are balanced with the need for focus, given the limits of our time and mental energies. However, this familiar “problem of pluralism” among values that are worth caring about is one that faces every theorist who tries to address practical normativity or “ethics” in the broad sense: it is a first-order ethical question.<sup>88</sup> Second, a person’s cares may conflict *essentially* if they are based on inconsistent strong evaluations of her goals and pursuits. By expressing opposed values, each care directly undermines the other: even given ideal external circumstances for pursuing both, the internal conflict will undermine her dedication to either of them. Gabrielle Taylor describes such cases in which “identifications .  .  . are mutually stultifying” as forms of “corruption” that make integrity impossible.<sup>89</sup> This is correct except when an essential conflict occurs unwittingly and the values grounding each care are ones whose conflict is hard to anticipate in advance. Sometimes it is only through caring that we can understand well enough the values involved in what we care about to recognize subtle ways in which they are opposed. For example, a committed political libertarian might not be reckless or dishonest in failing to see beforehand that her political doctrine is incompatible with the moral conditions implicit in a free agent’s control over her life that explain why voluntary slavery or selling one’s life for money are immoral. But she would lose her integrity if, on recognizing the conflict, she did not either give up her libertarianism *or* her commitment to laws upholding the inalienability of certain basic rights (though on the former option, one’s integrity would also be sellable, which is also contradictory). This illustrates how incompatibilities between cares, which are often described in synchronic form, can also be felt as diachronic problems in our ability to continue caring about one goal, person, or ideal versus others.

Although he did not clearly distinguish these instrumental and essential conflicts between cares, Frankfurt saw that a person’s higher-order volitions, or the cares in which they are embedded, can be incompatible: one’s higher-order will itself is “ambivalent” when one identifies with inconsistent first-order desires A1 and B1.<sup>90</sup> It is crucial to Frankfurt’s analysis 

*[p. 112]* ^pg131

that a person in this state is more autonomous than a wanton in acting on either A1 or B1 (though this point is forgotten in Frankfurt’s later work<sup>91</sup>). Likewise, in his accounts of “spirit” and “sin,” Kierkegaard recognized that someone can make volitional commitments or strive in the way that tends to illuminate the relevance of ethical ideals for character, yet remain divided in his will. I have argued such “halfheartedness” explains all of the following types in Kierkegaard’s psychological portraits: “heroic aesthetes” who are outwardly devoted to some great work or excellence in a practice; agents who only partially repent of some error; agents who will to remain alienated from some of their operative motives or who press ahead with evidently inconsistent cares; and those demonically opposed to the good in general.<sup>92</sup> All these types have volitionally deep forms of narrative continuity that are lacking in simple aesthetes. For example, Haufniensis writes that the demonic agent appears to have “an extraordinary continuity” when compared to the “vapid, enervating dissolution” of the lowest aesthete who is “continually absorbed in the impression” of the present moment. Yet their continuity is still subject to sudden reversals because they lack wholehearted coherence among their cares (CA 129–131).

Once again, however, Frankfurt’s definition needs some nuancing. To count as globally wholehearted, a person must avoid essential conflicts between higher-order volitions; and locally, any particular care is wholehearted only if it is not in essential conflict with any other of the agent’s cares. According to these negative conditions, if two cares conflict, both are halfhearted; whereas a third care might conflict with none of the others and thus count as wholehearted, though the agent would not be globally wholehearted with respect to all his cares. Wholehearted caring also meets a positive condition: the agent takes suffi cient account of the values worth caring about in her circumstances, and devotes serious attention to *each* of her cares, consistent with her whole practical identity. In other words, each is given its due in relation to the whole set, which in turn reflects her recognition of a plurality of values. This positive condition is thus more holistic, and it extends the requirement of volitional eff ort in revision (a) and the evaluative requirement (d) in the rational striving conception of autonomy. Without this condition, an agent could count as wholehearted by lacking cares; or he could get wholeheartedness ‘on the cheap’ by only forming *one* care—whether from monomania, lack of imagination, or commitment only to a highly abstract principle. This is the weak sense in which Blustein suggests that a utilitarian might have integrity; for “those who have integrity do not necessarily lead rich or well-rounded lives.”<sup>93</sup>

The first clause in my positive condition is not meant to require perfect well-roundedness, nor any requirement of maximal caring for as many values as we can recognize. It is consistent with a range of conceptions of adequate response to the diversity of values in one’s circumstances, including minimal conceptions of a *threshold of attentiveness* below which it might be thought that an agent is too axiologically blind to be able to satisfy conditions (i–iii) 

*[p. 113]* ^pg132

in the striving model of caring. For example, imagine a retired person who spent most of her time volunteering at a local aviary, but who would only tend *two* of the many species of birds living there; without further explanation, there appears to be a bizarre arbitrariness in this sort of restricted attention. Suffi cient attentiveness to one’s circumstances may also be compatible with devoting active eff ort to (and thus caring about) only a few of the potentially worthy objects of care, as long as one is not wholly insensitive to most other instances of the types of value they exhibit and so unconscious of the opportunities foregone. The freedom of authentic caring requires some awareness of other possible cares beyond one’s actual ones. Still, Blustein is surely right that, “\[a\]s a rule, the more one cares deeply about, the more diffi cult is the task of integrating one’s commitments and maintaining their integration over time,” and sometimes the best solution to pragmatic conflict is “simplification.” The good of wholeheartedness encourages that when necessary, but it should not be assumed always to favor greater simplicity. As Blustein says, even devotion to a small number of cares may involve an “extremely complicated” set of activities and relationships.<sup>94</sup>

Understood this way, your wholehearted caring for a project or person does not require that they be your highest priority, or that any of your cares constitute “ground projects.” Our wholehearted cares *may* be rank-ordered, and can be more or less central to our lives. The great importance of prioritizing them and finding ways to reduce pragmatic/circumstantial conflicts in our joint pursuit of them all comes from our unambiguous devotion to *each* of them. We are then fully autonomous in acting on any of our wholehearted cares, even if we do not succeed in striking a good pragmatic balance between them, as long as we are earnestly seeking such a balance. Thus the synchronic dimension of essential coherence among our identity-constituting cares is compatible with a certain amount of diachronic disunity in our instrumental planning and follow-through. There are also other kinds of narrative tensions (in mood, pace, motifs, types of social interactions) between the plot-lines of distinct cares that are neither essentially contradictory nor in pragmatic conflict. These types of tension are then quite compatible with wholeheartedness, and they may involve interesting contrasts that add variation, aesthetic value, and richness of life-meaning.

It would be circular to argue that teleologically distinct cares that constitute a deep practical identity cannot be understood in isolation from each other because they arise and have their meaning within a *single narravive*. But we have two independent phenomenological grounds for this claim. First, Kupperman’s analysis of character pointed to evidence that the *ways* that ends involved in one care are conceived and pursued affect the specific forms taken by the agent’s other cares: we see adverbial patterns across distinct teleological care-lines (see Chapter 1 §5). Similarly, Blustein argues that essentially particularistic love is possible without being arbitrary (or bestowing all of the beloved’s unique value) because the object of such love is a “whole person,” which is not a mereological sum of properties and 

*[p. 114]* ^pg133

“valuable qualities.” A person can be a nonfungible object of particularistic love because of the unique gestalt formed by the way she exhibits her features: “I love my wife for her high-spiritedness, charm, consideration, and her other qualities” in the distinctive manner that she exhibits them all together, “colored by her particular existence.”<sup>95</sup> Following Neera Badhwar, Blustein refers to this as a “stylization” of his wife’s qualities: “what makes these qualities uniquely hers is the very personal style of their expression.”<sup>96</sup> And this “how” or adverbial mode is essentially diachronic; it does not exist in an instant: “I cherish a particular configuration or gestalt of instantiated qualities as these are manifested in the loved one’s life over an extended period of time.” Moreover, this style is somewhat “open-ended,” not fixed but continuable in different ways.<sup>97</sup> In other words, it is a narravive unity, a single gestalt but still unfinished and developing.

On this view, the way we live out a particular care is part of a personal style that affects how we express our other cares or embody our commitments. Even our mode of action, temperament, and emotional tendencies are part of this narrative whole—and often we love people partly or largely for their personal combination of cares.<sup>98</sup> Person A might have the same three main cares, abstractly described, as person B; yet how they combine in A’s caring actions and feelings might be quite different than how they combine and are manifested in B’s (possibly making one more loveable to us than the other). Wholeheartedness is thus also important because cares inevitably stand in these narravive relationships; in a wholehearted person, they fit or gel together well. Wholeheartedness in our higher-order volitions is thus not simply a matter of existential coherence among our cares; it is a further level of unity in theme and ethos that I distinguish with the label unity-3: it obtains *across* the lines of our cares, even if the ‘plots’ of intentional action involved in pursuing each do not causally connect in direct ways.

Kierkegaard holds that the conditions of earnest willing point towards this kind of strong unity as part of the telos of selfhood. Frankfurt’s thesis is even stronger, as he holds that the will to wholeheartedness is implicit in caring about anything and thus it is *psychologically* impossible to alienate it.<sup>99</sup> In *The Concept of Anxiety*, Kierkegaard suggests that forms of perversity in which halfheartedness is intended are psychologically possible (CA 113–131), though they conflict with the reasons we have in virtue of our spiritual nature to become wholehearted, or clearly defined as selves, from which we cannot entirely close ourselves off while we live (CA 123). Thus for Kierkegaard, caring *rationally* commits us to pursue wholeheartedness, though some fail to pursue it due to weakness in their higher-order will.

#### 3.2 Wholeheartedness as a Regulative Good: Replies to Christman and Lippitt

Against an earlier version of wholeheartedness as part of our existential telos, Lippitt notes John Christman’s argument that an agent’s life can be 

*[p. 115]* ^pg134

suffi ciently meaningful to her in virtue of *separate* projects with no intrinsic relation to one another, forming no overall “grand scheme.”<sup>100</sup> We already considered a wider version of this objection in defining the unity-1 of a personal narravive (Chapter 2 §4), but we are now considering its narrower form with respect to cares and conditions of agent-autonomy. It should be clear from the first clause of its positive condition that the modified conception of wholeheartedness does not require a single “grand” purpose, if that refers to some substantive (first-order) end; in fact, only in rare cases would complete devotion of all one’s time to a single material goal be suffi ciently responsive to values worth caring about, as required for authentic caring. Instead of teleological unification under a single finite goal, wholeheartedness is normally realized in the practical harmony between the main themes of our life established by our primary cares. This point is aptly made in both of Rudd’s responses to this objection:

For most people, what unifies their life is not one project among others (e.g. a successful career) which is given overriding importance, but rather an attempt to lead a fulfilling, satisfying life. This is characteristically achieved in and through all the particular projects I pursue, not as one more particular project on the same level as them.<sup>101</sup>

This is exactly right: whether we conceive our telos as a flourishing life or as a meaningful life, wholeheartedness is a higher-order end regulating other projects and commitments, not to be confused with the goals constitutive of practices or other finite goods at which first-order cares appropriately aim. Call this (H) the *Regulative* thesis about unity-3, which we will also see in Kierkegaard’s famous discussion of “Purity of Heart.” In his second paper, Rudd develops this idea of harmony between first-order cares:

.  .  . in aiming, as I do, at various distinct goals, I have to take into account that they are all things that *I* am trying to achieve, and that how I set about one project will inevitably be affected by how I set about others. A typical life-narrative will not be the story of the pursuit of one single goal, but the story of how the protagonist attempts (successfully or not) to coordinate his/her different projects and goals with one another.<sup>102</sup>

Indeed it would be a pragmatic contradiction to care about different ends and relationships and yet *fail* to care about their practical interaction in my life. As noted above, caring about existential coherence is necessarily involved in my expressive devotion to persons or to first-order goals that I apprehend as worth caring about.

The strong evaluative account of caring makes this especially clear with regard to essential conflicts in valuation. Because our distinct projects involve commitments to ends and relationships made in light of putatively 

*[p. 116]* ^pg135

objective values, a person cannot just turn them off and on in different domains of her life without autonomy-undermining self-manipulation. In this sense, autonomous projects are not rationally separable. In fact, Christman’s own source-condition for personal autonomy leads to the same conclusion. For he holds that an agent’s projects and motives are autonomous only if “hypothetically, the person could reflect upon them without repudiation in light of how they came about.”<sup>103</sup> In other words, we would endorse, or at least not reject, the motives and values on which our various life-projects are based, even if we knew their causal origins in the history of our psyche. This test is meant to rule out most motives and beliefs resulting from manipulation or covert coercion, which we would presumably reject if we knew their true causes. But if this subjunctive endorsement (or nonrejection) involves objective value-judgments, it requires *consistency* to be even minimally rational. And Christman has rightly been persuaded by authors such as Marilyn Friedman and Diana Meyers that in addition to endorsement or nonalienation, autonomy requires various kinds of competence, including rationality:

An autonomous agent must be at least minimally rational in the sense of having a belief and desire set that does not contain manifest inconsistencies. Manifest inconsistencies are those that would involve manifest conflicts among beliefs (or values, etc.) if brought to consciousness. A related requirement is that the agent not suffer from self-deception: The autonomous person cannot be under the influence of desires or motives that are part of mere “cover stories” for other, incompatible and more deeply held, desires and motives.<sup>104</sup>

In other words, if I am devoted to two different projects *P*1 and *P*2, and they are based on strong evaluations *Ev*1 and *Ev*2, then *Ev*1 and *Ev*2 must at least be consistent if I am to count as fully autonomous when I act on either project, according to Christman. For if these strong evaluations conflict in any easily recognized way, then *P*1 and *P*2 are the result of incompetent, negligent, or self-deceptive agency. Some problems remain with Christman’s criteria for autonomy; for example, in this form his source-condition does not rule out being tricked or brainwashed into endorsing (or not rejecting) the dubious genesis of my desires and values. But if this is solved by adopting an objectivist conception of cares that rules out such higher-order manipulation, his account will agree with a Kierkegaardian narratival view that the cares, ground projects, or commitments that give the agent a deep practical identity have to *cohere* at least in the sense of being based on mutually consistent strong evaluations.<sup>105</sup> The need to balance pursuit of our different projects and concerns in a single life has the same basis: because *Ev*1 and *Ev*2 fit together as expressions of a coherent evaluative stance, our responses to them in pursuing *P*1 and *P*2 should also fit together instrumentally as best as the contingencies of our life and abilities allow.

*[p. 117]* ^pg136

This analysis shows that on the rational striving conception of formal autonomy, unity-2 is incomplete without unity-3. This largely completes the argument from the conditions introduced to fix the initial hierarchical model of formal autonomy (abbreviated by letters and small roman numerals) to strong narrative unity which we can summarize simply in a series of steps:

*The Deduction of Unity-3*

1\. Formal autonomy involves volitional identification with some first-order motives and alienation of others \[as per the initial hierarchical model\].

2\. The striving involved in identification via higher-order will \[condition (a)\] is normally a type of volitional caring, which is a self-perpetuating process with narrative continuity \[condition (d)\]

3\. Volitional identification involves strong evaluation of first-order motives \[condition (b)\], and so the caring in which volitional identifi - cation occurs also involves strong evaluation \[as spelled out in conditions (i), (ii), and (iii)\].

4\. Caring about first-order ends rationally commits us to caring2 that the putative values to which we respond in our central commitments, relationships, and life-goals are objectively or intersubjectively sound \[from (i), (ii), and (iii)\].

5\. Thus caring1 about different goals and persons rationally commits us to caring2 that the grounds for these cares1 are mutually consistent, or not in essential conflict \[from 2, 3 and 4\].

6\. Thus the formally autonomous agent is rationally committed to caring2 that her cares1 are negatively *wholehearted* \[from 1 and 5\].

To strengthen the result, we could also add steps to show that the striving will involved in caring also requires the positive conditions of wholeheartedness. As noted throughout, this argument only supports the conclusion that the persistence internal to caring (its unity-2) *rationally* commits the agent to care about unity-3 as a regulative good; these levels remain psychologically distinct, because caring agents do not all attain wholeheartedness. This result also supports the Existential Coherence thesis (F), which combines unity-3 and the source-condition (c) for autonomous cares (in either its weaker or libertarian form).

This completed argument allows us to address several of Lippitt’s objections. First, return to objection (3) concerning self-deceptive autobiographical reflections and indulgent self-images. We have seen why authentic caring requires (as a regulative ideal) honesty with ourselves about our commitments, their priority in our life, and their grounds;<sup>106</sup> thus the caring that is psychologically necessary for formal autonomy involves a rational commitment to avoid self-deceptive stories about one’s practical identity. *Akrasia* in understanding our own will, false “good conscience,” and self-indulgent 

*[p. 118]* ^pg137

illusions about our motives and commitments may be hard to avoid in practice—and maybe no one avoids them completely—but they are inconsistent with the cognitive bases necessary for sustainable devotions and cares to which we are rationally committed if we value our autonomy. The would-be autonomous agent who relies on such delusions is thus pragmatically inconsistent, and this also has psychological consequences: her control over her practical identity and sense of life-meaning are destabilized by failure to live up to the procedural norms implicit in the constitutive structure of caring. Even if luck and ongoing self-deceptive eff ort allow her to continue this way, her self-conception is liable to collapse rapidly under any significant pressure from external reality, as Flaubert’s character “Madame Bovary” so well illustrates.

A real example that illustrates the need for one’s cares to cohere with one’s value-judgments and connected knowledge is provided by Pawel and his wife Ola, who were ardent neo-Nazis in Poland until they discovered that they both had Jewish family roots (the suspicion of which they had repressed). Their last name was kept secret by journalists for fear that their old friends might attack them. Pawel reported that he could not look in the mirror for a long time on discovering the truth from his parents, but after “weeks of cloistered and tortured reflection” he was “finally overcome by a strong desire to become Jewish . . .”<sup>107</sup> Pawel changed his cares to recognize the value he had discovered in his own history and to make amends for violating it for so long. His case has much wider relevance, for it shows how restoring coherence to a narravive marred by wrongdoing requires a diachronic process that acknowledges and undoes (to the extent possible) the wrongs of one’s past. This is not self-punishment, but rather an extended eff ort that alters the significance of past actions by turning them into grounds for reparative work and witnessing into the future. Thus the narrative conception of existential coherence may even prove instructive for explaining the value of restorative justice in criminal contexts.

Second, Lippitt objects that the concept of “essential” conflict is unclear and insuffi cient for the type of narrative unity that “mature agency” requires.<sup>108</sup> The conflict is clear in such extreme, but (thankfully) rare cases as Pawel’s. A devotion to pedophilia is obviously inconsistent with the cares of a good parent or coach;<sup>109</sup> similarly, the committed Klansman who marries an African-American woman must not only lead a double life but also suffer from an unusual level of cognitive dissonance. Yet Lippitt worries, plausibly enough, that the verdict is not as obvious in more common cases. He off ers an agent who pursues both philosophy and novel-writing while knowing he risks failing at both. On my criteria, this is only a pragmatic conflict; though the stakes are high, an agent who risks pursuing both ventures could be wholehearted and thus fully autonomous in doing so. Failing at both pursuits, either as a result of bad luck or poor instrumental reasoning, need not threaten unity-3, though it would make for a very sad narravive. In some cases, the type of conflict between cares may be harder 

*[p. 119]* ^pg138

to classify, but that simply shows that we need to sharpen the notion of “strong evaluation” to clarify when ethical contradictions occur. But even when the conflict is only pragmatic, as Blustein says, one lacks integrity “in carelessly putting oneself in situations of conflict among one’s most basic commitments” when that could be avoided without giving up either care.<sup>110</sup> In other cases we have to judge the boundary between negligence and legitimate risk of pragmatic conflict. Thus the importance of unity-3 does not imply that we should never risk instrumental diffi culties due to plural cares;<sup>111</sup> the value of wholeheartedness for a meaningful life only implies that we should not take such risks lightly or unnecessarily.

Third, satisfying the negative condition for wholeheartedness (lack of essential conflict in grounding values) will not be enough to yield “a unified life-narrative by default,” as Lippitt fears,<sup>112</sup> for the positive conditions of wholeheartedness must also be satisfied. As we have seen, that does not require that I “bring each and every aspect of my life under one grand narrative”<sup>113</sup> in the sense of seeing each aspect as a means to one substantive end, or even ranking them all under a single priority principle that serves as one’s highest ideal. In response to objection (5), it means caring enough about their pragmatic compatibility and their worth that I am open to reconsidering my current ways of pursuing my cares, to constructive critiques of their perceived grounds, and to discoveries of value that could lead to new cares—given that my caring aims to be an attentive response to value in the natural, social, and spiritual worlds that together form the setting for narravives.

There are also unusual circumstances in which the importance of openness to different values might itself justify tolerating, for a time, conflict among cares that may turn out to be essential conflict. For example, Calhoun notes that “Maria Lugones has repeatedly argued for the value of conceptualizing oneself as a duplicitous or multiplicitous being whose identity is differently constituted in different cultural ‘worlds’ or meaning systems.”<sup>114</sup> For Lugones has good reason to value her Latina identity in resistance to racism; yet she also has good reason to value her lesbian identity, although in “Hispanic culture, lesbianism is an abomination. . . . As a result, ‘Latina lesbian’ is not a coherent identity.” Yet Calhoun thinks the resulting “lack of wholeheartedness” does not undermine Lugones’s autonomy; for “Agents can have reason to resist resolving value conflicts.”<sup>115</sup> I agree with that maxim stated in this abstract form because there are rare situations in which we should maintain conflicting loyalties at least long enough to figure out which underlying set of values has a better claim on us, or whether they can be reconciled. But such reasons can only operate provisionally, postponing the opposing demand for wholeheartedness that is internal to the structure of autonomous caring, while a solution is sought. In other words, volitional ambiguity can only be rational as a temporary compromise while we search for a wholehearted resolution. Surely Lugones, for example, would hope to transform at least her local 

*[p. 120]* ^pg139

Hispanic culture to recognize the legitimacy of a lesbian identity. If instead she sought to increase the conflict and relished acting out different personae in her two “worlds,” we would rightly question her integrity.

To envision the perversity in cavalier cultivation of essential conflict, consider the remarkable advice that C.S. Lewis’s imaginary devil “Screwtape” gives to a junior “tempter” on how to encourage insincerity in a nominally Christian man who is now enjoying the sophistication and worldly superiority of some new atheist friends he has made:

No doubt he must very soon realise that his own faith is in direct opposition to the assumptions on which all the conversation of his new friends is based. I don’t think that matters provided you can persuade him to postpone any open acknowledgment of that fact, and this, with the aid of shame, pride, modesty, and vanity, will be easy to do. As long as the postponement lasts he will be in a false position. He will be silent when he ought to speak and laugh when he ought to be silent. . . . I have known many humans \[to\] live two parallel lives; he will not only appear, but actually be, a different man in each of the circles he frequents. Failing this, there is a subtler and more entertaining method. He can be made to take positive pleasure in the perception that the two sides of his life are inconsistent. . . . You see the idea—the worldly friends touch him on one side and the grocer \[who attends his church\] on the other, and he is the complete, balanced, complex man who sees round them all. Thus, while being permanently treacherous to at least two sets of people, he will feel, instead of shame, a continual undercurrent of self-satisfaction.<sup>116</sup>

Unfortunately, I suspect that most of us easily recognize the dynamic that Lewis here describes; at least for me, his words ring all too true. This kind of ambivalence is surely not what Calhoun is defending, nor what Philip Quinn meant to promote when he praised Isaiah Berlin’s romantic endorsement of “the sense that human values are irreducibly plural, inexhaustible, sometimes incommensurable, and often conflicting” and recommended a narrative unity that retains plural “voices” in our “internal choir.”<sup>117</sup> We ought to be very careful that our appreciation of tension and “being responsive to voices that are not in perfect harmony” does not become merely a way of rationalizing the sort of insincerity that Lewis so damningly describes. Mill’s recommendation to cultivate a variety of interests will not justify a man who has two wives and two sets of children at the same time, while neither family knows about the other (a rare but real occurrence).

Of course, accepting that there is essential inconsistency in our cares can sometimes be extremely diffi cult, given how deeply we have cared about something or someone. Consider the appalling predicament of Cal, the father in a family wracked by the aftermath of a boating accident, in *Ordinary People*.<sup>118</sup> Cal finally realizes that his wife’s egoism is making her 

*[p. 121]* ^pg140

hate their younger son who survived the accident. She wishes her older son had survived instead, and she despises her younger son for his later suicide attempt, which she sees as resentiment-filled manipulation rather than as the desperate expression of self-blame that it was. Given her quasi-Nietzschian psychological convictions, her rejection of her surviving son is unmoveable.<sup>119</sup> So Cal *must choose*: either his wife or his remaining son. Only by breaking with one of the cares that has defined his married life can he return to unity-3. As awful as his recognition of this is, it is also absolutely necessary if he is to remain loyal to the values that gave his life meaning.

In summary, an autonomous agent must strive for wholeheartedness,<sup>120</sup> because existential coherence is part of the loyalty to goods that the very structure of caring involves. A caring agent who lacks this concern would be like the parent who says he loves each of his children but thinks nothing of his family—i.e., he would be guilty of a practical fallacy of division. A rich plurality of interests and well-roundedness is quite different from intentional cultivation of practical incoherence. We can certainly “imagine such an individual” who is not committed to practical unification,<sup>121</sup> but we will have to imagine her as volitionally self-undermining. Golda Meier, we are told, was not able to integrate her marriage with her unceasing devotion to creating and preserving the nation of Israel.<sup>122</sup> As there was no essential conflict here, such failure does not necessarily imply volitional ambiguity or division in her will. However, suppose she had said that she cared deeply for her husband and for her chosen country, but was not bothered that they were pulling her in different directions; we would rightly wonder what this could mean, for it borders on gibberish. Similarly, imagine an Abraham Lincoln who claimed to care both about the moral wrong of slavery and about upholding constitutional order as essential to the rule of law, but who told Thaddeus Stevens that he had no interest in reconciling these ideals in the 13th Amendment. Surely Thad would have rightly doubted that Lincoln *really* cared about both, or either. Thankfully the real Lincoln valued integrity. Thus the regulative concern for coherence is manifested in the importance we give to reducing pragmatic conflict and avoiding essential evaluative conflict altogether.

### 4 The Aesthetic and Ethical Stages Restated

#### 4.1 Kierkegaard’s Ethically Grounded Wholeheartedness

At this point, we can better understand Kierkegaard’s Ethical thesis (G). We have seen that the wholehearted relation between cares has a synchronic aspect, namely the lack of essential conflict between the values to which they respond. It also has a diachronic aspect in the development of our cares to overcome essential conflict when discovered and in adjustments to make them mutually pursuable in the long run. But an ability to 

*[p. 122]* ^pg141

recognize logical inconsistency between values, to avoid it and judge how to fix it when it arises, becomes reliable only when we can draw on a more embracing conception in which important relations between different kinds of value are grasped, rather than looking at each piecemeal. Such an evaluative framework enables a larger view, juxtaposing our extant cares with associated grounds for other possible cares, and guiding us towards bases for a set of cares that are not only logically consistent but also *mutually reinforcing* as parts of a single narravive (as suggested at the end of §2.2 above). For example, I may learn to love two friends not just for their individual sakes but also for their love of each other as well. Or I might adapt my interest in photography to help my child who is studying filmmaking. Or I might leave a company whose owner is funding right-wing think tanks that spread lies about our tax code and try to deceive the public about the causes of our national debt.

Now, to care2 about an encompassing evaluative view that makes wholehearted caring1 possible is virtually tantamount to caring2 about “the ethical.” To get to thesis (G), we only need to add that evaluative coherence cannot be reached without some higher-order norms that regulate our more particular values, or that obligate us in the moral sense. Then the recognition of ethical norms will be tied logically and dynamically to caring wholeheartedly about other nonmoral ends and ideals. For example, to the extent that cooperation with others is required by our cares, ethical virtues will also be required, as MacIntyre has argued. But even if we abstract from the social relationships involved in our cares, our loyalty to important values expressed in them cannot be stable without a deeper loyalty to ethical principles of consistency, honesty, and respect for persons as beings capable of caring. Agents who begin to form autonomous cares find that attentiveness to the values grounding these cares makes salient the deeper ethical considerations relevant to critically assessing nonmoral values and ordering cares together. This is the kernel of truth in the old saying that loving something or someone becomes a spiritual education.

Of course a full argument for this step would require a detailed deduction of ethical norms along the lines suggested by MacIntyre, Korsgaard, or Charles Taylor’s argument for the indispensability of a strong evaluative framework ultimately bound together by “hyper-goods.”<sup>123</sup> I am not suggesting that our operative evaluative framework has to be “comprehensive” or all-inclusive in order to facilitate the development of mutually reinforcing cares; people can also be wholehearted without working out an explicit ethical *theory*. My claim is simply that Kierkegaard is clearly allied with MacIntyre, Korsgaard, and Taylor against Bernard Williams in thinking that implicit rational commitment to ethical values is *eo ipso* involved in nonmoral caring, whether or not we recognize it or like it. This explains his idea that a person who commits to some significant and specific end with real pathos will discover his error if his choice is ethically mistaken (EO II 167). Values worth caring about are fully intelligible only in terms of a 

*[p. 123]* ^pg142

larger ethical framework, which is why an agent who cares but who denies the application of ethical obligations to her practical identity is missing latent implications of her own commitments.<sup>124</sup>

The arguments I have sketched for theses (F), (G), and (H) as extensions of the narravive realist conception of practical identity are important in their own right, for the objections they answer are primarily concerned with the plausibility of narrative identity theories in general. However, these objections were mainly formulated against narrative conceptions drawn from Kierkegaard’s distinction between the aesthetic and ethical “spheres” of existence, to which I now return. The defense of narrative and Frankfurtian explanations of the aesthetic–ethical distinction through the accounts of unity-1, unity-2, and unity-3 have strengthened the case that Kierkegaard’s texts support the conception comprised in theses (A)–(H) and that they offer vital insights in favor of such a strengthened narrative realism. To show this, I now draw together the suggestions made at earlier points concerning different types of aesthetes who are more than mere “wantons” but who nevertheless fail to attain the higher levels of narrative unity.

A complex answer has emerged to Lippitt’s argument that Kierkegaard does not mean to explain the superiority of the ethical in terms of narrative “unity or coherence *per se*”—which was included as (7) in the list of main objections to Kierkegaardian narrativism (Chapter 2 §1). Lippitt is correct that unity-1 does not distinguish ethical agents, but most of Kierkegaard’s aesthetes lack unity-2. He is also right that Kierkegaard off ers a spectrum of different aesthetic types. The Judge’s interlocutor *A* is more sophisticated than other aesthetes who simply aim to enjoy life or to achieve worldly success through use of a talent. But the Judge counters that when *A* goes beyond the simple aesthetes who pursue pleasure, honor, wealth, and status by seeing the “vanity” of their goals (EO II 194), this only makes *A* more *clearly* aware that he lacks any significant or lasting purpose that could give meaning to his life: *A* is in “thought-despair” (EO II 195). This agrees with what we find in his writings; *A* sees all particular human relationships and tasks as pointless busyness (EO I 34). Even though he cannot entirely avoid social interactions, he does not identify with them, and he tries to immerse himself in the moment (EO II 201). He does not sincerely will an ideal for his character, and so the Judge tells him, “the coherence in your view is broken” (EO II 202). This is why I wrote that aesthetes like *A* who are “awakened” to the existential need for a deeper identity do will something: if they do not form specific commitments to concrete roles in social life, they subsist in a shadowy, negative determination not to form any positive cares1 concerning any role, end, or task in the finite world.<sup>125</sup> *A* is really in a kind of ‘holding pattern’: his apparent commitment to an aesthetic project keeps him in the air so he can avoid landing in any determinate cares, and thereby avoid making the primordial choice to give ethical ideals a grip on his identity. As I explained in §2.2 above, *A’s* project is not *governed* by any values outside his psyche, 

*[p. 124]* ^pg143

and so it involves no earnest care. Thus the Judge denies that ‘aesthetic earnestness’ is really possible. When such an individual

.  .  . sets a task for his life, it is really the task of becoming absorbed in his own accidental traits, of becoming an individual whose equal in paradoxicality and irregularity has never been seen, of becoming a caricature of a human being. The reason we rarely meet such characters in life is that we rarely meet people who have a notion of what it is to live (EO II 261).

In other words, *A* is above the unawakened aesthetes because he *does* have some notion of what robust practical identity requires, but he is trying to avoid it by creating a self-deceptive persona designed to fascinate himself and to distract him from boredom. “You are sentimental, heartless, all according to the circumstances; but during all this, you are at all times only in the moment, and for that reason your life disintegrates, and it is impossible for you to explain it” (EO II 179). For honest recognition of how his psychological stratagem works would ruin it. The same applies to Johannes the Seducer’s “pact with the aesthetic,” which is an abstract project of cultivating “the interesting” in all things (EO I 437–438). This means focusing on their oddity, originality, difference from the norm, comic aspects, dramatic tension, and so on rather than values for which they are ordinarily pursued, e.g. any contribution they make to individual or social well-being. Perhaps the Judge’s critique seems unfair, for both *A* and the Seducer have their stories about what they are up to, and they spend lots of time in autobiographical reflection. But the Judge means that they fail the stronger Articulation condition for formal autonomy: they cannot sincerely explain their acts as expressions of a conviction that anything is objectively worth caring about.

But there is another type of aesthete in between these sophisticated self-deceivers and the simplest sort of unreflective hedonist caricatured in “Don Giovanni.” As Lippitt says, the Judge acknowledges that aesthetes who pursue “wealth, glory, nobility, and the development of a special talent” do find a certain coherence in their temporal goal.<sup>126</sup> Interestingly, then, these simpler, less bohemian aesthetes seem to have a better claim to narrative unity than dandies like *A* and the Seducer. Yet the modified hierarchical account of unity-2 outlined above highlights an ambiguity in this group. An aesthete who simply follows a passion to employ a talent because this is what she most enjoys does not earnestly care about objective goods that might be produced by excellent development of her talent. She has no more higher-order volition than a shopkeeper who passively follows his desire for gain, but who would abandon his shop immediately if a stronger desire took hold of him. “Claudine,” as described above, might be similar: she appears to have a project, but its basis is nothing more than desire or fancies of her imagination; she appears passionate, but it is all affect without formation by striving will.

*[p. 125]* ^pg144

On the other hand, some agents may dedicate themselves earnestly to the development of a talent for the sake of nonethical goods (say some kind of excellence in an art or science). Let us grant that this kind of agent *cares* about particular ends and the roles involved in pursuing them, and maintains his commitment through higher-order volitions responding to values taken as objectively worthy of attention. This is what I meant by a “heroic” aesthete who ignores morality or denies that it governs his ground projects but who earnestly identifies with some kind of “greatness” or nobility. Unlike *A* and the Seducer, such heroic aesthetes do not evade positive higher-order volition by self-deceptive sentimentalism. Because they exhibit unity-2, they actually pose a starker challenge to the simple formulations of the aesthetic–ethical distinction offered by Rudd and I in earlier works rebutting MacIntyre’s critique of Kierkegaard.

Here the answer depends on the importance of unity-3: according to Kierkegaard, such a heroic aesthete cannot be entirely wholehearted. At first, his higher-order volitions may seem not to conflict instrumentally or essentially; we can suppose for the sake of argument that he identifies with a single kind of excellence and the motives involved in developing one rare talent. But he has to work with others in this capacity, and organize other aspects of his life around his goal; fidelity to its value has to be an end-initself for him. Thus he must also care about what happens in the lives of others with whom he interacts, on whom he depends in seeking excellence. In diachronic perspective, he must consider what long-term pursuit of his practice will mean for the shape of his life as a whole, and in particular whether it will allow him to maintain his fidelity. In that perspective, our heroic aesthete has to consider what happens if (a) aspects of his project start to clash, or (b) they break down and cannot be pursued further, or (c) he successfully completes them—and what then? By hypothesis, he lacks an ethical framework through which his cares can be assessed and improved, or new cares can be articulated that count as continuations of the old ones, faithful to what was right in them, loyal to the same ultimate values. For he is not committed to any higher-order norms or ideals to which he can remain devoted through such changes. Thus even if he has other ways of modifying his cares, as regards responding to values worth caring about, he will almost be starting from scratch. Such an aesthete can thus manifest unity-2 for protracted periods in his life, but cannot become wholehearted while he refuses to recognize the unifying ethical frame of reference within which the values he serves are situated and rooted. Hence the ethical stage is distinguished from *all* subtypes of the aesthetic only by unity-3 in Kierkegaard’s analysis.

However, Lippitt objects to the idea that ethical agency requires considering the unity of one’s life as a whole. He is correct that it is unusual even for adults past adolescence to consider directly the meaningfulness of our “whole life;” but he adds that even then, the ability to make sense of our issues in a unified story is not the main problem:

*[p. 126]* ^pg145

But even in such moments—which are relatively rare—intelligibility is not the issue. When my wife has left me, my teenage daughter has told me that she’ll never speak to me again, and I have lost my job, all in the same week, I might well face despair in a more everyday sense than Kierkegaard’s. But even in such circumstances, I would be able to offer a perfectly intelligible narrative about why I married this woman; why I intervened to try and discourage my daughter from dating that Neanderthal suspected drug-pusher; why I took that job despite my knowledge of its insecurity. Intelligibility is not the problem. . . . \[So\] the concept of intelligibility will certainly not enable us to distinguish aesthetes from ethicists.<sup>127</sup>

Here the full importance of the level-distinctions is apparent. Lippitt is right that the intelligibility of *one familiar, basic kind* is not the issue in the poignant case he describes: for unity-1 need not be lost in such a series of unfortunate events. However, Lippitt’s protagonist needs a richer sort of intelligibility than would suffi ce for planning agency alone. Suppose he can articulate (to a reasonable extent) why he loves his wife and daughter, and why he had to oppose some of their decisions for their own good. He then has to ask whether his reasons fit together as practical considerations, to seek the best way to remain loyal to what was right in these cares, and to respond to whatever new values have been disclosed—in other words, how to go on in a way that develops earnestly from the cares that have defined him. Thus, Lippitt’s protagonist clearly sees his predicament *in ethical terms*, precisely as (G) predicts. While the basic intelligibility resulting from unity-1 is not his problem, ethical unity-3 certainly is.

By contrast, in this circumstance, simple aesthetes or care-evasive sophisticates would not even muster unity-2. If they experienced such misfortunes and did not despair of aestheticism, they would simply drop their old ties without regret, or make light of the situation, or regard the significant others in the story as fungible and seek quick replacements, or even perversely romanticize the rejections and focus on the aesthetic aspects of their situation (e.g. finding the interesting in a wounded ego). Most people would see such cavalier reactions as suspect because they lack ethical depth. They do not express ongoing commitments of deep significance to the protagonist; if he had such commitments, he would be profoundly affected by the losses. In a word, such aesthetic reactions are *careless*: someone who drops an alleged love-relation without the slightest emotional response surely had no identity-defining love at all. What he pretended was love was really no more than a passive desire without volitional devotion.

Instead, in the situation Lippitt describes, experiencing earnest grief and regret, and needing time to repair one’s narravive with new goals might look like disunity on the surface but would actually reflect the agent’s integrity. He would manifest retained concern for the values expressed by his earlier cares, and continued recognition of their significance within a 

*[p. 127]* ^pg146

broader matrix of values. It is only because the agent has unity-2 along different lines of cares that he can experience earnest distress when it becomes impossible to pursue them together, even if the conflict is pragmatic rather than essential. The fact that these are the cares of a single agent who wills to remain loyal to the same underlying values over time makes unity-3 an overriding issue. And this is exactly the emotional response that is implied in Lippitt’s description: true to the Judge’s prediction about volitional caring, it is clear that Lippitt’s protagonist experiences these problems as serious because of their importance to the well-being of others, and as existentially central to his life. Thus Lippitt’s description actually confirms the importance of unity-3.

#### 4.2 Strawson’s Aestheticism?

At this point, I can also briefly respond to Galen Strawson’s objections to the “ethical Narrativity thesis,” which is the claim that “a richly Narrative outlook is essential to a well-lived life, to true or full personhood.”<sup>128</sup> Obviously theses (F) and (G) together constitute a version of this thesis, because they define formal autonomy in robustly narrative terms. One of Strawson’s suspicions is that a concern about the ethical character of one’s whole life smacks of inflated self-importance, usually connected to religious beliefs about the self.<sup>129</sup> On the contrary, my analysis has disclosed a clear sense in which reflection on our cares, their bases, and their relationship is a precondition of sincere devotion to what *transcends us*—to the alterity of other persons, and to other values outside our psyche. Kierkegaard’s critique of aestheticism shows that sentimentalist forms of reflection-on-self prevent rather than inspire narrative unity-2 and -3. From the fact that there are indulgent forms of autobiographical contemplation it hardly follows that taking stock of the course and direction of one’s life is never needed—any more than the narcissism sometimes involved in an ostentatious hairstyle implies that we should not even wash or comb our hair. Moreover, my arguments for unity-2 and -3 have proceeded entirely within what Kierkegaard calls the ethical sphere without dependence on any religious doctrine or religious self-concern. Narrative conditions of deep identity also have a religious significance for Kierkegaard as we will see, but it builds on narrative unification in identity wrought by the moderate sort of autonomy that human beings are capable of attaining.

Strawson’s main objection is that he can live a decent life without focusing on the question “what has GS made of his life?” And he adds that “what I care about, in so far as I care about myself and my life, is how I am now.”<sup>130</sup> As arguments go, this seems fairly thin, since it is open to the narrativist simply to respond that a life without the unity-2 of cares and thus also without unity-3 is not fully autonomous or meaningful, even if the one living it does no wrong, is quite content, and can even provide a kind of ideology to justify his attitude—after all, Kierkegaard’s *A* does this too. 

*[p. 128]* ^pg147

But it is better to engage Strawson’s claim by noting that, taken literally, his focus on the “now” sounds as if he makes no plans and has no interest in the future of his relationships. This is incompatible with personal love, though it might be compatible with other kinds of classical Epicurean companionship. For personal love involves trajectory-dependent properties, as we learned from Blustein and Karen Jones; to appreciate our beloved only as she is now, at this instant, is not to appreciate her in her full uniqueness and irreplaceability. Strawson insists that “truly happy-go-lucky, see-what-comes-along lives are among the best there are, vivid, blessed, profound,” and insists that such episodics are capable of deep friendships.<sup>131</sup> Yet how would such a character respond when his life is falling apart as in Lippitt’s central example, e.g. when loved ones reject him? Maybe it would be with keen sorrow and sense of loss at the time? Yet if he truly lives only in the moment, either this sorrow would soon pass, or its persistence would seem unintelligible to him. Either way, his reaction would not be that of someone who earnestly loved and lost despite his best eff orts. Perhaps Strawson could respond that an “oh well” attitude open to “whoever-comes-next” would be a happier state and a profounder reflection of the human condition. But then his position would explicitly converge with that of *A* and Johannes, and would face all of the arguments that Kierkegaard’s Judge mounts against their aestheticism.

The basic point that Strawson’s critique of ethical narrativity misses is that ethical norms and ideals get much of their normative force for our lives from their importance in achieving the higher forms of narrative unity involved in formal autonomy. Some ethical norms may apply on a snapshot basis to time-slices, but many lose intelligibility in that perspective. This applies to guilt, which Strawson conflates with its aesthetic simulacrum—a sentimental and “self-indulgent” wallowing in self-hatred, or “narcissistic, masochistic guilt.”<sup>132</sup> But it applies even more profoundly to remorse, which names a moral emotion that *has to be* formally autonomous. The agent must fully identify with moral rejection of her past action and with her desire to make amends to count as remorseful, which distinguishes this state from “regret and contrition and just self-reproach,” which can occur in a more detached, less passionately appropriated way—and that seems to be why Strawson prefers these emotions.<sup>133</sup> There is thus a deep difference between remorse and affective refl exes that make up our most basic reactive attitudes; it is not just based on other people having expectations of me.<sup>134</sup> When Elwin Wilson, an ex-klansman, came to Washington D.C. in 2009 to apologize to Representative John Lewis for hitting him at a civil rights rally in May 1961, he was under no social compulsion to do this. Nor did he do it to get on television; that came unexpectedly when media picked up on this amazing story. He did it to repair, to some extent, the wrong done so long ago; it was a fully autonomous expression of his changed identity. Could he have been moved in the same way to do this without a sense that it those long-past KKK sentiments had been an integral part of *him*, or without any (even inchoate) sense of the 

*[p. 129]* ^pg148

importance of narrative unity in his life? Wouldn’t an episodic without such a narrative concern be likely to regret from a distance but to think it better to leave these old scars unopened? Obviously this does not mean that Wilson had to reflect on his life in explicitly narrative terms, as Strawson often seems to think narrativists contend.

Similarly, to think that “conscience” is fundamentally “directed on the present moment” without any essential concern for the past<sup>135</sup> is to imagine it as a kind of moral speedometer, and to miss that most of the facts it reports about our present state depend for their meaning on their development from the past and likely continuation into the future (more like an instrument that shows acceleration or deceleration rather than only present speed). As Walker argues, careful moral judgment about many issues also requires representing them in story-form: “Many situations cannot be reckoned with responsibly without seeing how people, relations, and even the values and obligations they recognize have gotten there.”<sup>136</sup> If this is plausible, then the moral aspect of personal autonomy—our ability to discern and respond well to moral reasons with which we identity—also a requires a narrative understanding of the individuals and groups that connect with our own narravive.

∞ In conclusion, personal autonomy involves forms of narrative unity described in theses (F), (G) and (H) that build on the basic unity-1 that constitutes a personal identity within a historically unique narravive. As Walker puts it, narrative understanding of our lives is “central to understanding how our responsibilities are kept coherent and sustainable over substantial stretches of lives that .  .  . remain people’s own,” or that exhibit integrity.<sup>137</sup> This idea is illustrated in Kierkegaard’s distinction between aesthetic and ethical agents, which corresponds both to differences in practical narrative unification and a connected difference in the presence or absence of higher-order volitions as part of cares that an agent actively sustains in response to perceived value outside his will. Recognizing the different types of aesthetes in Kierkegaard’s writings complicates earlier accounts of the aesthetic–ethical distinction, but shows that they were on the right track. On the enriched narrative model, aesthetes of every type—from the most unreflective wantons to sophisticated self-deceivers and even heroic pursuers of greatness without ethical concerns—lack “wholeheartedness” properly understood as devotion of one’s full energies to one’s cares without essential inconsistency in the values grounding them. Lippitt’s understandable worries about self-deceptive autobiographies, monomania, fanaticism, and the need for openness to novel perspectives in light of which one might change ground projects, can all be answered in a narrative account of practical identity by the importance of values as bases for commitments that, unlike mere sentimentalism, reach beyond pleasing self-images or the familiar and comfortable truisms of a given culture toward ‘what really matters most.’ What 

*[p. 130]* ^pg149

that is, of course, is something we cannot expect to derive primarily from a narrative theory of autonomous agency: we depend on human experience to reveal value, and we rely on there not being any absolute contradiction in the practical realm.

The connection Kierkegaard draws between earnest purpose in life and “appropriation” of an ethical framework to govern our cares will remain controversial, but it should be taken as seriously as Charles Taylor’s similar claims that authentic identity is defined against the background of horizons of shared value-contrasts transcending measures of desire-satisfaction. As I have argued, it is also supported by MacIntyre’s argument from the need for practices and traditions to the objective importance of certain virtues.<sup>138</sup> A response to the heroic aesthete can draw on Korsgaard’s argument from coherent practical identities to the moral law. These arguments for an implicit commitment to ethical norms are strengthened by the richer realist narrative account of caring and autonomy developed from Kierkegaard.
