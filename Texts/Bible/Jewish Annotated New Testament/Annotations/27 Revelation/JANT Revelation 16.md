### 16.1–21: Seven bowls

*[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-1|Text]], p. 564* · Since most temple rituals in antiquity involved the transfer and pouring of sacred substances, bowls were essential implements in ancient religion, including at the Jerusalem Temple (Josephus, *Ant.* 12.40,82; *Ep. Arist.* 33; 42; 79; *L. A. E.* 33.4) and, by extension, imagined in the heavenly Temple cult. ^16-1

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-1|1]]:** *Wrath of God*, comparable to the enumerations of the plagues in Ex 7–12 and subsequent aggadic versions (as in the Haggadah that Jews read at Passover), the ensuing details offer readers both joy in the downfall of the unrighteous and warning to the righteous to endure. These plagues are loosely based on those in Exodus (see [[Psalms 105#^105-28|Ps 105.28–36]]; [[Ezekiel 38#^38-19|Ezek 38.19–22]]). ^16-1-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-2|2]]:** *Sore*, see [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^9-8|Ex 9.8–12]]. *Mark*, see 13.16–17. ^16-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-3|3–4]]:** *Blood*, see 8.8–9; [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^7-14|Ex 7.14–25]]. ^16-3

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-5|5]]:** *Angel of the waters*, see 7.1–2; 14.18 for an angel in control of fire. In *1 En*. 60.10–20, various angels have charge of natural phenomena. ^16-5

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-6|6]]:** *Blood to drink*, [[Ezekiel 39#^39-19|Ezek 39.19]]; cf. [[Revelation 17#^17-6|Rev 17.6]]. The legend that Israel had slaughtered each prophet sent by God, and thus also Jesus, had much currency among Jewish Jesus-believers who viewed Jesus as an exalted prophet (see [[II Chronicles 24#^24-18|2 Chr 24.18–21]]; [[Matthew 23#^23-29|Mt 23.29–37]]; [[Luke 11#^11-47|Lk 11.47–51]]; *Jub.* 1.12; *T*. *Levi* 16.2; cf. [[Matthew 27#^27-25|Mt 27.25]]). ^16-6

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-7|7]]:** As in 9.13, the *altar* (lit., “one from the altar”) blesses God. In the Qumran Sabbath Hymns, every part of the furniture of the heavenly Temple breaks into songs of praise to God (4QShirShabbf). ^16-7

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-9|9]]:** As with all the plagues, the suffering multitudes do not praise but curse the God of judgment, a contrast to the repeated praise scenes in Revelation (see 9.20–21; 16.11,21). ^16-9

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-10|10]]:** *Throne of the beast*, 13.2. *Darkness*, see 8.12; [[Torah 15 - Bo#^10-21|Ex 10.21]]. ^16-10

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-12|12]]:** Drying of the *Euphrates* reverses the Red Sea crossing in [[Torah 16 - Beshalach#^14-21|Ex 14.21–15.19]] by allowing the invasion of *kings from the east*, as many end-time scenarios imagined (*1 En*. 56; *Sib. Or*. 4.137–39; *6 Ezra* 15.28–45). ^16-12

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-13|13]]:** If inspired by [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^8-1|Ex 8.1–15]], John reconceptualizes *frogs* as the form taken by the demonic spirits of false prophecy (hence false miracle-working, v 14). *Dragon*, 12.3. The *false prophet* was a common concern in the early Jesus movement: see [[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^18-20|Deut 18.20–22]]; [[Mark 13#^13-22|Mk 13.22]]; [[2 Corinthians 11#^11-13|2 Cor 11.13–15]]; [[1 John 04#^4-1|1 Jn 4.1–3]]. Early rabbis, following [[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^18-21|Deut 18.21–22]], held that the false prophet is one who prophesies what he has not heard (*m. Sanh*. 11.5). ^16-13

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-14|14]]:** *Signs*, see 13.3. ^16-14

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-15|15]]:** *Like a thief*, a prophetic oracle, in the voice of the risen Christ (see 3.30; [[Matthew 24#^24-42|Mt 24.42–43]]), displays true prophecy (see [[Mark 13#^13-11|Mk 13.11b]]). Abruptly interrupting the narrative, the oracle may be a later addition, like those in ch 22. ^16-15

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-16|16]]:** *Harmagedon*, probably biblical Megiddo, an ancient city mentioned in the Hebrew Bible (e.g., [[Judges 01#^1-27|Judg 1.27]]; [[Judges 05#^5-19|5.19]]), but never as an eschatological battlefield. The mountain (Heb “har”) as site of eschatological war is found in [[Ezekiel 38#^38-8|Ezek 38.8]]; [[Ezekiel 39#^39-2|39.2]],[[Ezekiel 39#^39-4|4]] and *4 Ezra* 13.34–35. ^16-16

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-19|19]]:** *Great city*, Rome, here called *Babylon*, anticipating its appearance in the form of a great whore in ch 17 (cf. 14.8). Jews identified Rome as a new Babylon because of its threat to and ultimately demolition of the Jerusalem Temple (e.g., *4 Ezra* 3.2; *2 Bar.* 6.1; 8.3–5; 11). This symbolic use of past historical names of cities and people preoccupied biblical interpretation and historiography at Qumran: e.g., *Pesher Habakkuk* (1QpHab: “Kittim”); *Cairo Damascus Document* (CD: “Damascus”); *the Qumran War Scroll* (1QM: “Kittim,” “Assyria”). *Split into three parts*, cf. [[Zechariah 04#^4-4|Zech 4.4]] (split in half). *Wine-cup*, see 14.10. In 17.4b the wine cup is filled with impurities rather than God’s wrath. ^16-19

**[[NRSVue Revelation 16#^16-21|21]]:** *Hailstones*, [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^9-13|Ex 9.13–35]]; [[Psalms 018#^18-13|Ps 18.12]]; [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 05#^5-22|Wis 5.22]]. ^16-21

> [!note] Women and the Symbolism of Pollution *(p. 565)*
> John of Patmos exhibits a concern with female sexuality that is unique among Jewish apocalypses. He accuses his rival “Jezebel,” a teacher perhaps influenced by Paul’s epistles (see 1 Cor 7–8), of engaging in *porneia*, impure sexual activity, and he compares her followers’ attention to her to adultery (2.20–22). His hatred of Rome/Babylon is subsumed into disgust for a giant whore (Gk *porne*) of kings’ pleasure (17.2), whose polluting fornication is likened to a dis-gusting liquid she holds in a golden cup (17.4b). The horror of her liquid impurities (menses do not lie far from John’s language here) is compounded by her drunkenness “with the blood of the saints” (17.6), an utter inversion of Jewish dietary and sexual purity (cf. [[Torah 02 - Noach#^9-4|Gen 9.4–6]]; [[Torah 25 - Tzav#^7-26|Lev 7.26–27]]; 15; 17.10–14; [[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^23-11|Deut 23.10]]).
>
> John’s horror of women as sexual bearers of pollution relates to a critical detail of the 144,000 saints: “who have not defiled themselves with women, for they are virgins” (14.4). In rejecting marriage and sexuality, the 144,000 become like angels; their apocalyptic celibacy, which some Jesus-believers seem to have embraced ([[1 Corinthians 07#^7-1|1 Cor 7.1]]), is comparable to the stringent sexual purity of some of the Qumran covenanters (12.1–2; see [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^19-15|Ex 19.15]]; [[Torah 31 - Emor#^22-1|Lev 22.1–9]]; CD 2.14–16). Yet it is not just ritual celibacy that John enjoins, for he specifically sees women as a source of “defilement” (Gk *mol-unō*). John’s preoccupation with female fluids also emerges in the vision of the woman and the dragon (ch 12). As the woman gives birth, almost directly into the dragon’s mouth (12.4b), so the dragon expels a flood of water (12.15), which is then swallowed by the helpful (and female) earth. The alternation of fluids, expelling and swallowing, amounts to a nightmarish image of parturition.
>
> Much Jewish literature from the Greek and Roman periods is preoccupied with community boundaries, and the breaching of these boundaries through either intermarriage or cultural influences is typically discussed in terms of impure sexuality: Heb *zenut* or Gk *porneia*. The most vivid example, projected into antediluvian myth, is the Watcher angels’ seduction of human women: through this impure sexual congress all kinds of foreign customs, magic, and divination practices entered human culture, thus polluting humanity (*1 En*. 6–9; cf. *T. Reuben* 3.11–14). By the Roman period, intermarriage and fornication had become dominant language in sectarian Judaism for discussing priestly purity, communal purity, and the incorporation or rejection of foreign ideas and practices; the image of women’s bodies, their orifices and sexuality, never drifted far from these discussions (cf. Prov 7).
