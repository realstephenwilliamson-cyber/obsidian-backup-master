THE EARLY TANTRA SERIES

Tantric scriptures form the basis of almost all the various theistic schools of theology and ritual in post-Vedic India, as well as of a major strand of Buddhism (Vajrayāna). Among these schools, those centred on the Hindu deities Siva and Visnu spread well beyond the Indian subcontinent to Kambuja (Cambo-dialLaos/ Thailand), Champa (Vietnam) and Indonesia, while Buddhist tantrism quickly became pan-Asian.

With this mini-series, launched within the ‘Collection Indologie, we are releasing some of the fruits of a Franco-German project funded fiom 2008 to 2011 by the Agence Nationale pour la Recherche and the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschafi. The series takes its name from the projects title: Early Tantra: Discovering the interrelationships and common ritual syntax of the Saiva, Buddhist, Vaisnava and Saura traditions. Our aim was to study the interrelationships between the tantric traditions on the basis of fundamental source-material, which we would edit and publish for the first time. For this we made use of some of the exceptionally rich manuscript resources gathered in the twentieth century and studied by German and French research institutes at opposite poles of the sub-continent.

In recent centuries, the Tamil-speaking South is the only area where a vast corpus of Sanskrit texts of what was long the dominant school of tantric ŚSaivism continued being copied and so transmitted to the present day. So when, in 1956, Jean Filliozat secured a foothold in Pondicherry for French indological research, he created an ideal institutional base for the study of a forgotten chapter in the religious history of Asia. Gradually, the largest specialised manuscript collection of texts relating to the Saiva Siddhānta was amassed, recognised in 2005 by UNESCO as a “Memory of the World” collection: The Śaiva Manuscripts of Pondicherry.

At the other end of the subcontinent, the cool climate of Nepal has preserved ancient manuscripts of texts of virtually every branch of Indian learning. Much of the early history of tantrism is thus preserved in the vast archive of Nepalese manuscripts microfilmed over more than three decades by the Nepal-German Manuscript Preservation Project (NGMPP), then partly catalogued by the Nepalese German Manuscript Cataloguing Project (NGMCP), run from Hamburg and from the NGMCPS base in Kathmandu, the Nepal Research Centre (NRC). One Nepalese treasure, also included, in 2013, in UNESCOS register, formed the cornerstone of our project: the ninth-century manuscript of the Nisvāsatattvasamhitā.

The Early Tantra Series is not a closed collection: as well as the editions and translations promised as part of the Early Tantra project, studies of numerous related works were inspired or further advanced during the projects workshops.

Preface

Portions of this book had their genesis in my doctoral thesis, “The Brahmayã-malatantra and Early Śaiva Cult of Yoginīs” (University of Pennsylvania, 2007), written under the guidance of Harunaga Isaacson. This has long been available as an open-access publication (HATLEY 2007). Though I received kind encouragement after its completion, the dissertation seemed satisfactory to me neither as a study of early tantric yoginā cults, nor as a critical edition of the Brahmayã-mala. I hence divided the material into three separate projects, which have taken much longer than anticipated to complete. Indeed this book, volume I of the Brahmayāmala, has been so long in the making that it is appearing three years after the publication of volume II, by Csaba Kiss (2015). Carried over from my doctoral thesis into the present volume are some sections of chapter 1, all of chapter 2, the edition and translation of Brahmayāmala, chapters (patala) 1 and 2, and appendix A—in most cases heavily revised or re-written. Chapter 3 was published earlier as an article in the Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies (Hariey 2016b). The remaining material has not previously been published.

This is an academic book intended to shed light on the literature and history ofan Indian tantric tradition. Influential though it was, the Brahmayāmala belongs to the most antinomian fringe of early-medieval Tantric Śaivism, and some may find its preoccupation with human remains, sexuality, and occult power troubling. Conversely, readers who might be tempted to dabble with the Brahmayāmala’s highly esoteric rituals and mantras do so at their own peril. These were surely not intended to be learned without authorization by a genuine guru. In the words of an oft-quoted verse of the Kulārnavatantra, “One who incants a mantra written down in a book does not attain its power, and experiences nothing but harm at every step, O beautiful one” (pustake likhito mantro yena sundari japyate | na tasya jāyate siddhir hānir eva pade pade ||). Those desiring to revive or reinvent Śaiva tantric traditions would do well to seek more innocuous material.

Heading the pantheon of mantra-deities at the heart of its cult are Kapālīśabhairava (“Bhairava, Lord of the Skull”) and the supreme Goddess, known primarily as Caṇndā Kāpālinī (“Grim Bearer of the Skull”), Aghorī (“Un-Dreadful”), and Bhairavi (“Terrifying”). A largely female retinue surrounds the divine couple (yāmala), completing the core pantheon of the deity mandala: the Four Devis or Guhyakās; the Four Attendants (kiñṁkarī), also called the Consorts (dúti); the Six Yoginis; and the Fight Mothers (mātr), in descending order of cultic status.\*™! These deities are ultimately subsumed within the Nine-Syllable Vidyā (navāksarā vidyā) which undergirds the BraYa’s ritual systems, and which is identical to the supreme Goddess herself. As Alexis SANDERSON (1988: 672) observed in his pioneering remarks upon the Bra Yā,

In the cult of Svacchandabhairava, in the Mantrapītha, the secondary deities surrounding the couple in the mandala were male and solitary. Here \[in the Bra Ya\] they are female, with subordinate male consorts in the densely populated mandala installed for exceptional worship, and alone in the much simpler pantheon of the private daily cult (zityakarma). Bhairava rules these secondary deities as the unifying holder of power (saktimat, sakticakresvara), in accordance with the general aiva conception of the divine nature. But this supremacy on the iconic plane is transcended by sakti on that of the deity-embodying mantras. As with so much of tantric Śaiva literature, significant strides in the study of the Bra Ya commenced with the early works of Alexis SAanperson. In “Śaivism and the Tantric Traditions,” his monumental 1988 essay mapping the cults and canon of Tantric Śaivism, SANDERSON advanced a compelling hypothesis concerning the position of the Bra Yā within Saiva traditions, already quoted above. He notes several significant ways in which the text appears archaic. Seeing within the Bhairava-stream of esoteric Śaivism an historical trajectory towards increasing emphasis on goddesses, he finds in the Bra Yā an early window into this process. While the Bra Yā ostensibly expounds a bipolar Godhead—a yāmala or god-goddess pair—the goddess Aghorī in fact transcends Bhairava, for her vidyā contains within its nine syllables the entire mandala of mantra-deities. And in the ritual practices of the Bra Ya, Sanperson identifies the radical mortuary (kāpālika) and exorcistic rites forming the earliest stratum of the yogini cults, which would undergo transformation as the Kaula movement came to permeate most cults of the Bhairavatantras.\* SANDERSON’s earlier articles make a number of other contributions to study of the Bra Ya, including reconstruction of its Nine-Syllable Vidyā, and demonstration that the Bra Yā is one of several Saiva sources redacted into the Buddhist Laghucakrasamvaratantra.\* In addition, he first identifed the reference to the BraYa in the old Skanda-purāna, discussed in §1.8, confirming the likelihood of the text's early period of composition.
