## Chapter 1 - Can We Infer the Existence of the Self

Background

I begin my translation and exegesis of passages concerning Rāmakaṇtha’s proof of the Self at the point in NPP where the Buddhist opponent enters the discussion for thè first time.' In order to understand his first sentence we need to know roughly the content of the previous section.” In it Rāmakantha debated with an Advaitavedāntin opponent who claimed that this world of everyday interaction and language (vyavahāra), characterized by difference (bheda), is unreal, Brahman alone being real. Rāmakanṭtha (who believes in the independent and real existence of perceivers, perceived objects and God) argues against the Vedāntin on the grounds that there are no means of know- ‘ing this non-dual Brahman. Direct perception and inference actually refute, rather than confirm, the oneness of everything in Brahman since, being based on sense-data and inferential marks, they have difference as their object (bhedaviṣayatva).\* Neither can scripture bring about knowledge of Brahman for the following reasoning. The Advaitins themselves assert that Brahman is beyond conceptualization, so how can it be that which is denoted by scripture? But this plausibility rests on equating two situations that the Buddhist can distinguish: 1) an object is experienced in one person and re-experienced subsequently by another; 2) an object is experienced by a cognition, which ceases to exist but causes another; which causes another ... which causes another that experiences the same object. Since the Buddhist can distinguish these in that in the latter information can be passed from one cognition to the next through the vehicle of samskāras, the impossibility of desire arising in 1 does not mean that it could not arise in 2.
