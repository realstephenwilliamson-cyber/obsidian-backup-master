## Chapter 6: ‘Let us now invoke the three celestial lights of Fire, Sun and Moon into ourselves’ 1 : Magic or everyday practice? Revising existentiality for an emic understanding of Śrīvidyā

*Monika Hirmer*

### 6.1 Introduction: Turning magic inside out

What can or cannot exist is determined by the coordinates of existentiality informing a specific world. Similarly, what can or cannot be known is delimited by the regimes of truth inherent to that worldview.

In the modern, Western-inspired, and science-based worldview, something, in order to exist, generally needs to be physical or, at least, empirically detectable. Furthermore, for it to be knowable and considered true, it has to be verifiable and satisfy, among others, the principle of non-contradiction. Given the way in which modernity defines conditions of existence and truth, bodies that are at once human and divine and practices that harness cosmic energies to influence worldly circumstances fall outside the purview of the existent and knowable and, as such, are confined to the realm of magic.

Tantric traditions, in particular, have been classified as irrational, occult, and corrupt for pursuing divinization and extraordinary powers, and contrasted to a supposedly rational, sophisticated, and moral West throughout colonial times.<sup>2</sup> Among these, figures prominently Śrīvidyā, a tradition revolving around the fertile and benevolent goddess Tripurasundarī, worshipped as mantra, Śrīcakra, or anthropomorphically. Despite enjoying pan-Indian recognition around the 13th century, like other Tantric traditions, Śrīvidyā declined in popularity from the 17th century onward and is nowadays largely practiced secretly or in a sanitized form, primarily in South India. While the exoticization of Tantric traditions has been criticized by a growing body of literature that highlights, instead, their polythetic nature and widespread influence,<sup>3</sup> Tantric practices remain uncomfortably associated with magic till date.

In light of the enduring portrayal of magic as the antithesis of science and modernity and, crucially, considering that none of the Śrīvidyā *sādhaka*s (practitioners) I lived and worked with ever referred to divinization rituals or cosmic powers as magic, I advocate a departure from the very concept of magic; as a starting point for a study of Tantric practices I suggest, instead, the radical acknowledgement of *sādhaka*s’ own worldviews and modes of being. When earnestly embracing the validity of practitioners’ standpoints, a 

*[p. 117]* ^pg130

fundamental reassessment of modern scientific discursive parametres occurs, through which it is possible to complement current scholars’ works, mostly based on the sole suspension of rational judgement. Whereas the latter’s charitable interpretations<sup>4</sup> yield detailed descriptions of Tantric bodies,<sup>5</sup> an analysis developed around *sādhaka*s’ own parametres can uncover the ontological tenets and regimes of truth that underpin divine bodies partaking in the Goddess’ energy.

In order to recognize *sādhaka*s as divine beings housing ‘the three celestial lights of Fire, Sun and Moon’ without (mis)interpreting them as magical, it is necessary to relinquish an understanding of bodies as exclusively gross and of existence as entirely unfolding within spatiotemporal coordinates. Epistemic-- ontological dislocations of this type are advocated by the ontological turn<sup>6</sup> and facilitated through a methodology situated in worlding: I illustrate in Section 6.3 how my active participation as fellow initiate in the South Indian Śrīvidyā tradition of Śaktipur<sup>7</sup> has, on the one side, prompted a reinscription of my beingness in accordance with the tradition’s everyday fabric and, on the other, rendered me a participant in Śaktipur’s concerted worlding.<sup>8</sup> Building on these methodological premises and the recoding of beingness they advocate, in Section 6.4.1, I proceed with a revision of the conditions of existentiality itself: consistently with *sādhaka*s’ modes of being, Śrīvidyā’s coordinates of existentiality exceed modern limitations, revealing that grossness is always preceded and exceeded by subtleness—the manifest ultimately being subtleness dislocated in spatiotemporality. With the underpinnings of beingness and existence thus redefined, expressions such as the one in the title can be appreciated for their effective—as opposed to metaphorical—meaning and need no longer be deferred to the realm of magic. Therefore, the illustration in Section 6.4.2 of existence and beingness through the lens of the Śrīcakra, albeit partially overlapping with previous works,<sup>9</sup> acquires a legitimacy hitherto precluded by the limits of modern scientific existentiality, to which scholarship largely subscribes. While offering new ethnographic insights, my intent is also, crucially, to reveal the constraints and arbitrariness of modernity and the problematic othering that is preserved, as long as Tantric and other non-Western traditions are viewed through exoticizing frameworks. Hence, in Section 6.2, I commence this exploration with an overview of the long history of interested exclusions that are upheld by recurring to the concept of magic.

### 6.2 Is it magic? From politics of marginalization to ethical research

What it is that is deep about magic would be kept.

Ludwig Wittgenstein<sup>10</sup> Albeit later removed, Wittgenstein’s opening lines of an early draft of his *Remarks to Frazer’s Golden Bough* (1979) distinctly point at the inscrutable nature of magic; apparently innocuous and mostly taken for granted, the notion of mystery surrounding magic lies at the heart of colonial politics of othering that have 

*[p. 118]* ^pg131

consistently, among other things, discriminated Tantric traditions. As anthropologist Mary Douglas (1966) notices, whatever is mysterious and ambivalent poses a threat to the orderly and intelligible and, as such, tends to be confined, if not suppressed. It is not surprising, then, that ideas about Tantric magic fuelled the dichotomy between a primitive, dangerous Orient and the rational, modern West. Despite anxiously warding off magic, however, as Meyer and Pels (2003) argue, the project of modernity is deeply indebted to, and entrenched with, magic: in fact, magic is essential for modernity’s own identity, as an Other against which to define itself.

Just how ‘magical’ Tantric traditions effectively are, remains to be questioned, and best answered through a framework embracing Tantric practitioners’ own ontological coordinates. Before doing so, however, it is important to acknowledge also just how much Tantric traditions’ classification as ‘magical’ still supports an interested modern discourse, by tracing salient moments demarcating the history of magic.

Both Wittgenstein and Douglas, one focusing on language and the other on ritual, are essentially concerned with symbolic representation. For Wittgenstein, it is fundamental to recognize that language is characterized by two levels of significance: surface grammar, which deals with syntax, and depth grammar, uncovering the meaning represented by words within a specific language game. While surface grammar boils down to the rules through which the constituents of a language interact, and is independent of an interlocution’s context, depth grammar is necessarily connected with a specific context, to which it stands in a symbolic relationship. According to Wittgenstein, science and magic, even if presenting the same surface grammar, pertain to two different language games, making them incommensurable. In his *Remarks to Frazer’s Golden Bough*, Wittgenstein suggests that Frazer wrongly believes that ritual practitioners resort to magic to resolve problems of scientific nature because he fails to recognize the difference between science and magic’s depth grammars. Actually, Wittgenstein sustains, ritual practitioners are well aware of science’s cause-and-effect dynamics and their difference from the mechanics of magic, while Frazer mistakenly attempts to interpret magic through the language game of science. As opposed to science, which is based on empirical findings, ‘magic’, Wittgenstein says, ‘is always based on the idea of symbolism and language … magic brings a wish to representation; it expresses a wish’ (Wittgenstein 1993: 125).

Like Wittgenstein, also Douglas avoids resorting to modern science as a hallmark to evaluate cultures. In her work, she states that differences across cultures in rituals concerning purity and impurity are not due to scientific misconceptions about the manifestation and transmission of dirt but to distinct symbolic systems used to uphold social boundaries. While Wittgenstein’s and Douglas’ analyses are welcome challenges to cultural evolutionist models invariably culminating in Western science (such as Frazer’s), they nevertheless fail to take at face value the essence of magic and ritual as soon as they explain these symbolically.

*[p. 119]* ^pg132

Along similar lines, Stanley Tambiah (1990), in his critique of rationality, magic, religion, and science, appeals to a presumed shared propensity to symbolize, as means to translate between different worldviews. Convinced that there are ‘cognitive structures and processes … that are known to be universal … such as mental operations of the metaphorical and metonymical varieties’ (*ibid*.: 112), he sustains that cross-cultural communication is feasible.

Without implying that the predisposition for abstraction is confined to certain peoples, I strongly reject approaches that view the inclination to symbolize as an absolute common denominator among cultures. In Western Europe, the emphasis on symbolic processes can be traced back to the increasing centrality that the mind-body dichotomy acquired from the 17th century onward: closely associated with René Descartes, the binary postulates the intellect’s superiority over physicality, thus paving the way for the eminence of metaphoric abstractions. In South Asia, similarly, the relationship to symbolic processes is not a uniform phenomenon: as I show elsewhere (Hirmer 2020), the efficacy of certain Tantric divinizing rituals depends on practitioners’ varying predispositions toward the mind-body dichotomy: the same rituals, while efficacious for some *sādhaka*s, are inefficacious for others, based on their propensity toward representational or embodied modes of being.<sup>11</sup> By resorting to symbolic approaches as overarching means for translating cultures and omitting to contextualize the tendency to abstract, authors such as Wittgenstein, Douglas, and Tambiah imply the universal predominance of representational modes of being, thereby unduly silencing the voices of those favouring embodied experiences.

Acknowledging, and duly acting on, the historical contingency of the propensity to symbolize, does not necessarily foreclose an honest cross-cultural communication. Rather, it invites researchers to undertake an epistemic-ontological dislocation which, firstly, allows them to examine which ontological underpinnings prompt a recourse to abstraction, thus preventing the unmediated appreciation of rituals and so-called magic; secondly, it creates the conditions for researchers to cultivate the embodied approach necessary for an ethical cross-- cultural encounter. The intent of such a dislocation is to interrupt the experiences of profound disorientation caused by the protracted imposition of alienating narratives by (mostly Western) inquirers on (mostly non-Western) interlocutors. The paucity of studies conducted following such methodological premises indicates scholars’ reluctance to expose themselves to the required ontological disruptions which, at the same time, is an indicator—albeit severely reduced—of how disconcerting the imposition of mismatched epistemic-ontological frameworks is for cultures that researchers attempt to ‘translate’ through their own existential outlooks.

A valuable attempt to obviate the power imbalances arising from the application of foreign frameworks is presented by Rosalie and Murray Wax (1962, 1963). The anthropologists convincingly argue that, for an emic illustration of magic, it is necessary to adopt a ‘magical worldview’ and avoid subsuming magic to a scientific outlook or the magic-religion binary. While such an approach enables the authors to study how power is surmised, transferred, and subtracted through 

*[p. 120]* ^pg133

rituals abiding by the magical worldview’s laws, they fall short of exploring the constitutive structures of that worldview.

More significant efforts to relate profoundly to practitioners engaged in ‘magic’ are undertaken by Burchett Patton (2008) and Loriliai Biernacki (2011). Both scholars critically reevaluate Tantric mantras and suggest a shift from their representational to their embodied meaning. Patton outlines that the understanding of mantras as magic is a consequence of how language is used in a Western modern setting, where the sole way of rationalizing sound embodying deities is to depict it as magic. Classifying mantras as magic, the author elaborates, is not a neutral act but entails the marginalization and de-authorization that modernity inflicts on anything falling outside the rationalist purview. Following a feminist-- psychoanalytical route, Biernacki instead proposes that mantras stand to representational language as Lacan’s imaginary phase stands to the symbolic register. Defying the patriarchal domain of symbolism, mantras offer a return to the primary mother, as utterances whose meanings reside in immanent corporality, rather than abstract referentiality. It is worth noticing that, despite questioning the referentiality of mantras, by proposing a feminist-psychoanalytical analysis, Biernacki propagates the interpretation of Tantric traditions through Western theoretical frameworks.

Expanding on Biernacki’s and Patton’s rejection of a representational understanding of mantras and, especially, Patton’s exposition of the marginalization implied in classifying mantras as magic, I proceed to critique magic beyond the domain of language in order to illustrate Tantric existentiality at large without resorting to the prism of magic. As observed earlier in this discussion, paramount for such an exploration is the deconstruction of one’s most innate assumptions; when earnestly undertaken, this deconstruction sets the ground for more ethical, decolonial, encounters, as it attempts to avoid the unequal relationships featuring in much cross-cultural scholarship by blurring the very boundaries separating self from other.<sup>12</sup>

### 6.3 On beingness, worlding, and ‘fieldwork as dwelling’ in Śaktipur 13

Deconstructing and exploring the elementary constituents of beingness is an exercise best situated where the generative processes of a world’s inhabitants unfold through the inhabitants’ mere act of being. With Martin Heidegger, I refer to beingness as a mode of existence rooted in dwelling, where dwelling entails an attitude of caring and nurturing that frees something into its essence:

\[T\]the way in which you are and I am, the way in which we humans *are* on the earth, is … dwelling … man \[sic\] *is* insofar as he \[sic\] *dwells* … this means at the same time to cherish and protect, to preserve and care for. (2001 \[1971\]: 145, emphasis original) Through dwelling, ‘we leave something beforehand in its own nature’; essentially, ‘we “free” it’ (*ibid*.: 147), enabling it to unfold into its very beingness. To dwell, 

*[p. 121]* ^pg134

therefore, facilitates and is itself building; in fact, ‘we do not dwell because we have built, but we build and have built because we dwell, that is, because we are *dwellers*’ (*ibid*.: 146, emphasis original). Such dwelling, or building with care and nurturing, unravels ‘earth and sky, divinities and mortals’ (*ibid*.: 147) in their ‘*primal* oneness’ (*ibid*., emphasis original), whereby each of them, through their own beingness, contains and tends toward the remaining components of the primal oneness. Therefore, significantly, human and non-human agents worlding the world through concerted dwelling do not pre-exist that world but essentially manifest with the manifestation of that world.

A world thus worlded radically challenges fundamental dichotomies that are mostly identified as objective within the scientific framework and, essentially, are constitutive of Western modernity. Discontinuities such as those between nature and speaking creatures, the human and the divine, created and creators, knower and known, are replaced by a joint generative process wherein everything existing continuously renews itself. Philippe Descola illustrates that there are various ways of dividing the human from the non-human and suggests that, when aiming for an emic classification of beingness, it is necessary to look ‘where basic inferences are made about the kinds of beings the world is made of and how they relate to each other’ (2014: 273). Tim Ingold, on the other hand, elaborates on the process of knowledge-making, which he ascribes, rather than to an abstract faculty of the mind, to the primary existential experience of the knowledge-producers, revealed in their mode of ‘being alive to the world’ (2004: 51). Taking a cue from Heidegger, Ingold explains that ‘the world is not an external domain of objects … but is rather going on, undergoing continuous generation, with me and around me. As such primary engagement is a condition of being, it must also be a condition of knowledge’ (*ibid*.: 52).

A concept of personhood that is not posited in advance, but is viewed as a continuous process of coming into being through its active engagement with the environment, is helpful to think about Śrīvidyā practitioners, whose ontology transcends humanness through divinization processes that evoke their presencing as the Goddess (Devī). Furthermore, a conception of the environment as actively engaged in the creation of existence, and whose understanding is rooted in the way knowledge-producers experience it, enables us to appreciate how Śaktipur and other territories are inhabited by deities, whose powers can be accessed by its dwellers.

Given these premises, an exploration of how *sādhaka*s’ world is worlded occurs necessarily at a level prior to, and more fundamental than, Śrīvidyā worldview since worldviews, as cognitive apparatuses orienting meaning-making, are already outcomes of a world’s inhabitants’ modes of being in and experiencing, that world. An analysis of the elementary tenets of beingness is, therefore, best embedded in embodied spatiotemporality: only when existence in a worlded world is *lived*, rather than intellectually understood, is the epistemic-ontological shift necessary for an ethical cross-cultural encounter possible, as only then are preconceived classifications about a world’s inhabitants and the world they dwell 

*[p. 122]* ^pg135

in averted. As Clammer, Poirier, and Schwimmer mention, ‘the labour of understanding goes beyond cognitive, intellectual analysis. It requires also a sort of initiation adding to the researcher’s own apprehension of reality’ (2004: 7).

Practically, a research of this type translates into what I call ‘fieldwork as dwelling’ and consists of the participation in a world’s concerted dwelling, or partaking in that world’s worlding—and, conversely, being worlded by it. By dwelling at the source where beingness unfolds, researchers are confronted with the indisputable primacy of concepts long-known to scholars, yet rarely dignified with validity: when participating in Śrīvidyā’s cosmo-centric worlding, divinized practitioners suffused with Devī’s power cease to be merely tolerated through charitable interpretation and appear obvious and inevitable. Before relaying some aspects of how *sādhaka*s rendered me part of Śaktipur’s collective dwelling, it should be noted that any illustration of worlding is inherently self-defeating: according to Heidegger, ‘\[T\]he world’s worlding cannot be explained by anything else nor can it be fathomed through anything else. … As soon as human cognition here calls for an explanation, it fails to transcend the world’s nature, and falls short of it’ (2001 \[1971\]: 177).

While my formal initiation into Śrīvidyā occurred on an early February morning, my informal initiation into Śaktipur was a continuous process that involved, on the one side, practitioners worlding me into the fabric of an everyday life permeated by Devī’s presencing and, on the other, me actively participating in the worlding of Devī’s world through everyday dwelling and ritual practice. This was not a straightforward process since for a considerable time, despite having become familiar with several of the rituals and friends with some practitioners, I sensed that, even when conversations appeared cogent, they were veiled by an untranslatability that could not be articulated, as it occurred beyond words’ signification. Only when, due to a lack of alternatives, I renounced intellectualizing my experiences and followed *sādhaka*s’ advice to concentrate on *practicing* rituals, did the disconnection begin to dissipate, allowing for my fruitful dwelling in Śaktipur.

One of the most noticeable signs of the continuous process of being worlded was the seamless ‘rescription’ of my name. About four months into fieldwork, conversing with *sādhaka*s after collectively chanting the *Lalitāsahasranāma* (*Hymn of Lalitā’s Thousand Names*), a senior priestess unintentionally addressed me with one of Devī’s thousand names. From that moment onward, she began calling me that way, and soon many *sādhaka*s followed suit, calling me by that name even today.

In addition to being involved in ritual practices, my participation in Śaktipur’s worlding manifested in attires and bodily dispositions increasingly similar to those of other *sādhaka*s: over time, I grew my hair, wore *sārī*s, and progressively added the ornaments women are expected to wear—flowers in the hair, earrings, glass bangles, toe rings, anklets, and a decorative *boṭṭu* in the form of a sticker, in addition to one made of red *kumkuma* powder on the forehead. If I forgot one of these, I was questioned, whereas when I mastered the look, during the first period, this was lauded with a ‘Now you are a real South Indian lady!’ I was addressed as ‘Ammā’, a term indicating mother and goddess, and saluted with ‘*Śrī Mātre namaha*’, a greeting common among Gurujī’s initiates.

*[p. 123]* ^pg136

As recurrent in intensive fieldwork, I became ‘daughter’ to a male *sādhaka* and a senior priestess. The latter has several such ties with younger practitioners, whom she trained in Śrīvidyā; accordingly, I had a particularly close sisterly relationship with one of her ‘daughters’ around my age, also living at the temple. Over and above these familial relationships, Śaktipur’s larger community is united by the fact that practitioners are directly selected by Devī: ‘Without God’s will you cannot come here, it is the Goddess who decides who can come’, is a typical way in which *sādhaka*s underscore their affiliation with the temple-complex.

As I was thus knitted into Śaktipur’s structure, a narrative orchestrated by Devī and centring around her worship regularly took precedence over secular accounts of kinship and geography. Throughout fieldwork, several worshippers visiting Śaktipur, curious about my Caucasian traits, inquired about my origin: disoriented by my attire and ritual dexterity, they were not easily convinced that I was not from the area; finally, upon asking my name—names being often assigned according to astral coordinates at birth—and Monika (as well as the newly given name) being a common Indian name, they would conclude that I am indeed Indian or, at least, was born there in a previous life. This conclusion does certainly not mean that my interlocutors were not conversant with secular geography and biological kinship, nor that these are unimportant to them; however, it indicates that the authority of planets and stars is superior to that of skin-colour, and the involvement in an environment’s practices is more relevant than one’s place of birth in determining identity.

The reconfiguration of my identity and its inclusion within, on the one side, a kinship system determined by Devī’s lineage and, on the other, a geographical setup informed by Devī’s presencing, can be viewed in light of Ingold’s relational model, which challenges the universality conventionally conferred to blood and land of origin as primary identity-parametres. Ingold observes that whereas in ‘the genealogical model, every person is a substantive entity, whose particular make-up is a function of biogenetic and cultural specifications received from predecessors’ (2000: 142), in the relational model ‘persons are continually coming into being—that is, undergoing generation—in the course of life itself’ (*ibid*.). Similarly, the land one engages with informs a person’s territorial belonging more than where one was born: in the genealogical model, where each person is conceived as constituted by the collection of bodily and cultural heritage received from ancestors, ‘the land itself can be no more than a kind of stage upon which is enacted a historical pageant consisting of the succession of generations’ (*ibid*. 139); in the relational model, instead, it is in the ‘relationships with the land, in the very business of dwelling, that … history unfolds. Both the land and the living beings who inhabit it, are caught up in the same, ongoing historical process’ (*ibid*.).

That I became a ‘real’ South Indian lady and someone’s ‘daughter’ is thus consistent with Ingold’s relational framework. Significantly, I was not metaphorically a daughter and a South Indian lady; in fact, the relationships with my father and mother in Śaktipur were not emulations of the relationship with my blood parents, but effectively configured me as a daughter and others as a mother 

*[p. 124]* ^pg137

and a father. Similarly, my attire, behaviour, and involvement with Śaktipur’s landscape were not analogous to those of Indian women but were actually generating me as an Indian woman. Personhood, instead of a linear accumulation of bloodlines and land affiliations inscribed in an ever-more remote past, is a process in constant (re)generation, emanating from the relationships with a territory and its (human and non-human) dwellers in an enduring present that expands into past and future.

When researchers participate in a territory and its inhabitants’ worlding, embracing the constant renewal of their beingness, fieldwork becomes dwelling; such engagement not only permits an ethical encounter but, also, allows explorations at the verge of a world’s existentiality, to which I turn next.

### 6.4 Śrīvidyā practitioners’ effective identity with Devī

The ultimate goal of Śrīvidyā practitioners is to recognize Devī’s all-pervasive nature and, in this process, discover their own divinity. Essential in this endeavour is Devī’s manifestation as Śrīcakra, a sophisticated diagram of interlocking triangles and lotus petals spreading across nine enclosures (Figure 6.1). Identified as the ‘genetic code of the cosmos’ by Rajeswaramma, one of Śaktipur’s senior-- most priestesses, the Śrīcakra constitutes the template for various divinization rituals, allowing to trace direct overlaps between Devī in her anthropomorphic form as Tripurasundarī, practitioners’ bodies, and the diagram itself; as Rajeswaramma says, through such practices ‘you will get rid of the body and take on another dress, that of Devī’.<sup>14</sup>

Whereas *sādhaka*s refer to Devī’s various forms—often alternating between her appearance as Śrīcakra, Tripurasundarī, practitioners, and other beings—in the same existential context and without signs of conflict, scholarship responding to a modern framework repeatedly renders the fundamental oneness characterizing various Hindu traditions as a mirroring relationship. White (2006: 3), for example, states,

\[Ā\]yurvedic and haṭha yogic traditions conceive of the human body as a microcosmic replica of the universal macrocosm, whence the following … aphoris\[m\], found in the *Caraka Saṃhitā*: ‘Earth is that which is solid in man, water is that which is moist, fire is that which heats up, air is breath, ether the empty spaces, *brāhman* the inner self (*ātman*)’. … Here, the links between the human body and the universe, between microcosm and macrocosm (or ecocosm), operate on the level of homology: the sun in the heavens is like the sun within the yogic body.

Despite the *Caraka Saṃhitā* clearly expressing that the solid in a person *is* earth, the moist *is* water and so on, White reframes these as ‘replica’ and ‘homology’, thus denying the identity indicated by the text. Similarly, Padoux writes about rituals ‘where the union of the yogin and his partner *mirrors*, at the human level, that of the male Godhead’ (1990: 46, emphasis added), while Khanna illustrates

a subtle body within a physical shell ‘which *reflects* all the stars, galaxies, astral planes and the elements *as a mirror reflects* the external world’ (2004: 228, emphasis added). In postulating bodies that merely *reflect* the macrocosm, their materiality and finiteness remain unchallenged, thus sparing the principle of non-- contradiction<sup>15</sup> and the dichotomous outlook, which underpin much of Western philosophy, from a fundamental revision. Since a biologico-scientific lens crucially impairs an accurate appreciation of Śrīvidyā, in the next section, I depart from Western ontological assumptions and illustrate what coordinates of existentiality inform Śrīvidyā cosmology (Section 6.4.1); in view of such a fundamental revision, the following description of *sādhaka*s’ identity with Devī acquires a significantly different tone from previous studies, as it avoids transposing cosmic oneness to metaphor or reflection (Section 6.4.2).

#### 6.4.1 Revising existentiality for an emic approach to Śrīvidyā

*[p. 126]* ^pg139

The *jñāna*, the knowledge of the world, comes to us through the *pañcāmṛta*s \[five nectars\]—*śabda* \[sound\], *sparśa* \[touch\], *rūpa* \[sight\], *rasa* \[taste\] and *gandha* \[smell\].

Saraswati 2016: 118 To avoid reducing *sādhaka*s’ identities with divine entities and the ultimate micro-- and macrocosmic oneness emerging from the preceding excerpts<sup>16</sup> to either metaphor or magic, it is necessary to ask which coordinates of existentiality deny ontological viability and truth-value to these Śrīvidyā tenets in the first place. As Charles Taylor (1989) observes, the idea of the modern self is based on a specific understanding of the body within a particular framework of existentiality. In accordance with the material and spatiotemporal coordinates defining (and confining) modern existentiality and knowability, the modern body is physical, sealed, and autonomous and subscribes to conditions of verifiability and repeatability. How, then, are Śrīvidyā bodies, which exceed materiality, bodied, and which existential domains do they respond to?

While the presumed ‘givenness’ of the body has been criticized by Jean-Luc Nancy (2008) and, with particular reference to Indian bodies, by Arno Böhler (2009), a relativization of the criteria of modern existentiality, specifically in light of Śrīvidyā cosmology, is still due. Just as Nancy suggests that ‘we didn’t lay the body bare: we invented it’ (*ibid*.: 9), I argue that the domain of existentiality and its correlated knowledge do not simply occur, but are produced. I build the following revision of existentiality on Böhler’s reframing of the body.

Resorting to Nancy’s work, Böhler suggests that Indian bodies are open systems, which always already contain the world they are embedded in. In fact, bodies are sites for macrocosmic entities to exist and manifest: ‘*\[M\]acrocosmic* dimensions \[are\] always already *embodied* in the local site of a body in order to reveal that bodies do … constantly *dislocat\[e\]* themselves … while corporally sensing the environment they are located in’ (2009: 121–122, emphasis original). The author’s reading of open bodies relies on a particular conceptualization of skin, where skin is not an envelope delimiting and separating bodies from the outside world but a surface that allows the subject ‘existing *partially* in a solid body, *partially* in an astral body … to *sense* its environment *directly on the periphery of its bodily surface*’ (*ibid*.: 125, emphasis original). Hence, skin is not a *delimiting* factor that through its contouring *makes* bodies and subjects; rather, skin is the surface that makes the *experience* of the worldwide macrocosmic network—pre-existing, constituting, and exceeding the 

*[p. 127]* ^pg140

subject—possible. According to Böhler, instead of supporting the subject’s identification of themselves as an individual and separate entity, skin favours the subject’s recognition of their inherent vastness and astral existence, leading to their own discovery as a ‘being-in-the-world’, as opposed to a ‘being-in-- itself’ (*ibid*.: 127).

Following this radical reversal concerning the function of skin in defining subjectivity, I advocate a similar perspectival revolution regarding the function of *grossness* in determining existence. While Böhler illustrates how bodies and modes of being can be conceived differently in view of skin, I lay out how conditions of existentiality, and related domains of knowability, can be formulated differently, based on particular conceptions of grossness. Thus, not only modes of being that are divergent from the modern idea of self become plausible but, also, the foundations of Western paradigms of existence and knowledge are exposed to the same arbitrariness that is commonly ascribed to non-Western criteria of existentiality whenever they are observed from supposedly neutral and secular scientific frameworks.

As Gurujī’s quotations indicate, existence and the knowable among Śaktipur’s practitioners are neither confined to the realm of grossness nor do they necessarily abide by its spatiotemporal laws. Like Devī’s status varies from gross to subtle, so do people present microcosmic and macrocosmic features; while some aspects of practitioners’ beingness respond to rules of physicality, others do not, without therefore being deprived of legitimacy and knowability since Śrīvidyā’s domain of existentiality foresees a worldwide subtle network connecting all entities.

Just as skin—when approached from an outlook privileging the perspective of the surrounding world, rather than the individual’s point of view—becomes a subversive tool that opens and connects (instead of confining), so the status of grossness—when observed from a perspective located beyond, as opposed to within, the boundaries of materiality—reveals itself as a stepping stone connecting to the realm of the subtle (instead of being condition sine qua non the existable and knowable are inconceivable). In the same way as a revisited notion of skin can redefine the ontology of bodies, a revised concept of grossness can redefine the ontological coordinates of the existable in general.

Even though grossness is the mode in which what exists is most palpably manifest, to reduce the totality of existence to the instances where it assumes a gross format and responds, more or less directly, to the laws of physicality, reflects a type of viewpoint that is firmly embedded in, and circumscribed by, the manifest world—just as skin, understood as a container, reveals a perceiving subject that is in-itself and closed, rather than in-the-world and open. Thus, an outlook privileging grossness reveals more about the beholder than the existable per se.

In order to divest grossness of its semblance as the absolute status of existentiality, it is necessary to undertake a meta-perspectival shift that leads beyond a positivistic outlook prescribing that what is manifest *is* and what is not manifest is-*not*. Then, that which is manifest is-*manifest* and that which is not manifest 

*[p. 128]* ^pg141

*is-*not-manifest instead of it *not-*being. Stepping outside the positivistic-- affirmative framework reveals that which is not—and often cannot be—defined in the positive yet, not for that, is to be defined negatively as *not-*being. A perspectival relocation at the verge of existentiality, before beingness is defined materially and positivistically, renders visible a range of existentiality-criteria defined otherwise, thus expanding the domain of the existable to territories hitherto condemned to non-existence.

The way skin does not necessarily define subjects, so the status of grossness needs not inevitably define the existent and knowable. Therefore, just how skin’s ‘making’ of subjects as fundamentally separate from the rest of the world is not universal, so grossness’ ‘making’ of materiality as substantially discontinuous from non-materiality is not absolute.

In Śrīvidyā, grossness is but *a* quality, one of the modes in which the existing exists. In fact, manifestation and differentiation are the most basic forms the existable can take, rather than exhaustive criteria of existentiality: deities, the rays of Sun, Moon and Fire, subtle rivers and *cakra*s permeate practitioners to the point that the latter, I suggest, are not merely sites for these entities but their *mate* *rial expression*. Before the material body ‘occurs’ through the prism of grossness, *sādhaka*s already *are* these deities, rays, rivers, and *cakra*s. This primordial, subtle beingness always spatially and temporally precedes and follows beings in their grossness, all the while itself not submitting to spatiotemporal coordinates. Beyond the spatiotemporal confines of grossness, thus, subjects pre-exist<sup>17</sup> themselves in their subtle essence.

At the same time, however, the subtle is intimately dependent on the gross for its own existence as subtle. Grossness is the indispensable spectrum for subtleness to be recognized as that which does not submit to grossness’ criteria of existentiality: the subtle presences as gross for its subtleness to *take place* and become manifest *by eluding manifestation*. Gross bodies are thus subtle yet, in order to exist as subtle, their subtle status has to be continuously displaced and deferred in grossness; this means that the subtleness of the existing is constantly reaffirmed through existence’s grossness. In Śaktipur, therefore, the gross existence of subjects is nothing but the presencing of their subtle essence (ultimately Devī, but also rays, *cakra*s, etc.), of which they are, at the same time, product and origin through their beingness.

Grossness, in being a palimpsest that always bears traces of the subtle despite having undergone a process of manifestation, and in invariably stretching toward the subtle, is thus no longer that which defines (and confines) the existing and knowable, but that which opens up one’s cognition to the subtle as that which *is-*not-manifest, rather than that which is-*not*. Having laid bare these criteria of existentiality, a mode of being that is primarily subtle, despite necessarily manifesting grossly, as advocated and lived by in Śaktipur, ceases to be magic or metaphor and displays ontological legitimacy. Through this radical revision of grossness—from being comprehensive status of existence to being subtleness deferred in spatiotemporality—it is no longer problematic to invoke ‘the three celestial lights of Fire, Sun and Moon into ourselves’ 

*[p. 129]* ^pg142

(Saraswati 2016: 8), ‘Sarasvatī on the tongue’, and ‘Gaṇapati at base *cakra*’ (*ibid*.: 60); ‘the *liṅga* of Śiva flowing into space, which is Devī’s *yoni*’, and ‘the flow of past and future through present’ (*ibid*.: 118) become not only plausible but obvious.

Bodies that exist exclusively in gross form and in-themselves are as inconceivable from the perspective of Śrīvidyā, as bodies traversed and inhabited by subtle rivers, rays, and deities are from the viewpoint of modern selfness. From these elaborations, it emerges that, instead of ‘being-*in-*the-world’ as proposed by Böhler (2009: 127, emphasis added), in Śaktipur, existence is best rendered as ‘being-*the-*world’ since grossness and subtleness, the microcosmic and the macrocosmic, *sādhaka*s and Devī, reciprocally world each other through their inevitably dislocated presencing. In the next section, I  illustrate how *sādhaka*s effectively *are* Devī and the macrocosm as, through their beingness, they manifest the cosmic Śrīcakra.

#### 6.4.2 The Śrīcakra, ‘genetic code of the cosmos’

Having illustrated the domain of the existable informing Śaktipur, it is now possible to reflect on the selfhood of Śrīvidyā practitioners, whose presencing is primarily subtle yet necessarily manifests grossly. As Rajeswaramma observes, ‘\[O\]ur *dharma* \[correct way of living\] is to know about ourselves, which means to know about God. To know about God is to know the self’.

Central to this micro-and macrocosmic identity is Devī in her manifestation as Śrīcakra. Traversed by five major downward-facing and four major upward-facing triangles, the former being female (*yoni*) and the latter male (*liṅga*), the Śrīcakra consists of a cascade of interlocking triangles and lotus petals emanating from its centre, *bindu*, and arranged across nine concentric levels, *āvaraṇa*s or *cakra*s. From *bindu* outward, the *cakra*s present, respectively, one, eight, ten, ten, and 14 triangles, followed by a level of eight and one of 16 lotus petals, which in turn are surrounded by a square enclosure with an opening on each side (Figure 6.1). In addition to their geometric configurations, each of the Śrīcakra’s enclosures embodies a specific sound, *bījamantra* (seed mantra).

The Śrīcakra’s geometric and sonic qualities reverberate throughout the cosmos, constituting the skeleton of the entire existence. Therefore, rituals tracing the Śrīcakra along one’s body reveal one’s divine essence and participation in ultimate oneness, so that, as Rajeswaramma says, to learn about the Śrīcakra means to learn about Devī and the self and, vice versa, to learn about the self means to learn about the Śrīcakra and Devī. The priestess explains:

If you worship the Śrīcakra in your heart, and have mantras on your tongue, then you have the highest knowledge. … Śrīvidyā means that by which you know everything else … that by which you know what your true nature is. That is why … the Śrīcakra is the highest knowledge … the Śrīcakra is … the genetic code of the cosmos. … We are the \[Śrī\]*cakra* … the *cakra*s are jewels in us, precious jewels … so our body is the Maṇidvīpam, the island of Devī.

*[p. 130]* ^pg143

In its most undifferentiated state, prior to cascading into a multiplicity of triangles, lotus petals, and mantras, the Śrīcakra is its own prospective manifestation, encapsulating the tension between expansion and contraction. At this stage, it is absorbed in a pulsating dot, *bindu*, associated with the *bījamantra śrīṃ*. In the practitioners’ bodies, *bindu* is the Sahasrāra *cakra*, the energy knot located above the head-- crown. The primordial expansion and first step toward differentiation occurs when, from *bindu*, unfolds the first triangle, *trikoṇa*. This outward movement is sonically rendered by the *bījamantra hrīṃ*. The tension between *śrīṃ* and *hrīṃ*, *bindu* and *trikoṇa*, non-manifest and manifest, encapsulates all existence—past, present, and future. Rajeswaramma explains,

\[T\]he cosmos \[is\] born from \[*bindu*\] and \[goes\] back into there … the triangle, when it collapses, it merges in the *bindu*, that is *śrīṃ* … when the *bindu* expands, it becomes the triangle, this is the *hrīṃ*. That is what she \[Devī\] did. First and foremost, expansion of *bindu*, *trikoṇa*, is a downward-facing triangle containing three *śakti*s, energies: *icchā* (will), *jñāna* (knowledge), and *kriyā* (action), or the goddesses Lakṣmī, Sarasvatī, and Kālī. This level of the diagram, in the practitioners’ bodies, is located in the Ājñā *cakra*, behind the centre of the eyebrows, and does not yet present separation: ‘*\[T\]rikoṇa* explains the connection between god, the cosmos and … *jīva* \[‘self’\]; all of them belong to the same category, they are no different’, says Rajeswaramma.

*Trikoṇa* is followed by an enclosure with eight triangles, on the body situated in the throat area of Viśuddha *cakra*. From here, differentiation begins. Further expansion leads to the first of two ten-triangle enclosures, embodied in Anāhata *cakra*’s heart area; this level is linked with the *pañcabhūta*s, the five elements constituting the universe—ether, air, fire, water, and earth—and their respective *tanmātra*s, qualities—sound, touch, sight, taste, and smell. Together, they comprise the macrocosm. With the next ten-triangle enclosure, in the body in Maṇipūra *cakra*’s navel area, originates the microcosm, presenting the five sense organs—eyes, ears, nose, tongue, and skin—and the five action organs—mouth, hands, feet, anus, and genitals—which jointly enable all activities. Thereafter comes a 14-triangle enclosure, corresponding to the universe’s 14 worlds (*loka*), of which practitioners occupy *Bhūrloka*, Earth. ‘The fourteen worlds’, Rajeswaramma observes, ‘are in our Svādhiṣṭhāna *cakra*, in our reproductive organs’. The following eight-petalled enclosure, in the Mūlādhāra *cakra* at the bottom of the spine, is where the serpent energy Kuṇḍalinī lies, waiting to be aroused and ascend to Sahasrāra. The eighth *āvaraṇa* is a 16-petalled lotus, constituting the 16 phases of the moon and corresponding to Devī’s thighs. Finally, Śrīcakra’s outermost level of three squares embodies Devī’s feet. This level is linked with powers (*siddhi*) associated with the beginning of the Śrīvidyā path.<sup>18</sup>

While the Śrīcakra is Devī in her most subtle and comprehensive form as cosmos, it also encodes her anthropomorphic form (Figure 6.2). Just as her body can be discovered on the diagram, so can *sādhaka*s’ bodies be traced on it, revealing 

*[p. 131]* ^pg144

a one-to-one identity between Devī, practitioners, the macrocosm, and the Śrīcakra (Figure 6.3). As it may be evinced from above, and as Rajeswaramma illustrates, this oneness is expressed clearly through the system of *cakra*s:

The \[Śrī\]*cakra* is related to your body. So the Śrīcakra *pūjā* represents the connection between you and the *cakra* … how you are connected to the Devī. These *cakra*s, these geometrical forms, are connected with … all your seven *cakra*s. Devī will guide you, in this path \[Śrīvidyā\] you will come to know. While Devī’s feet and thighs are the two outer circles of the Śrīcakra \[not corresponding to practitioners’ body parts\], there is a complete correspondence between her and our *cakra*s. So her Mūlādhāra is our Mūlādhāra; her Svādhiṣṭhāna is our Svādhiṣṭhāna, and so forth up to the Sahasrāra. There is no difference between her and us.

And:

I told you this is the body, the Śrīcakra. It is also called Maṇidvīpam. Devī resides in the Maṇidvīpam. … It is an island of nine precious jewels, these 

*[p. 132]* ^pg145

are the nine *cakra*s or enclosures. … The gods are in our system, in our body. When you see the anatomy of the body, you see how it is connected to all the other worlds, to all the systems! Just like a computer, when you open it, and see all the wires, which connect you to the entire world, our body is connected to all the other worlds and other systems. … All this is a description of the body system.

Each *cakra* has a specific shape and is associated with a particular sound, colour, and element that reverberate throughout practitioners’ bodies (Figure 6.3).<sup>19</sup>

Along with *cakra*s, divinized bodies present *nāḍī*s, energy channels. The main ones are Iḍā, the lunar channel, which is feminine and runs left of the spinal column; Piṅgala, the solar channel, masculine and occupying the spine’s right side; and Suṣumṇā, the central channel, running through the spine and the *cakra*s. Iḍā, Piṅgala, and Suṣumṇā are also three rivers in the body, respectively Gaṅgā, Yamunā, and Sarasvatī. Iḍā and Piṅgala are linked with Ājñā *cakra*’s two lotus petals, from where they unite into the Suṣumṇā before reaching Sahasrāra. Kuṇḍalinī, when aroused, ascends along the spine, activating the *cakra*s and making *śakti* flow. However, Rajeswaramma warns, ‘\[S\]ince there are seventy-two thousand *nāḍī*s, you need to give \[Kuṇḍalinī\] the path, and the path is given by the mantra, by the guru’. When directing the *nāḍī*s’ energies into Suṣumṇā, “Śiva and Śakti’s energies meet in the middle and become one” and, ‘When the Suṣumṇā *nāḍī* is opened, the body is removed: you will get rid of the body and take on another dress, that of Devī’.

### 6.5 Conclusion

Śrīvidyā, built around the ubiquitous reverberations of the Śrīcakra, takes *sādhaka*s on a liberatory journey through their bodies, gradually integrating deities, sounds, and geometries that reveal their ultimate oneness with Devī and the entire creation. Differently from other scholars, I argue that this expanded identity is neither magic nor metaphor, but *sādhaka*s’ *effective* mode of being-in-the-world.

When observing how the concept of magic has been deployed over time, it emerges that it is part of wide-ranging politics of marginalization, whereby everything falling outside the purview of a modern scientific existence is deprecated as irrational. The inability to appreciate at face value bodies that exceed materiality and forego spatiotemporal laws is thus more than a harmless oversight, as it perpetuates the colonial dichotomy of a rational West and a magical Orient.

To avoid reframing *sādhaka*s’ expanded existence as magical, it is necessary to undertake a profound dislocation that renders directly relatable that, which falls outside the purview of modern existentiality. Since such a dislocation needs to circumvent the intellectual mechanisms that abstract divine bodies into metaphors, it is best embedded in praxis and facilitated through ‘fieldwork as dwelling’. Specifically, it is positioned where beingness unfolds and entails participating 

*[p. 133]* ^pg146

in a world’s worlding and, conversely, being worlded by that world. Dwelling is successful when a world’s existential coordinates not only become accessible, but obvious and necessary.

Only when worlds, deemed magical from a modern outlook, cease to appear magical is the centuries-long imposition of Western modernity as the ultimate paradigm of existentiality put into question. Unfortunately, despite the urgency of such profound revisions, few researchers undertake the necessary epistemic-- ontological dislocations. While scholars’ reluctance to embed their studies in dwelling is an indicator of the vulnerability and disorientation that such a methodology often implies, it also sheds light on the distress inflicted on inhabitants of worlds eluding Western paradigms every time they are reframed as magical.

With regard to Śrīvidyā, when the presumed absoluteness of the coordinates of modern existentiality is relinquished, a world emerges, whose inhabitants are necessarily traversed by rivers and populated by deities and *cakra*s which, once awakened, obviously bestow *sādhaka*s with powers. Beingness is, then, no longer informed only by biologico-scientific parametres but assumes the sonic and geometrical configurations of the Śrīcakra. Existence becomes permeated with micro-and macrocosmic elements that merge in ultimate oneness and, finally, Rajeswaramma’s words no longer appear to summon magic, but illustrate legitimate modes of being:

When you worship with flowers and fruits an idol, that is the first stage. You knock on the door \[of Devī\]. She will open it. After some time, someone will come and tell you, she is not in the idol: she is within you. You have got the five elements within you … she is flowing within you in the form of energy. … You have seven peaks \[the *cakra*s\] in your body, and on the top of your head … there is nectar, the nectar flows and you have to bathe \[in it\]. … There is a deal between us and her: ‘Ammā, I am doing this \[*pūjā*\]. \[In exchange\] of this, put the milk from the Sahasrāra \[in my body\]’… it is like an elixir. So, this is within us … the moment I get that experience, I am drenched with that nectar: all the channels in my body are fully drenched with that lotion. That is what is called Gaṅgā *snāna* \[bath\] taking a dip into the Ganges.
