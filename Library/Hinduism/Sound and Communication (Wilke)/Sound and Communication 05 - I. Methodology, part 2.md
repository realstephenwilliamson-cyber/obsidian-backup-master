#### 1.4 Texts in Practice and the Performativity of Texts

Texts have been neglected both in the debate on Orientalism and in post-colonial criticism, and also in the formation of theories on the aesthetics of religion, because they were identified with teachings, i.e. purely cognitive dimensions, and not with actual religious practice. Even Singer’s “cultural performance” approach did not really break this one-sided view, although he emphasized the spoken word along with hearing and reciting. One thing that is noteworthy — and especially relevant for this study ~ was Singer’s purely formalistic definition of “spoken language as the pre-eminent cultural medium,” which does not concentrate on any sacred literature or type of literature, or even its contents. Instead, it focuses on performance and the spoken word. Yet Singer was not actually interested in these aspects because he saw them only as a vehicle. He was interested in how societal realities were expressed and reflected in the cultural performances. In the sense of a social semantic theory, he decoded the “cultural performances” (recitations, rituals, festivals, plays, etc.) as expressions of more abstract and

This “semantic” approach to culture as a system of symbols and (social) meanings diverted attention away from the physical presence of the spoken word or, to use Frits Staal’s words, the “sound of religion. ”” We wish to direct attention precisely onto this sound and, with this perspective, initially to pursue Singer’s socio-semiotic approach a little further which, however, we will then not continue programmatically. Instead, we are concerned with the new modern times in the past, and with the practice dimension of texts, the aesthetics of performances, the sacred power attributed to them, and the performativity of the texts as texts.

The label “sonic theology” is not sufficient to explain this phenomenon and can actually distort it. When Beck discusses indigenous linguistic theories, he tends towards mystification and rather too fast he sees sonic Nāda-Brahman theology when the point is actually grammar. Indian linguistics certainly grew out of the study of the Veda, but it has accessed autonomous areas of knowledge that were independent of religion. The polyphony of voices in the interpretive and receiving communities, which is manifested in the verbal and ritual evidence regarding language and sound in both everyday and scholarly culture, will occupy us particularly in this book as will the dynamics of a constantly changing flow of tradition and of continual reinterpretations. 

We therefore take a global message-oriented approach similar to that of Beck, but ours reflects more on the diversity of voices, the discursive transfers and the historical dynamics. We place less emphasis on “sacredness” than on “sound.” The particular nature of sound that it can be physical and immaterial at the same time, and therefore can link inner and outer worlds like no other medium, and trigger sensory aesthetic effects, appears to have interested and inspired many Indian thinkers, both religious and profane. The preoccupation with sound is doubtlessly most marked and most differentiated in the religious field. Our prime examples of sacred sound are the Veda and the Tantra, to which we devote an especially large amount of space and which frame our historical discussions. A large proportion of the sources are anonymous literatures, some of which are given dates fluctuating over centuries and to which additions were often made over centuries. The post-modern manner of speaking of an author-less text is very present here.

*[p. 66]* ^pg90

In the case of the Veda as “authorless hearing,” anonymity even became an ideology and a mark of the quality of genuine revelation. Yet this view of the Pūrva-Mīmāmsā loyal to the Veda did not remain unchallenged. Non-Vedic sacred literatures claimed to be more effectful revelations, while continuing the paradigm of holy hearing. Phonocentricity characterized the culture and generated religion. The “additions” to the existing texts created new situative contexts in which the ritual sound spaces were given a new form and the cultural knowledge of the intrinsic sacredness of sound took on new dimensions and qualities of experience. Yet challenges to the dominant pattern must not be ignored either, e.g. the language criticism of the (early) Upaniṣads. 

In Hindu India we definitely find a range of attitudes towards language: participatory holistic ones and emancipatory ones, i.e. analytically distancing, and ultimately also those that aim for a considered synthesis. Our study therefore also focuses on the strategic agents and cultural policy-makers that made language and sound strong key categories for describing the world as a whole and that reflected the language nature of meaning and the sound nature of language.
