## About

A late dialogue of Plato in which a visitor from Elea, questioning the young Socrates, seeks a definition of the statesman by the method of division. It continues the *Sophist* and turns on a myth of the cosmos, the craft of weaving and the place of law.

- **People:** Socrates; the mathematician Theodorus; the Eleatic Stranger, who leads; and Socrates the Younger, who takes Theaetetus's place as respondent. The *Sophist* had asked whether sophist, statesman and philosopher are one or three; Wikipedia, citing Mary Louise Gill (2012), says Plato probably never wrote a dialogue on the philosopher.
- **Dramatic date:** 399 BC in the SEP's chronology, the day after the *Theaetetus*, when Socrates holds the two long conversations promised at *Theaetetus* 210d. The *Statesman* begins just as the *Sophist* ends.
- **Composition and authenticity:** the IEP treats it as authentic and places it among the late dialogues (c. 355–347 BC), with the *Sophist*, *Philebus*, *Timaeus*, *Critias* and *Laws*. Jowett's introduction notes doubts raised by Socher, Schaarschmidt and Ueberweg, and defends it.
- **Myth:** the god guides the universe for one age, then lets go, and it turns back; in the age of Cronus people were born from the earth and lived without property or families (Jowett).
- **Weaving and law:** weaving serves as the model both for definition by division and for the statesman's art of joining society's warp and woof. Rule by a ruler with knowledge is best, since law cannot fit every case; written law is the necessary second best. Of six constitutions, rule by one is the best and the worst, and rule by many the least good and least bad (Jowett).
- **Pages:** Stephanus 257a–311c.
- **Why it matters:** the IEP counts it with the *Republic*, the *Laws* and a few shorter dialogues as the only strictly political dialogues of Plato. Wikipedia, following John M. Cooper, says it makes clear that ruling requires expert knowledge, which actual rulers only seemed to have.
