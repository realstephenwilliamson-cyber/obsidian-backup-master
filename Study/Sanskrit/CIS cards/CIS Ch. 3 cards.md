## The Nutshell: categories of the verb (p. 34)

Which persons does the Sanskrit verb have?::1 ‘I’ · 2 ‘you’ · 3 ‘he/she/it’

Which numbers does the Nutshell list?::Singular (Sg) · Dual (Du) · Plural (Pl)

Which tenses does the Nutshell list?::Present (Pres) · Future (Fut) · Past: Imperfect (Impf), Aorist (Aor), Perfect (Perf)

Which voices does the Nutshell list?::Active (Act) · Middle (Mid) · Passive (Pass)

Which moods does the Nutshell list?::Indicative (Ind) · Potential (Pot) · Imperative (Impv)

How do English and Sanskrit usually express these categories?::English adds independent words before the verb; Sanskrit adds affixes and endings onto the verb.

## The Nutshell: roots and stems (p. 34)

Root (in a nutshell)?::The minimal shape of a verb.

Stem (in a nutshell)?::The root plus different affixes. Add the ending and you have a complete word.

## Review questions (p. 34; answers from pp. 29–33)

What is the potential mood?::It generally expresses possibilities or wishes, i.e. things that could be (‘You should/might/could go to the city’, ‘If you go to the city…’, or even a future-like ‘You will go to the city’). It is regularly used only in the present tense, and is sometimes called the subjunctive or the optative. (p. 30)

What does ‘number’ mean when used as a grammatical term?::The singular refers to just one thing or person (‘I’, ‘she’, ‘the chocolate cake’), the plural to several (‘we’, ‘they’, ‘the cats’); Sanskrit also has a dual, for exactly two of something. (p. 29)

Which numbers does Sanskrit have?::Singular, dual and plural. The dual refers to exactly two of something and is translated ‘we two’, ‘the two of them’, or simply as a plural. (p. 29)

Which categories is the Sanskrit verb marked for?::Person and number; tense, mood and voice. (p. 29)

What is a verbal root? How is it different from a verbal stem?::The root (marked √) is the minimal form of a verb, the element that conveys its basic meaning, left over when all markers of person, number, tense, voice and mood have been removed; it is never found in a text. The stem is the form onto which the actual endings are added: in most verbs the root is changed first; where endings go straight onto the root, the stem is identical to the root (a root stem). (p. 32)

How is the present tense stem of a Sanskrit verb formed?::In most verbs by adding something before, into or behind the root: √viś adds -a- (viśa-), √dā adds da- in front (dadā-), while √as takes the endings directly (as-). Sanskrit has ten such ways; roots that form the stem the same way are in the same stem or verb class. (pp. 32–33)

What are ‘finite’ verb forms?::Any verb form that is marked for all the categories (person, number, tense, mood, voice), e.g. I will go: 1st singular future indicative active. (p. 30)

How does Sanskrit express a continuous present, such as ‘she is writing’ rather than ‘she writes’?::It does not distinguish simple and continuous forms: depending on the context, a form such as भरति bharati may have to be translated as either ‘(he) carries’ or ‘(he) is carrying’. (pp. 29–30)

What is the difference in meaning between the different verb classes?::None: the additions that form the present stems do not change the meaning of the root; the changes are purely formal. (p. 33)

## Notes to the vocabulary (p. 35)

What does ‘postposed’ mean?::‘Placed after’: said of words that appear after the word before which they must be translated e.g. in English: गच्छति भरति च gacchati bharati ca, ‘he goes and carries’.

Where may a postposed word stand in its phrase?::Behind the first word in a phrase or sense unit, or later on, but never first: अत्र स्मरति तत्र च विशति atra smarati tatra ca viśati ‘here he remembers and there he enters’.

Whom can the third-person ending refer to?::A man, woman or thing; only the context tells which. The book uses ‘he’ as shorthand for ‘he/she/it’.
