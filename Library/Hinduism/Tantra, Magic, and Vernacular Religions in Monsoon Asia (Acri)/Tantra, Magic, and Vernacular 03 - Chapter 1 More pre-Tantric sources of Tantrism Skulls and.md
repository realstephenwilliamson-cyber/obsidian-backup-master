## Chapter 1: More pre-Tantric sources of Tantrism: Skulls and skull-cups

*Ronald M. Davidson*

### 1.1 Introduction

This chapter continues a line of exploration I previously began, in that instance investigating the debt Tantric traditions owe to magical groups operating outside of Tantric lineages (Davidson 2017). There I argued that a significant lapse in the historical modelling of Tantrism is the absence of a sophisticated treatment of prior behaviours, practices, and attitudes that contributed to the formation of Tantrism but were not part of Tantric lineages and did not engage many of the signature elements of mature Tantrism. That is, there were various social groups and ritual actions attributed to them that have evident thematic and ritual continuities with the earliest Tantrism—emergent in the late 6th or early 7th centuries—yet cannot be considered ‘Tantric’ in the sense that this term is employed in the documents and lineages describing systems of liberation across sectarian domains. The forms of behaviour and attendant ideologies appear to have been dependent on small, localized groups so that no overarching narrative of their relationships to the emergence of Tantrism is possible. Nonetheless, it is clear that at different times and in various places, practices foreshadowing the better attested but later Tantric activities are in need of study, despite the incomplete nature of the witnesses for the many social systems about which we have little to no internal documentation. This is either because they were illiterate—as is most probable—or because any records they may have kept were not transmitted through the enduring institutions eventually emerging with mature Tantric lineages.

This chapter will concern itself with the discussion of the origins of the employment of a skull (*kapāla*) in Tantric traditions, especially that of the ‘skull bearers’ (Kāpālika), the heterodox tradition that has been discussed by Lorenzen, Sanderson, Törzsök, and others. It will present the received understanding, examine its complications, and present evidence not so far considered. The basic argument is that the use of skull-cups, and the use of skulls generally for religious and non-religious purposes, is much more widely spread than appreciated and, for some of its early history, was intimately connected with those engaged in the worship of the *piśāca*s or related spirits in India, from whom Śaivas and others appear to have appropriated that behaviour.

*[p. 13]* ^pg26

### 1.2 Is the Śaiva skull-cup use actually sourced in the Dharmaśāstras?

Since the publication of Lorenzen’s investigations of the Kāpālikas, the employment of skulls as vessels for food or drink has been generally attributed to those notorious Śaiva practitioners. This has been all the more true once it was realized that the modern Aghoris also employ skulls as similar vessels, as is now well-- known. Consequently, the idea came to be accepted that Śaiva traditions must have been the primary proponent of the ritual use of skulls as vessels, with the correlated understanding that other cremation ground (*śmaśāna*) practices were all aggregated together to articulate a nexus of such practices. Yet this was done despite the recognition that the practice proposed to have a relationship to expiatory rites in the Dharmaśāstras, which became accepted as their mythic source. Indeed, Lorenzen in several places (1991: 13–18) arrives at the conclusion that one or another literary figure ‘must be a Kāpālika’ only because of behaviours identified (we will reconsider some of his sources in this chapter). The noting of Kāpālika-like behaviour on the part of others was consequently considered necessarily to be an appropriation of that behaviour from Kāpālikas, centring it in the Śaiva worldview with all others on the periphery. However, it is clear that the focus on bones and relics of the dead implicates issues of pollution in the Indian ritual universe and brings to the fore a fundamental dissonance within Indian religions: how it is that traditions affiliated with Brahmanical discourse should still engage in these exceptionally polluted behaviours?

Those who were self-described ‘skull bearers’ are notorious for having a *kapāla* as one of their primary emblems, along with a staff (*khaṭvāṅga*) that may have a skull attached to its top and other emblems of their status. Yet recent research has revealed the existence of an intermediary group, designated the Lākulas. The *Niśvāsamukha* explains the ‘supermundane’ vow (*lokātītavrata*) as follows:

*khaṭvāṅgī ca kapālī ca sa jaṭī muṇḍa-m eva vā ||* *vālayajñopavītī ca śiromuṇḍaiś ca maṇḍitaḥ |* *kaupīnavāso bhasmāṅgī divyābharaṇabhūṣitaḥ ||* 4.89cd–90 Sanderson (2006: 164–165), on the basis of later explanations, identifies the group engaging this vow as the Lākula and translates the text as follows:

He should carry a skull-topped staff (*khaṭvāṅgaḥ*) and \[an alms-bowl fashioned from\] a human cranium (*kapālam*). He should have matted locks (*sajaṭaḥ*) or shave his head bald (*muṇḍaḥ*). He should wear a sacred thread (*yajñopavītam*) made from the hair \[of the dead\] and he should adorn himself with a chaplet fashioned from human skull-bones. He may wear nothing but a strip of cloth to cover his private parts. He must smear himself with ashes and decorate himself with celestial ornaments.<sup>1</sup>

*[p. 14]* ^pg27

These Śaivas employ such emblems, according to a rationale evident at least from the late 6th century, in imitation of Śiva or of the deity Bhairava, who mythically was said to have cut off the head of the deity Brahmā, thereby making him a ‘Brahman or foetus killer’ (*brahmahā* or *brūṇahan*)—carrying a skull being employed to identify the expiatory rites (*prāyaścitta*) for both varieties of criminals.

Lorenzen (1991), Brick (2012), and others point to statements in several Dharmasūtras/Dharmaśāstras that seem to identify these items as being involved in a penance. The statement in the *Yājñavalkyadharmaśāstra* is often provided as evidence:

*śiraḥkapāladhvajavān bhaikṣāśī karma vedayan* |
*brahmahā dvādaśa samā mitabhuk śuddhim āpnuyāt* || *Yājñavalkyadharma* *śāstra* 3.243 The murderer of a Brahman has as his flag \[i.e. signifier\] the bone of the head, and seeks his food by begging, while informing all of his criminal action. Let him achieve purity \[by practicing this penance\] for twelve years, eating little.

Sanderson (2006: 165, 212n15) and Törzsök (2011: 1) explicitly follow Lorenzen’s proposal that this model was the source for the Kāpālika practice.

There are several moving parts to this description, and it might be worthwhile to separate them, as they have not been granted sufficient critical examination. First, the behaviour of Brahman murderers found in the legal texts must be understood in context. Then, the mythology of Śiva/Bhairava’s skull carrying, as well as its origin, could be more fully investigated. And finally, the relationship of these to the description in the *Niśvāsamukha* and attendant statements might be revisited.

First, we may observe that the referent of the term *kapāla*—generally translated in these contexts as ‘skull’ or ‘skull-cup’—is not entirely clear, and the compounds in which this occurs are disputed in many traditional discussions. *Kapāla* is polysemic and can mean a skull, a skull-cup, a piece of a skull, a small dish, or a potsherd, the latter being a fragment of a pot that has been discarded. The *kapāla* as a small dish or potsherd is employed in *Śrauta* and *Śrāddha* rituals that require the offering of rice-cake (*puroḍāśa*)—whether three, five, six, seven, eight, ten, 11, 12 or some other number—pieces that are placed on the *kapāla* prior to being offered in the fire (e.g., *Taittirīyasaṃhitā* 1.5.1.4, 1.5.2.1, etc.). The polysemy of these is expressed as a simile in the *Śatapathabrāhmaṇa* (1.2.1.\[2\]) which indicates that, just as the plate bones for the head (*śīrṣṇaḥ kapālāni*) support the brain, the potsherds support the *puroḍāṣa* cake. Similarly, the *Vaiṣṇavadharmaśāstra* mentions that ascetics contemplate the four bones of the skull (96.79: *catvāri kapālāni śirasaś ceti*). In both instances, the sentences and the plural number suggest that skull plates are indicated rather than a complete skull.

When the Dharmaśāstras employ the term *kapāla* in the context of the expiation of Brahmanicide, they are not unequivocal in either the meaning of these 

*[p. 15]* ^pg28

items or the penance to be performed. If they use the term, most identify the *kapāla* as one of the signifiers (*dhvaja*/*cihna*) of crime rather than an item of utility for begging. The *Mānavadharmaśāstra* (11.73) includes most of the *Yājñavalkya* elements seen earlier, but the ‘flag’ is the head of a corpse (*śavaśirodhvaja*), which is very similar to the language of the *Vaiṣṇavadharmaśāstra* (50.15 *sarveṣu ca śavaśirodhvajī syāt*); this language is replicated elsewhere, as in the *Auśanasasmṛti* (8.5). *Gautama* states that the penitent’s hands should grasp a staff and skull-bone (3.4.4 *khaṭvāṅgakapālapāṇi*) while entering a village and proclaiming his crime. *Baudhāyana* is similarly obtuse, seeming to incorporate a *kapāla*, a *khaṭvāṅga*, and the head of a corpse all together, along with wearing the skin of a donkey and living in forests or in a cremation ground (2.1.3 *kapālī khaṭvāṅgī gardabhacarmavāsā araṇyaniketanaḥ śmaśāne dhvajaṃ śavaśiraḥ kṛtvā*).

In fact, the only legal text explicitly mentioning the use of a skull as a bowl instead of a sign is the *Āpastambadharmasūtra*, which describes a *brūṇahan* as using a skull for a drinking vessel (1.28.21 *puruṣaśiraḥ pratīpānārtham ādāya*). In context, this indicates penance for abortionists so that the skull in question is that of the foetus, which would mean a tiny skull of questionable utility, given that the frontal and parietal bones of the head would have yet to fuse. Brick (2012: 37–39) has cogently argued that this penance for abortion (*brūṇahan*) was conflated with the separately described penance for the murder of a Brahman sometime after *Āpastamba* was codified. With reference to Brahmanicide, the penance is different:

*araṇe kuṭiṃ kṛtvā vāgyataḥ śavaśiradhvajo ’rdhaśaṇīpakṣam adhonābhy uparijānv ācchādya | tasya panthā antarā vartmanī* | *dṛṣṭvā cānyam utkrāmet* | *khaṇḍena lohitakena śarāveṇa grāme pratiṣṭheta | ko ’bhiśastāya bhikṣām iti saptāgāraṃ caret* || *Āpastambadharmasūtra* 1.24.11–15 Olivelle translates:

He should build a hut in the wilderness, curb his speech, carry a skull as a banner and cover himself from the navel to the knees with a scrap of hempen cloth. His path is the gap between the tracks of cart-wheels, and if he happens to see another person he should step aside. He should set out to the village carrying a broken metal bowl and visit seven houses saying, ‘Who will give almsfood to a heinous sinner?’

(Olivelle 1999: 34–35) Just as important to *dharmaśāstrin*s are the alternative penances, whether we are considering those other Dharmaśāstras that do not employ any sort of bones or alternative penances for Brahmanicide. The *Vāsiṣṭhadharmaśāstra* (21.23–30), for example, enjoins the offering of body parts in a fire, or trial by combat, indicating that a murderer (*brūṇahan*) of a Brahman or foetus is cleansed by confessing his sin and surviving three encounters with an enemy. *Parāśarasmṛti* (3.64–70) 

*[p. 16]* ^pg29

requires a 12-year penance and a pilgrimage to Setubandha (Rāma’s Bridge/
Adam’s Bridge), the limestone passage leading from Rāmeśvaram to Sri Lanka. He is to beg his way, proclaiming his sin incessantly, finally purified by a bath in the ocean. *Nāradasmṛti* (14.9) invokes another section of the *Mānavadharmaśāstra* (9.237), that the killer of a Brahman should have his forehead branded as a criminal (*lalāṭe ’bhiśastāṅkanaṃ*) with the image of a headless man (*kāryaṃ brāhmaṇy aśirāḥ pumān*). The *Sāmavidhānabrāhmaṇa* (3.2.11) states that all the grievous sins (*mahāpātaka*), of which Brahmanicide is the foremost, can be relieved by the *atikṛcchrā* penance (a fast), but elsewhere (1.5.15–16) it provides a year-long fast and penance of confession to all passers-by at the entrance to the village; in neither case does it mention either the *kapāla* or *khaṭvāṅga*.<sup>2</sup> The most common element in all these penances is the unrelenting public confessions, wherein the penitent tells anyone and everyone his crime, and that is the rationale for the skull of the murdered person—it is the undeniable physical sign that a murder was committed. And in fact, most Dharmaśāstras identify alternative penances involving an elaborate sacrifice (e.g., *aśvamedha*), combat, pilgrimage, or exorbitant gifts of cattle or gold listed after the use of signa made of bones. Bharadwaj’s review (2014) of the Brahmanicide penances in the literature does not even mention the *kapāla-khaṭvāṅga* system. If the standard *śāstric* logic is in play, then the alternatives stated later in the texts would seem to have been preferred.

This diversity of penances became a point of contestation in the commentarial literature. An objection is raised in the extensive treatments of Brahmanicide: how is it that there are so many penances, and how to deal with the conflict of interpretation (*vikalpa*) in the Dharmaśāstras? In the major comments on Brahmanicide that consider this question, the answer offered is that these alternatives are not necessarily conflicts of interpretation but options to be employed by a judge or court, given the various circumstances attendant on the crime—intentional or not, multiple or not, the caste or gender of the killer, extenuating circumstances, and so on.<sup>3</sup> These are all recognizable elements in juridical decisions, but it is not clear that they were written to be exercised that way, even if the alternative prescriptions gave the *dharmaśāstrin*s the latitude to interpret them as such.

Given the plethora of penitential options for Brahmanicide, one might conclude that the lack of unanimity about the meaning, function, or even necessity of bones would elicit from commentators a range of opinions, but this is not the case. As Kane has already mentioned, according to Dharmaśāstra commentators, the skull is *not* to be employed as a begging bowl.<sup>4</sup> Instead, the *kapāla* is carried as a sign (*dhvajaṃ cihnaṃ*). Almost universally, commentators contradict the idea that the skull is used as a skull-cup. The only major commentator I have been able to discover who even considers a skull-cup as an option is Haradatta’s commentary to *Gautamadharmasūtra* 3.4.4, where he quotes *Āpastamba* 1.28.21 and affirms that the *khaṭvāṅga* is held in the right hand, the *kapāla* in the left for the purpose of begging (*khaṭvāṅgaṃ dakṣiṇe pāṇau kapālaṃ savye bhikṣārthaṃ pāṇīyapānārthaṃ bhojanārthaṃ ca*). But this  he immediately contradicts by quoting *Āpastamba* 1.24.14, stating that the penitent should approach a village with a bowl that is a ‘red piece’ (*Āpastambadharmasūtra*, p. 142: *khaṇḍena lohitakena śrāveṇa grāme* 

*[p. 17]* ^pg30

*pratiṣṭheta*). Olivelle, as noted earlier, translated this *khaṇḍena lohitakena śarāveṇa* as a ‘broken metal bowl’, but this is not how the tradition takes it. Instead, it is a fragment or a whole bowl, not previously used (*anāprīta)*, made of mud or clay where food remnants are collected (*Yājñavalkyasmṛtivivaraṇa-Bālakrīḍā* p. 94: *mṛṇmayapātrapatitaśiṣṭānnabhojī*).

This contradiction is in great part due to the fact that *dharmaśāstrin*s rely on a series of appendices or modified readings to the older Dharmaśāstras that reconcile the various tangents by defining the bowl employed as made of mud. The most notable of these appendices is the *Gautamadharmasūtrapariśiṣṭa*, which is  quoted as *Gautamasmaraṇa*/*smṛti* but does not occur in the received *Gautamadharmasūtra*. *Gautamadharmasūtrapariśiṣṭa* 2.6.11 modifies the *Āpa* *stamba* statement to specify its composition: ‘\[L\]et him enter a village to beg with a broken piece of red bowl made of mud’ (*lohitakena mṛnmayakhaṇḍaśarāveṇa bhikṣāyai grāmaṃ praviśet*). This statement is further modified in quotations and other appendices to harmonize the various tangents by declaring that it is a *kapāla* made of mud (*mṛnmayakapālapāṇiḥ bhikṣāyai grāmaṃ praviśet*). Apparent supplements or modifications from *Yamasmṛti* (*mṛṇmayena kapālena svakarma khyāpayaṃs tathā*) and *Śaṅkhasmṛti* (*brahmahā pariṣadānumataḥ khaṭvāṅgī gardabhājinī mṛṇmayapātrapatitabhikṣānnabhojī svakramavikhyāpanena caret*) have been employed by Mādhavācārya, Aparārka, Nanda, Viśvarūpa and Vijñāneśvara in their different commentaries to the Dharmaśāstras, and they are all in accord on this point.<sup>5</sup>

Vijñāneśvara is typically adamant:

*etac ca kapāladhāraṇam cihnārthaṃ no punar bhojanārthaṃ bhikṣārthaṃ vā* | *mṛnmayakapālapāṇir bhikṣāyai grāmaṃ praviśet iti gautamasmaraṇāt* |
And that carrying of the *kapāla* is for the purpose of signifying \[the murder\], not for the purpose of \[taking\] food or begging. As *Gautamasmṛti* declares, ‘With a dish (*kapāla*) made of clay, let him enter the village to beg.’ (*Yājñavalkyasmṛti-Mitākṣarā*, p. 384) And that is as we should expect, for the purpose of this penance is the confession, debasement, and subsequent purification of the Brahman murderer or a killer of  cows (*Yājñavalkyasmṛtivivaraṇa-Bālakrīḍā* p. 94: *tato ’ntarā brāhmaṇaṃ maraṇān mocayitvā gavāṃ vā dvādaśānāṃ paritrāṇāc chuddhiḥ*; *Aparārka* p.  1053: *karma svakṛtaṃ brahmabadhākhyaṃ vadayan kathayaṃl lokānāṃ śudhyati brahmabadhapāpān mucyate*), not his continued ingestion of impurity as drinking from a skull would imply.

One possibility is that the commentators on the seminal texts were introducing a level of discourse that amounted to an innovation so that they would deny the practices previously observed. Yet there seems to be little justification for that perspective, as specialists in the Dharmaśāstra have affirmed in their examinations of various rules. In his rumination on the development of legal procedures, Rocher affirmed the conservatism of the *dharmaśāstrin*s: ‘\[T\]he commentators did not aim at introducing any novelties. Their sole 

*[p. 18]* ^pg31

purpose was a correct interpretation of the ancient texts as such’ (Rocher 2014: 427; compare Davis and Brick 2018: 32). If this is applicable here, we may infer that it is the Śaiva interpretation of the penance that is being categorically rejected by the authoritative commentators. Indeed, if Śaivas had not proposed this interpretation, it is improbable that it would be a topic of conversation at all. In any event, given the uniformity of the legal opinions, it is unlikely that the penance for the murder of a Brahman characteristically included the use of a skull as a begging bowl since the penance identified a skull as the sign of the perpetrator’s sin, to be accompanied by his constant confession of murder to any and all around.

The relationship of the Dharmaśāstra-based penance for Brahmanicide and the Purāṇic mythology of cutting off Brahmā’s head does not seem to have been so clearly assessed, with some significant discontinuities having been glossed over. The previous statement from the *Niśvāsamukha* identifies devotion to Rudra as the focus of the practice, and this seems in accord with the *Mattavilāsaprahasana* attributed to Mahendravikramavarman (ca. 590–630).<sup>6</sup> The author relates that Satyasoma, the Kāpālika portrayed there, declares that it is Śiva who cuts off Brahmā’s head, thereby incurring the penance of Brahmanicide, rather than Bhairava as found in the later Purāṇas:

*āsthāya prayato mahāvratam idaṃ bālenducūḍāmaṇiḥ svāmī no mumuce pitāmahaśiraśchedodbhavād enasaḥ |* *nātho ’pi tridivaukasāṃ triśirasaṃ tvaṣṭus tanūjaṃ purā hatvā yajñaśatena śāntadurito bheje punaḥ puṇyatām* || 17 ||
Our Lord, with the crest gem of the waxing moon, having taken up with intent this *Mahāvrata*, was released from the sin generated by slicing of the head of the Grandfather \[Brahmā\].

\[This was\] just as the Lord of dwellers in the triple-heaven \[Indra\] had his sin pacified once he pursued purity through a hundred sacrifices, having previously killed \[Viśvarūpa\] the three-headed son of Tvaṣṭṛ.

The Indra-Tvaṣṭṛ battle yields the slaughter of a demon-Brahman-*purohita* Viśvarūpa, which Indra expiates with a hundred sacrifices since sacrifice is one of the alternatives for penance provided for Brahmanicide in the Dharmaśāstras, as we have seen. Here the identity of that expiation with the *Mahāvrata* penance is explicit, but it is in this play tied to the behaviour of a Kāpālika, who claims that Śiva adopted the same penance.

And that is the story we find in arguably the earliest surviving narrative, in the *Matsyapurāṇa* (183.87–104) where Mahādeva, having cut it off in a fit of anger, takes Brahmā’s *kapāla* in his hand. He is condemned by Brahmā to visit the various *tīrtha*s, just as the Dharmaśāstras propound as part of the standard penance. However, the skull continues to be attached so that the penance articulated in the Dharmaśāstras is ineffective, and this point in the story has been to date overlooked, as scholarship has generally sidelined the site-specific purposes of all the Purāṇic narratives. In pursuit of a solution to rid himself of the skull, Mahādeva 

*[p. 19]* ^pg32

visits Nārāyaṇa in the Himalayas and begs from him, apparently now employing the skull as a skull-cup. Yet, the skull-cup cannot be filled with the immeasurable blood Nārāyaṇa offers from cutting the side of his own body for a thousand years of the gods. Finally, Mahādeva goes to Vārāṇasī, to the site of Kapālamocana, where the skull of Brahmā falls off and shatters into a thousand pieces, disappearing into the earth ‘like wealth obtained in a dream’ (*svapnalabdhaṃ dhanaṃ yathā*). This early narrative clearly signals the aggregation of the deity Śiva and his penance in order to attach them both to the specific pilgrimage site of Kapālamocana (release from the skull) in Vārāṇāsī, the city where Śiva never leaves, for he is Avimukteśvara.

Lorenzen (1991: 79) has already shown that the attached skull motif is foreshadowed in the *Śalyaparvan* of the *Mahābhārata* (9.38.8–17), which presents a story of Rāma’s beheading a *rākṣasa* demon, whose head rolled down hill and accidentally happened to pierce the thigh of the sage Mahodara, where it remained immovably attached. Mahodara visited various *tīrtha*s to no avail, finally receiving release of the head at Auśanatīrtha, on the Sārasvatī River, which becomes known as Kapālamocana as a result. It would seem that this designation was appropriated by Śaivas in Vārāṇasī when the new Kapālamocana site was established along with images of Śiva and Kālabhairava, and the myth undoubtedly developed there. Moreover, the shift to Vārāṇasī is the consistent theme of the later narratives of Bhairava beheading Brahmā.

The attachment of Bhairava to the Brahmā-beheading narrative is demonstrably later. Our earliest origin myths for Bhairava do not involve him with Brahmā’s beheading, but instead most clearly involve him in the conflict of the gods with Andhaka, the *daitya*, and the Mothers (*mātara*s) described in *Matsyapurāṇa* Chapter 179; this is identified as the origin story of Bhairava, but it is apparently taken as an epithet of Mahādeva rather than signalling a deity possessing a separate birth and identity. In his fight with the gods, Andhaka has the capacity to generate more of himself from each drop of his blood—a quality also found in the *asura* Raktabīja in the *Mārkaṇḍeyapurāṇa*—and finally, Viṣṇu is called on to assist. Viṣṇu generates a goddess, Śuṣkarevatī, who drinks all the blood, and the thousands of Andhakas produced from the drops of blood then die. Śarva-Śiva is ready to impale the primary Andhaka with his trident, and the demon begs forgiveness, whereupon Andhaka is given permanent Gaṇeśa status (179.40cd *pra* *dadau nityaṃ gaṇeśatvaṃ tathaiva ca*). The Mothers then threaten the universe but are quelled by the Nṛsiṃha form of Viṣṇu, and at the place of their quelling, the Kṛtaśauca-tīrtha was formed. Then the Mothers are given the Raudrī form by Rudra, who became enshrined in their midst, apparently also at Kṛtaśauca-tīrtha ‘the crossing where purification is accomplished’. The dissociation of Bhairava from skulls is reinforced in *Matsya* Chapter 259, where both Śiva’s iconography and Bhairava’s is described. While Śiva is to have at his left side a *kapāla*, a *nāga*, and a *khaṭvāṅga*, Bhairava is simply ferocious: ‘His teeth and the point of his nose are sharp, his great face having tusks. Praised in the world, he is set in every shrine. But Bhairava should not be placed in the main shrine, as he is fearsome’.<sup>7</sup> Instead of necessarily being associated with Brahmanicide, Bhairava thus most closely appears associated with the defeat of Andhaka and with a mythic 

*[p. 20]* ^pg33

unification of local goddesses and pilgrimage sites.<sup>8</sup> We note the *Matsya* combines the formation of a *tīrtha*, the goddess Śuṣkarevatī, and the recasting of the Mātaras as Raudrī in form. As Törzsök has shown, in the old *Skandapurāṇa*, the goddess Udakasevikā—hideously ugly and born from water of Pārvatī’s heart—is paired with Bhairava, himself generated from the ferocious desire (*kāmo bhairavaḥ*) of Śiva for Pārvatī.<sup>9</sup> In both the *Kūrmapurāṇa* and the *Śivapurāṇa*, among others, Kālabhairava takes Śiva’s place in the story of the beheading of Brahmā but is still condemned to follow after the feminine personification of the sin, *Brahmahatyā*, a goddess who leads him to all the pilgrimage sites, finally to disappear once they arrive in Vārāṇasī at Kapālamocana.<sup>10</sup> *Kūrmapurāṇa* describes her as having ferocious attributes, especially the tusks and a flaming *mālā* (*Kūrmapurāṇa* 2.31.67cd: *daṃṣṭrākarālavadanāṃ jvālāmālāvibhūṣaṇām*), and the other goddesses in association with Bhairava are almost invariably ferocious or unattractive.

If we circle back and compare the catalogue of items in the *Niśvāsamukha* against the requirements for penance in the case of Brahmanicide, we can see that the lists have only very modest continuities, primarily through the use of the two distinctive terms from the Dharmaśāstras. The *kapāla* seems different, if we are to believe the consistent Dharmaśāstra tradition, but the *khaṭvāṅga* is the same, and this is recognized in Haradatta’s commentary to the *Gautamadharmasūtra* (4.4: *khaṭvāṅgaṃ pāśupatānāṃ prasiddham*). However, the entire purpose of the penance is clearly the public confession and acknowledgement of the crime, and some Dharmaśāstras indicate that the penitent should recite the *Kūśmāṇḍamantra* as well (i.e., *Vājasaneyisaṃhitā* 20.14–16). None of these vocal events are emulated in the Śaiva context—there is no mandate for constant proclamation of Śiva’s crime of beheading Brahmā, and the *Niśvāsamukha* makes no mention of that myth. Additionally, the clothing for the penance and the Lokātītavrata of the Śaivas is entirely different: the Śaivas dress in a loincloth, whereas the Dharmaśāstras take great care to specify what would be the equivalent of a hair shirt—a garment of the skin of a donkey (*Baudhāyana* 2.3: *gardabhacaramavāsā*) or one woven from the hair of the tail of a cow (*vālavāsā*) or coarse hemp fibres (*śāṇa*) or bark clothing (*Saṃvartasmṛti* 112 *Saṃvartadharmaśāstra* 6.2 *valkavāsā*). Śaivas are to cover themselves in ash, but that is not part of the Dharmaśāstra penance either. In any case, the punishment for Brahmanicide is for 12 years, and there is little indication in the *Niśvāsamukha* of such a defined time for the Lokātītavrata of the Śaivas. Moreover, Śaivas have no mandate to visit every pilgrimage site, for Śiva’s/Bhairava’s penance was not released in any other *tīrtha*, and the Rāmeśvaram site is specifically precluded by Kapālamocana. Consequently, the Dharmaśāstra-related site remedy fails in the Śaiva narrative, as we have seen.

Finally, we may note that according to the *Niśvāsamukha*, Lokātītamārga Śaivas are to wear a chaplet of skulls. This is seemingly attested in Xuanzang’s discussion of Kapīśa, where he mentions that there are ten *deva* temples there, with a thousand followers. They are divided into those who are naked, those smeared with ash, and those who thread together skulls, wearing them as a diadem (*Datang Xiyuji* T.2087.873c21–22: 天祠數十所。異道千餘人。或露形。

*[p. 21]* ^pg34

或塗灰。連絡髑髏以爲冠鬘). While Xuanzang does not identify them as specifically Śaivas, his time is shortly after that of Mahendravarman I of the Pallava Dynasty, the reputed author of the *Mattavilāsa*, so we may have some confidence that the code described in the *Niśvāsamukha* was probably already in effect for some Śaivas, whether or not the *Niśvāsamukha* had yet been composed.

What does this all mean with respect to the carrying of a skull-cup? I believe that we should take the *Śatarudrīya* sections of the *Taittirīyasaṃhitā* (4.5.1–11) and the *Vājasaneyisaṃhitā* (16.1–66)—where Rudra’s attributes are given and no *kapāla* is mentioned—as a point of comparison. Between that text and the later restatement of Rudra’s attributes in the *Vāyupurāṇa* (10.43–67)—where the Rudras thus created do carry skulls (10.46 *vivāsān harikeśāṃś ca dṛṣṭighnāṃś ca kapālinaḥ*)—at some time in that interim, Śiva became equipped with a *kapāla*, most likely carried as a skull-cup rather than as a diadem or *mālā*. As Śaivism matured, Śaivas became involved with Vārāṇasī and appropriated the identity of Kapālamocana from the Auśana-tīrtha and the purificatory function from the Kṛtaśauca-tīrtha. These designations were both linked to an interpretive reading of the Dharmaśāstra penance for Brahmanicide, turning the *kapāla* from its purpose as a sign to that of an implement. The myth was constructed at a moment of the rising tide of sectarianism, and Śaiva authors certainly employed this mythic construct to promote their divinity and disparage the others, but they also realized that it came with a cost. So, while the *Śatarudrasaṃhitā* section of the *Śivapurāṇa* espouses the myth, the *Vidyeśvarasaṃhitā* section (8.1–11) of the same Purāṇa has Śiva relent in commissioning Bhairava to cut off Brahmā’s head (8.8 *nivartayām āsa tadā bhairavaṃ brahmadaṇḍataḥ*) because of the intercession of Viṣṇu so that Brahmā is not beheaded in that version.

The driving force for all of this appears to be the motivation of Śaivas in Vārāṇasī to find an orthodox rationalization for behaviour some apparently had already adopted from an unorthodox source and attributed to Mahādeva—the narrative was, therefore, a moment of mythic legitimation. Seemingly the reason modern scholars have accepted this mythic rationalization invoking an orthodox solution was their desire to base ritual on mythology, much as the Śaivas themselves wished to do. While Lorenzen at one point (1991: 79) voiced uncertainty as to whether the behaviour or the myth came first, he also stated, ‘Every ritual has a divine model or archetype, and the penance Śiva performs is the model of the Mahāvrata penance for the killing of a Brāhmaṇa’ (*ibid*.: 78). Much the same could be said for those more interested in the narratives of the gods than seeing behind all these myths and rituals the evident rationales that the Śaivas at Kapālamocana wanted pilgrim traffic and that Lākulas wanted legitimacy. The ex post facto evolving myth sets the stage for both.

### 1.3 Bones, skulls, demons, and the dead

If the scenario I have outlined has merit—and it appears the best interpretation of the available evidence—it still begs the question as to the primary source of the behaviour in the first place: where did Śaivas get the practice of carrying 

*[p. 22]* ^pg35

skull-cups if not from the Dharmaśāstras? To correctly assess this practice, we might at the beginning acknowledge that the use of skulls as implements of dining and drinking cannot be identified as first occurring within Indian religious traditions at all. The oldest archaeological evidence known to me is from the Upper Palaeolithic site at Gough’s Cave in the United Kingdom (Magdalenian 17K–12K BP), but a plethora of related sites throughout Britain and Europe reveals the evidence of Neolithic and bronze age skull-cup manufacturing practices.<sup>11</sup> Moreover, the archaeology of Göbekli Tepe in Southeast Anatolia has yielded a Neolithic site (9600–700 BCE) that reveals practices ‘distinct to the site of Göbekli Tepe’ involving the sacramental use of skulls and bones (Gresky, Haelm, and Clare 2017: 1).

Indeed, it has long been recognized by historians of Indo-European traditions that the description of Skythians in Herodotus (*Histories* 4.65) relates how these groups made bowls of the skulls of their most hated enemies and drank from them long before Śiva became *kapālin*. This textual narrative has been supported by the excavation at Bel’sk, which uncovered a ‘skull-cup workshop’.<sup>12</sup> Not long after, the early Chinese historical text, the *Shiji*, records the well-known story reported by Zhang Qian (d. 113 BCE) that the nomadic Xiongnu Chanyu cut the head from the king of the Yuezhi and made it into a skull-cup for drinking (匈奴破月氏王以其頭為飲器), thus bringing the practice on the Indian periphery in Central Asia.<sup>13</sup> The proposition by Khazanov and others of related cultural affinities between Central Asian Skythians (Saka) and the Xiongnu nomads has received biological support from the genetic studies of several teams, including the recent study of a Xiongnu family grave by Keyser *et al*. (2020), which conclusively demonstrated that the Xiongnu had intermarried with Scythians from West Asia.<sup>14</sup> Apparently on a continuum with these, Zhou *et al*. (2020) have reviewed recent archaeological literature and concluded,

Meanwhile, from the Zhou Dynasty to Ming Dynasty, another type of decapitation is implemented by victors of the war. The heads of their enemies are collected as trophies to construct a mound named ‘Jingguan’ or the skulls are made into cups, to show military achievement and express contempt to the enemies.

(*ibid*.: 2) Thus, it would be an error to identify the specific ritual practice of consuming food or drink from skulls as having been inaugurated in India, and instead, we might acknowledge that it was very widely distributed, particularly in steppe areas where the horse culture—to which the Skythians and the Xiongnu belonged—was celebrated. These practices involve the themes of warfare and domination of the hated enemy by an opponent, sometimes coupled with an ancillary practice of the ritual preparation of skulls for an ancestral cult.

In Indian literature, related rituals are embedded in the mythologized treatment of Vṛtra by Indra in the *Taittirīyasaṃhitā* to fashion the *droṇakalaśa* vessel used in the *śrauta* rites:

*[p. 23]* ^pg36

*indro vṛtram ahan tasya śīrṣakapālam ud aubjat sa droṇakalaśo ’bhavat tasmāt somas sam asravat | Taittirīyasaṃhitā* 6.5.9 Indra slew Vṛtra; he forced out his skull-bone, it became the wooden tub, from it the Soma flowed.

(Trans. Keith 1914: 2, 544) This appears to be the earliest of such *droṇakalaśa* myths, but not the only one. A similar story on the creation of the *droṇakalaśa* occurs in the *Jaiminīyabrāhmaṇa* (I.73), where Agni is angered by Prajāpati, who wishes to create Agni from his mouth, a polluted orifice; Agni kills the creator, removes his skull, which the gods give to Bṛhaspati to use as the *droṇakalaśa*.<sup>15</sup> Apparently, this was a regular event for Prajāpati; the solar deity Āditya, having been produced by Prajāpati in *Pañcaviṃśabrāhmaṇa* VI.5.1, cut off Prajāpati’s head and similarly created the *droṇakalaśa*.<sup>16</sup> In these Brāhmaṇas, the manufacture of this cranial ritual implement for the *soma* rite was clearly contested, with Bṛhaspati protesting that it was raw and unusable. Yet the *droṇakalaśa*, even if actually made from a wooden tub in the performance of the ritual, was understood to be the remanufacture of an enemy’s skull, much as Brahmā is portrayed as Śiva’s enemy in Purāṇic stories. While we recognize echoes of the Indo-European practice in such narratives, we lack definitive understanding of whether skulls and bones were employed in real events.

It is so well-known that Buddhists established a relic cult surrounding the sanctification of the remnants of the Buddha’s and other saints’ cremation that is often overlooked in its significance for the larger domain of Indian ritual. Yet the preparation, preservation, and worship of the physical vestiges of the Buddha, the arhats, and the various personnel of Buddhist institutions is a fundamental fact related frequently in inscriptions and in texts, as well as in contemporary practice wherever those institutions are still found. Bronkhorst, as a consequence, has argued that the Buddhist employment of bones was unique in India.<sup>17</sup> Equally overlooked is the fact that Buddhist monks are also ritually polluted by their having stepped outside of the system of the *varṇāśramadharma*, accepting food and water from any source, and adhering to none of the caste prohibitions incumbent on the twice-born. Moreover, the Buddhist meditative activity of *aśubhabhāvanā*—contemplation of the unwholesomeness of the physical body, which was intermittently performed in charnel or cremation grounds—led them to involvement with human remains. Greene (2013: 275) translates a section from the *Mahīśāsakavinaya* (T.1421.22.134b28–c4) in which monks are forbidden from dragging human skulls and bones into their residential quarters for meditative purposes, a prohibition required because that behaviour had become a practice in some monasteries. In a different vein, the 3rd-to 4th-century Chinese translation of the story of King Pukkasāti on his begging trip to Rājagṛha has him employing a skull fallen down from an old corpse as a begging bowl, to illustrate his renunciation: ‘Because he didn’t have a utensil to eat and drink with, he went to a cemetery to obtain a skull from a person long dead. He cleaned, scraped and washed it, and took it for his utensil’ 

*[p. 24]* ^pg37

(無飯食應器. 便取塚間. 久死人髑髏. 淨刮洒以爲應器).<sup>18</sup> Clearly, such kinds of behaviour led to various interactions with the other denizens of the charnel grounds.<sup>19</sup>

Thus, within India proper, textual sources that might be worth investigating include the Buddhist and Jain records, for they appear to be better witnesses to the diversity of practices at the margins of the major traditions and provide a baseline of ritual performance long before any form of Śaiva sectarian tradition emerged. Consequently, when we encounter some specific admonitions in the Buddhist *Vinaya* concerning the use of skull caps as drinking bowls, it should command some historical attention:

*tena kho pana samayena aññataro bhikkhu sabbapaṃsukūliko hoti, so chavasīsassa pattaṃ dhāreti. aññatarā itthi passitvā bhītā vissaram akāsi:* *abbhuṃ me, pisāco vata man ti. manussā ujjhāyanti khīyanti vipācenti:* *kathaṃ hi nāma samaṇā Sakyaputtiyā chavasīsassa pattaṃ dhāressanti seyyathāpi pisācillikā ’ti*.

*bhagavato etam atthaṃ ārocesuṃ. na bhikkhave chavasīsassa patto dhāretabbo. yo dhāreyya, āpatti dukkaṭassa. na ca bhikkhave sabbapaṃ* *sukūlikena bhavitabbaṃ. yo bhaveyya, āpatti dukkaṭassā ’ti. Vinayapiṭaka Cullavagga* 2.115 So then at that time, there was some *bhikṣu* who practiced in all the circumstances the picking of his robes from discarded rags (*paṃsukūlika*). He also made a begging bowl from the skull of a corpse. Some woman saw him and became frightened and screamed, ‘How horrible! A Piśāca is coming for me!’<sup>20</sup>

People became angry, offended and annoyed. ‘How is it that these “disciplined sons of the Buddha” carry around skulls as begging bowls, just like the *piśāca-*cultists?’

\[The story was related to the Lord.\] The Lord announced, ‘Do not, O monks, make a begging bowl out of a skull. Who carries one incurs a fault. Nor should one be exclusively wearing robes picked from rags.’ This episode informs us that there was a recognizable group of men who carried a skull-cup as a begging bowl and that they were associated with cults to the *piśāca* spirits who feast on flesh, generally in charnel grounds. It is furthermore interesting that the attribute of carrying skulls as begging bowls would be associated in some way with the ascetic practice of making garments exclusively out of rags—which included rags selected from charnel grounds—and that the tradition foregrounds the skull-cup as the more offensive of the two behaviours. It also affirms that, as we saw in the case of Pukkasāti, some monks did exactly this so that the prohibition was required.

However, this warning in the Pali *Vinaya* is not the only ominous attribute of the ‘*piśāca-*cultists’ recorded in the Pali canon. In an earlier place in the *Vinaya*, the *Mahāvagga*, monks were depicted as taking their rains retreat in 

*[p. 25]* ^pg38

a hollowed-out tree (*bhikkhū rukkhasusire vassaṃ upagacchanti*), and the same response happened, that this seemed to be emulating *piśāca-*cultists, who lived in hollowed-out trees so that the practice became forbidden (*Vinayapiṭaka* 1.152). More bizarrely, some monks elected to allow their nasal hair to grow long (*dīghāni nāsikālomāni dhārenti*), about which the populace complained that this was just like the dreaded *piśāca-*cultists (*Vinayapiṭaka* 2.134).

Were these the only instances of the practices associated with *piśāca* spirit cults, we might wonder at the isolated citation, but that is not the case. In the third book (*prapāṭaka*) of the *Sāmavidhānabrāhmaṇa*—largely devoted to optional (*kāmya*) rites—the text discusses a similar practice:

3.7.3 *atha yaḥ kāmayeta piśācān guṇībhūtān paśyeyam iti saṃvatsaraṃ caturthe kāle bhañjānaḥ kapālena bhaikṣaṃ caran prāṇāḥ śiśur ity antyaṃ sadā sahasrakṛtvaḥ āvartayan paśyati* ||

3.7.4 *ayācitam etena kalpena dvitīyaṃ prayuñjānaḥ pitṝn paśyati* ||

Now one who might wish, ‘I would like to see *piśāca*s under my control’ should do as follows. Eating but the evening meal every other day for a year, while begging a meal with a skull-cup, he should recite the final *sāman* ‘*prāṇāḥ śiśur*…’ (*Grāmageyagāṇam* 570/5). Reciting this a thousand times a day \[for the year\] he will see (*piśāca*s under his control). 3.7.3

Employing this same ritual—but reciting the second *sāman* ‘*prāṇāḥ śiśur*…’ (*Grāmageyagāṇam* 570/2) for a year—\[and begging with a skull-- cup, but\] not specifically requesting food (i.e., silently begging), he will see the Fathers. 3.7.4 One of the curiosities of this statement is that, whereas the *piśāca*s will be brought under the control of the practitioner (*guṇībhū* glossed as *vaśīkṛ*), the Fathers will be seen rather than controlled. I would interpret this as an orthodox attempt to differentiate by theological typology, whereas it is doubtful that the earlier statement in the *Mahāvagga* referenced a group that observed such selective assignments.

We also note that both the commentators (Sāyana, Bharatasvāmin) deny this ritual is to be done with a skull-cup but instead affirm that it refers to a piece of a cracked pot (Bharatasvāmin: *kapālena bhinnaghaṭādyekadeśena*), the other meaning of *kapāla*. Yet since its origin derives from a cult to *piśāca*s—as opposed to being an orthodox penance—it is clear in this instance that it is an interpretive strategy to validate the practice in an orthodox environment and to bring it into line with normative expectations. Bharatasvāmin also includes the Fathers and the Gods (*prayuñjānaḥ pitṝn pitṛdevān paśyati*) to be seen by the practitioner so  that the movement from demon to ancestor to divinity is acknowledged here. Certainly, it would be an error to attribute the *Sāmavidhānabrāhmaṇa* the same antiquity as the *Śatapathabrāhmaṇa*, the *Jaiminīyabrāhmaṇa*, or the *Pañcaviṃśabrāhmaṇa*. It is much later, and despite its name, it is properly a member of the *vidhāna* supplementary literature of the Gṛhyasūtras, but that still 

*[p. 26]* ^pg39

situates it comfortably before the *Niśvāsamukha* period.<sup>21</sup> The early mention of Kāpālika behaviour in *Gāthāsaptaśatī* 5.8 may reference these earlier cultic activities since they were exhibited in several traditions.

One of the unfortunate suppositions made by some scholars studying the sectarian Tantric traditions is that of ‘absorption’: the assumption that, once a sectarian author or tradition incorporates a behaviour into the sectarian literature, it suddenly disappears. I have already argued that in the case of magical performance groups—highly influential for Tantric traditions during their entire history—the absorption model is unwarranted (Davidson 2017). This appears to be another instance, for we encounter a much modified but still recognizable example of the practice later in the *Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha*. There Naravāhanadatta, who is to become the Vidyādharacakravartin, is passing his time in Champa with friends and his wife, Gandharvadattā.

*ekadā prāsakaiḥ krīḍan saha gandharvadattayā* |
*sahasā pramadāveṣam apaśyaṃ puruṣaṃ puraḥ* ||19.2 *kapālaśikhipiñchābhyāṃ virājitakaradvayam* |
*ayuktendradhanuśchāyaśṛṅgāragalakaṇṭhikam* ||19.3 Once, playing dice with Gandharvadattā, suddenly I saw a man before me, dressed like a young woman.

His two hands were resplendent with a *kapāla* and a peacock feather. The ornament at his neck was sensuous like the colours separated from a rainbow.

Naravāhanadatta cannot understand when his wife immediately acts as if this person is a great guest, washing his feet and placing her own ornaments on him. For his part, Naravāhanadatta is incensed and makes caustic comments, with a surprising result:

*eṣa strīpuruṣaḥ śocyo yo na strī na pumān iti* ||19.6 *mām uddiśya tatas tena krodhāruṇitacakṣuṣā |* *vadatā nihato ’sīti vimuktaḥ śikhipicchakaḥ* ||19.7 *sa me keśakalāpāgram īṣad āmṛśya yātavān |* *nāgeṣur iva karṇāstraḥ kirīṭāgraṃ kirīṭinaḥ* ||19.8 *svayam eva ca tat tasya kapālam apatat karāt |* *niḥsthāmnaḥ kuñjarasyeva vidhānaṃ vinaśiṣyataḥ* ||19.9 *so ’tha grāmeyakeneva dhiyā dhūrto ’tisaṃdhitaḥ |* *dhūmrachāyaḥ śanair jalpan dhig dhiṅ mām iti nirgataḥ* ||19.10 \[Narvāhanadatta continues:\]

‘This girly-man is pathetic—neither woman nor man!’

Then he gestured toward me, his eyes inflamed with anger, Saying, ‘you’re a dead man’, he released the peacock feather,

*[p. 27]* ^pg40

which glided past my piled up hair on a glancing blow, like Karṇa’s *nāga-*arrow off the top of Arjuna’s crown.

Just then, of its own, the *kapāla* dropped from his hand, like fodder from \[the trunk of\] a weak, dying elephant.

And he was like a thief who was himself cheated by a wily villager—his face drained of color and quietly muttering, ‘Damn, I’m screwed!’ he departed.

Naravāhanadatta’s wife Gandharvadattā tells him to calm down and explains that she had treated this transvestite man the way she did because he is Vikacika, the brother of the King of Sorcerers, and is a *sādhaka* of Gaurī engaged in the *bhūtavrata* (spirit’s vow) that has many problems. Because she had greeted him the way she did, he was robbed of the power of his spell, which relied on the disparagement of him by others.

The explanation articulates three terms for this practice: *bhūtavrata*, *gaurīvrata*, and *mahāvrata*. Given what we know of the period, it seems that the *Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha* describes an evolving practice in which three elements are brought together: (1) the cultic behaviours associated with the *piśāca* spirits, here granted the more general designation of *bhūta* ‘spirit’; (2) a practice of dressing in women’s clothing and carrying a peacock feather associated with goddess worship generally; and (3) the ideology that power may be gained from unmerited disparagement by others, most notable in the case of the Pāśupatas. The designation *mahāvrata* here does not seem to identify the *kāpālika* penance for Brahmanicide and instead is more generally a ‘great practice’. There can be little doubt that men dressed in women’s clothing for religious reasons, often for the purpose of propitiating goddesses, and the history of this practice is too lengthy and too tangential to the present discussion to be pursued.

In the instances of the practice associated with the *piśāca*s, the purposes appear standard: securing powers by emulating the behaviour of *piśāca*s through carrying a skull-cup and living in a hollowed-out tree, seeing *piśāca*s for the purpose of control and manipulation, and associating with polluted or transgressive materials to secure powers associated with goddesses and sorcerers. As these goals are not much different from the kinds of benefits (*vara*) obtained from the gods, *yakṣa*s, and other supernatural sources, we may ask, why were the *piśāca*s thus pursued? These are demonic spirits, after all, noted for feasting on human flesh in the charnel grounds and the possession of unwary monks. Some notices identify the impotence of *piśāca*s, such as the *Ayakūṭājātaka* (*Jātaka-aṭṭhakathā* 3.146–7) where their howls are brushed aside. But another place in the Pali *Vinaya* indicates that the affliction of *piśāca* possession is a legitimate cause for the shortening of the rains retreat (*Mahāvagga Vinaya* I.149). In some Buddhist texts, the *piśāca*s are treated as simply deluded spirits, such as the amusing tale of the two *piśāca*s in the *Hundred Examples Sūtra* (百喩經). Two *piśāca*s are depicted haggling over a magic bag, a magic staff, and a magic pair of clogs, none of which they could control; they were eventually duped out of their magical items by a sagacious man 

*[p. 28]* ^pg41

who tricked them into surrendering the three bits of equipment (*Baiyujing* T.209.4.549b1–14). Other notices of the *piśāca*s, however, suggest greater power and authority for some and the keeping of magical elements for others.

The most baroque, awe-inspiring *piśāca* description I have seen is in the Jain *Nāyādhammakahāo*. There is much that could be noted in the extensive description in the text, of which the above is only the most interesting part of a more lengthy, somewhat redundant portrayal. However, for our purposes, the assignment of polluted substances with the *piśāca* is of great interest, as is the statement, ‘His sign as a *piśāca* was that he was enveloped by a garland of human heads’ (*nara-sira-māla*). Here the attribute is symbolic, as in the Dharmaśāstras, but for very different reasons. Yet the text proposes we understand that *nara-sira-māla* is a primary signum for a *piśāca*, language similar to the description in the Pali *Vinaya*, seen earlier.

An argument could be made that the *piśāca* description in the *Nāyādhammakahāo* appears to be a literate version of site-specific understandings, where those in charge of local cult sites to *piśāca*s would elaborate on their attributes. Specific sites associated with *piśāca*s are relatively rare, but two areas at least are evident in the epigraphic and literate record. The first is in the Valkhā kingdom in the temples of Bappa-piśācadeva. The horde of copper-plate grants discovered in 1982 yielded four grants by the Valkhā King Mahārāja Bhuluṇḍa to those temples; two grants are dated Gupta Era 54 (374 CE) and one each in Gupta Era 57 (377 CE) and 59 (379 CE). In the first three, the inscriptions indicate that the deity has been installed by Bhojikābhaṭṭa Bandhulā at Valkhā, whereas in the final grant, another temple to Bappa-piśācadeva is mentioned at Narmmadāparapāraviṣaya.<sup>25</sup> In each of  these grants, it maintains that Pāśupatas and temple servants (*deva* *prasādaka*/*devakarmmiṇa*) are to attend to the villages granted to the deity. Again, Indologists have attempted to interpret these through the lens of received traditions, whether as ancestral temples—interpreting *piśāca* as if it were coterminous with *pitṛ*—or as redolent of Śaivism. The simpler and more elegant interpretation is to affirm exactly what the inscription says: the two temples are sites of the *piśāca* Bappa, so they are places where the worship of the spirit is acknowledged. The other place of *piśāca* worship is in Kaśmīra, where the worship of *piśāca*s is 

*[p. 30]* ^pg43

acknowledged in the *Nīlamatapurāṇa* (659–665). On the 12th of the bright half of the month of Caitra, an image of a *piśāca* is to be made out of clay and leaves, installed in every house, to which is offered meat, liquor, weapons, cakes, etc. A  similar series of offerings for *piśāca*s is mentioned in the *Vāyupurāṇa* (69.286–288).

Spirit categories in India are certainly not impermeable, and the overlap between *piśāca*s and *bhūta*s has already been seen in the carrying of the skull-- cup, from the *Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha*. Chapter 69 of the received *Vāyupurāṇa* contains physical and ideological descriptions of spirits on a continuum with the *Nāyādhammakahāo*, while also outlining several tortuous genealogies for *bhūta*s, *rākṣasa*s, and *piśāca*s, apparently combining several sources into an aggregate narrative of these spirits. Four pericope are of particular interest: (1) the two sons of Kaśyapa and Khaśā became *yakṣa*s and *rākṣasa*s, and the younger marries the daughters of the *piśāca*s Aja and Khaṇḍa, by whom generations of spirits are born (*Vāyupurāṇa* 69.75–163); (2) *piśāca*s have 16 *gaṇa*s and 16 *kula*s, each with their special abilities and attributes, even if all of them are described as lesser in power than *yakṣa*s and *rākṣasa*s and of lower intelligence (*Vāyupurāṇa* 69.257–279); (3) those who worship *piśāca*s—who live in deserted areas, *caitya* trees, and side paths—are assigned the livelihood of artisans and craftsmen, and are *adharmika* people living in violation of the *varṇāśramadharma* (*Vāyupurāṇa* 69.271–285); and (4) even though both *bhūta*s and *piśāca*s are under the dominion of Giriśa (‘inhabiting mountains’, i.e. Rudra-Śiva), only the *bhūta*s are devoted to him (*Vāyupurāṇa* 69.242–256, 289).<sup>26</sup>

In the *Vāyu* (69.252), it is the *bhūta*s, not the *piśāca*s, who are *kapālin*s, and this is in consonance with other descriptions of malevolent spirits. So, in the mention of a ‘black-cloud woman with a *kapāla* in her hand’ (*strī meghakālī tu kapālahastā*) among the demon army (*bhūtacamū*) of Māra in *Buddhacarita* 13.49, the text reflects the expectations of spirit equipment. And in a collection of *avadāna*s translated by Guṇabhadra between 435 and 443 CE, Māra is said to have two sisters—Meghā and Kālī—each holding a skull-cup in her hand (*Guoqu xianzai yinguo jing* T.189.3.640c24–26: 魔有姊妹.一名彌伽.二名迦利.各各以 手執髑髏器). Likewise, one category of the dreaded spirits that afflicts the birth of a son is ‘the night-movers, with ornaments on their chests, lances in their hands, drinking from skull-cups’ (*Hiraṇyakeśigṛhyasūtra* 2.3.7: *naktaṃcāriṇa uraspeśāñ chūlahastān kapālapān* = *Āpastambīya Mantrapāṭha* 2.14.1). Indeed, the list of demons in some of the Gṛhyasūtras that are warded off at the birth of a child overlaps with some names in the sixteen families of the *piśāca*s identified in the *Vāyupurāṇa*, mentioned earlier, suggesting a distribution of spirit lore in North India.

The most robust description of spirits with skull-cups I have encountered is in a description of *rākṣasa*s causing an epidemic in Uḍḍiyāna-nagara, in a mythology associated with Kaniṣka translated between 351–431 CE. After half the population has died, Kaniṣka decides to put an end to the carnage. In an episode that foreshadows later stories of kings and spirits, Kaniṣka smears his body with magical medicine, ties a mantrified noose around himself (*mantrapāśa*), puts on 

*[p. 31]* ^pg44

his armour, and grasps his sword. He surveys the neighbourhood and meets several *rākṣasa*s, each with more heads than the next, all described in a manner similar to that in the *Vāyupurāṇa* and the *Nāyādhammakahāo*. Kaniṣka finally encounters the chief epidemic spirit, an enormous *rākṣasa* surrounded by a sea of *rākṣasa*s. The chief demon is conquered by Kaniṣka’s wiles and the magical medicine on his body. But in most cases, the *rākṣasa*s are equipped with a skull-cup, each ‘holding in their hands a \[human\] skull-cup filled to the brim with blood, fat  and brains’ (*Wuming luocha ji* T.720.16.851b8 手捉髑髏器盛滿血髓腦). That is their most recognizable quality, beyond their propensity for impurity and destruction.

### 1.4 Skulls—Liminal signs of the spirit world

In these instances, and many others too numerous to detail in the space of a short chapter, central among its several meanings is that the skull operates as a liminal sign mediating the human realm and the spirit world. In this, it is analogous to the  significance of its complements—ritual beheading and raising the dead (*vetāla/vetāḍa*). The liminal status of the Brahmanicide is signified by the skull, as we have seen, and only when Śiva has completed his penance for beheading Brahmā does the skull fall at Kapālamocana-tīrtha. The skull’s power as a sign is the trope invoked by *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* 9.25, where the astrological conflict between Rohiṇī and Venus causes Vasudhā, the earth goddess, to be covered with bones of the dead, as if she were observing the *kapālavrata*, thus being marked as a place of the spirits of the dead.

Similarly in the *Mārkaṇḍeyapurāṇa*, when Dharma approaches Hariścandra with a skull in his hand, his appearance represents a prototype that contributed to the ritualized behaviour of the Lākulas and Kāpālikas.

The death of Dharma is the focus of a folk-*śrāddha* ritual in the *Kaccānijātaka*, where a woman is expelled by her son and daughter-in-law, who subsequently have a son. Deciding that Dharma must be dead, she goes to a charnel ground carrying rice, ground sesame, a pot and spoon; builds an oven of three skulls; and begins to cook the rice as an offering to Dharma. (*Jātaka-aṭṭhakathā* 3.425: ‘*dhammassa matakabhattaṃ dassāmīti*’ *sā ekadivasaṃ tilapiṭṭhañ ca taṇḍulañ ca pacanathālikañ ca divviñ cādāya āmakasusānaṃ gantvā tīhi manussasīsehi uddhanaṃ katvā aggiṃ jāletvā … taṇḍule dhovituṃ ārabhi*). The oven (*uddhana*) of skulls seems gratuitous—*śrāddha-homa* is not performed with a skull oven—until it becomes apparent that it is the sign of the folk offering to the spirit of deceased Dharma, about which the bodhisattva incarnated as Śakra immediately responds.

The centrality of a skull conveying information between the human realm and the divine is also found in the story of Vaṅgīsa, the skull-tapper, a translated version of which is attributed to Gautama Saṃghadeva in 397 CE.<sup>27</sup> In the story, the future Arhat Vaṅgīsa makes a living in Rājagṛha by securing information derived from tapping on skulls of the dead. He is accorded the capacity of learning in which state of existence a person has been reborn and makes his living telling the meaning of their rebirths, satisfying their anxious kinsmen. The Buddha plays a trick on Vaṅgīsa by setting up five skulls—four whose rebirths were in the four destinies of hell, animal, human, and god—along with a fifth, the skull of an Arhat. Vaṅgīsa correctly identifies the rebirths of the first four but can’t comprehend the rebirth of the Arhat. He asks the Buddha for this mantra that would allow him knowledge (*Dhammapadaṭṭhakathā* IV.227: *detha me imam mantam ti*), but the Buddha won’t teach him unless he becomes a monk. Vaṅgīsa’s hagiography thus embeds a clever narrative whose plot turns on the trope of an Arhat’s non-rebirth, yet also reinforces the conceptual framework that—for those who know—skulls yield information about the destiny of the spirits of their previous owners.

Ironically, the attribute of skulls to divine the future was apparently employed with the *uṣṇīṣa* relic of the Buddha, according to Xuanzang. At Haḍḍa, in Kapīśa (Afghanistan), the *uṣṇīṣa* relic was featured, especially in the festival surrounding the relic. Individuals (probably wealthy) would be able to employ the relic to answer questions of fortune. ‘Those wishing to know good or bad signs (of the future) could take scented powder mixed into a paste and impress it on the Buddha’s *uṣṇīṣa* relic; then their fortune would emerge and the text of the answer would radiate out (through the paste)’ (*Datang Xiyuji* T.2087.879b2–3: 欲知善惡相者. 香末和埿以印頂骨. 隨其福感其文煥然). ### 1.5 Conclusion

The employment of skull-cups by the Lākulas and the Kāpālikas cannot be confidently assigned to the emulation of Bhairava’s expiation for the murder of Brahmā since a review of Dharmaśāstra statements yields reasonable doubt that this was actually done as represented in both sectarian Śaiva and modern scholarly literature. Instead, the use of skulls as bowls is most closely associated with spirit cults, especially those concerning the *piśāca*s. Most of the non-- sectarian tangents foregrounded in scholarly literature as ‘these may have been Śaivas/Kāpālikas’ can be shown to have been more convincingly connected with a wider distribution of spirit-cult behaviour and, incidentally, constitutes evidence of the ‘fallacy of possibility’ where something that might be the case is taken to be validated without any actual supporting evidence. Conversely, the evidence for early Indo-European employment of skull-cups from the heads of detested enemies—apparently attested in the mythology of the formation of the *droṇakalaśa* in the *soma* rites—is suggestive of a larger ritual field of skull employment from a very early period. It is likely, given the robust data, that this use played an important part in the employment of the *kapāla* in mediaeval Indian religion.

Given the explicit association of Pāśupatas with the *piśāca* temples in the Valkhā kingdom in the late 4th century, and the well-known Śaiva strength in Kaśmīra, it would seem that these two locales would be natural places for the prior practice of employing a skull as a cup to have been transferred to followers of Śiva as Bhūteśvara, the lord of the spirits. Other possibilities, of course, may be proposed, but these places have the virtue of being indicative of a specific cultic system that is harmonic with that found in Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical literature. The fact that the Kaśmīra version also identifies offerings that eventually become offerings associated with Bhairava, among others, would also speak of some degree of ritual continuity, although further information would assist in developing the model of the appropriation of this behaviour by Śaivas and others.

Thus, specific conclusions may be proposed, based on the evidence assembled in this chapter:

	•	

The ancient Indo-European employment of skulls was attested early, and the use of skulls as cups appears in the mythology of the formation of the *droṇa-* *kalaśa* in the *Taittirīyasaṃhitā* and early Brāhmaṇa literature.

	•	

Shortly before the common era, the employment of skulls as cups was noted in the Pali *Vinaya* as emblematic of those involved in cultic systems associated with *piśāca*s, verified by later notices in the *Sāmavidhānabrāhmaṇa* and the *Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha*.

	•	

Skulls and skull-cups operated as mediating signs of the spirit world and were important attributes of *piśāca*s, *bhūta*s, and *rākṣasa*s, as well as anyone involved with these.

At some unspecified time, Rudra-Śiva became iconographically equipped with a skull-cup, influenced by spirit cults, in his position as Bhūteśvara.

*[p. 34]* ^pg47

There is little evidence that the *kapāla* in the Dharmaśāstras was used as a skull-cup, but rather it constituted an emblem of crime and penance. •	

The mythology of the decapitation of Brahmā was formulated to validate the prior iconography of Śiva and the prior employment of skulls by Śaivas of various types and to tie both of these to the promotion of the new site of Kapālamocana in Vārāṇasī, for the purposes of patronage and status. To do this, the Dharmaśāstra penance was re-interpreted so that the *kapāla* as a sign (*cihna*) became a *kapāla* as a bowl (*pātra*).

By no means does this exhaust the topic of the employment of skulls or bones in Indian ritual environments in the classical and mediaeval eras. Skulls are noted in a variety of mythological and ritual environments, whether material or ideological. Bones are also noted elsewhere, but space prohibits following up on most of these tangents at this time.
