## Brudzińska po przełamaniu

*Jagna Brudzińska* 

LIVED BODY AND INTENTIONAL EMBODIMENT. 

NEW PERSPECTIVES 

ON PHENOMENOLOGICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

*ABSTRACT* 

The body, the bodily condition of the human being, or embodiment as an essential aspect of the human situation in the lived world are important topics of phenomenological research and phenomenologically oriented anthropology. On the other hand, today also cognitive research and neurosciences are dealing with the topic of embodiment, mainly focusing on so-called *embodied cognition*. Modern neuroscience claims that both, thought and action can only be interpreted in the light of interactions between brain, body and environment. New trends in phenomenology stress their familiarity with this position and focus on *naturalizing phenomenology*. In my view, this development disregards fundamental Husserlian claims concerning the naturalization of human subjectivity. In order to avoid such naturalizing effects, I focus on the transcendental-phenomenological interpretation of the lived body, and underline the intentional-genetic potential of Husserlian analyses. On this path, instead of relying on naturalizing embodiment, I develop a genetic understanding of the *intentional embodiment* of subjectivity and describe a peculiar form of intentionality as *trans-bodily intentionality*, thereby stressing its anthropological and socio-theoretical significance. 

Keywords: lived body, embodiment, genetic, intentional, kinaesthetic, movement, naturalization, transcendental, trans-bodily, phenomenology, anthropology, Edmund Husserl. 

1\. NATURALISTIC AND NON-NATURALISTIC APPROACHES 

TO SUBJECTIVITY 

The body, the bodily condition of the human being or—in recent debates—embodiment as an essential aspect of the human situation in the lived world have been regarded for a long time as important topics of phenomenological 

*[p. 246]* ^pg248

research and phenomenologically oriented anthropology.[^1] Engagement with this field of research in phenomenology often ties in with the now classical theses of Maurice Merleau-Ponty,[^2] and reaches out to contemporary research inspired by cognitive science, psychology, and psychopathology, such as the work of Thomas Fuchs, Dan Zahavi or Dieter Lohmar.[^3] Cognitive research, in turn, nowadays takes into account the phenomenological perspective, e.g. Francisco Varela or Evan Thompson.[^4] Both, cognitive research and contemporary phenomenology are committed to modern neuroscience. It is the question of embodied cognition that brings these diverse research approaches together. 

In fact, embodied cognition has attracted the attention of neuroscientists for a while now. So far, one of the basic assumptions of neuroscience has been the conviction that the brain is the supreme controller responsible for generating thought and action by translating abstract representations of the world into commands to the body. However, newer approaches revise this view. According to new interpretations not the brain itself generates thought and action. It might rather be the case that cognitive processes are rooted in the body’s dynamic and reciprocal interactions with the environment.[^5] Modern neuroscience claims that both, thought and action, can only be interpreted in the light of interactions between brain, body and environment.[^6]

2\. HUSSERL ON THE LIVED AND MATERIAL BODY 

Husserl’s approach aims at the description of a bodily subject within a meaningful world. For this reason, he introduces the concept of life-world into the general frame of transcendental philosophy, thereby suggesting that only an evidence-loaded life-world can provide the right and complex horizon for the understanding of the embodied subject. Already in *Ideas II* Husserl distinguishes between the body as a material substratum (*Körper*) and the body as an organ of expression (*Leib*).[^13] This differentiation already challenged the idea of the body as a passive materiality. But the idea of *Leib* as an internal complement to the material body, i.e. as the same body *felt and lived from within* is not enough to explain the originality of Husserl’s position. 

The lived body is not restricted to the borders of the material *Körper*. Its functions and potentials reach far beyond the physical position of the material body in the here and now. The lived body as a dynamic component of living subjective experience is able to influence the world in a broad sense; its effects upon reality are broader than the causal succession of facts that a hypothetical pure nature could account for. Even the separation of different subjects by means of their physical bodies has to be put into question. The lived body is better to be understood as the bearer of relations and connections than the source of distance and separation. 

The differentiation of lived and material body further leads to the discovery of different experiential attitudes, i.e. the naturalistic and personalistic attitude in human experience[^14] and, furthermore, the transcendental-anthropological attitude.[^15]

The personalistic attitude stresses the social positioning of the person in the lifeworld. And the social character itself refers to the original capabilities of the lived body as an organ of expression and medium of communication. As such, the personalistic attitude is not an artificial one. According to Husserl, it is on the contrary our entirely ordinary attitude; even more than that: Husserl stresses that the so-called naturalistic attitude is subordinated to the (natural) personalistic attitude.[^16] This attitude provides the grounds for the transcendental reduction of concrete subjectivity enclosing the originally bodily, practical and social character of the transcendental person. 

We can find the roots of this position even earlier than the *Ideas II*, in Husserl’s *Grundprobleme der Phänomenologie* lectures from 1910/11. Here, the question of the personal body arises within the context of the development of the idea of transcendental experience, which is not accounted for by the theory of essence.[^17] This line of thought will be further unfolded in the lectures on ethics (1920),[^18] in the already mentioned *Berliner Vortrag* (1931), in his late manuscripts on intersubjectivity and temporality from the 1930s, and last but not least in works related to the *Crisis* (1936).[^19] As we can see, questions concerning the body cannot be considered as delimited problems within phenomenology. The problem of phenomenologically accounting for the body much rather is at the heart of the peculiarity of the phenomenological setting and the self-understanding of Husserl’s philosophy as a revolutionary approach to transcendental philosophy. 

The most important discovery of this approach does not consist in describing a purely abstract correlation between subject and object, but rather is tuned towards the dynamic interrelation and mutual determination between an acting, intentional, bodily, affective person and (at the same time) a complex, manifold, and meaningful world. The lived body is not only connected with the subject of the personalistic or transcendental-personalistic attitude, but is, in fact, its constituting condition. While the physical body as a material datum is measurable from outside, obeys causal and physiological laws and openly belongs to the constituted natural world, the lived body is seen the constitutive one, as the subjective and communicative center of the personal world. The lived body with its perceptive functions, its differentiated sensitiveness, and its movements is the zero-point of all orientation. It is not a delimited thing, but rather a system of sensations which are, in a sense, free from a strict localization in space. The sensations of the living body are not originally and immediately localized in

3\. THE LIVED BODY AS THE SYSTEM 

OF INTENTIONAL CONSTITUTION OF SENSE 

In 1927 in a manuscript posthumously published in the second volume of Husserliana dedicated to intersubjectivity with the title *Die Innerleiblichkeit.* *“Das ‘Psychophysische’ in der originalen Erfahrung,”*[^22] Husserl deals with the manifold subjective modalities which are all concentrated in a single lived body. His idea is that the lived body entails and expresses different moments of one and the same subjectivity. From the transcendental-phenomenological point of view, the perceptive lived body, the subject of perception, is characterized by the capacity to move. This is not merely movement in a geometrical, neutral space. Husserl rather employs the term kinesthesis to indicate a movement which is at the same time sensation and self-sensation of the body, i.e. the self-sensation of movement itself. This is a genuinely subjective ability. However, Husserl’s analyses are not restricted to the double-structure of the bodily sensation of subjective movement. He further develops his intentional-genetic perspective to make sense of this phenomenon. This allows him to see kinesthesis as a transcendental moment of consciousness, and to put it in the context of the inner teleology of subjective life. We are thus pointed towards the goaldirectedness, the voluntary trends of life and the subjective development of the inner processes. The movement of the body does not happen in the outer world and is not merely regulated by linear causal regularities. It rather unfolds in a multi polar inner connection of personal tendencies and willings, in the striving toward goals and meanings. These teleological inner processes underlie the lawfulness of motivation. They are not explicable on the basis of mere causal laws. They do not even take place in a naturalistically understood outer world, but in the inner world of our personal development, biography, culture, desires

It is a completely subjective achievement, it is part of my inner life and performance. All these external events do not affect me as a mere natural being, an object among objects. Moreover, these analyses open up towards the formulation of the intentional-genetic meaning of the fundamental bodily experience. We are thereby turned towards the role of the body in the genesis of subjectivity, in the process of the self-constitution of the I as a concrete person in the life-world. 

4\. THE BODY AND THE I 

In the 1930s Husserl sees the bodily subject as a temporal structure connected to a flowing egological being. This interpretation expands the meaning of the “lived” body. To live means to be embedded in a temporal stream of consciousness. This stream of consciousness is not temporal in the simple sense of a line-

This whole stream of consciousness does not exist and work in an abstract, immaterial dimension. It rather explicates itself in the dynamics of kinesthesis, with its motivated and motivating potentialities and activities. From the experienced inner perspective, all this striving complexion of experiences appears as organized in a kinaesthetic system and lies under a kinaesthetic principle. The implementation of this complex and dynamic sphere of experience into the egological characterization of subjective life requires a new interpretation of the I itself. Husserl speaks of the I in this context as a “centering pole of potentialities (*Vermöglichkeiten*), of Kinaesthesia.”[^28] The egological sphere is no longer centred on an abstract I, but rather evokes a concrete subject of actual potentialities, of practical possibilities, faculties, tendencies. These are expressed in kinesthesis where kinesthesis is taken to mean the general potential for movement. 

In his analyses, Husserl focuses on one ambiguity of the body in particular, namely that it can be understood both as the subject and the object of living processes. From the transcendental phenomenological perspective, the ego can be taken into consideration in this double sense. On the one hand, the transcendental subject is a concrete embodied I. Here the lived body has the function of mundanisation (*Verweltlichung*) of the transcendental subject. (And *mundanisation* does not mean *naturalization*!). On the other hand, the I itself is a “subjectivized body (lived-body-turned-I)” (*verichlichter Leibkörper*).[^29] From the intentional-genetic perspective, especially the latter presents us with a crucial discovery. Thus, thinking of the I means that the starting point of subjectivity does not coincide with its cognitive performances, representations, decisions a.s.o. as processes that appear to be relatively independent from the body. Much rather, the I as subject arises from actual bodily activity. The body is neither a complement nor an obstacle to the egological activities. Accordingly, any and all presupposed body-mind dualism has to be completely overcome and dismissed if we want to make sense of the complexity of the bodily experience as the proper root of crucial subjective performances and effect a theoretical account of our lived experiences. 

This analysis should not be misunderstood to amount to a naturalism of the ego. I am not pleading for some kind of emergentist position, according to which the natural, material, biological elements of life precede and determine the formation of the subject, and subjective performances only appear as superficial features of physical life. The need itself which is the motor of this activity is not a pre-delineated, not a ‘known’ need, not a desire, not a representation. It is rather a blind direction towards an unknown, undefined goal which will be defined only when it is achieved. The fulfilment of this primary tendency within the intentional process completely relies on the kinaesthetic capacity of the body, and at the same time makes place for the consecutive rise of the properly subjective. This aspect leads us to a further fundamental moment of bodily experience: the intersubjective dimension of our subjective bodily life. 

4\. TRANS-BODILY INTENTIONALITY 

In the the analyses of drives and instincts and primary affections, Husserl introduces the notion of “pre-I” and describes its first genetic-intentional activities.[^32] These activities indicate a form of intentionality based on the deepest, bodily and non-representational levels of subjective experience—a kind of preintentionality

. They are preliminary tendencies that go through my body before they come to a precise, mental representation of goals and aims. However, the general structure of intentionality is here preserved. It is the structure of fulfilment. Already in the *Sixth Logical Investigation* (1901) Husserl describes this intentional relation as a dynamic connection between the so-called empty intention and fulfilment.[^33] The former designates a course of being-directed, while the latter points towards the goal, the striven evidence that orients the experiential process. Yet, in the *Logical Investigations* the subject of this activity is still an *empirical* human being in the real world; it is a psychophysical being bound by a physical body. 

 We are still used to think about intentional activity as connected and bound to the real conditions of experience. We should be aware, though, that, transcendental phenomenology defines this connection in a completely new way. To repeat a point made earlier: from the transcendental point of view the ego appears no longer as an empirical subject which is bound to its physical body. The focus of the philosophical consideration is no longer the empirical subject bound up in the activity of *finding* meanings and contents in the outer world while using intentionality as its instrument. Transcendental phenomenology discovers the subjective activity of *producing* meanings through performing consciousness. Intentionality thus appears as an immanent structure of consciousness inseparable from its life. The subject emerges as a living, fully capable structure responsible for bestowing sense on the world and itself. 

However, the sense-bestowing subject is not an idealistic abstraction or mere cognitive structure. Its own sense-bestowing performance is inseparable from its bodily, affective, kinesthetic, and even instinctive constitution. Moreover, this structure is not embedded in a singular, isolated subject. It rather implies an egological intersubjectivity, i.e. an immanent plurality, a plural structure of subjects connected in reciprocal intentional relationships, perceiving, evaluating, acknowledging each other.[^34] Other subjects are highly influential in my life, they produce crucial effects within my world, they even affect my self-constitution. Husserl’s late analyses of bodily-instinctual self-constitution show that the self emerges only through the primary intersubjective or even primary fusional relationship. I have elsewhere described this structure through the notion of sympathetical intentionality.[^35] In this context, however, I want to stress how this process is necessarily rooted in corporeality. We can affirm that the primal sympathetical relationship between subjects cannot be realized in other ways than through a kind of bodily intentionality. 

The basic structure of intentionality includes a subjective and an objective pole. As already mentioned, the transcendental perspective transforms the subjective

These are the concrete, practical bodily forms of intentionality and, like every intentionality, they strive towards fulfilment. However, while Husserl’s early analyses on intentionality tend to identify such a fulfilment with cognitive evidence, with a full perceptual givenness of the thing, in this late phase of his reflection it becomes clear that intentional fulfilment is not always and even not in a privileged sense reached on a purely cognitive or representational level. Intentionality as a bodily rooted tendency orienting the whole of consciousness and of subjective life strives towards more complex and concrete forms of *satisfactions* on a pre-reflective or non-representational level. 

These other-than-cognitive fulfilments do not even lie within the reach of an isolated mind. The pre-reflective forms of intentionality rather depend on the bodily constitution of the I and on its social embedding. We fulfil our intentionality through our body by reaching *into* the body of somebody else, and conversely, we fulfil in our bodily movement and kinesthesis the intentional strivings of others. To illustrate this we can come back to Husserl’s example of breast-feeding. On the one side, the new-born moves towards the mother’s breast, seeking for some kind of satisfaction, which is in no way clearly represented or depicted in its mind. On the other side, the mother is not simply a passive object of its desire. She rather needs to move, to adjust, to develop her own intentional activity in order to allow the fulfilment of the baby’s intention. Moreover, the satisfaction of her own intentionality in this process can only be realized in the action of the baby, in successful feeding. We face here a common intentional achievement on the basis of a common intentional process. The proper analysis would be to say that in this situation one single intention is active, and is realized in and through two different bodies. Better still, from the empirical perspective we observe two separated bodies acting together towards a somehow common goal. From the transcendental perspective, however, we discover one single extended sympathetic body structure, a fusion of bodies into one active, intentional, lived body. In order to grasp such activity conceptually, I introduce the term *trans-bodily intentionality*. 

The whole process cannot be fully realized on the basis of mere perception. It rather necessarily relies on a specific anticipative form of *bodily phantasy*. Psychological and psychoanalytical literature offers plenty of examples of this process. Among others, the Italian psychoanalyst Eugenio Gaddini has investigated the imitation processes involved in the development of babies.<sup>36</sup> Observing the mutual relationship between babies and caregivers he refers to a peculiar form of imagination which has nothing to do with the production of mental

pictures. It is an imaginary process happening directly in the body, or, more precisely, in the relationship between extending bodies. He calls this phenomenon “bodily phantasy.”<sup>37</sup> His descriptions show very incisively how such prerepresentational forms of imagination rely on primary sympathetic processes which take place between bodies. He also stresses how the baby engages in imitation in order to develop its own psyche. But this type of imitation is not a mere reproduction or mirroring of the observed behaviour of the other. The constitutive process is much deeper and relies on a complex *participation* in the intentions and actions of the other as the baby works towards becoming itself. 

This kind of fusional imitation should be distinguished from mature forms of identification and introjection, which presuppose already individuated subjects. From a phenomenological point of view, this amounts to descriptions of the primary form of the intentional process of becoming a self. The pre-reflective fusional experience discloses an essential capacity for resonance as the ground of our mature relationships with others. Moreover, this description promises to disclose a new view on the structure and the functioning of human beings at large. The understanding of others does no longer appear as a late result of discursive or intellectual processes, of education, of social constriction, and normativity. Neither is this founded on an abstract or simply perceptive analogy between the other and me. The process of mutual recognition rather relies on a basic, bodily and affective touching and being touched that involves a specific form of intentionality—prereflective trans-bodily intentionality. This intentionality realizes the dissolution of the boundaries of my lived body. My lived body experiences an intentional extension through the performance of bodily phantasy, and this in turn allows me to encompass the other and its (intentional) aims without denying its alterity and individuality. 

A new anthropology is then required, one that does not exhaust its potential in naturalizing descriptions of bodily experience, but one that takes into consideration the whole potential of the genetic-phenomenological account. Instead of *naturalizing embodiment*, we get a genetic understanding of *intentional embodiment* that sheds a new light not only on the bodily nature of the human being, but also on the original social competencies of human life.

ABOUT *DIALOGUE AND UNIVERSALISM* 

*Dialogue and Universalism* tends to show that philosophy is an essential eternal domain of human culture and an inevitable element of the nowadays human world. Critical and creative rational thinking is an opportunity for humankind to resist the lies and illusions of ideological manipulations that serve as instruments of enslavement and oppression. This open and broad vision of philosophy as an expression of human rationality offers a chance to free people’s awareness, to open their minds, and to extend their possibilities of thinking and acting. In doing so, philosophical reflection is able to refine and renew old ideals and values as well as to create new ones. It is these two aspects—free consciousness and new ideals—that are necessary to build a more decent human world. The International Society for Universal Dialogue (ISUD) community is convinced that philosophy has an important role to play in the struggle for the future of humanity. Philosophy with its amazingly sophisticated ways of thinking disposes a tremendous power to cope with the world and to change it. Philosophy is free from technical and practical interests, and constituted by the pursuit of removing—from a highly distanced and neutral perspective—falsehood, prejudices, mental, cultural, religious and social slavery. So it gives a hope for human beings’ emancipation as well as for an alteration of the world. 

*Dialogue and Universalism* is wholly open for all scholars in the world, not being a publishing forum for the ISUD members only. All contributors are equally kindly welcome. 

*Dialogue and Universalism* publishes monothematic issues. However, each monothematic issue of the journal is completed with a few texts thematically different from the main theme of the issue. This decision allows for a broader thematic diversity of problems examined in *Dialogue and Universalism.* The forthcoming main themes are announced in advance at the *Dialogue and Universalism* website. The announcements should be treated as an open invitation for every scholar in the world to participate in *Dialogue and Universalism* projected enterprises. Besides, proposals of themes and contents of next *Dialogue and Universalism* issues are kindly welcome. 

*Dialogue and Universalism* is published since 1973, first, under the title *Dialectics and Humanism,* since 1990 under the title *Dialogue and Humanism.* *The Universalist Quarterly*, and since 1995 under the present title.
