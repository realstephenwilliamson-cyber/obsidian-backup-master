## Gniazdowski PO KOREKCIE

*Andrzej Gniazdowski* 

THE POLITICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

OF EDMUND HUSSERL 

*ABSTRACT* 

The aim of this paper is to contribute to the debate on the relation between phenomenology and philosophical anthropology by analyzing it in the selected, theoretical as well as historical contexts. The author focuses primarily on the problem of Edmund Husserl’s criticism of anthropologism and analyzes the practical meaning of the rejectionby him of anthropology as a true foundation of philosophy. The thesis of the paper is that already by rejecting anthropologism in the logic and theory of knowledge, Husserl presupposed some idea of philosophical anthropology in the “foundational” sense he criticized, and that this implicit idea was pursued by him not only from pure theoretical reason. In reference to Leszek Kołakowski and the methodology of the Warsaw School of the History of Ideas, which he applies in his interpretation of the idea of phenomenology, the author of the article attempts, unlike Kołakowski, to reveal not only the “religious” (in a vague sense), but also the specific political meaning of this idea. What is argued here is that the only possible reconciliation between anti-anthropologism on the one hand and the outspoken humanism of transcendental phenomenology on the other lies in the adoption by Husserl of Johann Gottlieb Fichte’s ideal of humanity as its practical, worldview framework. The practical, if not directly political, motif of Husserl’s radical criticism of anthropologism is, in author’s interpretation, Husserl’s attempt to answer, in the reference to this ideal, to the main political question of his times as consisting in the rising racist and anti-Semitic tendencies in the German naturalistic anthropology. 

Keywords: Phenomenology, philosophical anthropology, practical philosophy, political religion, humanism, naturalism, race theory, history of ideas.

INTRODUCTION 

There is no need to recall that Edmund Husserl criticized anthropologism as a form of skeptical relativism. By anthropologism he meant the assumption that the true foundation of philosophy lies in the science of the human being. Not only in his *Logical Investigations*, but also in his 1931 lecture *Phenomenology and Anthropology*, he replied negatively to the question whether philosophy—as a genuine science that strove for absolute, definitive truths—could find its methodological grounding in a philosophical anthropology. In his lecture, Husserl also pointed out that over the decade after World War One the younger generation of German philosophers had been gravitating with “ever increasing speed” toward this kind of philosophical science. Without mentioning Heidegger by name, he noted that even the so-called phenomenological movement had got caught up in this new trend. As he wrote, “some view this as a necessary reform of the original constitutive phenomenology \[...\] to be constructed entirely anew from out of human *Dasein.*”[^1] 

It is broadly known that the idea of a philosophical anthropology in the phenomenological movement can be traced not only to the writings of Martin Heidegger. As Martin Buber noticed, “two significant attempts of our time to treat the problem of man as an independent philosophical problem have come from the school of Husserl. The first is the ‘fundamental ontology’ of Heidegger, the second is the ‘anthropology’ of Scheler.”[^2] There is no doubt, that apart from these attempts, a kind of a philosophical science on man also underlay the research of many other Husserl students in Göttingen and Freiburg. Philosophical anthropology in the sense of an eidetic science concerning the “ontic structure of the human person” has been developed (mainly in reference to Scheler’s *Formalism in Ethic*s[^3]) primarily by Edith Stein[^4] and Dietrich von Hildebrand.[^5] To some extent it also motivated the research of Gerda Walther,[^6]

 What I aim for in my paper is not to reconstruct Husserl’s scattered insights into the domain of thus-understood phenomenological anthropology and argue for their significance in life. The thesis I will maintain here concerns not so much the theoretical, as the practical meaning of the rejection by Husserl of anthropology as the true foundation of philosophy.[^17] What I will argue is that already by rejecting anthropologism in logic, Husserl presupposed some idea of philosophical anthropology in the earlier-mentioned “foundational” sense, and that this implicit idea was pursued by him not only from pure theoretical reason. As I will argue, the meaning of the idea of taking into account the “full human being in its pure generality” as a scientific foundation of moral philosophy consisted in the last resort also for Husserl in its fundamental ontological function with regard to the idea of phenomenology itself. As such, it can be understood in my opinion as the idea of not only philosophical, but also political anthropology: as Husserl’s attempt at a philosophical answer to the political questions posed to the representatives of the phenomenological movement of his times.

 The basic theoretical motif Husserl offered for his rejection of anthropologism in logic and theory of knowledge was the same as that which stood behind his criticism of psychologism, which he outlined in the first volume of the *Logical Investigations*. THE HUMANISM OF PHENOMENOLOGY 

AND MODERN POLITICAL RADICALISM 

The answer to the question, “what Edmund Husserl’s philosophy is for” and how to reconcile its outspoken “humanism” with its anti-anthropologism, requires considering the idea of phenomenology in a broader, not only theoretical-philosophical but also historical-practical context. What perhaps most distinguishes the allegedly “neo-Platonic” phenomenology from other philosophical currents of the twentieth century, that is from not only neo-Thomism, neo-Kantianism or neo-Hegelianism, but also, so to say, “neo-anthropologism” as a form of “neo-Hobbesism” or “neo-sophistics,” is the idea of both “a radical new grounding” and “a radically new way of doing philosophy.”[^48] Husserl’s attempt at breaking with abstract, metaphysical, hence equally “onto-theological” as “onto-anthropological” speculation, as well as his call for giving up idle historical research and returning not to this or that historical philosophy but to “the things themselves” and their concrete living experience, is reflected in the philosophical categories and research topics that are most specific to his phenomenology. Among its peculiarities, like the idea of absolute philosophical presuppositionlessness, the reduction of the world in the act of *epoché* to the achievement of pure transcendental consciousness, or that of the intuition of essences, there is one that seems more significant for life than all the others. This characteristic of phenomenology, which is of not only theoretical, but also practical importance, presents itself in the idea of the radical, i.e. not only palliative, overcoming of what Husserl perceived as the “crisis of European Man.”[^49] 

Obviously, the idea of the crisis of man, which consisted for Husserl in the crisis of his own humanity itself, goes beyond the domain of phenomenological anthropology as a constitutional theory of the human being. Since it presupposes a kind of “metaphysical philosophy of history,”[^50] it has for sure nothing to do with the ontological descriptive-genetic analysis of that complex phenomenon as some “being in the world.” The idea of the crisis of man thus surpasses all the more the theoretical framework of “either the empirical or *a priori*” approaches to man, which Husserl would have called the “regional anthropologies,” focused on the aspects of the human condition, like identity, contingency, corporeality, work, suffering or love.[^51] In fact, the idea of such a crisis, and especially the call for its “radical” overcoming, for mere theoretical reasons also

In order to answer this question, if not even to pose it, there is no other way as to ignore the warning forwarded by Husserl, who was strongly opposed to dealing with his phenomenology “as the historian does.”[^53] As he wrote, “remaining immersed in the historical, forcing oneself to work therein in historical-critical activity \[…\], all that leads to nothing but hopeless efforts.”<sup>54</sup> Nonetheless, in order to “penetrate the soul” of Husserl’s words concerning both the “crisis of European Man” and the need for philosophical radicalism as the only path to overcoming it, it is certainly not enough to limit oneself to letting his phenomenology just “work” as an inspiration “in accord with its spiritual content”<sup>55</sup>. Understanding the motifs and practical meaning of Husserl’s call for philosophical radicalism also requires, in defiance of Husserl himself, losing oneself “in the developmental relations” in which his phenomenology, like other great philosophies, has evolved. It requires thinking through to the end the problem of the “religious background” of Husserl’s “search for meaning” which Kołakowski posed by specifying the historical as well as sociological constituents of that background.

PLAY AND SELF-REFLECTION. EUGEN FINK’S 

PHENOMENOLOGICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

*ABSTRACT* 

The paper takes into consideration the relationship between philosophical anthropology and phenomenology from the point of view provided by Eugen Fink’s philosophical path. Starting with phenomenological researches into the structure of constitution and reduction, after the Second World War Fink puts forth an anthropological theory based on the notion of play. This paper identifies the self-reflective and practical structure of *Selbstbesinnung* as a constant element of Fink’s analysis of the phenomenological method, of consciousness, and of the anthropological dimension of play, thus suggesting a profound continuity in his philosophical thought. 

Keywords: phenomenology, anthropology, Eugen Fink, play, self-reflection, *Selbstbesinnung.* 

1\. PHENOMENOLOGY AND ANTHROPOLOGY: 

A PROMISING CONTROVERSY 

Phenomenology’s relationship with anthropology has been quite controversial. In his talk in Berlin in 1931,[^1-p215] Edmund Husserl openly takes distance from the anthropological turn impressed to philosophy by Max Scheler with the publication of his influential work *Die Stellung des Menschen im Kosmos* in 1928. In Husserl’s view, the anthropological approach exposes philosophy to the risk of a renewed psychologism. Thus, his opposition to philosophical anthropology is not an occasional and contingent dispute, but the coherent consequence of his philosophical setting: by way of the implied equivalence with psychologism, Husserl’s contrariety to the anthropological inquiry traces back to the original 

[^1-p215]: Husserl, E. 1988. “Aufsätze und Vorträge 1922–1937.” In: *Hua* XXVII. von Nennon, T., H. R. Sepp (Eds.). Kluwer: Dordrecht–Boston–London. —————————

*[p. 216]* ^pg218

motives of phenomenology elaborated at the beginning of the 20th century in the *Prolegomena* and *Logical Investigations*. 

However, the new anthropologism appears as an even more dangerous threat. Namely, it immediately concerns the conception of subjectivity and the possibility— crucial to phenomenology as a *philosophia prima—*to conceive of a non-empirical subject of experience. Therefore, the essential theoretical goal of a full-fledged and non-contingent theory of subjectivity endows at the same time hostility and the mutual interconnection between both the philosophical approaches. Ultimately, Husserl faces the difficulty of preventing the reductive empiricism of the anthropological approach, without giving up the idea of the subject as the original source of meaning, rooted in the world and in its own body, and living in a constant exchange with the surrounding nature by the means of perception, emotion, and action. In his reading, he puts in question not the results, but the original question of Scheler’s book: the purpose of the philosophical investigation shall not be the identification of the peculiar “position” (*Stellung*) of man in the world, but the unfolding of diverse forms of the active, constitutive and ultimately transcendental relation between the two poles: man and world. Accordingly, while Scheler’s formulation seems to assume the world and the human as empirical, given entities, essentially independent from each other and only contingently related, Husserl pursues the disclosure of the unexhausted process of becoming of both, the I and the world, as inseparable sense-structures. 

The project of such a transcendental anthropology was destined to remain in the form of unpublished manuscripts. Nevertheless, this did not stop the dialogue between phenomenology and anthropology. Many phenomenological scholars and phenomenologically oriented philosophers such as Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Jean-Paul Sartre, Karl Jaspers, Helmuth Plessner developed anthropological reflections based on phenomenological tools. Each of them dwelled with a peculiar aspect of this debate, thus showing the promising richness of this interdisciplinary endeavour. Yet, phenomenological anthropology remains an open project or, better, a “generative concept.” Husserl’s approach to transcendental anthropology has not been left in form of an unfinished project by chance. It rather provides a leading question that cannot be exhaustively and systematically answered, but constantly inspires and challenges phenomenology.

This plays a crucial role in Fink’s early phenomenological works (*Vergegenwärtigung und Bild* and *Sixth Cartesian Meditation*) in the double-meaning of self-reflection (*Selbst-Besinnung*) and self-condition (*Selbst-Bedingtheit*). Fink’s choice of “unreality” as the topic for his dissertation might be interpreted in both senses: as a bold challenge to Husserl, his supervisor and mentor, who centred his analysis on the intuitive and self-evident character of lived experience, or as the testimony to a fruitful cooperation in the common field of the analysis of experience.[^18-p220] Actually, as he states at the beginning of the unfinished second part, “unreality” does not indicate for Fink the negation of reality, i.e. the opposite of the self-evident experience that constitutes the living relation of subject and world: “the being or not being of intentional intended objects is not of interest here, but the non-being that is incorporated (*mitaufgenommen*) in the being of the intended objects, that is included (*mitbeschlossen*) in their sense”[^19-p220]. The missing giveness at stake in Fink’s analysis is not a denial of the evident giveness of the things themselves pursued by Husserl. It rather is a part of it. His analysis does not aim at a restriction or negation of Husserl’s descriptions of the life of consciousness, but rather at its culmination. Expanding on the indications already entailed in Husserl’s investigations into the original passivity of conscious performances, Fink explores the necessary interrelation between giveness and non-giveness of experience. He thereby reaches a representation of consciousness as a continuous modification. The original experiences, direct and fulfilled perceptions, are only a small part of our relationship with the world and serve in any case as the starting point of an unexhausted flow of transformations. 

Focusing on the dynamics of transformation of experience, Fink’s phenomenological analysis puts into the foreground the difference between presentation (*Gegenwärtigung*) and presentification (*Vergegenwärtigung*) subsequently distinguishing several forms of presentification such as de-presentation (*Entgegenwärtigung*),[^20-p220] dream, fantasy, imagination. His detailed differentiation brings forward a complex phenomenology of fantasy, but his most important result is a new representation of the life of consciousness. 4\. PLAY AS THE HUMAN PRAXIS OF SELF-GRASPING 

The prominent character of self-reference distinguishes both Fink’s idea of the subject and of philosophy in the strictly phenomenological phase of his thought. However, I believe that self-reference is for him not only one of many features of subjectivity and phenomenology, but rather works as the guiding principle of his entire phenomenological research. In both cases, *Selbstbezogenheit* may not be reduced to a theoretic self-reflection. Fink seems rather to be aware that such a constant grasping of oneself can only be achieved in an uninterrupted praxis. This observation in turn helps supporting the claim of a continuity in Fink’s thought not only before and after the war, but between his phenomenological interests and his late anthropological research. A clue for unfolding this thesis is provided by the notion of play (*Spiel*) that is tackled by Fink from 1953 until 1973 as attested not only by his famous work from 1960 *Play as Symbol of the World*, but also by several other talks and papers collected in 

*[p. 224]* ^pg226

2010 as volume 7 of the *Eugen Fink Gesamtausgabe*. Among those, an essay from 1957, *Oase des Glücks*, which the editors of the *Play as Symbol of the World* chose to put in front of the main text in the German edition of Fink’s masterwork, is of particular interest. Here, the author moves from the observation that our epoch is characterized by a nearly infinite offer of recreation that has transformed the experience of play into an empty, i.e. meaningless and inessential possibility.<sup>28</sup> Such a transformation is even more severe since it corrodes the very essence of human play. This consists in being the origin of a whole world of possibilities, which, however, shall not remain empty, abstract, theoretical possibilities, but are meant to be embedded in human activity, embodied, acted out and made substantial by human praxis. 

Fink’s entire reflection about play as a fundamental anthropological dimension is oriented by the opposition between play, on the one side, and thinking (*Denken*) and work (*Arbeit*), on the other side. As for his notion of thinking, his phenomenological training was already characterized by a suspicion toward abstract concepts. The same position is the centre of the essay from 1966 dedicated to the *Play and Philosophy* in which playing and thinking are recognized as two opposite attitudes mixed up in human life, but essentially giving rise to different understanding of being. The second opposition—that between play and work—echoes the Heideggerian and, on different presuppositions, the Frankfurt School’s critique of technique and of its alienating effects on human life. In the *Spiel und Feier*, published after Fink’s death but delivered as radio podcast in 1974, he explicitly specifies this point opposing play not only to work, but also to war and technique. The opposition becomes even more significant, since it allows play to emerge as the space where an inner tension of human being becomes visible. Play, work, war, and technique are not simply valid alternative options equally open for the human being. The consideration of the essence of play rather helps unveiling an inner fractiousness (*Spaltung*) of human life, an inner competition between ways of life that constantly dismiss each other’s validity, thereby implying radically diverging paths of self-realization. 

The clarification provided by the *Spiel und Feier* leads to a specification of paramount importance. Play is not opposed to every kind of human activity. On the contrary, Fink repeatedly stresses that the tendency to confine play to the realm of shallow, passive and meaningless entertainment is one sign of the dreadful blindness of our epoch. It is exactly against such a misunderstanding leading to deep ignorance about the significance of play for human existence that all his theoretical effort is oriented. Play is not a simple suspension of the activity and the seriousness of life. Play is not opposed to praxis, but rather discloses authentic features of human activity. 

The strong connection between play and human praxis is attested already in the *Oase des Glücks* where Fink describes the essence of such phenomena with

Bracketing this pressing need, play realises a distinctive form of existential flight. Not flight into the future or the past, but flight into the fullness of present, into the “pacified present.”[^34-p227] 

Also, in this peculiar relationship of play with the human experience of temporality we can find an echo of Fink’s phenomenological research into reduction.[^35-p227] In the *Sixth Cartesian Meditation* he had found the living present (*lebendige Gegenwart*)[^36-p227] as the proper result of reduction. This does not simply indicate a temporal dimension on the same level of the future and the past but designates “a streaming present that is present to itself” (*strömende selbstanwesende Gegenwart*).[^37-p227] This, and not the intellectual or psychological reflection that arises as a particular and contingent performance of a concrete subject, provides the core of the self-awareness of consciousness. This is the starting point of every phenomenological investigation. But it is as well the original dimension unveiled and made available by play. Phenomenological reduction and play appear as two fairly different, but consistent access modalities to the same sphere of self-aware, living present. Reduction works as the starting point of a regressive analysis, a methodological exploration of the constituting performances of consciousness, while play highlights the human capacity to act into this living dimension. Fink remarks how play seems to suspend time and space transporting the players in a different spatiality and temporality. In actuality, time and space are of course not dissolved or denied by the playful activity. Play rather reveals the mobility

Activating the subject’s possibility of constituting and transforming one’s own time and space, play emerges as a radical re-appropriation of the own position in the world. The practical self-reflection operated by playing assumes an anthropological significance that surpasses the psychological experience of individuals. For this reason, Fink’s observation about role-playing in *Die Weltbedeutung des Spiels* seems to concern not only a particular kind of game, but name a fundamental function of play as human activity. Here, the playing subject stands in a world that “involves” him, draws him in, and engulfs him—sometimes with such an intensity that the player “gets lost” in his role. And yet he never actually loses himself; he remains the actor who is presently portraying King Lear or Wallenstein.”<sup>38</sup> Not only the actor on the scene, but the human being in general seems to authentically realize his position in the world by playing, i.e., by achieving this constant transformative and practical self-reflection, by re-grasping and therefore transforming its own time and space. The human being does not simply “have” its position in the world, but acts it out, constitutes it, realizes it by playfully producing its own perspective and visibility. We are “involved, drawn in and even engulfed” in the world, but always as “actors,” i.e. as playing subjects, as intertwined co-sources of an intersubjective plot. 

This explains Fink’s statement, “the human being plays—always from the core of his life.”<sup>39</sup> We have said before that play constitutes a periphery of life that helps illuminating the whole of existence. Does Fink contradict himself now? I do not believe this to be the case. Our common experience confirms that play remains at the periphery of our constituted, settled, established life. With regard to work and institutions, to our social identity and public sphere, play remains at the border of our recognized activity. Nevertheless, if we shift our attention from the constituted to the constituting life and, following the path of phenomenology, we bracket the established meanings of facts and things and try to trace these back to their origin, there we may find what Fink in *Play and Philosophy* (1966) calls *homo ludens*. The anthropological key-figure summoning the complexity of Fink’s researches on play indicates a subject that discloses its essence in play, throwing itself in a double movement of self-appropriation and constitution of the lifeworld. This is a philosophical subject that finally promises to overcome the opposition between play and thinking, between life and philosophy, thanks to an unexhausted praxis of self-grasping and self-transforming. *Homo ludens* as the subject that not only “plays from the core of its life” but has also recognized the living centrality of play for its
