## About

A dialogue of Plato in which Socrates questions the famous rhetorician Gorgias, then his pupil Polus and the Athenian Callicles, about what rhetoric is and which life is best. It moves from rhetoric to justice, power and pleasure, and closes with a myth of judgment after death.

- **People:** Socrates; Gorgias, the visiting rhetorician; Polus, his student; Callicles, an Athenian aristocrat with political ambitions; and Chaerephon, Socrates' friend. The SEP notes that nothing is known of a historical Callicles, and he may be Plato's invention.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** Gorgias has just given a display speech. Wikipedia places the talk at Callicles' house, where Gorgias is a guest, and gives no dramatic date. Harold Tarrant (*Antichthon*, 2008) argues that Plato was not much concerned to keep to a consistent dramatic date in this dialogue.
- **Composition:** Wikipedia gives around 380 BC. The IEP lists it among the early dialogues, before Plato's first trip to Sicily in 387. The SEP says it was almost certainly written before the *Republic*. None of these sources questions its authenticity.
- **Argument:** Socrates calls rhetoric, as practised, a knack of flattery rather than a craft, and argues that doing wrong is worse than suffering it. Callicles answers that by nature the stronger should have more, and that justice and moderation are conventions of the weak. The SEP divides his position into a critique of convention, a claim of natural justice, a theory of virtue and hedonism; Socrates' refutation (497d–499b) targets the last two.
- **Closing myth:** under Cronus men were judged just before death, and judges were deceived by appearances; Zeus had them judged naked after death by his sons Minos, Rhadamanthus and Aeacus (523a–527a).
- **Place among the dialogues:** an early Socratic dialogue; the SEP pairs Callicles with Thrasymachus of *Republic* I as the two great exemplars of contemptuous challenge to conventional morality, and calls Callicles Socrates' philosophical antithesis and double. Stephanus pages 447a–527e.
