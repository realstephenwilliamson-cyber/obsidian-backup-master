## About

A dialogue by Plato in which the Eleatic philosophers Parmenides and Zeno meet a very young Socrates. Parmenides raises objections to Socrates' theory of Forms, then leads an exercise in reasoning about the One.

- **People:** Parmenides (about 65), Zeno (about 40), Socrates, and an Aristotle who later joined the Thirty, not the philosopher. Wikipedia makes Socrates nineteen, which makes this dramatically the earliest dialogue; the SEP reports Peterson's view that he was most likely 16.
- **Frame and setting:** Cephalus of Clazomenae narrates. Adeimantus and Glaucon take him to their half-brother Antiphon, who recites the conversation as Pythodorus reported it; it took place at Pythodorus's house during the Great Panathenaea. The SEP calls it almost certainly fictitious, and Wikipedia says most scholars agree. The sources consulted give no dramatic year.
- **Composition:** Wikipedia cites a date of about 360 BC; the IEP puts it in the late-transitional group (c. 360–355 BC) with the *Theaetetus* and *Phaedrus*. The SEP's *Theaetetus* entry says the two are normally taken as closely connected, probably written in that order. None of these sources questions its authenticity.
- **Structure (Stephanus 126a–166c):** Zeno's argument against plurality and Socrates' reply (126a–130a); Parmenides' objections to the Forms (130a–134e), including the Third Man (132a–b); then deductions from hypotheses about the One (137c–166c): eight plus an appendix, which the Neoplatonists counted as a ninth.
- **Reception:** a frequent subject of Neoplatonist commentary (Proclus, Damascius; an anonymous commentary possibly by Porphyry). William of Moerbeke's 13th-century translation of Proclus' commentary sparked medieval interest, and Ficino, Pico della Mirandola and Nicholas of Cusa drew on it.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it "the most challenging and enigmatic" of Plato's dialogues. Scholars still debate the Third Man Argument, and the SEP surveys rival readings of the deductions, from the Neoplatonists' hierarchy of being to readings that take them as meant to perplex.
