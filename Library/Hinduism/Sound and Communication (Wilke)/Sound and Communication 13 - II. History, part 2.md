It is noteworthy that in the late Rk-Samhitā hymns to Brhaspati occur more and more rarely, while independent hymns to the goddess Vāc start to take their place. It cannot be denied that this shift of interest from Brhaspati to Vāc corresponds to the development in thought that Ernst Cassirer has postulated as a transculturally valid law: the historical transition from the mythical to the terminological as a fundamental movement in all history of thought: 

“This characteristic ‘wholeness? of the mythical picture of the world, in which all the differentiations of things are dissolved into a mythical-magical chain of causality, carries with it a significant consequence for man’s approach to language. As soon as myth rises above the level of the most primitive magical ‘practice,’ which strives to obtain a specific effect by the use of a specific means, which accordingly links one particular with another in immediate action — as soon as mythical thinking seeks, even in the crudest, most imperfect form, to understand its own activity, it has penetrated to the sphere of universality.””6 According to Cassirer, the driving force behind this movement from the mythical and magical into the terminological, or from the individual to the general, lies in a culture’s advancing capacity for self-reflection. He links this to a clear value judgment: myth is the “primitive,” the unreflected, while the terminological and reflected represents an “elevation” above this primitive substrate. There is, however, no reason for such a judgmental view at all, for the transition from one frame of thought to another is also always associated with losses. In India people have actually always been aware of this, which is why Indian linguistic philosophy was never a one-way street. Particularly these still semi-mythical and already semi-terminological views of Vāc in the Rk-Samhitā were to become an important hinge-pin for the re-mythification by linguistic philosophers such as Bhartrhari and Abhinavagupta. It seems as though people imagine the goddess Vāc as a type of primeval spring of all sound. It is not logical and comprehensible language that is the focus of attention. Instead, Vāc images always indicate the expressive aspect as the dimension of depth of sound that can never be exhausted, in contrast to the measurable and reducible Jogos. Above all, this abundance of the expressive is illustrated with the image of the cow that gives milk unceasingly. So in a certain way the goddess Vāc represents (even if not exclusively) an anti-myth to the aggressive “bel-lower” Brhaspati. Brhaspati is associated with metaphors involving fire and light, while the character of Vāc by contrast is “gentle” (mandra). The Sanskrit word mandra (“gentle”, but also “dark”) is always the complementary term to dīpta (“shrill”, but also “bright”). In music these two terms are still used today to indicate the lowest and the highest octaves. Here “mandra” appears to mean primarily the “gentle nature” of the cow, but it also alludes to the timbre of the lowing and deep thunder. 

2.3.5.1 

“The Language Speaks”: The Vāg शाता Hymn 

In a very general sense, the hymn of the Vāg-Āmbhrnī (RV 10.125) is concerned with the goddess Vāc. Incidentally, this hymn is thought to have been composed by a woman. It is still popular today as the “hymn to the Great Goddess” (Devī-Sūkta). Historically it represents a new type of hymn, which only became popular at the time of the late Samhitās. This new type is not only concerned with the reiteration of the traditional mythogemes, but lives much more from the philosophical speculation and the passing-on of esoteric secrets. The evocative aspect takes a back seat behind the informative aspect. Thoughts about views of the world are developed and transmitted. Whenever mythogemes appear, they should preferably be understood as allegories, and their numinous character fades. Often the hymns in the early Rk-Samhitā could not be interpreted rationally. Mostly we find nothing more in them than free associations and series of associations. But such free series were never linked together to form a logically constructed and complete thought structure. All things are gathered together in language. Language is essential. Above all, contact with the gods requires language, as stated in RV 8.101.16: 

“The cow, who lifts up the beautifully speaking voice, who is attended by all good thoughts, who, being a godess \[even\] for the gods, moves around \[with them\] — let not the mortal be so silly as to relinquish her.”® All the Archái of a god gain substance in this world solely through language, which is why all the gods only exist where language also exists. Here again there is an echo of the idea of the “hymnic food”: after all, the immortal gods themselves are only alive by means of the constant re-invocation in their sacrificial hymns. 

But it is not only intercourse with the heavenly entities that arises from Language, but also knowledge about the world. Anyone who can speak well has a career: “Whomsoever I love I shall make a king, a Brahmin, a seer, a sage.” Despite the fact that this is mixed with pragmatic arguments, there is still true metaphysical substance. “Without knowing it, they dwell close to me”. Language makes the world possible and is present in absolutely everything. This cosmic relevance is underscored by the image of the Macranthropos (purusa), which remains popular even in later Hinduism. The feet of the goddess Language reach into the underworld and the top of her head touches the heavens. Even a metaphor that tends towards transcendency is used to describe Vāc: “Beyond the heavens and beyond the earth I am endowed with this greatness.” 

Here the central image of water has been adopted from the cosmogony. Language pushed up out of the depths, her actual origin is in the ocean (samudra), meaning the world-creating “primeval floods,” which surround the Earth on all sides. This birthplace is contrasted with the Father of Heaven (dyaus-pitr), who lives at the highest point of the heavens and is in turn created by Vāc. Language is at once the epitome of the ordered cosmos and the formless primeval flood. 

It is certainly not by chance that the hymns concerning “language as such” reflect a resolution of the original mythical unity, which still reigned uninterrupted in the early Samhitā — for example the mythogeme of Brhaspati — and in this they could be called partially rational. As long as we are dealing with mythogemes, only recursive definitions are possible: Brhaspati is what Brahman speaks and Brahman is what Brhaspati speaks. In completely mythical thought, in which there are only holistic entities and relations cannot be considered in any way other than participatory, abstract terms remain completely unthinkable.

###### 2.3.5.2 "Sifted words" - The Brhaspati Āngirasa Hymn

Typical of the older hymns in the eighth book of the Rgveda are pure mythogemes such as the gentle-natured cloud-cow, whose lowing is the thunder, while the Vāg-Ambhrnṇnī hymn demonstrates less sensory orientation, but instead exhibits greater clarity. Vāc, whose crown reaches into the heavens, is more allegorical, more abstract, and more metaphysical. In connection with the continuing abstraction of the notion of language, causal patterns of explanation of Language are now also emerging.

While in the early Vedic culture the priest is the speaker for a group and as a rule the “we” form is used in the hymns, in the mid-Vedic and late Vedic periods an individualization takes place. The priests are now “hired” and “paid” by wealthy patrons (yajamāna). In some cases the texts mention horrendous sums (mostly in cows) paid to the priests as rewards for carrying out the sacrifice. Whereas previously it was the collective that determined everything — with certain exceptional individuals such as the “choir leader” (gana-pati) - and the principle of primus inter pares ruled, now we find individualization. Rituals are now performed by individuals for individuals - i.e. by Brahmin priests for wealthy worldly patrons.”

From here on the sacrifice of fire starts to lose its original character as a “banquet for the gods” completely, and at the same time the way in which the hymns to these gods are regarded mutates. They are no longer seen as expressive poetry, and their evocative character fades, making way for numerological speculations and manipulations. The hymns are now linguistic material sanctified by time, material to which a mysterious force is ascribed, but not necessarily a specific semantic meaning. Cosmic rites appear on the scene, absorbing the individual sacrifices previously made to various deities and putting them into a comprehensive cosmic context. To fulfill these needs new literatures arise - the Brāhmanas. They present complex exegeses of the events during the sacrifice in the light of this new demand for an esoteric and cosmizing ritual. 

What is characteristic of the literature of the Brāhmaṇa epoch is a marked tendency towards the monomythical. Now all mythical substances are understood as being gathered together at one single location. This place is the fire sacrifice, where all the Archaí present in the universe take place at the same time. The sacrifice is regarded as the cosmos-maintaining force. So actually only one sole myth remains in existence — the fire sacrifice itself. Knowing and applying the complicated relations between the sacrifice of fire and the cosmos becomes the task of a specialized elite priesthood, whose most important instrument is language. By mastery of the language they even subordinate the gods to themselves and are thus entitled to set themselves above all earthly rulers, as expressed in the following riddle: 

“I ask you about the edge of the earth. I ask you about the center of the world. ... I ask you about the highest seat of language. 

The altar, that is the farthest edge of the earth. The sacrifice, that is the center of the world ... The priest, that is the highest seat of language.” The complexity of the fire sacrifice is manifested in such things as the fact that up to 16 different priests are active at the same time. The “singer” (udgātr) performs specific sacred melodies (sāman), part of whose text material is based on verses of hymns, although part of it consists of syllables devoid of semantic meaning. At certain very special rites no longer used, he would also accompany the sacrificial events with instrumental music played on a harp or drum. 

The “sacrificer” (adhvaryu) has the main task of performing all the nonverbal actions: setting up the fire altar, the sacrificial gifts, the symbolic manipulations of the sacrificial equipment, and other items. But the “sacrificer” is not ‘speechless’ — in fact every action is accompanied by a sacred verse (yajus). Unlike the “caller” and the “singer,” however, he usually speaks his texts in a low voice (upāmśu). 

The “Brahmin” priest (brāhmana) is the only priest in the sacrifice who stays silent most of the time. He performs the sacrifice only in spirit, and only takes action if there is a mistake in the sacrificial rites or in the recitation. Should that happen, he speaks the three absolving words “earth, air, heaven” (bhūr-bhuvah-svah). 

The fifth party at the sacrifice is the patron (yajamāna) and his wife. They are the sponsors of the sacrifice for whose benefit the ritual is performed. 

The fire sacrifice thus contains virtually every form of language and vocal sound: verses and prose, singing, reciting aloud, soft murmuring and silence. 

Following the example of the samhitā (“collection”) of the “callers,” i.e. the Rk-Samhitā, the families of the “singers” and the “sacrificers” also compiled their own samhitās, containing the sāmans and yajus, that is, the important linguistic material for the “singers” and the “sacrificers.” In this way, at the time of the late Rk-Samhitā there came about a Samhitā for the S\$āma- Veda and one for the Yajur- Veda. Parallel to these there was also a separate Brāhmaṇa literature for each of these three offices. Originally the only office for which there was no special literature was the fourth office, that of the “Brahmin priest,” as he was responsible for all the Vedas at the same time. In later periods he was allocated (more for systematic reasons) the Atharva- Veda, a “magic book” based on the Rk-Samhitā.

##### 2.4.1 The Voice and the "Inexhaustible": the Late Rgveda

In principle the Rgveda hymn 1.164, which we will discuss below, is already completely embedded within the mentality of the Brāhmaṇas, although in the Veda text in canonical form it appears in the Samhitās. All the features of the Vedic change that we have mentioned culminate in this hymn, which already comments on the new cosmic fire sacrifice: the hypostasis of the sacrifice, of the language and of the priest, and the progressive emancipation of the priest from the myth. Regarding the late literatures of the Rgveda, there are two quite opposing ways of understanding and viewing language. The first way is found principally in secret texts. The prevailing notion here is that language arose from a dark, amorphous, mysterious primeval sound, which at the beginning follows on from the old ideas of Vāc as the language-cow, and later on is interpreted ever more metaphysically and ever less mythically. A completely opposing aspect manifests itself in the use of the Rc hymns in the ritual: here we find marked ideas of harmony and order, and numerical correspondences are extremely important. Consequently the Brāhmaṇas of the Rgveda concentrate less and less on the poetic magic, and instead build up excessive, homologizing classification mechanisms, primarily magic numeric combinations with numbers of syllables, various meters and numbers of verses. Often there is hardly any interest any more in the individual words and sentences, but only in the metrical mathematical properties of the verses of the hymns. Now and again one has the feeling that even in the Brāhmaṇa period the old Vedic Sanskrit was already becoming difficult to understand. If in the Brāhmaṇas the words of the hymns are actually of some interest and there is some hermeneutic activity, these explanations of the “true” meanings often have something forced about them. The understanding of the ancient Sanskrit of the Rc hymns was apparently so poor that people were either not able to combat such philological brutality, or did not want to. After all, every part of the cosmized fire sacrifice had to have a coordinated symbolism. And so the Rk-Samhitā specialists constructed completely new sets of meanings, which were no longer based on the original semantic meanings of the verses of the hymns. 

This means that here we are dealing with two opposing ideas — that of an amorphous sonic primordial entity, and that of linguistic material that can be expressed mathematically. However, they are not felt to be opposing, and do not behave in opposition to one another. It is much more the case that language reflected in the Brāhmaṇa literature is always antistructure originally, and it is the ritual that develops the structure from the antistructure. This process within the language is strictly analogous to the fundamental way in which the whole late Vedic ritual

functions. Performing a ritual means (here) creating relations between the meanings of things. There occurs a constant re-creation of sense from nonsense. The ritual of the late Veda thus has a claim to universality. All the forces of the cosmos must be symbolically present in the sacrifice. Therefore the sacrificial ritual contains not only semantically meaningful speech, but also music, nonsense sounds and expressive calls which, however, always have a particular function. By contrast, even semantically meaningful utterances such as bhūr-bhuvah-svah (“earth, air, heavens”) often do not necessarily make sense in terms of their semantic meaning, but solely on the basis of their ritual function. The elements that evade being structured (such as chaotic music or inarticulate shouts) are an important part of the intended cosmic fire sacrifice. The Brahmins see themselves as specialists in discovering symbolic relations between things rendering them meaningful and significant. Just as they have semantically meaningful “human” language at their unlimited disposal, they are also masters of the seemingly meaningless, but magically potent, “pure” sounds whose “true” meaning always remains hidden from mere mortals: 

“If language is measured, it has four parts. Wise Brahmins know them \[all\]. Three lie hidden in the depths and remain unnoticed. The fourth \[part\] of language is spoken by men.” ” 

This cryptic puzzle of a verse has been interpreted in various ways. The Śatapatha-Brāhmana sees in the hidden three quarters the language of animals, which differs from that of human beings in that it is “uninterpretable” (anirukta). The same combination of numbers is also found in the Purusa-SṢūkta, with a variation of meaning: “One foot of him is all creatures, three feet are immortality in the highest heaven.” Wherever this numerical combination appears, the three quarters mean something hidden and uninterpretable. To a well-versed Brahmin, the meaning of the riddle should be clear: structure and antistructure, sound not belonging to the structure of language, and structure-forming semantics and meters, all belong to the Arché of the fire sacrifice.

##### 2.4.2 The Riddle in the Hymn of Dirghatamas

The Riddle in the Hymn of Dīrghatamas 

The famous catvari vāk verse (on the four-fold speech which we have quoted above) is part of the Dīrghatamas hymn RV 1.164.” The language verses of this hymn are a very early example of the notion of an “amorphous sonic primordial entity.” The hymn is largely dark and mysterious. ##### 2.4.3 Metrical Magic with Numbers

The actual mystery of language, the “inexhaustible” (aksara), that element of language that is at once one and many, becomes something quite simple at the profane level: the actual syllable in the holy Veda text. This means that the “inexhaustible” can be any syllable and any syllable is in turn the “inexhaustible,” since there are neither structure nor measurable units within a syllable; it sounds for as long as one maintains it. The level of the measurable only begins at the verse. Symbolic relations and references are only possible from this level upwards. The Arché, which is replayed over and over again in the cosmic fire sacrifice, that is, the creation of sense from nonsense, of structure from antistructure, of order from chaos, also forms the basis for how we deal with the phonetic dimension of words. 

The explanation for meter and the counting of syllables being so important for the Brāhmaṇa texts probably lies in the fact that syllables link both dimensions, structure and antistructure.” The syllable is sound substance that cannot be reduced and is therefore at the same time the primary metrical unit of measurement. In the case of Anustubh, the Aitareya-Brāhmana provides a quite different reasoning for allocating this meter to the “heavenly world,” to which one goes after death. Here more importance is attached to numerological speculations. One Anuṣtubh has 4 times 8 = 32 syllables, and so two Anuṣṭubhs consist of 64 syllables. Now, there are the three worlds: the earth (bhūr), the atmosphere (bhuvah) and the heavens (svah) , and each of these worlds contains 21 subworlds. The number 21 is probably derived from the notion that the numbers three and seven are universal and especially holy. There are not only three worlds, but also three Vedas (without the late Atharva- Veda, which does not yet belong to the canon) and three sacrificial fires in every sacrifice. In this sacrifice, seven meters are sung, sacrifices are made to the seven planets (sapta-dhisnya), and in the form of the constellation of the Great Bear, seven seers (sapta-rsi) keep watch over the borders of the heavens. Three times seven is 21. According to the Aítareya-Brāhmana, it is thus possible to divide this cosmos by means of multiplication with the three worlds, into three times 21 segments, which makes 63 segments altogether. The 64" segment is the highest heaven, which is above all the worlds and is the home of the gods:

#### 2.5 Semanticization and Language Act: the Yajur-Veda

Interestingly, the various tasks of the priests correlate with various different views of language, which are also visible in the relevant literature. In the Rgveda the linguistic material is more like something that exists in its own right. The hymns can be selected as desired and are not necessarily linked to a ritual act. As messages to the gods the hymns have their own power. Early on it was the expressive aspect, the poetry, the musicality of the sounds and rhythms and the visionary element, that gave the hymns their power. In the late Vedic period we tend to find more focus on hidden, secret meanings, such as that found in the representation of the “thousand-syllabled monosyllable,” the riddle rituals, and the speculations with numbers. However, looking at these dimensions of language is typical of the calling priests of the Rgveda only.

##### 2.5.1 Mantras as Speech Acts and Language Acts

The word “mantra” has many and varied uses, but always denotes a speech act, as defined in Austin and Searle’s speech act theory: the linguistic utterance itself performs an act. The classical example in speech act theory was the statement “I baptize.” In this case not only is something said, but uttering the words also performs the act of baptizing. The situation is similar with the Sanskrit term “mantra,” which in a worldly setting includes speech acts such as invitations, diplomatic notes, council decisions, etc. 

As well as this profane meaning, the term “mantra” also has a specific religious meaning. Used in the religious sense, the term is often translated as “magic formula.” We prefer to speak of “sacred formulas.” In the 

sacred context mantras were probably originally thought of purely as “statements of truth,” giving precise expression to something that should be brought about by the mantra, and thus comes to pass. In terms of speech act theory, the Vedic mantras can be defined as those sacred formulas whose purpose is to accompany the sacrificial proceedings, whenever the ritual act itself is not sufficiently powerful to produce the desired effects. Owing to the cosmization of the sacrifice, mantras, i.e. accompanying speech acts, have become essential. For example, the bricks of the fire altar only become the cows that the patron will possess in Heaven because the sacrificing priest speaks a mantra in which it is stated explicitly that the bricks shall be cows from now on: 

“O fire, may these bricks be cows to me — one, one hundred, one thousand, one hundred thousand ...”'\* However, speech act theory is insufficient to explain mantras fully, because they are regarded as effective of themselves. The mantras bring us to a radically participatory interpretation of language and to true language acts, i.e. to a use of language that can be categorized in speech act theory and yet at the same time goes beyond it. If, for example, the formula “for my life” is spoken over the rice cooked on the daily sacrificial fire, this formula is effective of itself, owing to the sacrificial context. In the understanding of the Indian priest, mantras are therefore not a speech act at all in the strict sense. It is not the priest who performs something through language, i.e. brings about the blessing. The blessing is brought about by the formula itself. 

So there exists something like a mantra substance, or to put it better: sound substance. This can be illustrated by the following simple example. According to the Western Christian view, the formula “Ich
