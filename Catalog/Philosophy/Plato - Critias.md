## About

A dialogue by Plato, the unfinished sequel to the *Timaeus*. Critias begins the story of a war between ancient Athens and Atlantis, an island empire, but the text breaks off just as Zeus begins to speak.

- **People:** Critias, Socrates, Timaeus and Hermocrates, almost certainly the Syracusan general known from Thucydides. Since Burnet (1914) scholars have divided over whether this Critias is the oligarch of the Thirty (460–403 BC) or his grandfather.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** the speeches belong to a Panathenaic festival, continuing the *Timaeus* (SEP). The sources consulted give no dramatic year. Critias places the war nine thousand years back; the tale passed from Egyptian priests to Solon, through Dropides, to an elder Critias, who told it at 90 to his ten-year-old grandson.
- **Composition and authenticity:** the IEP places it in Plato's late group (c. 355–347 BC), after the *Timaeus*, which points ahead to it. Wikipedia raises no doubt about authorship, but the SEP's *Timaeus* entry lists a 2017 article by Rashed and Auffret arguing that it is inauthentic.
- **Plan:** the second of a projected trilogy, to be followed by a *Hermocrates* that may never have been written. Some classicists treat *Timaeus-Critias* as one work.
- **Contents (Stephanus 106a–121c):** ancient Athens is described as an ideal city; Atlantis, allotted to Poseidon, is described in detail, with its temple and the metal orichalcum, until its virtue decays and Zeus resolves to punish it. Jowett judged the whole narrative Plato's invention.
- **Why it matters:** the SEP says the war story was meant to show the ideal society of Socrates' speech in motion. Wikipedia reports Welliver's reading of its core: a good city corrupted and divinely punished by defeat at the hands of the good city.
