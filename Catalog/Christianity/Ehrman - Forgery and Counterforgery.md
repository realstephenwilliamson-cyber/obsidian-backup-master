## About

Bart D. Ehrman's scholarly study of early Christian writings that make a false claim about who wrote them. He argues that such writings were, in general, meant to deceive, and he reads them in the light of the disputes in which they were written.

- **Author:** Bart D. Ehrman, an American New Testament historian.
- **Publication:** Oxford University Press, November 2012. Sources differ on length: Wikipedia gives 628 pages and Boylan's review x + 628; Ehrman's own site and Lincicum's review give 624.
- **Argument:** reviewers quoted on Ehrman's site say the book rejects the claim that forgery was an accepted literary practice in Greco-Roman antiquity, and that it treats over fifty examples in their polemical settings. Joel Marcus, quoted there, says it updates Speyer's thesis that pseudepigraphy was usually an attempt to deceive. Lincicum adds that Ehrman links some forgers to the reasoning of Plato's noble lie (*Republic* 459c–d).
- **Texts:** Lincicum says they run from the first to the fourth century, and calls Ehrman relatively maximalist about New Testament pseudepigrapha. Boylan's review names James, 2 Thessalonians and the Pastoral Epistles among the canonical books Ehrman judges forged, and the *Apostolic Constitutions* among the noncanonical.
- **Reception:** David Brakke (*Journal of Religion*, 2016) expected it to open new conversations about pseudonymous writing. Tony Burke (*Theology*, 2014) thought it would be easier to read with less background on each text. Lincicum (*Marginalia Review of Books*, 2013) questioned organizing the material around polemic and found claims stated as certain that were only possible or probable. Boylan (*Interpreter*, 2013) noted that the case against James is contested.
- **Why it matters:** Lincicum says it spans the boundary between New Testament studies and patristics. Wikipedia describes it as a scholarly examination of literary deceit in early Christian polemics.
