Berkeley Stephen Williamson

My question/comment on Berkeley’s *Treatise* has to do with this existence of abstract general ideas or perhaps more appropriately the lack of their existence. In the class discussion I centered my comments around paragraph 10 of the introduction, but one could say that the identification and subsequent refutation of abstract general ideas are one of the major themes in the entire work. In fact, I believe it is this discussion of abstraction rather than his denial of matter that should raise Berkeley his infamous place in the history of philosophy though I am not sure that he succeeds in his goal of removing the "doubt, uncertainty, absurdity, and contradiction in philosophy" as he sets out to do.

Of specific importance to the historical context of Berkeley’s argument is its bearing on language, particularly on names and other proper nouns. Berkeley is first and foremost writing a critique of Locke's assertion that names (or perhaps words in general) signify general albeit abstract concepts. As to their generality Berkeley concedes, but as to their abstraction he vehemently disagrees saying that general ideas can only be in the form of things previously perceived, that is particular things the perceiver has already seen, heard, smelled, etc. To accentuate his point Berkeley challenges his reader to name any "extended moveable substance or, in general, ~~for~~ any one idea or anything like an idea, \[that\] exist\[s\] otherwise than in a mind perceiving it." (Paragraph 22)

Anticipating a response along the lines of "I am imagining an object that is not perceived;" he counters with, "what is all this, I beseech you, more than framing in *your* mind certain ideas...and at the same time omitting to frame the idea of any one that may perceive them? But do not you yourself perceive or think of them all the while?"(Paragraph 23) Here I am reminded of the poem of Parmenides, "*Thinking and the thought that it is are the same; for you will not find thinking apart from what is..."* (B 8.34-36) I think one of the reasons Berkeley's argument (and Parmenides' for that matter) is studied to this day is that its refutation is problematic at best.
