> [!quote] Me
> Tell me about Minhat Yehudah

"Minhat Yehudah" (מנחת יהודה — "the offering of Judah," from [[Malachi 3#^3-4|Malachi 3:4]]) is a title used by several Jewish works. The two best known:

**The supercommentary on Rashi** by Yehudah ben Elazar, a French scholar of the Tosafist orbit, completed in the early 14th century (1313). It's one of the earliest sustained attempts to explain *why* Rashi comments where he does — resolving apparent difficulties, defending his readings against alternatives, and drawing on the Tosafist exegetical tradition. It circulated widely in manuscript and is a standard reference for anyone studying the reception history of Rashi's Torah commentary.

**The kabbalistic work** by Rabbi Yehudah Fetaya (Baghdad, 1859–1942), a major Iraqi Lurianic kabbalist. His *Minhat Yehudah* mixes commentary with striking first-person material — dream reports, accounts of souls in transmigration, and cases of possession he was asked to address. It's read alongside his commentaries on Etz Hayyim and Sha'ar ha-Gilgulim, and it has a devoted following in Sephardic and Hasidic kabbalistic circles.

A number of later halakhic and homiletic volumes also carry the name, since it's a natural title for any author named Yehudah. If you tell me which one you have in mind — or what context you ran into it — I can go deeper.

> [!quote] Me
> The author reads as Yehuda Hayyat

Then you're looking at Judah ben Jacob Hayyat (c. 1450–c. 1510), and his *Minhat Yehudah* is a commentary on *Ma'arekhet ha-Elohut*, the anonymous late-13th/early-14th-century systematic kabbalistic treatise (variously attributed to Perez ha-Kohen or Todros Abulafia, neither securely). It was printed with the text at Mantua in 1558. He wrote it in Italy at the urging of Joseph Jabez and other Mantuan scholars.

Two things make it important beyond its commentary function. First, the introduction contains Hayyat's own account of the 1492 expulsion — Portugal, a sea voyage that ended in capture, then destitution and imprisonment in North Africa before he reached Italy. It's one of the more vivid firsthand expulsion narratives. Second, that same introduction includes a critical guide to kabbalistic literature: which books he thought a student should study, which to handle carefully, and which to avoid. He was hostile to Abraham Abulafia's prophetic kabbalah and to attempts to fuse kabbalah with philosophy, and he put the Zohar at the center of the canon. That list had real influence on what Italian kabbalists read.

He also doesn't simply defend his base text. A notable disagreement: *Ma'arekhet ha-Elohut* tends to identify Keter with Ein Sof, and Hayyat rejects this, holding Keter to be the first emanated sefirah, distinct from the Infinite. More broadly he functions as a conduit of Spanish theosophic kabbalah into Italy, and the work is standard evidence for how that transmission happened after 1492.

Your paragraph has one structural problem, and one unclaimed asset.

**The problem.** The criterion you're using — the word requires a correlate — drags *Father* across the line with the rest. *Father* requires someone fathered just as *Almighty* requires something held. An unfriendly reader will notice that immediately, and the paragraph gives them nothing to answer with. The fix is to make the criterion "requires a *creature*" rather than "requires a correlate," which means saying explicitly that *Father* names an eternal relation internal to the divine life. One sentence buys the whole argument.

**The asset.** παντοκράτωρ is not "all-powerful." It's from κρατέω — to hold, to grasp, to rule. All-holding. Your instinct that "Almighty requires something to hold" is not a gloss you're imposing; it is what the Greek word says. The Latin *omnipotens* is what obscures it, because omnipotence *is* a property of the divine nature considered in itself, and would sit comfortably on the theologia side. So the English word you're reciting argues against you and the Greek word behind it argues for you. Say so.

**It's disclosed rather than returned.** Part One says what God is with nothing else in view. Nothing returns because nothing left; something becomes legible. That's your *spanda* and your whole soteriology in the structure of the book.

The Greek and Roman cults had rites, festivals, calendars and obligations, but no creed, no confession, and nothing a worshipper was required to hold. *Orthopraxy* is itself a modern coinage, built by analogy on *orthodoxy* to name the thing nobody had previously needed a word for — the word arrives only once its opposite has become the assumed shape of religion. Judaism belongs to the older pattern. Its disputes are overwhelmingly about what is to be done, and its central text is a set of instructions containing one declarative sentence.
>
> The obvious objection is Maimonides, and it should be met rather than avoided. The Thirteen Principles — set out in his commentary on *Sanhedrin*, at the tenth chapter, around 1168 — are the one serious attempt to state Judaism as a list of things believed, and they entered the liturgy as the hymn *Yigdal* and the *Ani Ma'amin*. But they entered by popularity rather than by ratification: no council adopted them, and no body could have. They were disputed almost at once, on the enterprise as much as on the contents — Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1410) against the framework, Joseph Albo (c. 1380–1444) reducing the number to three, Isaac Abravanel (1437–1508) objecting that if the Torah is from G-d then every commandment in it is a principle, and singling out thirteen diminishes the rest. Nor was Maimonides inventing the concern: the mishnah he was commenting on lists who has no share in the world to come, and those disqualifications are stated as things said. Belief is not absent from the tradition. What is absent is the habit of gathering it into a text and reciting it.
>
> Which is what the Creed does, and the reason is worth saying plainly. Judaism's communal bond was carried by descent, by land, and by the practice that came with both. Something had to carry the bond, and what carried it was shared confession. **The second principle**

The illustration should come from the book rather than from philosophy, and it should be one where the two views genuinely cannot be ranked. Your seated-at-the-right-hand material is the natural candidate:

> There are two ways to say that a thing looks different from different positions. Take the ascension. From where we stand, something left — a man went up and a place he had occupied is now empty. From the Word's side there is no interval and nothing was vacated, because nothing was ever set outside G-d to be returned to him. One way of handling that is to pick: the departure is how it seemed, the non-interval is how it was. That is the move I never make. The other way is to hold both as true and irreducible — the leaving is not an illusion corrected by the metaphysics, and the metaphysics is not a consolation offered to the leaving. I use the second throughout and the first nowhere.

What makes it work is that the collapsing version is genuinely tempting, so the reader feels the refusal rather than just reading about it.

**Bridging paragraphs one and five**

The risk you name is real, and it comes from a specific shape: *they had X, we needed more than X.* Any sentence built that way reads as supersession no matter how it's softened. The way out is to make the difference a matter of what each community's situation required, with no evaluative axis at all — and to let the Creed's *dependence* be the thing that's noticed, not its expansion.

A bridge sentence at the end of your fifth paragraph:

> Which is why the borrowing at the start is not decoration. The *Shema* is the sentence Israel says to be Israel, and when the Church needed a sentence to say, it did not compose one. It reached for that one, in Greek, and went on from there because its own situation gave it no descent and no land to lean on — not because the sentence was insufficient for the people who had it. One line was enough for a people who were already a people.

Three things are doing the work there. *It did not compose one* makes the borrowing a dependence rather than an appropriation. *Its own situation* locates the difference in circumstance rather than in adequacy. And the last sentence gives the *Shema* the stronger position — sufficient, and sufficient for the case that needed less machinery to hold it together.

If you want to close the door entirely, the sentence that does it is the one you already believe: the Church's need for more words is a fact about the Church, not about the *Shema*.
