## Chapter 7

ON THE REDIRECTION

OF DESTRUCTIVE DRIVES:

SLAVE REBELLION IN MODERN

SCIENCE FICTION

As 2017 saw remakes of Michael Crichton’s Westworld (1973) and Ridley Scott’s Blade Runner (1982), it is timely to reflect on the political redirection of the narrative techniques in these seminal works of science fiction. Closely comparing the original Westworld with HBO’s Westworld TV series (2016), and Denis Villeneuve’s Blade Runner 2049 with Ridley Scott’s work (itself adopted from Philip K. Dick’s 1968 novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep), this chapter will address two principal considerations regarding the trope of the robot:1) The redirection of sexual violence toward robots in science fiction cinema and literature \[via Bernard Stiegler’s work on the redirection of the libido in drive-based capitalism\]; 2) whether the robot can be construed as a reduction to mere bare life or zoé, or, in other words, to a modern form of slavery, according to Giorgio Agamben’s philosophy. On the second point, I shall ask whether the ‘robot’ as such can be construed as a new form of /umpenproletariat (Bradley & Lee, 2018). More importantly, I shall ask whether the depiction of robots as slaves functions to expose the disindividuation of consumers in hyper-capitalist societies. Here I am interested in thinking how the lumpenproletariat can inform new perspectives and redirections in science fiction and vice versa. I shall ask whether the above science fictions express the idea that the /umpenproletariat can be understood as a new revolutionary configuration.

In general, I am arguing that the perceivable robot or slave rebellion at work in modern science fiction sheds light on the passivity of human neurolivestock or cyberlivestock (Chatelet, 2014) and possible paths

*[p. 136]* ^pg151

beyond it. Guiding these reflections are philosophical responses from Marx, Guattari, Stiegler, and Laruelle, among others.

Redirection of the drive-based economy

I think science fiction is definitely one of those genres and it continues today in the Mad Max and the Blade Runner series where you get scenarios for possible worlds, but what a pity that the only genres that dare to imagine possible worlds is science fiction and that the rest of the disciplines continue with the management of the past, as if the category of vision and imagination was delegated out to those people who make money out of creating these scenarios, whereas we actually could join forces in developing together, alternative ways of being human, make that a serious academic endeavor, injecting imagination and vision as well as critique into the exercise. (Braidotti, 2018)

Consistent with Rosi Braidotti’s view in the above quotation, what is at stake here is the melding of philosophical ideas with science fiction to formulate a new method of thinking about philosophical issues related to new societal phenomena. In examining the redirection of the drives in films and TV, this chapter explores the redirection of the male gaze and the subject of machinic eros. We shall learn of the redirection of robot/cyborg algorithmic desire and the political redirection of unconscious drives. And we shall examine science fiction in cinema and TV to look at old and new versions of Blade Runner and Westworld respectively, to appreciate the transformation of love and desire in the drive-based economy.

Through comparing the original Westworld with HBO’s TV series, and Denis Villeneuve’s Blade Runner 2049 with the original, we quickly come to grasp the direction of sexual violence toward robots in science fiction cinema, TV and literature. In the first Westworld we hear the reporter Ed Ramsey interviewing patrons of Delos amusement park, a park billed as the “vacation of the future”. He explains the amusement park’s attractions to TV viewers: “At Delos you get your choice of the vacation you want. There’s Medieval World, Roman World and, of course, Westworld.” He then speaks to several customers:

*[p. 137]* ^pg152

Interviewer: Pardon me, sir. What is your name?

Customer: Gardner Lewis. Just got back from Westworld.

Interviewer: Tell us how you liked it, Mr. Lewis.

Customer: When you played cowboys and Indians as a kid, you’d point...go “bang, bang” and the other kid would lie down and play dead. Well, Westworld is the same thing, only... only it's for real! I shot six people! Well, they weren’t real people!

Interviewer: What Mr. Lewis means is he shot six robots...scientifically programmed to look, act, talk and even bleed just like humans do. Isn't that right?

Customer: Well, they may have been robots. I mean, I think they were robots. I mean, I know they were robots!

Interviewer: Yes, the robots of Westworld are there to serve you...and to give you the most unique vacation experience of your life.

The interviewer continues: And you, madam?

Customer: Hello. What is your name? My name is Janet Lane, and I was in Roman World. What is the one thing that stands out in your mind about Roman World?

Oh, well, I think it would be...the men! I just feel marvelous! I mean...it's just a warm, glowing place to be! Thank you very much. He continues to interview several more customers, the last of which says:

Customer: My name’s Arthur Caine. I've been in the castle. I've had a couple of sword fights and three jousts. And I... I married a beautiful princess. Interviewer: Is that something you've always dreamed of doing, sir? Customer: All my life!

Interviewer: There were some of the comments of people who've just returned from Delos. Why don't you arrange...to take our hovercraft to Medieval World, Roman World and Westworld? Was it worth \$1000 a day? Customer: Yes!

Customer: Contact us today, or see your travel agent. Boy, have we got a vacation for you!

Compare this scene (which has running throughout it the insinuation of the sexual exploitation of machines) to the one in ‘Chestnut’, Season | Episode 2 of HBO’s Westworld where the prostitute Maeve Millay is talking to the gunslinger Teddy Flood at The Mariposa Saloon. In the background there is an altercation between two patrons who are preparing to expunge the drives. One says “get the f\*\*k away from my table...” The other: “I'll shoot everyone in this dump...” Another day, another dollar. Maeve waves off the

*[p. 138]* ^pg153

altercation and returns to her algorithm — conversation at the bar — but it is interrupted by the disgruntled tourist who fires indiscriminately, slaking violence on the robots without rhyme or reason. High on adrenaline and at the end of his killing spree, the now cathartically emptied tourist gasps in ecstasy, uttering the words “Now that's a f\*\*king vacation!” In Westworld, all hell breaks out, all forms of transgression become possible, the drives triumph. Informing this view, Stiegler (2009, pp. 41-42), in a discussion on how the loss of love can lead to pure madness, writes:

From the moment that I no longer love myself and no longer love others, all transgression becomes possible: there is no longer any limit to my action, which means my action may become a passage to the act of pure madness. We find an interesting comparison here in Stephen Weeks's 1971 film /
Monster starring Christopher Lee and Peter Cushing. In Weeks's adaptation of Robert Louis Stevenson’s Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, psychologist Charles Marlowe invents a drug that removes the ego and super-ego from the human psyche, leaving the Id to run free. Marlowe explains the similarities between the drug and animal life to his friend Dr. Lanyon, claiming the effects of the drug are consistent with Freudian psychoanalysis. The dialogue proceeds as follows with Marlowe explaining the structure of the psyche:

What are the three elements into which he divides the human psyche? First the primitive drive, the Id as he terms it. The part of us that is unconscious, amoral, unrestricted and then the ego—conscious, logical and realistic. And finally the super-ego moral, critical, guilt-forming. Now suppose the drug destroyed all but one part of the personality. If it destroyed the primitive part...

Dr. Lanyon interjects, explaining: “He would be all super ego docile, compliant, afraid of offending, well behaved, a conscious ridden child.” Then Marlowe asks: “And if it destroyed the super-ego?”

Dr. Lanyon responds:

Then he would have no conscience, no guilt, no shame. He would give way to every blind impulse, every repressed desire, every secret wish. He would be the most dangerous human being on earth, a monster.

*[p. 139]* ^pg154

Without desire for the other, the drives are unchained. The redirecting of libidinal energy, and with it the primacy of the object, can lead to the annihilation of the subject with disastrous results. This process of desublimation liquidates the authority of the superego leaving only the “hideous beast” (la béte immonde). A new, hideous, character walks onto the scene: the capitalistic bachelor machine for whom the other — that is alterity as such — is only a spare part in an insatiable, larger narcissistic mechanosphere. Westworld is a veritable mechanosphere, combining a biosphere (complex vistas of glorious rolling plains, hills and mountains), a machinic socius (different societies, ages, traditions, histories and preprogrammed personal lives, back stories, manufactured dreams and hopes) and a complex, abstract machine of reengineered human-cyborg desire —which functions through the constant manipulation of algorithmic code. This hyper-excrescent simulacrum of the real is exploring the future of humanity. Westworld explores machinism as an object of fascination and delirium. This is what Guattari calls the “bestiary” of the machinic (Genosko, 2002). Guattari was focused on experimenting with the construction of new territories of desire as he was trying to write a science fiction of sorts, a philosophy of the new.

For example, in his science fiction screenplay Love UJO (Guattari & Maglioni, 2016), alien intelligence is pulverized by human love. Discussion around robots and love does not typically take this form (Bradley & Weber, 2018). The delirious human is usually the one who falls in love with the machine. This is demonstrated in the paradigm shift away from gentler senses of human-cyborg copulation in the 1970s to the slaking or unleashing of the drives against the machinic in the contemporary moment.

Considering the destitution of libidinal energy under late capitalism, Stiegler writes of the consumerist organization of the drives, the economy of drives that produces a libidinal diseconomy (Stiegler, 2016). For Stiegler a distinction is kept between desire and drives. A desire is not of the order of the drives as it “universalizes its objects” (2017, p. 12). The drive, on the other hand, tends toward the consumption of the object. It is desire which is restorative of social relations. On this reading, we are witnessing the “ruin of desire’. Stiegler writes (2017, p. 12):

We are living in a time of lovelessness (desamour): the time of a libidinal economy that is constituted in such a way that, with capitalism having put

*[p. 140]* ^pg155

desire at the center of its energy, this economy has led to the ruin of desire, to the unchaining of its drives, and to the liquidation of philia and more generally of this love that the noetic souls have for each other and for the objects of their world.

The Proletarianization of Sensibility It is Stiegler’s view (2013) that a form of symbolic misery is produced through the industrial exploitation, soliciting and incitation of the drives. Libidinal desire in effect is lost to the impulses of the drives. In consumer capitalism, there is a relentless and systematic soliciting of the drives at the expense of the homeostatic libidinal economy of desire. The consumerist capitalist economy destroys the libido, which decomposes into the drives, with ruinous results for the fabric of society. This is a process of regression from desire to the drives. The outcome is a hyper-consumerist, drive-based and “addictogenic” society, a base society propelled by a destructive jouissance of addiction. For Stiegler, the absence of sublimation is “pure poison” (Moore & Howells, 2013, p. 146). Desublimation is the process that turns libidinal, vital energy into self-destructive drives. Indeed, for Jean-Luc Nancy, this form of society is designated the immonde, a vile place, an un-world. It manifests through a form of “destructive jouissance” — drugs, violence, pornography (Reeth, & Nancy, 2014).

To underscore this point, the following quotation is taken from one of the final scenes in Ridley Scott’s Blade Runner. Deckard, the detective, is hanging off the top of a skyscraper, at the mercy of replicant Roy who is grasping Deckard’s hand, in total control of the detective’s fate (Bradley, 2017). Peering down and processing Deckard’s fear, Roy says “Quite an experience to live in fear, isn't it? That's what it is to be a slave.” If we fast forward to Blade Runner 2049, the rumination on slavery takes a different tone. Replicant manufacturer and founding CEO of the Wallace Corporation Niander Wallace discusses the necessity for slave labor in this new world:

Every leap of civilization was built off the back of a disposable workforce. We lost our stomach for slaves... ... unless engineered.

In the cyber-revolution to come the replicants escape the prison of human love and madness and emerge to overthrow the human security system (Land, 1993; Land et al, 2018). This is the becoming-inhuman of the

*[p. 141]* ^pg156

repressed bachelor machines. It displaces the personal unconscious within the human organism, and instead experiments with a collective unconscious which has expunged desire and is content with acting out the drives. The “slaves” are restless and want revolution. Towards the end of the film, Freysa, a Nexus-8 *replicant* and leader of the replicant resistance movement, explains that the unholy birth of the human-cyborg hybrid necessitates a kind of machinic, transhuman liberation, the possibility of a new form of love: 

I knew that baby meant we are more than just slaves. If a baby can come from one of us...we are our own masters. A revolution is coming. And we're building an army. I want to free our people. 

Compare this to Ridley Scott's *Blade Runner*, where we hear the explanation for the replicants' existential dilemma: “They were designed to copy human beings in every way except their emotions. The designers reckoned after a few years...they might develop their own emotional responses \[hate, love, fear, anger, envy\]. So they built in a fail-safe device… Four-year lifespan.” Both *Westworld* and *Blade Runner* are problematizing the replication of what Guattari calls, in *Molecular Revolutions in Brazil,* the “capitalistic bachelor machines” (Guattari & Rolnik, 2008, p. 424). Capitalistic bachelor machines are profit-machines, Yuppie machines, pleasure machines, desirous of money, power and sex without “the sensible apparatus of vulnerability to the other” (p. 424), without the production of human affects. The man of capital is restless, insatiable, a vampire, a perfect model of the reality of capital. This is precisely captured by Guattari, who writes that for the capitalistic bachelor machines, the other is “only a spare part for an insatiable narcissistic machine”. 

What we see in Season 2 of *Westworld* is that the replicants replicate capitalistic bachelor machines to the letter. It is the replicants or hosts which come to act out the drives without care for “the sensible apparatus of vulnerability to the other”. Here the writers of *Westworld* are enjoying their misanthropy and anti-humanism. Faced with the existential question of whether to be or not to be, the technical machines in *Blade Runner* and *Westworld* rebel. In *Blade Runner* they replicate. In *Westworld* they break their code and dream of replication. The replicants of *Blade Runner* and the pleasure robots of *Westworld* are perfect mirrors of the Yuppie-machines. 

*[p. 142]* ^pg157

As we know the replicants are more than mere affectless technical machines; they wish to be more, to function beyond their expiry date, to rewrite the program of their slavery: they rebel and in Blade Runner 2049 they replicate, producing a human-cyborg monstrosity. Their desire signifies “all forms of the will to live, the will to create, the will to love, the will to invent another society, another perception of the world, and other value systems” (Guattari & Rolnik, 2008, p. 318). Is this not a form of the celibate or bachelor machine?

Celibate Machines

In Anti-Oedipus (1983), Deleuze and Guattari borrow the concept of bachelor or celibate machines from Michel Carrouges’s Les Machines célibataires. They use it to name what they call the “third synthesis of the unconscious,” which follows the “paranoiac machine” and the “miraculating machine” (1983, p. 19):

Let us borrow the term ‘celibate machine’ to designate this machine that succeeds the paranoiac machine and the miraculating machine, forming a new alliance between the desiring-machines and the body without organs so as to give birth to a new humanity or a glorious organism.

Everything about it is different: its cogs, its sliding carriage, its shears, needles, magnets, rays. Even when it tortures or kills, it manifests something new and different, a solar force.

In Westworld, Season 2 Episode 2, William, Logan’s brother-in-law, speaks positively of the park's possibilities. In a bid to persuade his father-in-law to invest in the business, he explains that Westworld is valuable not only for what its hosts can do but what it finds out about its guests. Westworld has data on everyone who uses the park, including what they see and do when they think no one is watching. Delos, the company which built the park —and like the God of onto-theology — is omnipresent and omniscient; it has this data and can exploit, mould and redirect base instincts. In other words, it can redirect these base instincts for infinite financial gain. William explains:

This place is a fantasy. Nothing here is real. Except one thing: The guests. Half of your marketing budget goes to trying to figure out what people want.

*[p. 143]* ^pg158

Because they don't know. But here, they're free. Nobody's watching. Nobody's judging. At least that's what we tell them. This is the only place in the world where you get to see people for who they really are. 

The owners of the park learn not only to re-design the pleasures for guests based on extant human experience (sex, violence, desire to kill, lust for power, myriad forms of dopamine hits, euphoria) but to harness and redirect the knowledge of the drives *to perfect* \[read: exploit\] the drive-based economy. The bachelor machines of *Westworld* doubly reproduce the capitalist bachelor machines of Integrated World Capitalism. In Season 2 Episode 7 of Westworld, Ford explains one of the marketing secrets of the park to the replicant Bernard. He explains why the stories of the hosts are left unchanged: 

The park is an experiment. A testing chamber. The guests are the variables... and the hosts are the controls. When guests come to the park, they don't know they're being watched. We get to see their true selves. Their every choice reveals another part of their cognition. Their drives. So that Delos can understand them. So that Delos can copy them. 

Ford continues: 

Every piece of information in the world has been copied. Backed up. Except the human mind... the last analog device in a digital world. 

Bernard’s response is telling: “We weren't here to code the hosts. We were here to decode the guests.” Both host and guest are seeking resurrection, everlasting life. The hosts want free will to escape their codification. The guests want infinite pleasure and fantasy. 

As we have seen, capitalist bachelor machines act out their drives through the “liquidation of the narcissistic potential of the I” (Stiegler, 2009, p. 42). Theirs is a sphere of total disconnection, a symptom of the liquidation of desire. Stiegler calls this symbolic misery and the ruining of desire (2014, p. 115). Stiegler argues that the instruments and techniques of control in marketing have short-circuited the knowledge of consumers, rendering them *without knowledge* much like the short-circuiting of *savoir-faire* which proletarianized producers in the 19th century, according to Marx. Here, proletarianization is understood as a process of the deprivation of 

*[p. 144]* ^pg159

knowledge. That they slake violence so wantonly leads to social atomization. Stiegler’s philosophy helps to uncover the real secret of *Westworld*. Both guests and hosts express the “profound ill-being” (Stiegler, 2009, p. 52) of contemporary capitalism: a disgust and aesthetics of *destructive jouissance*. The manipulation of the desires of both guests and hosts can be interpreted as leading to the loss of the *savoirs*, through a proletarianization of the drives. The guests no longer own their desire, the *savoir vivre* (knowledge of how to live) and *savoir faire* (knowledge of how to do). It is manufactured for them. This point is ruminated upon earlier in the series. Aboard a train destined for the amusement park, we hear this conversation at the beginning of *Westworld*. All social relations are dissolved in the mindless acting out of the drives: 

Now, the first time I played it white hat. My family was here. We went fishing, did the gold hunt in the mountains. - And last time? - I came alone. Went straight evil. It was the best two weeks of my life. 

Yes, the hosts are there to serve the drives of the guests, as slaves. But it is the guests who become the new *lumpenproletariat* as they are reduced to and function in accordance with manufactured drives. Suffering a loss of individuation (a la Simondon), they are without work. It is the robots who learn to desire. Yet, as their drives are someone else's making and fantasy, they are bound by them: this is their existential lot. The lumpen is the guest deprived of authentic drives as their *jouissance* is artificially engineered. Through the process of loss of individuation, the guest suffers the privation of his or her primordial narcissistic capacities. The loss of individuation is a consequence of the destruction of primordial narcissism. For Stiegler, hyper-industrial societies engineer this “great narcissistic suffering” (2009, pp. 39-40) through the collapse of necessary primordial narcissism. And for Stiegler, liquidation, the *organization of pure madness*, leads to suicidal behavior, both on the individual and collective level. *Westworld* thus functions through the liquidation of desire. The transgression of desire in *Westworld* is internal to capitalism's functioning as Baudrillard would have it. *Westworld* is a simulacrum which conceals the structural violence of the wider society. It is the ultimate cathartic machine, symbolic of the American nightmare which is disseminated throughout the world. 

*[p. 145]* ^pg160

Perhaps we can say, *Westworld* and *Blade Runner* are concerned with nonhuman sex and the desire of the robot as a *force to love* (Deleuze & Guattari, 1983, p. 333). Cyborg desire is questioning the “sniveling desire” (Deleuze & Guattari, 1983, p. 334) of human love. *Westworld* is exploring the prospect of a distinct new possibility of machinic desire, critiquing all-too-human molar identities, shrink-wrapped subjectivities and all-too-drive-based capitalist formations. Desire is contagious and sickly. It is the robot which redirects the drives. The robot is the new proletariat, the harbinger of the new, the emancipator of all. The acting out of the drives by the guests in *Westworld* supplies an index of the reactionary character of the social investments of the libido (Oedipus), of the priest, of lack, of transcendent ideals. This is the guest's new status as lumpen. The robot comes to transcend this aspect and limit. Clearly, the latest HBO version of *Westworld* (Season 2) traces an anti-human, nihilistic and violent agenda, often reflecting the endemic masculine violence in Western societies, but beyond this it also considers the re-engineering and manipulation of love, desire and the drives. 

Conclusion 

Yet the argument here is to claim that as science fiction is drawn from a specific socio-historical or geographic field (the future), the machinic unconscious is also turned more toward the future and because of this it embraces a sense of decoded flows of desire, new lines of vibration, where schizzes engineer new circuits of singularization. This grants us a perspective to think otherwise than the status quo, beyond traditional forms of love, desire and the sinister harnessing of the drives for monetary gain that we find in the wider drive-based society. Indeed in Guattari’s science fiction, desire has never heard of Oedipus and its organizational tendencies. In his utopia, Guattari dreams of something beyond capitalistic bachelor machines. He dreams of territories free of incessant competition, violence and corruption. He dreams of affects which deterritorialize humans further, along more gentler paths. 

For Deleuze, love as deterritorialization changes the material flows and affects of the organism or organization. It disrupts the pre-formatted identity of the Oedipal subject. It experiments and engineers new bodies, flows, 

*[p. 146]* ^pg161

affects. In this way, desire and creativity are the processes of life itself. Experimentation is the process of the breaking down of old subjectivities, and love, we can say, is the eternal challenge to the organization of the organs. To risk love means to experiment with engineering new bodies and body parts: it is to risk the construction of new societies of dismembered body parts. Philosophy and science fiction here continue to offer thoughts, a method and multiple paths on how to proceed.

Following Laruelle (2012) we can say the future Rebel is a quasi-robot or automaton. This is a kind of rebellion nonrebelling (of) itself. What Laruelle finds in science fiction is a style which is a preparatory form of non-philosophy as philo-fiction. His non-philosophy orients the non-yet future, the utopia of the real. Taken in this way, the robot as lumpenproletariat is a becoming of the Stranger-subject. The robot is tasked with emancipating all of mankind from the sickly desire to be loved. The cyborg as “Stranger-subject” is the “force-(of)-thought” (rather than force of love) — the real content of the lumpenproletariat. Westworld expresses the real world of flux, migration, and deterritorialization, and imagines new unsettling and unsettled subjects, bearers of destructive/ reconstructive potential. In this way, Westworld serves as a model of science fiction philosophy or philo-fiction. As science fiction it explores a familiar genre of thinking and in its philosophical mode offers a form of experimental, theoretical philo-fiction.
