## About

A short dialogue attributed to Plato, on the love of gain. Socrates questions an unnamed friend about who the lover of gain is and whether gain can ever be bad, and along the way tells a story about Hipparchus, son of Peisistratus, whom Wikipedia calls an alleged tyrant of 6th-century Athens.

- **People:** Socrates and an unnamed friend. Hipparchus, who gives the title, appears only in Socrates' story.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** none is given in the sources opened.
- **Argument:** the friend says lovers of gain profit from worthless things; Socrates replies that no one who knows his trade can (225a–226d). Gain is good and loss bad, so all people love gain (227a–c). The friend's revised definitions fail, and he agrees, unwillingly, that all gain is good.
- **Digression (228b–229d):** Socrates presents Hipparchus as known for maxims such as "Do not deceive a friend," and gives an unusual account of his killing by two Athenians over a young boy.
- **Authenticity and date:** the IEP lists it among works whose authenticity was doubted in antiquity but which are sometimes defended today. Wikipedia notes its likeness to the *Minos*: they are the only dialogues between Socrates and one anonymous companion, the only ones named after a long-dead man, and the only ones that open by asking what something is. Many scholars, it says, conclude that one author wrote both, probably soon after the middle of the 4th century BC. Luc Brisson's French translation (2014) places both among works attributed to Plato that entered Thrasyllus' edition.
- **Text:** Stephanus pages 225–232 (Perseus catalogue). W. R. M. Lamb's Loeb translation is in *Plato* vol. 8 (Harvard, 1927).
- **Why it matters:** besides the definition, Wikipedia names a second theme, intellectual honesty in dialectical discussion: Socrates accuses the friend of arguing in bad faith. It also figures in the debate on which works in Plato's corpus are genuine.
