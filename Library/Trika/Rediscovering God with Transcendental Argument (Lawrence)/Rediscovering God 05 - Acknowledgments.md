## Acknowledgments

Zol

This book, a revision of my 1992 University of Chicago Divinity School Ph.D. dissertation, would not have been possible without a great deal of help from a number of people. First I thank the editors of the SUNY Series, Toward a Comparative Philosophy of Religions. Paul Griffiths has provided much valuable advice about revising the work, with regard to both my substantive philosophical arguments and the overall structure of the presentation. I have also found Laurie Patton’s detailed comments and suggestions in these same areas very helpful. Thanks are likewise due to the anonymous reviewers of the manuscript for their useful comments.

At Chicago, I am greatly indebted to my dissertation committee. Paul Griffiths was also one of the co-chairs of that committee. I began working with him during the last year of writing, and then also he gave me detailed and sensitive advice on developing my philosophical arguments throughout the study. Wendy Doniger, the other cochair, helped me relate my work to the interpretive concerns of the history of religions, and was a wise adviser throughout my years in graduate school. David Tracy through his guidance, as well as in his theological and hermeneutic writings, gave invaluable assistance in my formulation of the book's theoretical frameworks concerning in-

terpretation, philosophical rationality, and recognition.

Originally, I had on my committee the late Bimal Krishna Matilal. I first worked with Professor Matilal while he was in Chicago in 1983- 84. With him I studied Abhinavagupta’s Koarapratyabhijāðviortivimarsint (IPVV), the Spanda Kārikās, Bhartrhari’s Vākyapadīya, and the Buddhist-Naiyāyika debates. Between January and May 1991, I visited Professor Matilal in Oxford on a grant from the University of Chicago Committee on Southern Asian Studies. Professor Matilal answered many of my difficult questions, and confirmed the main features of my interpretation

*[p. xiv]* ^pg16

. It happened that my visit coincided with the recurrence of his terminal cancer. Those who were there know his superhuman dedication in arranging to meet his students while extremely ill. He died about ten days after we said good-bye. Professor Matilal’s contributions to Indian philosophy, comparative philosophy, and Indology are of course enormous. I might add that he was a great inspiration for me in settling on Indian philosophy as my academic specialty.

During the early eighties in Chicago I did some of my beginning work on Pāṇini, Bhartrhari, and Abhinavagupta’s aesthetics and philosophy with Edwin Gerow. Throughout my time in Chicago, I was assisted by several grants from the university’s Committee on Southern Asian Studies.

A lot of the Sanskrit scholarship presented here was done while I was in India between March 1987 and March 1989 on an American Institute of Indian Studies Junior Research Fellowship. There also I had excellent teachers. With Hemendra Nath Chakravarty of Varanasi, a student of Gopinath Kaviraj, I studied intensively Utpaladeva’s Isvarapratyabhijñākārikā (IPK), Abhinavagupta’s Iśvarapratyabhijñāvi-marśinī (IPV), and Utpaladeva’s Ajadapramātrsiddhi; and also sections of Abhinava’s IPVV, Tantrāloka, Tantrasāra, and scriptures and other works.

With Navjivan Rastogi of the Abhinavagupta Institute of Aesthetics and Śaiva Philosophy at Lucknow University I studied the sections of the IPK, IPV, and IPVV on action and causality. We also had invaluable discussions on several key features of my overall interpretation of the system. With Srinarayan Mishra of Banaras Hindu University I studied the discussions of memory and recognition in the same IPK, IPV, and IPVV, along with background discussions in various Nyāya works. We also studied Utpaladeva’'s Sambandhasiddhi, and the Sambandhasamud-deśa and Kriyāsamuddeśa of Bhartrhari’s Vākyapadtya. With Radheshyam Chaturvedi of Banaras Hindu University I studied the whole Śivadrṣṭi of Somānanda. I also had useful shorter series of conversations about the Śaiva-Buddhist arguments with the late Swami Yogindrananda of Varanasi and Ram Shankar Tripathi of Sanskrit University.

While writing up my dissertation in the summer and fall of 1990, I studied the Kārakāhnika of Patañjali's Mahābhāṣya with Saudmini Deshmukh of Maryland. I also mention that while in Oxford with Professor Matilal in 1991 I had one long and helpful discussion of the religious-historical context of the Pratyabhiñā system with Alexis Sanderson.

The revision of the dissertation as a book has been done at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (HKUST), where I

*[p. xv]* ^pg17

have been an assistant professor of humanities since fall 1992. Here I have benefited from working through some of my ideas with the students in my graduate seminars on Comparative Philosophy of Religions, Religious Theories of Language, and Relativism and Dialogue in Cross-Cultural Interpretation.

I have also been assisted by an HKUST Direct Allocation Grant. This enabled me to make trips to India in winter 1993 and spring 1994, during which I again consulted my former teachers Hemendra Nath Chakravarty, Srinarayan Mishra, and Navjivan Rastogi about a number of difficult interpretive questions. Professor Rastogi was particularly helpful in sharing with me detailed comments on the entire manuscript. The grant also permitted me to bring Professor Rastogi to Hong Kong in fall 1993. During this visit we continued our discussions of various aspects of my interpretation, and also engaged in further research together on the backgrounds to the Pratyabhijñā in Naiyāyika and Buddhist theories of recognition.

While working on this book, I benefited from developing some of the material herein as conference papers; these papers were then revised as journal publications. These are “Tantric Argument: The Transfiguration of Philosophical Discourse in the Pratyabhijñā System of Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta,” presented at the 22nd Annual Conference on South Asia, Madison, Wisconsin, 1993, enlarged in Philosophy East and West 46 (April 1996); “Śiva's Self-Recognition and the Problem of Interpretation,” presented at the Annual Meeting of the American Academy of Religion, Chicago, 1994, enlarged in Philosophy East and West 48 (April 1998); and “The Mythico-Ritual Syntax of Omnipotence,” presented at the 24th Annual Conference on South Asia, Madison, Wisconsin, 1995, enlarged in Philosophy East and West 48

(October 1998).

I thank my parents for their great support during graduate school and since then. I also thank my wife, Caroline, for all her encouragement in bringing this project to completion.

I am deeply indebted to all of the people mentioned, and to others too numerous to list. However, any errors I have made are

naturally my own responsibility.
