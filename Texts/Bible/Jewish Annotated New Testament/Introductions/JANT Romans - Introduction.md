## Title, Meaning, and Authorship

Romans is the longest, and thus the first, of the epistles as they are traditionally arranged. One of Paul’s latest letters, it has been enormously influential for the development of Christian ideals and identity. It has been equally important for Christian conceptualizations of Jews and Judaism as inferior to Christians and Christianity — although it can be read differently. Romans 11, which explains God’s commitment to Jews because of the irrevocable promises made to their ancestors, is a key text for reversing the legacy of contempt for Jews and Judaism present in this letter’s typical interpretations.

The traditional Christian view of Paul is that he was a “convert” to a new religion called “Christianity” and that he acted as have many converts before and since, by denigrating his previous religious commitment. This view is anachronistic, since Christianity did not yet exist as a separate religion in his time. Paul is also frequently understood as the great evangelist of Christianity who proclaimed liberation from the bonds of legalism (attention to external ritual rather than heartfelt repentance, humility, and belief) and “works-righteousness” (the view that one had to earn God’s grace by performing good works) that, according to this conception, characterized Judaism. Historically, this understanding has been reinforced by some Protestant Reformation theologians, such as Martin Luther (1483–1546), who saw medieval Roman Catholicism as a “works-righteousness” religion. Luther projected this view of “works- righteousness” onto Judaism and thus contrasted Protestant Christianity, as a “religion of grace,” with Judaism and Roman Catholicism as “religions of law.” In Luther’s view Paul rejected post-biblical rabbinic legalism just as Luther himself rejected post-biblical Catholic tradition for a “pure,” Protestant Christianity.

Most scholars today find this view of Judaism as a legalistic, works-righteousness system to be mistaken, even as they find Luther’s representation of contemporary Catholicism misrepresentative. The study of Romans presented here provides an alternative and more nuanced analysis of Paul’s thought that locates Paul within Judaism (see “Paul and Judaism,” p. 633), instead of outside of or against the Jewish communal way of life.

## Literary History

The epistle reflects both the mid-first-century, Roman cultural and political contexts of its recipients as well as the dynamics of the contemporaneous minority Jewish community. In Rome, Christ-following groups (Gk *ekklēsiai* = assemblies, usually translated “churches”) faced tensions within the Jewish communities (*synagōgai* = assemblies, usually translated “synagogues; *ekklēsiai* and *synagōgai* are synonyms). Paul was a Jew who saw the Christ-following groups as an authentic expression of Judaism; he understood his mission to be one of bringing about the fullness of the aspirations of the Jewish people for the benefit of all people; he was not seeking to found a new religion. He engaged in an outreach to the Gentiles/pagans (Gk *ethnē*; Heb *goyim*) because he believed that in Jesus the awaited age of God’s restoration and rule, beginning with the Jewish community (i.e., Israel), and extending beyond it, had dawned.

The letter’s intended recipients were Christ-following Gentiles. These Gentiles needed to understand how they should live as well as how they should interact with the Jewish community, which was larger than their own subgroup, although the Jewish community itself was a minority in Rome. Paul explains how, although they did not practice proselyte conversion — which he refers to by terms such as “circumcision” and “works of law” (or “rites of \[related to the\] convention \[of circumcision\]”) and so do not become Jews — they must understand themselves to be full members of the Jewish communities, even though many Jews were not welcoming them as more than guests. In other words, Paul encourages them to practice Judaism (the Jewish communal way of life) without becoming Jews. Paul also explains that their turning from idols to the worship of the One God alongside Jews, a sign that the messianic age had begun, would prompt his fellow Jews to reconsider the gospel’s claims.

## Interpretation

Since Paul cannot get to Rome until he has brought to Jerusalem the collection he has been gathering from among the Gentiles in Asia Minor (Turkey) and Greece, he writes to prevent further deterioration in the relationships between Christ-follower Gentiles, who are resentful that their claims for full inclusion are not accepted, and the Jews who refuse to accept them. These Gentiles are tempted to presume that they are replacing the Jews, whom they see as having lost God’s favor by not accepting the gospel message. Instead of advancing the gospel, their attitudes threaten to undermine God’s designs, and Paul writes to change their attitude. Now that they have acknowledged the God of Israel — without becoming Jews by undertaking the rite of proselyte conversion (symbolized by “circumcision” of males) — Paul imagines that these Gentile followers will help persuade his fellow Jews to see that God’s plans are being fulfilled in Paul’s ministry. In Paul’s view, some Jews would initially reject the gospel message in order that the Gentile mission might begin. But when these Jews see Gentiles turning from sin to righteousness, as expected in the messianic age, they will then want to emulate Paul in proclaiming the good news (“gospel”) of the arrival of that age to the Gentiles. Thus the salvation of the Gentiles is in part contingent on the original response of some Jews not yet being persuaded that the messianic era has arrived, but that nevertheless all Israel will be preserved ([[Romans 11#^11-26|Rom 11.26]]). Paul is claiming that the Gentiles’ humble and respectful attitude toward that Jewish community plays an important role in his ministry. When this occurs, Paul’s claim to be carrying out Israel’s awaited task of turning all people to the One Creator God worshipped among the Jewish people is validated.
