## 7 Conclusion

Mysticism is a complex of associations, with the individual’s experiences necessarily situated in a special context. In assessing the two mystical traditions of beguine spirituality and tantric experimentalism, it is not immediately obvious that there might be any correspondence. However, I have argued for at least a degree of correspondence of these two imaginative traditions; and the methodological justification for this was based on phenomenographical grounds. This recent research method postulates that there are a limited number of qualitatively different ways that people view and comprehend the world. The basic premise for the argument in this thesis is that there exists a Supreme Being, or Energy, which manifests itself to our perspective; and this perspective is, to be sure, a culturally specific one, as well as a differently comprehending one. While the expressions of the understanding of mystical experience will invariably differ, I would contend that in so-called extrovertive or ‘paintable’ mysticisms, the view of the divine imaginary is roundly similar, just because the framework of the human mind and body is universal. Both mystical outlooks examined here conveniently fall within the ambit of Bernard McGinn’s definition of mysticism: that it may refer to the immediate or direct experience of the *presence* of God. I emphasize ‘presence’ because both bridal mysticism and tantric mysticism demonstrate the way of visionary expedition to the godhead, through the senses of the body. 

The beguines presented a novel kind of mystical endeavour, although antecedents can be found in Bernard of Clairvaux’s exegesis of the Song of Songs. Its novelty lay in the attention given to the physicality of God as expressed in the Incarnation, or in Christ’s manifest presence in the Eucharist. This corporealization was envisioned well by the virgin Mechthild of Magdeburg, who used it to anchor her intense feelings of love for the trinitarian God. For the tantric practitioner, the nearness to, and conjunction with, the opposite sex constitutes the presence of the divine; indeed, the path to realizing (aspectual) Brahman is through accepting the holistic nature of spirit, mind and body; the sexual is not to be denied, since the divine is inherent in the triple self. I argued that both mystical traditions encompass ‘sensational’ mystical experiences, in which especially the senses of touch and sight are extolled; consequently they are understood as representing ‘embodied’ forms of mysticism. It is true that Mechthild disdains and deprecates the body, vehemently so on occasion, but still her mystical outlook verges on acknowledging the body as integral to being with God. Its earthly existence is, after all, an adumbration of its eventual heavenly existence (assuming of course one follows a Christian holy life while on earth). The view of embodiedness as avenue to the divine was grounded in the psychoanalytic feminism of the French philosopher Luce Irigaray, who proposed that the transcendent is immanent in the body, and who coined the term ‘sensible transcendental’ to describe this state. This happens to be an entirely apposite description for Tantrism. 

Basically, the sensational Christian mystic and the Śākta Tantric seek the same objective: fusion with the source of life. One calls this God, and the other calls it Śiva–Śakti (who is the manifestation of Brahman). They both seek eternal life: the one in the embrace of God in Heaven, and the other through co-inhering the Self (*ātman*) on Earth. However, while Christian theology maintains a dichotomy between ‘life’ and ‘afterlife’, earth and heaven, there is no such distinction for a realized *tāntrika*, for whom life pervades the universe in all its realms. Paradise is not only for afterwards, but for now, on earth. Esoterically, there is no ultimate split between ‘life’ and ‘death’. To take an analogy from chemistry: if the (crystalline) mind is pictured as a block of solid ice then this represents the normal conscious state. During ‘superconscious’, or ecstatic states, when the mind comes under the influence of

the (extra-mundane) divine, it begins to melt under the heat of the sun (Spirit). The mind liquefies into the soul (call it *jīvātman*); but it is still present, only in a different state. Emerging from the ecstatic condition the soul cools and resolidifies into mind. This can be extended to the process of dying, except here the mind (ego) sublimates (ice to vapour), for the instance of sudden death from a waking state, or evaporates (liquid to vapour), for the occasion of a slow death. Death then is really a phase transition.[^137] Concomitantly, it was argued that Tantrism sees reality as a continuous state of being, with the divine reaching into the mundane, which is why the notion of *realization* is stressed. If Mechthild was not constrained by theological doctrine she may have realized the attitude of continuity. 

I maintained that the element of love exists in both mystical traditions, although it is differently anchored. In the affective tradition it becomes a ‘love of love’, where love itself is adored in embodied form, that is, God in Christ, or as an abstract synopsis, God in the Eucharistic form. I called this a ‘gold love’, as gold is immutable, and therefore aptly signifies the separation of God and his soulful creation. In the tantric tradition it becomes a ‘love of beauty’, where beauty itself is adored in projected form, that is Goddess Lalitā, or as beauty in design, Lalitā in the geometric form of *śrīcakra*. I called this a ‘diamond love’, as diamond is cleavable, and therefore aptly signifies the identity of Śakti (Brahman-upon-reflection) and Self (*ātman*). Can it be said that these two are gendered? Is gold love a feminine kind and diamond love a masculine kind? Is the admiration of beauty just the ‘male gaze’? The issue may not be so black and white. Christian bridal mystics expressed a desire-laden love, while *tāntrika*s express an attachment-free love. Whatever the shortcoming of the two approaches, surely the highest expression of each is an altruistic regard. The two are, at the end, essentially the same, for love is just a dagger of beauty. 

I sketched a new paradigm in which the conventional Christian dichotomization of spirit and matter may be overcome and transformed through adopting a tantric viewpoint. In this, I argued for a threaded, or knitted,[^138] correspondence between Śiva and Śakti, and Father God and Holy Spirit. The first of these, that between Śiva and the Father God, is not so strange perhaps; although, Śiva is already a reflective manifestation of the One (Brahman), whereas the Father God is just that One. The second alleged correspondence, between Śakti and the Holy Spirit, does appear strange, not least because orthodox Christian theology understands the Holy Spirit in masculine terms, at least semantically (but still effectively). Where does this leave the second person of the Trinity, and the Virgin Mary? Jesus Christ, as the reification of the Word, is an adscititious element, which can be associated with the *verbum* that is collapsed within the emanatory role of Śiva–Śakti. As for the Virgin Mary, she must conform to the world-wide tradition of Mother Goddess figures, and might be equated with Sophia (Wisdom); in the paradigm presented here, she can in turn be equated with the Holy Spirit. Investigating these various correspondences would, I venture to say, prove fruitful.
