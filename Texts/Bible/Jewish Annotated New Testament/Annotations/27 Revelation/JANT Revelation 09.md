### 9.1–12: The fifth trumpet

*[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-1|Text]], p. 554* ^9-1

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-1|1]]:** *Star that had fallen from heaven*, is charged with opening temporarily the portal of the underworld, allowing the limited escape of demons; see 8.11. This is a feature of Revelation unique among sequences of eschatological catastrophes. *Bottomless pit*, or “abyss,” a terrestrial domain that John’s audience regards as a source of evil (11.7; 17.8). ^9-1-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-3|3]]:** *Locusts*, if inspired by the eighth plague (see [[Torah 15 - Bo#^10-12|Ex 10.12–15]]), John has magnified the horror from mere agricultural threat (see 9.4) to monstrous danger to humans (9.5–10), recalling military vehicles (9.9). ^9-3

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-7|7]]:** The *appearance* of the *locusts* recalls the prophet Joel’s image of a locust plague as a manifestation of the Day of the Lord, 1.4–7; 2.1–11. As in Ezekiel’s vision of the punishment of Jerusalem ([[Ezekiel 09#^9-4|Ezek 9.4–9]]), the locusts do not harm the 144,000 bearing God’s seal. ^9-7

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-11|11]]:** *Abaddon*, angelic king of the *bottomless pit*, Abaddon/*Apollyon*, an ancient Hebrew parallel to Sheol: [[Proverbs 15#^15-11|Prov 15.11]]; [[Job 26#^26-6|Job 26.6]]; 1QH 3.16–19. In the Tanakh, Abaddon usually refers to places where the dead live, a synonym for Sheol (e.g., [[Psalms 088#^88-12|Ps 88.12]]), though in [[Job 28#^28-22|Job 28.22]], as here, it refers to a demonic personification of death. The authority of Abaddon as the death-angel may also be related to God’s “Destroyer” in [[Torah 15 - Bo#^12-23|Ex 12.23]]. A curse against demonic powers from Qumran includes “the Angel of the Pit and the spirit of Abaddon” (4Q280 10.2.7). John is here introducing a novel demonology, which is why he gives both Hebrew and Greek names (9.11b). ^9-11

### 9.13–21: The sixth trumpet

*[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-13|Text]], p. 555* ^9-13

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-13|13]]:** *Horns of . . . altar*, quarter-round protuberances on Israelite and other ancient altars; see [[Torah 19 - Terumah#^27-2|Ex 27.2]]. ^9-13-2

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-14|14–15]]:** Unlike more traditional stories of the binding of demons in distant places (see [[LXX Tobit 08#^8-3|Tob 8.3]]; [[Daniel 10#^10-13|Dan 10.13]]), the angel *released* four vengeance angels. Such angels of punishment appear in early Jewish literature (*1 En.* 66.1), though they are usually not enumerated. They may be inspired by the six “men” of Ezekiel (9.1–2), who come to Jerusalem from the north. *Euphrates*, at that time, the Roman Empire’s eastern border, marking the boundary between Rome and the Parthian Empire. ^9-14

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-16|16–19]]:** John envisions the angels’ armies as cavalries with monstrous *horses*. *Fire and smoke and sulfur*, constitute three plagues. *Two hundred million*, a clearly exaggerated number meant to frame the sound the horsemen make (v 16: *I heard their number*). On contrast numbers meant to signify heavenly order, see “The Numerology of Revelation,” p. 550. ^9-16

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-20|20–21]]:** Allusion to idolatry as well as bodily sins recalls Ezekiel’s vision of the punishment of Jerusalem (Ezek 8–9; see [[Isaiah 44#^44-9|Isa 44.9–20]]; Ps 115). ^9-20

**[[NRSVue Revelation 09#^9-21|21]]:** *Sorceries . . . thefts*, crimes associated with idolatry ([[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 12#^12-3|Wis 12.3–11]]). ^9-21
