# Mark 7 (JANT notes)

[[JANT Mark 06|← Mark 6]] · [[Jewish Annotated New Testament|JANT index]] · [[JANT Mark 08|Mark 8 →]]

Text: [[NRSVue Mark 07|NRSVue]] · [[Mark 07|BSB and SBLGNT]] · Introduction: [[JANT Mark - Introduction]]

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-2|2]]:** *Defiled*, see “The Law,” p. 655 and 1.40–45n. ^7-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-3|3–4]]:** That Mark explains these practices may indicate an anticipated Gentile audience; Matthew (15.2) omits the explanation; Luke and John lack this story. The Pharisees observed *traditions of the elders* including hand washing (an observance that may assume the importing of the sanctity of the Temple into the home), but not *all the Jews* practiced this; Sadducees did not. ^7-3

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-6|6–8]]:** [[Isaiah 29#^29-13|Isa 29.13]]. Mark recognizes the *commandment of God*, but disputes arise over which remain binding (v 9) and which are merely tradition. *Hypocrite*, a term from Greek drama, means one who plays a part; here it indicates a person who has only the appearance of righteousness (12.15; Mt 23; *Did.* 8.1). As in 2.25–26, there are multiple responses to the challenge. ^7-6

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-11|11]]:** The controversy involves both determining which commandment, honoring parents or keeping vows, holds precedence, and whether vows can be repudiated. The Mishnah (*m. Ned.* 9.1) discusses “opening the way” to repentance (i.e., allowing a vow to be released if it leads to a conflict with something more important). *Corban* (Heb “korban”), a sacrifice or gift to God. When something had been declared devoted to God, it was generally not permitted for the giver to retract the gift. Rabbinic tradition also allowed release from “korban” when it deprived parents of their due. ^7-11

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-14|14–16]]:** Jesus’ comment is exaggerated, humorous and even scatological (as vv 18–23 will make clear). The witty rejoinder is now treated as a special revelation: *Listen . . . understand* (v 14), and *parable* (v 17); see 4.10–12n. ^7-14

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-17|17–23]]:** Like other parables, this one requires interpretation. ^7-17

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-19|19]]:** *Declared all foods clean*, lit., “cleansed all foods.” The first issue above, eating with unwashed hands, is expanded here to the abrogation of kosher laws. Although it is possible that the declaration reflects Mark’s interpretation of a saying of Jesus (found also at [[Romans 14#^14-20|Rom 14.20]]), this is unlikely. The controversy over Torah among Jesus’ early followers (e.g., Gal 1–2) would not have been as intense had it been known that he nullified kosher laws. Rather, the saying may reflect an older Jewish apocalyptic tradition concerning the elimination of impurity at the *from the marketplace, they do not eat unless they purify themselves* c Other ancient authorities add *and beds* d Gk *walk* e Gk lacks *to God* f Other ancient authorities add verse 16, *“Let anyone with ears to hear listen”* end of time ([[Zechariah 14#^14-20|Zech 14.20]]; see also 1.40–45n.). ^7-19

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-21|21–23]]:** The list of *evil things* that *come from within* includes violations of the Decalogue, but lesser sins as well. ^7-21

### 7.24–30: Syrophoenician woman

*[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-24|Text]], p. 85* · ([[Matthew 15#^15-21|Mt 15.21–28]]). ^7-24

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-26|26]]:** *Gentile*, lit., “Hellenis,” i.e., “Greek,” here a general term for non-Jew. *Syrophoenician*, Phoenicians from Syria as opposed to North Africa. The woman follows the tradition of the righteous Gentile, such as Ruth the Moabite, Achior the Ammonite (Judith), or the craftsmen of Tyre and Sidon, Phoenician (Canaanite) cities ([[I Kings 05#^5-15|1 Kings 5.1–12]]). Elijah and Elisha also healed Gentiles ([[I Kings 17#^17-8|1 Kings 17.8–16]]; [[II Kings 05#^5-1|2 Kings 5.1–14]]). The Syrophoenician woman may represent Gentile converts who believed in Jesus as Lord. ^7-26

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-27|27–29]]:** *Dogs*, a generic insult; dogs were regarded as shameless and unclean ([[Revelation 22#^22-15|Rev 22.15]]); the woman turns the tables on Jesus by figuratively taking this slur on herself. ^7-27

### 7.31–37: Healing a deaf man

*[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-31|Text]], p. 85* · ([[Matthew 15#^15-29|Mt 15.29–31]]). Some rabbinic sources consider a deaf person, “ḥeresh,” similar to being a minor (Heb “qatan”) or mentally ill (“shoteh”); such people are not considered responsible for observing the law ([[Yevamot 99#99b|*b. Yebam.* 99b]]). ^7-31

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-33|33–34]]:** *Spat . . . touched . . . sighed*, Jesus’ healings were usually by word alone, but here and at 8.23 physical manipulations are also involved. *Ephphatha*, see 5.41n. ^7-33

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-36|36]]:** Mark emphasizes the ironic contrast between the messianic secret and the thronging followers. ^7-36

**[[NRSVue Mark 07#^7-37|37]]:** Citing [[Isaiah 35#^35-5|Isa 35.5–6]], of the ideal future; elsewhere ([[Torah 13 - Shemot#^4-11|Ex 4.11]]), only God has such power to make the mute speak. ^7-37
