> [!quote] Me
> Let's talk about the katechon. As an Episcopalian (even one interested in philosophy and theology) I have never encountered this word before but after reading the Wikipedia entry I would like to know more. Also there may be a place for the idea in my system.

**Katechon** is a Greek participle from [[2 Thessalonians 02#^2-6|2 Thessalonians 2:6–7]]. The passage uses it twice: τὸ κατέχον, "what restrains" (neuter, v. 6), and then ὁ κατέχων, "the one who restrains" (masculine, v. 7). Something is holding back "the man of lawlessness" until he is revealed "in his own time." Meanwhile "the mystery of lawlessness is already at work," and the restrainer holds only "until he is out of the way." The writer tells his readers they already know what it is. No one since has agreed. In *City of God* 20.19 Augustine surveys the options and then admits he does not know what Paul meant. There is also a simple reason you've never met the word: the RCL reads this chapter once, on Proper 27 in Year C, as vv. 1–5 and 13–17. That skips exactly this passage.

**The main identifications**

- **The Roman Empire.** This was the dominant patristic view. Tertullian says Christians pray for the empire because its continuance holds off the end, and they pray *pro mora finis*, "for the delay of the end" (*Apology* 32, 39).
- **God or the Spirit.**
- **The Gentile mission.** Cullmann and Munck argued the gospel must reach the nations first, and that Paul himself is ὁ κατέχων.
- **The archangel Michael.** Colin Nicholl makes this case from [[Daniel 12#^12-1|Daniel 12:1]].
- **A hostile power.** Giblin reads κατέχω as "seize" rather than "restrain."

Many scholars also doubt Pauline authorship of 2 Thessalonians, which complicates reading it alongside Romans.

**Its modern political career**

Carl Schmitt, in *Der Nomos der Erde* (1950), argued that the medieval Christian empire understood itself as the katechon. In his hands the concept became a theological warrant for order-keeping power against chaos. That he joined the Nazi party in 1933 is not incidental to its appeal for him. Jacob Taubes answered from Jewish apocalyptic: Schmitt thinks from above, like a restrainer, and the apocalyptic thinks from below. Agamben inverts Schmitt. For him the katechon is any constituted power that masks the lawlessness at its own core, and the revelation of the lawless one is that unmasking.

**Where it might fit your system**

*The strongest fit is formal.* The passage uses revelation vocabulary (ἀποκαλυφθῆναι in vv. 3, 6, 8). Verse 7 says lawlessness is *already* operative, so the apocalypse adds visibility, not content. That is close to "nothing is added in the seeing." The katechon is also a divinely sanctioned concealment that is not a fall, which is structurally like tirodhāna among the five acts (Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam 10). The lexicon supports this from another direction. In [[Romans 01#^1-18|Romans 1:18]] the same verb names humans "suppressing" the truth, so κατέχω can hold down the truth or hold down lawlessness.

The misfit is real, though. What is concealed here is anomia, not the Self. And 2 Thessalonians needs real sequence ("unless the apostasy comes first," v. 3) and a real destruction (v. 8), while you treat sequence as perspectival. Using it this way would be avowed interpretation against the grain, and should be marked as such.

*The most natural fit is delay as mercy.* Three sources point the same way:
- Tertullian's *pro mora finis*
- [[2 Peter 03#^3-9|2 Peter 3:9]]: patience, "not wanting any to perish"
- the Cullmann–Munck mission reading

Read together, the interval exists *for the nations*. That lines up with [[Romans 11#^11-25|Romans 11:25–26]], "until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in." It gives a universalist, non-supersessionist account of why there is a delay at all, without making Israel the thing waiting.

Your study partner would have Jewish material to push back with:
- Sanhedrin 97b–98a, against calculating the end, including the reading of [[Isaiah 60#^60-22|Isaiah 60:22]] ("in its time I will hasten it") as two alternatives
- the Three Oaths (Ketubot 110b–111a), which forbid forcing the end
- Scholem's "life lived in deferment"

These are analogues, not sources.

*The warning concerns John.* The Fourth Gospel leaves almost no interval to restrain: "the hour is coming and now is" (5:25), "now is the judgment of this world" (12:31). The Johannine letters are the only NT texts that use ἀντίχριστος, and they pluralize and interiorize it: "many antichrists" ([[1 John 02#^2-18|1 John 2:18]]). That suits recognition better than 2 Thessalonians does.

But the Antichrist tradition that grew up around [[2 Thessalonians 02#^2-1|2 Thessalonians 2]] has an anti-Jewish strand that runs straight through John. Irenaeus read [[John 05#^5-43|John 5:43]], "if another comes in his own name, you will receive him," as the Jews receiving the Antichrist (*Adv. Haer.* 5.25.4). He also derived the Antichrist from the tribe of Dan (5.30.2). If the katechon enters the Gospel of Recognition, it will need to be explicitly separated from that reading of 5:43.

> [!quote] Me
> How does Irenaeus determine that the antichrist comes from the tribe of Dan?

Irenaeus combines two texts in *Adversus Haereses* 5.30.2.

The first is [[Jeremiah 08#^8-16|Jeremiah 8:16]] in the Septuagint: "From Dan we shall hear the sound of his swift horses; at the sound of the neighing of his horses the whole land shook; and he will come and devour the land and its fullness, the city and those who dwell in it." Irenaeus takes the "he" to be the Antichrist and reads ἐκ Δαν as his origin. On his reading, Jeremiah shows not only that the Antichrist comes suddenly but also which tribe he comes from.

The second is [[Revelation 07#^7-4|Revelation 7:4–8]], where Dan is missing from the twelve tribes sealed among the 144,000. Irenaeus says that is why: the tribe is not counted among the saved because the Antichrist comes from it. Each text supplies what the other lacks. Jeremiah links a destroyer to Dan, and Revelation excludes Dan without giving a reason. As far as I know, this is the earliest surviving explanation of the omission in [[Revelation 07#^7-1|Revelation 7]].

The Jeremiah reading doesn't hold up in context. [[Jeremiah 08#^8-16|Jeremiah 8:16]] describes invasion from the north, and Dan is Israel's northernmost city, so it is where the enemy's approach is heard first. [[Jeremiah 04#^4-15|Jeremiah 4:15]] has the same geography: a voice announcing disaster "from Dan" and from Mount Ephraim. Irenaeus turns the place where the invader is first heard into the tribe he is born into.

Dan was an easy target because of earlier material:
- **Judges 18:** the Danites carry off Micah's idol and set up their own shrine.
- **[[I Kings 12#^12-29|1 Kings 12:29]]:** Jeroboam places a golden calf at Dan.
- **Testament of [[Daniel 05#^5-6|Dan 5:6]]:** "your prince is Satan," though that text's date and Christian reworking are disputed.
- **Rabbinic tradition:** as far as I know, Numbers Rabbah 2:10 also ties Dan's northern camp to darkness and idolatry.

A generation later, Hippolytus (*On Christ and Antichrist* 14–15) adds two more texts. [[Torah 12 - Vayechi#^49-17|Genesis 49:17]] has Dan as a serpent biting the horse's heel. [[Torah 54 - V'Zot HaBerachah#^33-22|Deuteronomy 33:22]] has Dan as a lion's whelp. Hippolytus uses the lion image to make the Antichrist a counterfeit of Christ, the lion of Judah.

This matters for your 5:43 concern. The Dan derivation is what makes Irenaeus's Antichrist an Israelite. With that in place, the Jews receiving "another who comes in his own name" (5.25.4) and the Antichrist sitting in the Jerusalem temple form a single picture: Israel rejects Christ and accepts his counterfeit. Taking Dan away removes the genealogical basis for that picture, though not its polemical force.

> [!quote] Me
> Switching gears. One interesting aspect of the Sukkot eschatology imagery is palm Sunday, which I read as conflating Jesus's arrival with hosana raba

The Sukkot reading holds up well, and John is the Gospel where it's strongest. Tying it specifically to Hoshana Rabbah is harder.

**What points to Sukkot**

[[Psalms 118#^118-1|Psalm 118]] by itself doesn't point there, because Hallel belongs to Passover too; the Levites sang it during the slaughter of the lambs (m. Pesachim 5:7). What is specific to Sukkot is the *combination* of palm and hosanna. The lulav was shaken at the words "Ana Hashem, hoshi'ah na" (m. Sukkah 3:9), and only during Sukkot.

Zechariah supports the reading from two sides. It supplies the donkey (9:9), and it supplies the eschatological Sukkot in which the nations go up yearly to worship the King (14:16). It also has the LORD's feet on the Mount of Olives (14:4), which is where the procession starts in Mark and Luke.

The markers are unevenly spread across the Gospels:
- Mark and Matthew have hosanna and generic leafy branches.
- Luke has neither.
- John alone names palms (τὰ βαΐα τῶν φοινίκων) and adds "the King of Israel" to [[Psalms 118#^118-26|Psalm 118:26]].

**Where Hoshana Rabbah is harder to show**

The seventh day's distinctive features are the sevenfold circuit of the altar and the willows (m. Sukkah 4:5), and the entry has neither. The Mishnah describes that day's ritual without yet using the name Hoshana Rabbah, which appears in later midrash. What the entry shares with the seventh day is the hosanna cry itself, but the cry was said on every day of Sukkot.

In John, the stronger Hoshana Rabbah candidate is 7:37: "on the last day, the great day of the feast," Jesus cries out about living water. Commentators debate whether that is the seventh day or the eighth. If it is the seventh, John may be splitting Sukkot across his Gospel:
- chapters 7–8 keep the festival's climax, with water and then light (8:12);
- chapter 12 moves the palms and hosanna to Passover.

On that reading, the conflation happens across the book rather than inside the entry narrative.

**Two features of John's version**

First, βαΐον is the word 1 Maccabees 13:51 uses for Simon's victory entry into the Akra. Also, 2 Maccabees 10:6–7 says the first Hanukkah was kept "in the manner of Sukkot," with palms. So palm-waving already carried Maccabean royal overtones.

In John alone, Jesus finds the donkey *after* the acclamation (12:14). That makes [[Zechariah 09#^9-9|Zechariah 9:9]] read as his answer to "King of Israel." It is also worth noting that "King of Israel" appears in John only here and at 1:49.

Second, 12:16 says the disciples did not understand these things at first, and "when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered." John marks the entry's meaning as recognized only afterward.

**For the Creed Gloss**

The later tradition that Hoshana Rabbah seals the Yom Kippur verdicts would suit your judgment-then-banquet order. But it is medieval, drawn largely from the Zohar. The early Sukkot judgment is m. Rosh Hashanah 1:2: at the festival the world is judged for water.

The fullest New Testament Sukkot scene is [[Revelation 07#^7-9|Revelation 7:9–17]]. It has palms in hand, the cry of "salvation," a multitude from every nation, God "tenting" over them (σκηνώσει), and springs of living water. It comes immediately after the tribal list that omits Dan.

> [!quote] Me
> I have actually been thinking about starting the gloss with a disclaimer section (rather than a preface or intro as in early versions) so that I don't have to repeat or defend myself "historically" but only explain myself theologically.

That's a good fix, and the title already does half of it: *What I Mean When I Say the Creed* is itself a disclaimer. The section only has to state the terms once, so the body never has to.

Going by what you've already settled, it could hold four things:

- **The criterion.** The gloss records what is on your mind when you say the words, not what the councils meant. Historical meaning appears only where it is part of what's on your mind, never as the standard.
- **Anachronism.** Later readings are permitted. Where one could be mistaken for an early one, a footnote dates it without arguing for it.
- **Outside sources.** Material from beyond the Creed's own tradition serves as the reciter's frame. If a parallel is structural rather than a claim of influence, say so here once instead of at each occurrence.
- **Faith-based.** The gloss is an account of saying the Creed, not an argument that it is true.

Two cautions.

First, the disclaimer removes the need to *defend* historically, but it doesn't remove the dating. The footnotes still give plain dates, as you settled last turn. Otherwise a reader can't tell a Zoharic reading from a Nicene one.

Second, opening a book with a disclaimer puts what it isn't before what it is. If the section leads with the criterion, which is a positive statement, and the exclusions follow, it orients the reader the way a preface would without becoming one.

There is a precedent in Kierkegaard. He called his upbuilding discourses "discourses, not sermons," because he had no authority to preach, and he said so in their prefaces. Disclaiming authority let him write theology without having to rule on doctrine. Whether you want that comparison in the text is up to you, but it shows the move has a respectable history.

> [!quote] Me
> I like the precedent but I am saying something different. Something along the lines of even Popes and Primates van only say the Creed for themselves.

That's a stronger claim than Kierkegaard's, and a better one. He disclaims his own authority. You're saying that authority, however much of it someone holds, doesn't reach the act of confessing.

Augustine is a closer precedent. In *De Trinitate* 13.2.5 he distinguishes the things believed from the faith by which they are believed; the later terms *fides quae* and *fides qua* come from that distinction. He then says each person sees their own faith in their own heart, but cannot see anyone else's and can only believe it is there. The content is shared, but the act can't be transferred. That is your point, made by a Doctor of the Church.

Two things need settling for the claim to hold.

**What "say" means.** Popes and councils can define what the Creed teaches, and your claim doesn't need to deny that. It only needs "say" to mean "confess," not "interpret." If you mean the stronger version — that no authority can fix what the words mean when *you* say them — that's defensible too. But it is an ecclesiological claim some readers will contest, so the disclaimer should say which version you mean.

**The plural.** The councils wrote Πιστεύομεν, "we believe," and Rite II keeps it. The bishops at Nicaea were confessing on the church's behalf, which looks like exactly what you say can't be done. There is an answer: "we" is still said one mouth at a time, and saying it claims membership rather than permission to speak for the others. But the disclaimer should take on the plural directly, because your Episcopal readers say "we" every Sunday.

> [!quote] Me
> My point is the stronger claim. Every person confessing the Creed gives it their own meaning. I was thinking along the lines of Augustine. He really is genius

He is. And the more directly relevant passage for your stronger claim is *Confessions* 12, not *De Trinitate*. There Augustine defends several true readings of [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-1|Genesis 1]] and says that if he had been Moses, he would have wanted to write so that each reader could find whatever true meaning they were able to find (12.26). That is plurality of meaning in the same words, endorsed as a good thing.

You should know one limit before citing him, though, because this is where the precedent stops short of your claim. Augustine bounds plural meaning twice:
- Readings must not contradict the rule of faith, which is the Creed's content in substance.
- Readings must build up love of God and neighbor (*De doctrina christiana* 1.36.40).

For him, the Creed is the fixed measure that other texts are read against. Applying plurality *to the Creed itself* goes a step past him.

So Augustine supports you on two points: faith is personal and can't be seen from outside (*De Trinitate*), and many meanings in one set of words can all be legitimate (*Confessions*). He doesn't support the move from the second point to the Creed. The disclaimer can say this plainly: you are taking his hermeneutic of Scripture and applying it to the text he used to govern that hermeneutic.

The charity test in *De doctrina* is a boundary you could adopt as your own if you want one. If your universalism means you don't want one, the disclaimer is the place to say so, once.

> [!quote] Me
> That's funny though. What that says is what was virtue in Moses is vice in the synod?

It's funny, and it's close to right, but Augustine has an answer. For him the two texts do different jobs. Scripture's openness is a mark of its divine author: it holds more truth than any one reader can exhaust. A rule of faith has the opposite job, to fence the field. An open fence isn't generous, it's just broken.

The history, though, tips in your favor. The synod was explicitly trying to close what Scripture left open. Athanasius (*De Decretis* 19–20) says the bishops at Nicaea first proposed scriptural phrases like "from God" and "the power and image of the Father." The Eusebians kept agreeing to them, because they could read each phrase in their own sense. So the council reached for a non-scriptural word, *homoousios*, precisely to stop plural readings.

The word promptly acquired plural readings anyway. To its critics it sounded modalist, and Marcellus of Ancyra's use of it seemed to confirm that worry. Athanasius himself rarely used it for decades afterward. The fourth-century fight over what *homoousios* meant is about the best evidence you could ask for that no synod can give a word one meaning in every mouth.

There's also an older sense of the Creed that suits your disclaimer. Rufinus explains *symbolum* as a token or watchword, like the password soldiers use to tell friend from foe in a civil war. A password checks that you say the right words. It doesn't check what you mean by them. On that understanding, the Creed was always a sign you say together, and the meaning was always each speaker's own.

> [!quote] Me
> It is a bit cliche (because it is one of my oldest beliefs) but that's what Jesus is getting at when he includes mind in his modes we are to love God in.

It's an old belief, but it's not a cliché, and the text is on your side, with a small adjustment.

The adjustment is that "mind" probably isn't Jesus's addition. [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^6-5|Deuteronomy 6:5]] has three terms: heart (*levav*), soul, might. In the Septuagint manuscripts, *levav* is sometimes rendered καρδία ("heart") and sometimes διάνοια ("mind"); Codex Vaticanus has διάνοια. [[Mark 12#^12-30|Mark 12:30]] seems to keep both renderings, which gives four terms. The Gospels then vary among themselves:

- [[Matthew 22#^22-37|Matthew 22:37]] has heart, soul, mind, and drops strength.
- [[Luke 10#^10-27|Luke 10:27]] has the lawyer recite it, not Jesus.
- [[Mark 12#^12-33|Mark 12:33]] has the scribe repeat it as heart, understanding (σύνεσις), strength.

The reason one Hebrew word splits into two Greek ones is that in Hebrew anthropology the heart is where thinking happens. So the demand to love God with the mind is already in the Shema. That strengthens your point and makes it Jewish before it's Christian.

The inference from "love God with your mind" to "your meaning is your own" rests on the possessive. The command is "all *your* mind," and the Shema's grammar sharpens this. It is addressed to Israel collectively ("Hear, O Israel"), but the verb "you shall love" is singular. That is the same structure you want for "We believe": a collective address, answered by each person singly.

The Mishnah gives you one more piece. Berakhot 9:5 notes that Deuteronomy spells "heart" as *levav*, with a doubled letter, instead of the shorter *lev*, and reads the doubling as "with both your inclinations," the good and the evil. Loving God with the whole heart means the whole of it, including what hasn't been set right. Extending that from heart to mind is your step, not the Mishnah's, so mark it as yours. Extended that way, the claim is that God asks to be loved with the mind you actually have, not with one a synod has corrected.

---

**To the Reader**

This is not apologetics. Nothing here argues that the Creed is true, and nothing in it is meant to persuade anyone who does not already say it.

Nor is it catechesis. It does not set out what the Church teaches. Where I depart from that teaching I say so, and I do not present the departure as the teaching.

Nor is it a history of doctrine. I do not argue that the Council meant what I mean, and in several places I know it did not. When a reading is larger than the clause was drafted to carry, or later than the text it is read into, or borrowed from a tradition that would not follow me where I take it, the footnote says so and dates it. It does not defend it. History comes into the body only where it is part of what I am thinking when I say the words. At the crucifixion, it is most of what I am thinking.

And it is not a proposal for how anyone else should say the Creed, because no one can say it for anyone else. That includes Popes and Primates.¹ The councils fixed the words and chose them to rule meanings out, and they had the standing to do it. What no council can do is say the words in another person's mouth. The Rite II text says *we*, and I say it with everyone else in the room, but *we* is said one mouth at a time. Augustine puts it exactly: the faith that is believed is held in common, but the faith by which each person believes it is in that person's own heart, where he can see it and no one else can.² The commandment is to love G-d with all your mind, and the mind it asks for is the one you have.³

So the first person throughout is not a matter of style. It is the only person in which the Creed can be said.

What is left, once all of that is set aside, is testimony: a set of words I did not write and do not own, said every week, and what I am thinking while I say them. The body is that. The footnotes try to make it legible to someone else. If you have come for a defense or an explanation, it is not here, and it is done better elsewhere.

¹ Kierkegaard published his upbuilding discourses "without authority," disclaiming his own. This disclaims everyone's.

² *De Trinitate* 13.2.5. Augustine distinguishes the things believed from the faith by which they are believed, the later *fides quae* and *fides qua*, and says each person sees his own faith and can only believe that another's is there.

³ [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^6-5|Deuteronomy 6:5]]; [[Mark 12#^12-30|Mark 12:30]]. Mark's Greek has *dianoia*, mind, beside heart. The Hebrew *levav*, heart, was already where thinking happened, so the demand is the Shema's before it is the Gospel's.

**What eternal begetting does**

Without it, your teleology becomes Hegel's or Whitehead's. In Hegel, God comes to know himself through the world. In Whitehead, God's consequent nature takes in every creature's experience and grows by it, which is very close to "the aggregate of all beliefs." Either way, God needs the world.

Eternal begetting blocks that. If the Son is God's self-knowing (*vimarśa*, in your II.7), and that self-knowing is eternal, then God's self-knowledge is complete apart from creation. Creation adds standpoints, not knowledge; that is your own line, "nothing is added in the seeing." It also makes creation free rather than needed, which is *svātantrya*.

Maximus gives the Christian form: the Word of God "wills always and in all things to accomplish the mystery of his embodiment" (*Ambigua* 7). The Jewish form is the midrash that Tanya ch. 36 builds on: God desired a dwelling in the lower realms (Tanchuma, Naso 16).

**"Is and is beyond"**

This fits your Part One distinction. "God is the aggregate" is true in the register of *oikonomia*, God toward us; "beyond" is *theologia*. Your own rule then applies: nothing true of the economy licenses a conclusion about the theology. That rule is what keeps the aggregate from defining God.

**Two words I'd push on**

*Aggregate.* An aggregate is a sum of parts, and you've written against that twice. At XI, a particular "is not a portion of the universal." In your note on *echad*, you use Maimonides against compound-unity readings. What you seem to mean is that the whole is present at each standpoint, seen from there. Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam 4 has the vocabulary: the individual knower is consciousness contracted, containing the universe in contracted form. Leibniz has a Western version in *Monadology* 57: one city seen from different sides, so there are as many universes as there are perspectives on it.

*Beliefs.* Beliefs include false and cruel ones. If God is the aggregate of all beliefs, God includes the belief that the Jews killed Christ, which your crucifixion clause spends pages undoing. Your framework already has the distinction you need. A belief is a standpoint, often combined with the seeming of separateness, and that seeming is "not a thing." So God includes every view as a view from somewhere, not every assertion as true. "Standpoints" or "perspectives" says that; "beliefs" doesn't.

**One tension**

"Purpose" usually implies an end state, and your eschatology has none: restoration is asymptotic, and arrival would be smaller than approach. So the purpose has to be operative rather than terminal, realized in each moment and never finished, like the booth. That is also where it meets the *krīḍā* note at XIII. Play is for nothing outside itself, and a medium whose purpose is to be the medium has the same shape.

**Mostly yes, with one asymmetry.** The Anglican form of the celebrant principle is Article XXVI, whose title says the unworthiness of ministers "hinders not the effect of the Sacrament." Your disclaimer is the other half of it. The minister's inner state doesn't reach the rite, and the minister's authority doesn't reach anyone else's inner state. The rite stands between two interiors: it doesn't depend on the one and can't supply the other.

The asymmetry is that the recipient's interior matters in a way the minister's doesn't. Article XXV says the sacraments have their wholesome effect only "in such only as worthily receive the same." The scholastic terms mark the difference: *ex opere operato* for validity, which doesn't depend on the minister, and *ex opere operantis* for fruitfulness, which depends on the recipient. So the minister can be left out of the account, but the recipient can't. That suits your disclaimer. The one inner state the rite needs is the one nobody else can supply.

**On the priest who suspects insincerity**

Absolving on suspicion isn't a sin. Refusing on suspicion might be.
- **He can't see the heart.** By Augustine's account, he can only believe another person's faith is or isn't there. Withholding absolution on suspicion means judging something he has no access to.
- **The words don't lie.** One of the Prayer Book's absolution forms, taken from 1662, absolves in the name of the Lord who left power to absolve "all sinners who truly repent and believe in him." The condition is written into the formula. If the penitent isn't repentant, the priest has spoken a true sentence whose condition wasn't met. The Rite II Eucharistic absolution is a prayer, "Almighty God have mercy on you," and praying for someone who may be insincere is no sin.
- **The line is knowledge, not suspicion.** Catholic canon law says that if the confessor has no doubt about the penitent's disposition, absolution is to be neither refused nor deferred (can. 980). The moral tradition generally resolves doubtful cases in the penitent's favor. Deferral is for the penitent who has made their lack of repentance plain.

The Mishnah puts the whole weight on the penitent. Someone who says "I will sin and repent" is not given the chance to repent. Someone who says "I will sin and Yom Kippur will atone" finds that Yom Kippur doesn't atone for them (Yoma 8:9). The day hasn't failed, and the high priest hasn't sinned. It simply doesn't work for that person.
