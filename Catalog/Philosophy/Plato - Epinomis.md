## About

A short dialogue transmitted with Plato's works as a sequel to the *Laws*: the same three speakers ask how a mortal may acquire wisdom. Its title is given as "or On the Laws"; Werner Jaeger called it an appendix to the *Laws*.

- **Characters and setting:** an unnamed Athenian, who does most of the talking; Clinias of Crete; Megillus of Sparta. No place or dramatic date is stated.
- **Subject:** wisdom is the greatest virtue and needs knowledge of number, a gift of a god. Soul is older than body; the heavenly bodies are intelligent and divine; daemons in the region between sky and earth interpret between gods and humans. The way to wisdom is astronomy, with arithmetic, geometry, solid geometry and harmony, and those who master it should hold the chief offices in old age.
- **Authorship:** Diogenes Laertius (3.37) reports that Philip of Opus copied out the *Laws* from wax tablets and was said to have written the *Epinomis*. Most scholars accept Philip (Wikipedia, "Philip of Opus"). A. E. Taylor and H. Raeder argued for Plato, and Gerard Ledger's stylometry points that way. Jaeger saw it as a supplement authorized by the Academy, not a forgery; Leonardo Tarán (*Academica*, American Philosophical Society, 1975) leaned toward Philip. The IEP says it was doubted in antiquity. Vera Calchi's 2024 study (Taylor & Francis) calls it likely Philip's.
- **Date:** none in the sources consulted. If it is Plato's, the IEP would put it in his late group (c. 355–347 BC).
- **Place among the dialogues:** Thrasyllus put it in his ninth and last tetralogy with the *Minos*, *Laws* and *Letters*; Diogenes Laertius gives its subtitle as "Nocturnal Council" or "Philosopher" (3.60–61).
- **Why it matters:** Jaeger read it as filling a gap in the *Laws* on the education of the ruler. Tarán found it at odds with Plato in ranking astronomy above dialectic. Calchi sees its theology of gods in the heavens as a step toward Hellenistic cosmic theology.
