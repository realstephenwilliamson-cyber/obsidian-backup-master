### 1.1–4: Salutation

*[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-1|Text]], p. 450* · The longest opening of the Pastoral Epistles, the passage may have been intended to introduce all three letters. ^1-1

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-1|1]]:** *Servant*, lit., “slave” (Gk “doulos”) *of God*, a frequent title in Jewish literature (see [[II Samuel 03#^3-18|2 Sam 3.18]]; [[Jeremiah 07#^7-25|Jer 7.25]]; Jon 1.9\[LXX\]; [[Psalms 019#^19-12|Ps 19.11]],[[Psalms 019#^19-14|13]]; [[Psalms 027#^27-9|27.9]]; [[Ezra 05#^5-11|Ezra 5.11]]; [[Ezra 09#^9-11|9.11]]; and [[Nehemiah 01#^1-6|Neh 1.6]],[[Nehemiah 01#^1-11|11]]). *Apostle*, “messenger,” used seventy-nine times in the NT but only once in the LXX ([[I Kings 14#^14-6|1 Kings 14.6]]). *God’s elect* alludes to the election of Israel (see [[Isaiah 65#^65-9|Isa 65.9]], which also links election to servanthood), although the LXX does not use the exact phrase; Christ’s followers are “elect” through faith and discipleship (e.g., [[Matthew 22#^22-14|Mt 22.14]]; [[Mark 13#^13-20|Mk 13.20–27]]; [[Titus 01#^1-1|Titus 1.1]]; [[1 Peter 01#^1-2|1 Pet 1.2]]; [[1 Peter 02#^2-9|2.9–10]]). *Knowledge of the truth*, God’s revelation through Christ; the phrase in the Pastorals is almost formulaic ([[1 Timothy 02#^2-4|1 Tim 2.4]]; [[2 Timothy 02#^2-25|2 Tim 2.25]]; [[2 Timothy 03#^3-7|3.7]]). *Godliness*, living in accordance with truth, an important virtue in Hellenistic Jewish (Philo, *Decalogue* 119) and Gentile (Epictetus, *Encheiridion* 31) literature. ^1-1-2

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-2|2]]:** *Eternal life*, [[Daniel 12#^12-2|Dan 12.2]], [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 05#^5-15|Wis 5.15]], and *Pss. Sol.* 3.12 in reference to the resurrection of the righteous. *Never lies*, God’s fidelity and honesty are central in the Jewish Scriptures ([[Torah 40 - Balak#^23-19|Num 23.19]]; [[I Samuel 15#^15-29|1 Sam 15.29]]). *Before the ages began*, the timelessness before creation (see [[2 Timothy 01#^1-9|2 Tim 1.9]]); for Philo, time begins with creation (*On the Creation* 26) and in some DSS God’s plan is established before creation (e.g., 1QS 3.15). ^1-2

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-4|4]]:** *The faith*, communal confession of God’s revelation through the Christ; the definite article makes it a technical term, unlike Paul’s use of “faith” (Gk “pistis”), without the article, which connotes relationship rather than doctrine (see [[Romans 03#^3-22|Rom 3.22]]). *Savior* (Gk “soteros”), used only once in the undisputed Pauline corpus ([[Philippians 03#^3-20|Phil 3.20]]) but ten times in the Pastorals. Both God and human rescuers are called “savior,” but the word is not associated with the title “Messiah” in Jewish literature, except in the NT, where God’s saving actions are made manifest through Jesus (whose name means “God saves”). Salvation includes present-oriented righteous living and future-oriented eternal life, in contrast to Paul who understands salvation only as a future event for which one must wait. ^1-4

### 1.5–9: Qualifications for elders

*[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-5|Text]], p. 451* ^1-5

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-5|5]]:** Only NT reference to a Pauline mission to *Crete*. Tacitus (*Hist*. 5.2) states Crete had a sizable Jewish community. *Elders* (Gk “presbyteroi”), an early church office (see [[Acts 11#^11-30|Acts 11.30]]; [[Acts 15#^15-4|15.4]]; [[Acts 20#^20-17|20.17]],[[Acts 20#^20-28|28]]). A fourth-century ce epitaph from Crete identifies as Jewish a woman named Sophia as both “elder” (“presbyter”) and “synagogue leader” (“archisynagogos”). In Jewish contexts, elders (usually older men) were respected for piety and wisdom ([[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-16|Ex 3.16]]; [[Torah 36 - Beha'alotcha#^11-16|Num 11.16]]; see also [[LXX Judith 08#^8-10|Jdt 8.10]], Sus 1.5; Philo, *Cont. Life* 67); in the Mishnah the elders are the bearers of the tradition (*Avot* 1); the NT frequently lists elders with chief priests ([[Matthew 16#^16-21|Mt 16.21]]; [[Luke 09#^9-22|Lk 9.22]]). ^1-5-2

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-6|6]]:** *Married only once*, no remarriage after divorce or wife’s death. Marriage was an important qualification for Jewish community leaders because of the emphasis on family (see [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-28|Gen 1.28]]), although some early apocalyptic communities advocated celibacy or separation from one’s spouse after the commandment to procreate had been fulfilled (1QSa 1.6–13; 1QM 7.2–4); in rabbinic texts marriage is enjoined on all Jewish men (e.g., *Yebam*. 6.8: “. . . The man is required to be fruitful and multiply but not the woman. R. Yohanan b. Beroqah says, ‘Concerning both of them does Scripture say, And God blessed them and said to them, Be fruitful and multiply’”). Only in the Middle Ages did the Roman Catholic Church require celibacy for clergy (see [[Matthew 19#^19-12|Mt 19.12]]; [[1 Corinthians 07#^7-7|1 Cor 7.7]],[[1 Corinthians 07#^7-32|32–35]]), a rule never required by the Eastern Orthodox Church. ^1-6

> [!note] Jewish Myths (TITUS 1.14) *(p. 451)*
> The Greek word “mythos” means “legend,” “fable” or “story.” Although originally a neutral term in Greek, the word carries a negative connotation in Jewish and Christian texts alike ([[LXX Sirach 20#^20-19|Sir 20.19]]; *2 Clem.* 13.3). In some Greek writings, “myths” are frivolous stories about the gods that feature sensual or immoral conduct deemed unbefitting of divinity. Plato, for example, employs this word in his critique of a Cretan story about Zeus’s affair with Ganymede (*Laws* I.636C-D). The word occurs in the New Testament only in the Pastorals ([[1 Timothy 01#^1-4|1 Tim 1.4]]; [[1 Timothy 04#^4-7|4.7]]; [[2 Timothy 04#^4-4|2 Tim 4.4]]; [[Titus 01#^1-14|Titus 1.14]]) and in [[2 Peter 01#^1-16|2 Pet 1.16]]. In Titus, myths are promulgated by “many rebellious people,” including “those of the circumcision.” They are erroneous and dangerous.
>
> Elaborations of biblical stories were quite common in Judaism (see *1 En*., *Jub*. and 1QapGen), and such imaginative engagement typified rabbinic Judaism through midrash and aggadah. The myths mentioned in Titus may reflect these Jewish interpretive practices. However, the writer uses prej-udicial language—an ethnic slur against Cretans (1.12) and dehumanizing language recommending bridling his opponents like horses to silence them (1.11). Thus, labeling the stories “Jewish” may not accurately describe their contents but rather may simply be a way to disparage them. The myths in question may be Gnostic texts, which also rewrite Jewish Scripture and contain genealogies (see Philo, *On the Creation*). Another alternative is that the myths are stories about Jesus that the writer deemed inappropriate. Different and diverse gospels circulated in the early centuries of the common era, but most of them were ultimately excluded from the canon (e.g., *The Infancy Gospel of Thomas*).

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-7|7]]:** *Bishop* (Gk “episkopos”), lit., *overseer*, an early Christian office ([[Philippians 01#^1-1|Phil 1.1]]; [[1 Timothy 03#^3-1|1 Tim 3.1–7]]; [[1 Timothy 05#^5-17|5.17–22]]). In the LXX the priest who supervises ritual objects (e.g., [[Torah 34 - Bamidbar#^4-16|Num 4.16]]); the NT meaning seems to be roughly equivalent to “archisynagogos,” head of the synagogue. However, unlike Timothy ([[1 Timothy 03#^3-1|1 Tim 3.1]]; [[1 Timothy 05#^5-17|5.17]]), Titus equates bishop and elder. ^1-7

### 1.10–16: Community controversies

*[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-10|Text]], p. 452* ^1-10

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-10|10]]:** *Of the circumcision*, a reference to followers of Jesus, whether Jewish or Gentile, who submitted to and insisted on circumcision (see [[Acts 11#^11-2|Acts 11.2]]; [[Acts 15#^15-5|15.5]]; [[Romans 02#^2-25|Rom 2.25–29]]; [[1 Corinthians 07#^7-18|1 Cor 7.18–19]]; [[Galatians 02#^2-12|Gal 2.12]]; [[Colossians 04#^4-11|Col 4.11]]). ^1-10-2

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-11|11]]:** S*ilenced*, lit., “reigned in,” refers to the bridling of horses. ^1-11

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-12|12]]:** The proverb is attributed to Epimenides, a sixth-century bce Cretan. In its original context, it was a paradox. Paul quotes Epimenides in [[Acts 17#^17-28|Acts 17.28]]. The dishonesty of Cretans was proverbial in classical sources (Cal-limachus, *Hymns* 1.8–9; Plutarch, *Aemilius Paulus* 23.4). ^1-12

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-14|14]]:** *Jewish* (“Ioudaikos”) occurs only here in the NT (and, in the LXX, only in [[LXX 2 Maccabees 08#^8-11|2 Macc 8.11]]; [[LXX 2 Maccabees 13#^13-21|13.21]]), although both Josephus and Philo use the adjective. *Myths* . . . *commandments*, may be a pejorative reference to interpretations of stories and law (later called “aggadah”and “halakhah”). On “myths” see [[1 Timothy 01#^1-4|1 Tim 1.4]]; [[1 Timothy 04#^4-7|4.7]]; [[2 Timothy 04#^4-4|2 Tim 4.4]]; [[2 Peter 01#^1-16|2 Pet 1.16]]. The reference is probably to mid rashic and pseudepigraphical stories, but it may also refer to stories about Jesus. See “Jewish Myths,” p. 451. ^1-14

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-15|15]]:** *Pure*, possible reference to Jewish purity codes, especially concerning food (Lev 11). *Consciences*, moral faculty (see [[Acts 23#^23-1|Acts 23.1]]; [[Acts 24#^24-16|24.16]]; [[1 Timothy 01#^1-5|1 Tim 1.5]],[[1 Timothy 01#^1-19|19]]; [[1 Timothy 03#^3-9|3.9]]; [[1 Timothy 04#^4-2|4.2]]; [[2 Timothy 01#^1-3|2 Tim 1.3]]; [[Hebrews 10#^10-22|Heb 10.22]]; [[Hebrews 13#^13-18|13.18]]; [[1 Peter 03#^3-16|1 Pet 3.16]],[[1 Peter 03#^3-21|21]]). ^1-15

**[[NRSVue Titus 01#^1-16|16]]:** *Actions*, indicators of proper belief, as in 1.1, which links “knowledge of the truth” to “godliness.” ^1-16
