They are called the Sādhakas, most of whom live in uninhabited regions, in woods, on mountains or in cremation grounds. The name “Sādhakas” could be translated as “those who strive.” The Sādhakas claim that their mantras have the power to change reality itself. For the Sādhakas, mantras possess absolute power of creation, and also absolute power of destruction. For example the texts speak of mantras that are so powerful that whole kingdoms can be founded with them, but they can also destroy whole stretches of land.“ If the total power of the mantra is transferred to the Sādhakas and they have turned their bodies into “divine bodies” or “mantra bodies,” they become Siddhas, i.e.

Instead, they are aiming for nothing less than participation in the power of the deity, that is, achieving a very specific type of union with the Divine. But they are worlds away from the concept of the divine found amongst the highly learned Sanskrit elites. For these early Tantrists, it is not static being in which the empirical world is rooted, but instead the world is understood to be the stage for powerful forces, and it is these forces that are really speaking the Numinous. For them, Śiva is not so much the Lord God bringing liberation — as the dominant discourses of the high culture Āgama Śaivism or the popular Purāṇic lore would maintain — but for them iva (Rudra, Bhairava) is primarily the epitome of all forces within the universe. ##### 5.4.1 The Heavenly Ear

The Sādhakas and the Siddhas imagine the universe in a dynamistic way as a complex fabric of forces and the deity as immediately immanent in this fabric. 

A Siddha sees and hears things that normal people do not, for he sees and hears the numina in this fabric. In the visual realm, this particular ability goes under the name of “heavenly sight” (divya-caksus). In the auditory realm one speaks of the “heavenly ear” (divya-Śravana) or “hearing heavenly sounds” (divya-Sabda-śravana).® For example, a Siddha can hear the drums of the gods, the instrumental music of the Gandharvas and the whispering of the ghosts. These cosmic auditory experiences are accompanied by out-of-body experiences and wondrous visions of light: 

“From the ninth year \[of his mantra practice\] onwards he sees with his own eyes many wondrous things which fill him in his heart with joy and delight. He sees them physically and they give him immediate refreshment. For an instant he turns rigid, for an instant he is aroused without reason. For an instant he hears from above the beating of drums. For an instant he hears instruments playing in various different styles. For an instant he smells the scent of camphor and musk. For an instant he sees himself rise into the air. For an instant he sees the sky lit up by the sun and the moon simultaneously. For an instant he hears the noise of cows, horses and elephants. For an instant he hears the pounding of a waterfall. For an instant he sees bolts of lightning striking all over the earth. For an instant he sees the whole globe flooded. For an instant he hears the noise of the Rg Veda, Yajur Veda and Sāāma- Veda. For an instant he sees in the heavens in the form of stars various awe-inspiring Yogins, who are scared of him. For an instant he hears a ghostly whispering and the crackling of fire ...”®° 

The heavenly eye (dibba-cakkhu; divya-caksus) is known already in early Buddhist literature, such as the Dīgha-Nikāya of the Pāli canon. Correspondingly, the heavenly ear (dibba-stota) is known, but it is not very significant.

The link between the numinous and light, or even visions of light, is known to be a common one in religions. What is less familiar is the idea that there are also numinous sounds. The paranormal auditory experiences described concentrate not only on the sounds of orchestral instruments but also on chaotic, unstructured and powerful sounds such as kettle-drums, thunder, waterfalls and the sounds made by herds of animals. The idea that the sound of a waterfall or the roaring of animals should be especially numinous seems strange at first. But “instruments of noise” are very widespread amongst vastly different religions and appear to be particularly popular as ritual devices. In this context the anthropologist Donald Tuzin presents a number of cross-cultural ethnographic data and facts showing that “the operations of ritual sound-making devices, religious consciousness, and thunder have much to do with one another;” he claims in particular “the worldwide symbolic association between bullroarers and thunder.” ® The music scholar Reinhold Hammerstein -- while drawing on sources from ancient Israel and Europe - speaks in a similar vein of a “pre-musical, numinous thundering.””! One only has to think of giant Tibetan alphorns, East Asian gongs, Indian conch shells, ram’s horns in Judaism, or Christian church bells, organs and trumpets. Many of these instruments, such as the conch shells in India or the trumpets in Europe, were originally instruments used in war, intended to frighten the enemy with their great noise. Other instruments, such as the Tibetan alphorns, only take on their huge size in the religious setting. The loudest instrument in Southern

##### 5.5.2 Morphology of the'Seed Sounds'

The mantra compendia and similar texts reveal a good deal of systematic vigor and esoteric sophistication. Scholarly Tantrists created a real “mantra science” formulating something like grammatical rules for the correct formation of mantras. There was even a lexical system of mantra syllables following the model of the Pāṇinaeic list of the roots of words. 

In this mantra science the non-semantic syllables of a mantra bear the generic name of “core sound” or “seed sound” (bija, biījāksara). The prevailing notion is that this one sound contains, “folded up,” the numinous power of the mantra. The seed sound is not regarded as being devoid of meaning or imparting no information, but at no time does it have an unequivocal lexical meaning. Only in this sense can one call it non-semantic. Although hybrid mantras exhibiting a mixture of semantic 

and non-semantic elements are the rule, the mantra science also allows the formation of mantras that consist solely of such seed sounds. By contrast, however, we do not find any purely semantic mantras, which shows that the seed sounds are seen as the most important elements. 

The Candrabindu (m)! and the Visarga (h), that is to say both the important final sounds that mantra syllables can have, are regarded as the crucial parts of a seed sound. The interpretation of these linguistic elements is strongly metaphysical: the Candrabindu final sound (m) is interpreted in the mantra science based on its written form, which is a half-moon with a dot over it, as a mystic interplay of sound (वकर) and light (bindu). The name bindu for “light” seems to be derived from the name and the form of the Candrabindu character (= “\[half\] moon and droplet”). Sound and light initiate and re-absorb creation and are the innermost activity within the godhead. Here sound and light are seen as perfect theophanies of the deity in its cosmic form. This idea has certainly been influenced by the Yoga tradition, where the deity is also heard in numinous sounds and seen in amorphous light visions. So ultimately it is the Candrabindu in which the deity is present in shapeless form, simultaneously compact and subtle, and in which it wields its power.

A new development that is unusual for an Indian context is the use of writing in Tantric ritual performance. Here we find additional differences between Vedic and Tantric mantras. With the Veda embedded in the concept of the Vāc (lit. “voice”), it seems absolutely impossible that a written Veda verse could develop numinous power. Furthermore, the sacredness of the Tantric mantra does not reside exclusively in the sound by any means. For example, the mere syllable hum on its own is certainly not a mantra. It only becomes one when it is understood as a mantra granted by the deity and used in a ritual manner. So the magic power and the soteriological substance lie not only in the sound, but also in the act of granting. This type of substantial transfer is a common phenomenon in religions. For example, the pope is not regarded as infallible because he is especially intelligent, but because “in apostolic succession” and as a representative of the whole church he embodies the “soteriological reality” given by Jesus Christ, as theologians would express it.
