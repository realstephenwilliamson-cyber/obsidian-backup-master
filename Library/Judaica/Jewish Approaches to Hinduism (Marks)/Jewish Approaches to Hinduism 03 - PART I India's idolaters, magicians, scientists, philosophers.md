## PART I: India’s idolaters, magicians, scientists, philosophers, and ascetics: 12–14th centuries

*[p. 10]* ^pg23

*[p. 11]* ^pg24

### 1. Judah Ha-Levi: “Idols, Talismans, and Ruses”

Medieval Muslim images of India

The Jews of medieval Islamic Spain lived at a time when India, though far distant, inhabited the world imagined by educated Muslims and Jews living  in the lands of Islam. “The cultivated Muslim public had a pronounced interest in the history and geography of the world known at this time,” writes Jacques Waardenburg in his study of Muslim perceptions of other religions in the medieval period.<sup>1</sup> This Muslim public sought information on other cultures, and books were written to satisfy this interest. The upper-class educated Jews of Spain, fluent in Arabic and interested in secular knowledge, also acquired this information, so that India entered their intellectual world, and – as problem, explanation, or curiosity – demanded integration into their views and values.

Muslim images of India came from a variety of sources during the 9th through 13th centuries. First arrived reports about India from Muslim travelers. In the 9th century a merchant named Sulayman journeyed from Iraq to China by way of the Indian coast; his observations, along with those of other merchants, appear in an early book reporting aspects of India’s culture, government, and geography.<sup>2</sup> Waardenburg observes that such travelers “were neither professional discoverers nor primarily interested in religion,” and had a limited interest in intellectual matters. “They were attentive to those customs which were opposed to their accustomed way of life – for example, statues of divinities in India or funeral customs in China.”<sup>3</sup> A later traveler and better observer, Abu al-Hasan al-Mas\`udi, spent twenty-five years traveling in the Middle East, Africa, China, Tibet, and India in the 10th century and wrote several books. These travelers provided reports for scholars who stayed at home and composed geographies and histories of the wider world. Ahmad ibn Rustah, Abu al-Qasim ibn Khurradadhbih, Abu al-Faraj al-Nadim, and Abu Abdullah Muhammad al-Idrisi are some of the more influential of these writers. Their books synthesized older reports and added the latest accounts of India to a growing picture of the region.

Another type of book, sometimes called a heresiography, focused entirely on religions, categorizing and evaluating Muslim sects, Judaism, 

*[p. 12]* ^pg25

Christianity, and various foreign beliefs, including those of India. The goal was essentially polemical, aimed at identifying how each sect and philosophy differed from the author’s view of true Islam. One example, written in the 10th century in Spain, was Ali ibn Hazm’s *Book of Opinions on Religions, Heresies, and Sects*. Perhaps the best heresiography of the time, because of its wide research, was written by Muhammad al-Shahrastani in Persia in the 12th century.

From these various reports, the Muslim world learned – to select a few details – that Indians exhibited outstanding intellectual abilities and civic virtues. An early travel account reported, “India is the land of medicine and of philosophers.”<sup>4</sup> Ahmad bin Ya\`qub (d. 900): “Indians … are superior to all people in all branches of philosophy”; and Ibn al-Faqih al-Hamadhani (10th century): “India has an abundance of philosophers, astronomers, and astrologers.”<sup>5</sup> Idrisi (d. 1164) noted that Indians are naturally inclined to justice and always honest in their transactions, an appraisal echoed by other writers.<sup>6</sup> As to religion, we find many accounts of the splendid temple at Multan (located today in Pakistan’s Punjab Province) housing a large statue to which a constant stream of pilgrims brought expensive gifts.<sup>7</sup> Most writers reported that Indians were divided into numerous sects holding different beliefs. Ibn Khurradadhbih (d. 912) specified 42 sects, some believing in God and prophets and some denying prophecy, and “some denying everything.”<sup>8</sup> Muslim writers identified various groups who worshiped specific beings such as Water or Fire; other groups practiced great austerities, and others followed the teachings of various prophets or angels. Nadim (d. 995) wrote about a god named Mahakaliya represented by a smiling blue-colored statue with four arms and a crown of skulls, and described one sect who venerated the sun and another who venerated the moon.<sup>9</sup> Some writers showed interest in the doctrine of metempsychosis (the round of rebirths) and some in the caste system.<sup>10</sup> Dietary practices drew further interest; one travel account reported that Indians eat rice rather than wheat,<sup>11</sup> and Idrisi wrote that the Indians of Nahrwara (located today in north Gujarat) never kill animals and show great veneration for oxen; they eat rice, peas, lentils, fish, and animals that die naturally.<sup>12</sup> But according to Mas\`udi, only Brahmans refrained from eating meat.<sup>13</sup>

Most Muslim geographic works were written in the Middle East, so one might wonder how much of this geographical knowledge reached the Muslims and Jews of Spain. A book composed by a Muslim scholar living in Toledo in the 11th century, however, shows us one particular picture of India’s national and doctrinal characteristics that circulated in the intellectual environment of Spanish Jews. The scholar’s name is Sa\`id al-Andalusi, born in 1029, and his book is *Tabaqat al-Umam* (Categories of Nations), written in 1068, mainly about the cultivation of the sciences by various nations of the world. He devotes one brief chapter to each of the world’s seven original nations, quickly delineating their essential cultural and ideological history before describing at greater length their scientific 

*[p. 13]* ^pg26

contributions. In his chapter on India, Andalusi praises the nation for its “advancement in all the branches of knowledge,” particularly its achievements in astronomy, music, mathematics, and moral education. Like most medieval scholars, Andalusi believed in the power of the stars and planets to control all aspects of human life, including character formation, physical appearance, and inborn abilities; in the case of India, he explained the people’s “intellectual power and fine spirit” from the influence of the planet Mercury which together with Saturn gave Indians “correctness of reasoning and depth of perception.” Andalusi includes a long paragraph on Indian beliefs and practices. Some Indians are Barahima, he writes, but most are Sabians. Both groups believe in the unity of God, but the Barahima reject prophecies, prohibit the slaughter of animals, and refrain from eating them. The Sabians believe in the eternity of the world but expect no help from God and instead construct representations of the stars and venerate them with sacrifices, seeking power from them for use “in the lower world in accordance with their wishes.”<sup>14</sup>

Medieval Jewish merchants who traded in India: 

Evidence from their letters

Another possible source of information about India might have come from Jewish traders who traveled from Mediterranean lands or Egypt down the Red Sea via Aden to India, and who resided along the west coast of India or in Ceylon for years at a time, trading in pepper, ginger, iron, silk, medical herbs, pearls, and other products.<sup>15</sup> The business letters they wrote during the 10th through 13th centuries have survived and one might hope to find in them descriptions or opinions about Indian religion and culture. But they contain no such topics. Shelomo Goitein, who collected and studied them, expressed surprise about this: “Hindu business friends are mentioned with much warmth and are called ‘brothers,’ but nowhere do we find any remark about Hindu religion, whose complete difference from everything known to them could not have escaped those merchants from Mediterranean countries.”<sup>16</sup>

Goitein wrote about the friendly cooperation among merchants of different belief, exhibiting an “almost complete absence of animosity against other communities.” Partnerships and other close business relationships were common among Jews, Hindus, and Muslims. Goitein found in these relationships, as exhibited in the letters, “a spirit of all-embracing brotherhood,” which he explains from an awareness of “the great dangers shared in common, the feeling that everyone’s lot was in the hand of the same God.”<sup>17</sup> Ranabir Chakravarti likewise points out “the remarkable cultural plurality observed and maintained by Jewish, Muslim and Indian merchants, among whom little religious intolerance is recorded.”<sup>18</sup> One particular letter, written by an influential Jewish merchant of Aden named Madmun ben H . asan-Yafet (d. 1151), expressed longing for his Hindu 

*[p. 14]* ^pg27

business associates, which Mordechai Friedman considers “especially noteworthy” for its expression of Madmun’s “close relationship of mutual trust and friendship with them.”<sup>19</sup> But the letters, written in a careful formal style, focus on commercial matters, business associates, and sometimes family affairs, while saying nothing about Indian culture.

Judah Ha-Levi and his two Indias

Judah Ha-Levi and his younger colleague, Abraham ibn Ezra (the subject of my next chapter), both grew up in a Muslim environment that offered depictions of India derived, at least indirectly, from Arabic travel reports, geographies, and histories. Ha-Levi was born around 1075 into a cultured Jewish family of Muslim Toledo or Tudela. He later traveled to the Jewish centers in Andalusia, where he gained botha deep rabbinic education as well as a broad secular education in Arabic sciences, literature, and philosophy. He enjoyed the comfortable life of the courtier class and began writing Hebrew poetry that celebrated friendship, wine, gardens, and women, as was the style of the time. But military and political events had begun to shatter the security of the Jewish communities of Andalusia. The Almoravides, a militant Islamic dynasty from North Africa, conquered the small states of Andalusia between 1086 and 1110. Their attacks on the Jewish quarters sent Jews fleeing to the north. At the same time, the *Reconquista*, the reconquest of Spain by Christian forces from the north, was progressing gradually southward, taking more and more territory, and creating more Jewish refugees. Ha-Levi began a period of wandering among cities in the Christian north and Muslim south, and took up the vocation of physician. But he saw that the position of Spanish Jews, caught precariously between warring Muslims and Christians, was untenable. “Between the hosts of Seir and Kedar,” wrote Ha-Levi, referring to Christians and Muslims, “My host \[the Jews\] is lost. They wage their wars and when they fall, we fall.” Referring to Christian forces, Ha-Levi prayed, “Forget not Edom, the yoke of whose oppression is so bitter and harsh …. Remember the day he drove into exile men of prominence and simple folk, without leaving even a dog in my home.”

At this time Ha-Levi wrote some of his most moving theological poems and personal lyrics, which express fear for his people’s destruction, yearning for messianic redemption, a lover’s unconditional faithfulness to his beloved (God), dread of sin, willingness to die for God, and selfless joy in God. He also began to question his own way of life and to reject its political and cultural foundations, including its comforts. He found particular fault with the neo-Aristotelian philosophy popular among the Jews of his class because, among other reasons, it weakened their adherence to the Judaism of revelation and law that he now saw as true Judaism.<sup>20</sup>

Around 1130 Ha-Levi began work on his great prose book, commonly called in Hebrew the “*Kuzari*,” which argued for the superiority of revealed 

*[p. 15]* ^pg28

Judaism over Aristotelian philosophy, Christianity, Islam, and the Karaite interpretation of Judaism. The full title of the book was *A Defense and an Argument on behalf of the Abused Religion*, written in Judeo-Arabic as a dialogue between the king of the Khazars and a Jewish sage. By 1140 he had finished the book and had also decided that the only way to serve God with his whole life, and the only way for a Jew to live fully as a Jew, was to emigrate to the Land of Israel, for only there did land, language, the people Israel, and the Torah truly unite. This is when he wrote some of his most famous poems, “the songs of Zion.” He died during his journey east around 1141, possibly in Egypt, or on the way to Jerusalem, or soon after arriving there. Judah Ha-Levi is one of the great poets of the Hebrew language, and his book one of the most important and creative interpretations of Judaism in the Middle Ages.<sup>21</sup>

India enters the *Kuzari* as two very different lands, both called India (*al-Hind*) – a land of good kings and citizens, and its opposite, a land of devious quarrelsome idolaters. (It also enters through two brief and incidental sentences as a land of science and a people with laws, which I will discuss at the end of this chapter.) We begin with the good India, portrayed by two parables that the Jewish sage of *Kuzari* composes to clarify the ideal relationship with God.

In the first parable, some friends gather in a desert and one of them journeys to *al-Hind* and meets its king. When the king learns of the man’s ancestors, he honors the man and charges him with commandments and a pact of loyalty. The king sends the man back to his community accompanied by members of the royal court. Emissaries from the community can now travel to India with little trouble, through the directions given by the king’s representatives. The community discovers that anyone who commits himself to serving the king can reach India and approach him. “They did not question why they should follow these directions, for the reason was clear, obvious, and simple: to reach the king and to associate with him was itself happiness” (1.109).<sup>22</sup> The sage of the *Kuzari* then explains the parable: the friends are the Jewish people, the first man who travels to meet the king is Moses and the later travelers are the other prophets, and the king’s representatives are the Divine Presence (*shekhinah*) and angels. We are to understand that the commandments and travel directions are the laws of the Torah, which Ha-Levi interprets as service to God; their ultimate reward is not practical benefits but the simple joy of God’s presence.

The second parable depends for its effect on the great distance between India and Spain. The subject is how one can know of God’s existence and God’s nature if one has not directly encountered God. Speaking to the Khazar king, the sage begins, “If they said to you that a certain king of *al-Hind* was virtuous and that you should revere him, praise his name, and recount his exploits because of what had come to you\[r attention\] about the justice of the people of his country … would this compel you to do so?”<sup>23</sup> The Khazar king objects that the people’s good behavior may be their own 

*[p. 16]* ^pg29

doing and not the influence of some king who may not even exist. The sage then supposes that this far-away king sends a messenger with typically “Indian gifts” that could have come from nowhere else, as well as medications that cure illnesses and poisons that miraculously defeat one’s enemies in battle. The Khazar king then admits that such gifts would end his doubts (1.19). In this parable, Ha-Levi argues that one can deduce an invisible God’s existence and benevolence through the excellent character and just behavior of his subjects, the Jews, and especially through wondrous gifts that come from beyond this world, which apparently alludes to miracles performed by God for the Israelites in biblical times.

Thus, for purposes of two abstract arguments, Ha-Levi invents two tales of a faraway land named *al-Hind*, whose king is good and powerful, and whose people, through their life of justice, give evidence of their king’s justice. But these tales consist of generalities lacking realistic details. In fact, the second tale begins as a mere supposition: “if they said to you …,” not explicitly claiming that Indians really are virtuous. On the other hand, one could point out that Indians did have a reputation for good rulers and citizens, which may have led Ha-Levi to choose them for his parables. Andalusi wrote, “Their kings are known for their good moral principles, their wise decisions, and their perfect methods of exercising authority,”<sup>24</sup> and Idrisi, as earlier noted, characterized Indians as “naturally inclined to justice.”<sup>25</sup>

Ha-Levi’s other India, in contrast, is not a perfect land of abstract goodness, but a maddeningly real people whose beliefs resemble those of other pagan nations and challenge central ideas in the *Kuzari*. This India enters the book in the context of one of the sage’s proofs for the authenticity of Jewish tradition. The Khazar king raises a serious problem with this claim by pointing to reports he has heard about the great antiquity of Indian “remains and buildings”: if these objects were built thousands and thousands of years ago, as the people of *al-Hind* assert, then do they not invalidate some of the content of Jewish tradition, especially the biblical date of creation?

More broadly, this pre-biblical date threatened one of Ha-Levi’s main arguments for the superiority of Judaism over philosophy (and philosophy was arguably his book’s main opponent and what made the subject personal to Ha-Levi). He attacked philosophical reasoning as not only ultimately unpersuasive because it is never conclusive and always elicits further disagreement, but also because its conclusions, lacking a credible tradition of knowledge to rely on, are inherently flawed. Without a reliable tradition, Greek philosophers were left with only their own individual reasoning to guide them.<sup>26</sup> Judaism’s truth, Ha-Levi asserted, is based instead on a history of meetings with God that Jews experienced directly and publicly, such as the events of the Exodus and the revelation of the Torah, and on revelations received by extraordinary people, the prophets. Knowledge of these events, with exact years assigned to 

*[p. 17]* ^pg30

each, comes from reliable records preserved in the Bible through “uninterrupted tradition.” This “truthful tradition” was transmitted not by “a few individuals” but by “a multitude consisting of scholars and persons of high degree.”<sup>27</sup> The entire Jewish people have accepted this testimony, including the revelations of Moses and the other prophets. Indeed, according to Ha-Levi, most nations of his time, including Christianity and Islam, accept the revelation of Moses.<sup>28</sup> Hence, the possibility of a pre-biblical date of India’s ruins subverted Ha-Levi’s central argument against philosophy.

Ha-Levi has the sage address this threat by attacking all Indian claims to truth:

This \[claim to pre-biblical ruins\] would have weakened my belief \[says the sage\] if they had in regard to these dates a soundly established doctrine or a book on which a multitude had agreed without division. No such thing, however, exists. Rather, they are a derelict people and have nothing well-founded. Thus, they vex the adherents of religions with speech like this, as they vex them with their idols, talismans, and ruses, saying that these benefit them. They also scoff at those who say they possess a book from God. Furthermore, they possess only a few books which a few human beings composed, by which the weak-minded are deceived, such as some books by astrologers who put dates in them of tens of thousands of years, like the Book of Nabataean Agriculture, in which are mentioned the names of Yanbushad, Sagrit, and Duani. It is claimed that they lived before Adam, and that Yanbushad was the teacher of Adam, and such like this.

The king then agrees that the people of India are “a calamitous multitude and a people who do not agree upon a single doctrine.”<sup>29</sup>

This long complex paragraph is the most antagonistic statement about Indian beliefs to be found in medieval Jewish literature. We notice a string of condemnatory words: derelict, vexing, scoffing, deceived, weak-minded, and calamitous, along with things such as talismans, ruses, idols, astrology, and a book called *Nabatean Agriculture*. Abraham Melamed, in a recent article on the image of India in medieval Jewish culture, interpreted the content of Ha-Levi’s paragraph with only two words: “pagan barbarians.”<sup>30</sup> But Ha-Levi was not portraying Indians as uncivilized, and the word “pagan” is vague and says little. What is the relationship between the Indians’ bad character traits and their idols, talismans, and rejection of books from God? What do the astrology books show Ha-Levi about the Indians? Is there anything wrong with astrology? What does Ha-Levi mean by talismans and what does their use show about Indians? Why does it matter to him that Indians disagree among themselves about doctrine? What was this book called *Nabatean Agriculture* and if Indians believed it, what did this show about them?

*[p. 18]* ^pg31

If we want to understand better what Ha-Levi meant by the specific elements in his enigmatic portrayal of Indian religion, we must analyze his words more thoroughly. We must look both outside and within the *Kuzari*. Outside it, we must search for similar objects and themes in the images of India available in Ha-Levi’s cultural setting in order to ascertain what these images usually meant. We can also turn to a later Jewish author, Moses Maimonides, to clarify the implications of the talismans and astrology in Ha-Levi’s paragraph. From within the *Kuzari*, we must, further, analyze words such as “ruse” and “religions” that are unclear in his paragraph on Indians to learn what they mean elsewhere in the book. Looking further within the book, we discover that Ha-Levi developed a sophisticated theory of the origins of non-monotheistic beliefs, their goals, and their failures. This theory will illuminate his assumptions about the goals and failure of India’s beliefs and will lead us to Ha-Levi’s suppositions about the essential differences between Judaism and Indian beliefs and practices.

Ha-Levi’s picture of Indian beliefs: The Barahima

What can the sources of Ha-Levi’s image of Indians tell us? That image, I contend, draws from four main sources: Muslim traditions about two ideological groups, the *Barahima* and the Sabians; his further interpretation of the “Sabian” aspects through a work called *The Nabatean Book of Agriculture*; and a few ethnographic details that most likely came from travelers’ reports like those found in Muslim geographical works.

We begin with the Barahima. Recall that Andalusi characterized them as people who “ban prophecy.” Ha-Levi’s Indians resemble these Barahima because they “scoff at those who say they possess a book from God,” which implies a similar denial of prophecy; it is this phrase in his portrayal that reflects current Muslim views of the Barahima.

Medieval Muslim discussions about Barahima have a puzzling history, however, because the earliest descriptions of them have no Indian features, do not even mention India, and are conceptualized in terminology derived from current theological debates among the Mutazilites and then of Asharites attacking Mutazilites.<sup>31</sup> As an example of these earlier discussions of Barahima, let us consider a statement from Ahmad al-Rawandi (d. 911), one of the more famous of the early authors who wrote about them: “The Barahima say … that the intellect is the greatest gift of God (may He be praised) to His creation and it is that by which the Lord and His graciousness are known, and on account of it, commandment and prohibition, encouragement and warning are verified.”<sup>32</sup> Therefore, since Barahima think that the God-given intellect of human beings can independently establish God’s existence and the rules of proper behavior, prophecy is unnecessary.

Patricia Crone summarized additional arguments against prophecy ascribed to the Barahima by Rawandi and later authors:

*[p. 19]* ^pg32

That miracles claimed by prophets rested on magic; … that it would be neither wise nor just for God to communicate the truth to one man alone, given that it was a cumbersome method conducive to warfare; and that it would make more sense to endow humans with reason so as to make them follow the truth on their own.<sup>33</sup>

However, as Muslims learned more about actual Indians who called themselves Brahmans, they began in the 10th century to merge the early meaning of Barahima (deniers of prophecy) with ethnological reports about the Brahman class. The image of Barahima gained a few realistic, if inexact, features. The *Kitab al-Fisal* of the Spanish theologian Ibn Hazm defined the Barahima as an Indian tribe claiming descent from a king named Barahmi. They wore red and yellow threads from which they hung their swords.<sup>34</sup> Other Muslim authors, writing during the 10th and 11th centuries, reported that Barahima living in India prohibit wine and animal sacrifice, are skilled in arts, technologies, and tricks of illusion, refuse to inflict pain on animals, and are forbidden to cross the Ganges River.<sup>35</sup> Andalusi’s image of Barahima combines the early element of rejecting prophecy with a few realistic Indian features.

Rejection of prophecy is the feature of Muslim discussions of Barahima that entered Ha-Levi’s picture of the Indian people. This doctrine may then have led Ha-Levi to his idea that Indians “have nothing well-founded,” that is, no book or reliable tradition based on a solid revelation from God; and because of this, Indians turn to deceptive books composed by a few human beings. Perhaps too, this lack of revelation explained for Ha-Levi why Indians disagreed with each other about what to believe, so that they split into many quarreling sects. He writes that they lack a “soundly established doctrine or book on which a multitude had agreed without division.”

The Barahima in 10th-century Jewish books

The Barahima had already appeared in earlier Jewish texts written in Arabic. In a code of Jewish law called *Book of Lights and Watchtowers*, Jacob al-Qirqisani (first half of 10th century), who belonged to the Karaite sect of Judaism, repeated the theme of the Barahima rejecting prophecy and insisting on the exclusive use of human reason for all theological matters. However, realistic pictures of Indian customs and beliefs also appear in his book. Among its frequent comparisons of Jewish law with the practices of other religions, we find at least nine comparisons with Indian customs. The practices listed by Qirqisani include, for example, setting mosaic patterns of small pebbles in the images of gods, building stone platforms for purposes of worship, engraving images of gods on Indian jewelry, and methods of Indian divination. Indeed, Qirqisani states that he himself witnessed such practices in the countries of *al-Hind*, which, if true, marks him as the first medieval Jewish scholar known to have visited India.<sup>36</sup>

*[p. 20]* ^pg33

In addition, the Barahima appear in the writings of Dawud ibn Marwan al-Muqammis and Sa\`adiah ben Joseph Gaon, 10th-century Jewish theologians living in Muslim Babylonia and influenced by the thinking of the Muslim *mutakallimun*. But these Barahima lack any connection with India, serving merely as theological abstractions cited for purposes of polemical debate. Muqammis portrays Barahima as deniers of prophecy: “Monotheists (are divided into) two groups: some of them think the sending of prophets by God to be necessary, and some of them deny it. Those who deny it are the Barahima.”<sup>37</sup> Sa\`adiah, however, attributes a slightly different doctrine to them: accepting the prophecy and laws of Adam while rejecting all later claims to prophecy.<sup>38</sup>

Ha-Levi’s Indians as Sabians

Two words, “idols” and “talismans,” connect Ha-Levi’s Indians with Muslim images of a second type of belief, that of the Sabians. These two words do not fit the usual depiction of Barahima. That Ha-Levi’s Indians follow books written by astrologers is also relevant.

“Sabians” (*sabi’un*) are mentioned three times in the Quran, twice associating them with Jews and Christians in a way that implies that Sabians too are “people of the book” (*‘ahl al-kitab*).<sup>39</sup> But their historical identity remained unclear, and various Quranic exegetes of the first two centuries A.H. tried to identify them. They were usually considered a tainted monotheistic community somewhat like Jews or Christians or Magians. Some scholars said the Sabians believed in one God, others that they worshipped angels or the sun or read the Book of Psalms as their scripture or followed the prophecy of Noah. Some said they believed in prophets and others that they lacked prayer, scripture, and prophecy. If they read Psalms, they were to be considered *‘ahl al-kitab*; if they worshiped angels, they were polytheists and could not be *‘ahl al-kitab*. The scholars who designated their location named cities in Mesopotamia.<sup>40</sup>

Sinasi Gunduz shows that Muslim scholars of the 10th and 11th centuries specifically identified the people of Harran as Sabians while at the same time applying the term “Sabian” in a general sense to all beliefs in Asia, the Middle East, and Europe that were not Muslim, Christian, or Jewish (thus meaning something like “heathen” or “pagan”). Gunduz summarizes the views of Mas\`udi, Nadim, Abu Mansur al-Baghdadi (d. 1037), and Abu Rayhan al-Biruni (d. 1050) in support of this generalization. When these authors specify the beliefs and rituals of Sabians, they depict them sometimes as monotheists and sometimes polytheists, but always as people who worship the stars and planets by means of images representing them.<sup>41</sup> Let us consider how two Muslim authors that I introduced earlier, who lived in Spain around the time of Ha-Levi, viewed Sabians. Ibn Hazm places Indian beliefs in the same category as Sabian beliefs – in his fourth type (out of six), the people who believe in more than one creator of the 

*[p. 21]* ^pg34

world. The Sabians introduced their beliefs to the Indians, who, imitating them, made pictures and statues of the stars which they then worshiped.

They \[Sabians\] believe in the eternity of the two elements or principles, as we have mentioned about the Majis. However, the Sabians revere the seven stars or planets and the twelve signs of the zodiac. They put pictures or images of them in their temples. They offer animal sacrifices and incense offerings …. They observe five worship-prayers very close to the Muslims’ prayers.

Ibn Hazm then tells a brief history of early human religion. The Sabian religion was “the oldest religion in the world and dominant throughout the whole world.” This was a universal primordial monotheism, but the Sabians eventually altered and distorted it by worshipping planets and fashioning statues of them. “Then Allah sent Ibrahim to them with the religion of Islam” to correct their inventions and fabrications. When they accepted these corrections, “they were called Hunafa.” Ibn Hazm thought that a few of these Sabian monotheists still lived in Harran.<sup>42</sup>

Andalusi, the other Spanish author, begins his world history at a time when all seven nations of the world – Persians, Chaldeans, Greeks, Copts, Turks, Indians, and Chinese – had been Sabian. “They were all Sabians, worshiping idols representing celestial objects, including the seven planets and others.” Later their religions changed, except in the case of India, where “the overwhelming majority” are still Sabians. In every case, Andalusi associates Sabian religion with worship of stars by means of statues placed in temples. For example, the ancient Egyptians “worshiped idols and maintained temples” and the ancient Greeks “venerated the heavenly bodies and persisted in their worship of idols.” Sabians were not necessarily polytheists, however, for the Arab Sabians were monotheists who believed in “the unity of Allah, the Highest,” as they simultaneously “worshiped idols” and “venerated the planets and the statues that represent them in temples.” Andalusi objects to the “ignorant” misunderstanding that idol-worshipers believe the idols themselves are the gods who created the world. Rather, he asserts, the Arabian Sabians said, “We worship them \[the idols\] only to bring us closer to Allah.”<sup>43</sup> This clarifies what Andalusi means when he depicts the Indian Sabians as monotheists who “revere the stars and represent them in paintings and approach them with offerings.” Since they also believe in “the eternity of the world” created by “God Himself, may His power and glory be proclaimed,” they conceive of a creator God who does not act directly on the world but has created heavenly powers (stars and planets) that control events in the lower world of human beings. To gain power over one’s life, one had to try to influence those celestial powers by offering gifts and sacrifices to objects that evoke them. For Andalusi’s Indian Sabians, therefore, there was no contradiction between 

*[p. 22]* ^pg35

believing in a single creator God while trying to control one’s world through the structure of powers that God had placed into it.

Judah Ha-Levi’s picture of Indian beliefs and practices reflects these Muslim images of Sabians. His Indians possess “idols and talismans” (*awthan wa-talasim*) and they follow books of astrologers. Based on Muslim understandings of the Sabian nature of Indian beliefs, we can fill out his few words by inferring that his Indians make statues representing stars and constellations to try to influence the celestial powers that control the world. Astrologers advise them on the best ways to do this.

Do Ha-Levi’s Indians worship one or many gods? From the perspective of the concept of monotheism that we just examined, people who worship statues and use talismans are not necessarily polytheists. Ibn Hazm’s Sabians venerate many celestial powers and yet remain, in his mind, “dualists”; and Andalusi considers the Sabians of India to be monotheists even as they construct statues for the worship of various astral beings. Hence, the “idols” in Ha-Levi’s account do not preclude his conceiving of Indians as monotheists.

Themes derived from Muslim geographical accounts

One of the most striking characteristics of Ha-Levi’s Indians is their divisiveness. The sage in *Kuzari* says they cannot agree on a doctrine or book “without division” and the king repeats, “They do not agree upon a single doctrine.” I earlier suggested that Ha-Levi may have seen this divisiveness as the consequence of rejecting prophecy. But there is another possible source for the theme. Muslim travelers had been reporting that India had many ideological sects who disagreed about doctrine as well as ritual, and this became the standard image of Indians. Mas\`udi, for example, had written that Indians had at first followed the wisdom of seven great sages, but later disagreed about their doctrines and split into seventy sects.<sup>44</sup> Sharaf al-Zaman al-Marwazi (d. 1125) counted 99 creeds and sects found among seven distinct castes; he summarized the beliefs and practices of twenty-one of them.<sup>45</sup> Andalusi reported that Indian Sabians believe in the eternity of the world but are divided into “numerous views and a variety of doctrines” about aspects of this belief; and that the Sabians also disagree with the Barahima, who in turn disagree among themselves about whether the world was created or is eternal.

Ha-Levi accuses Indians of perpetrating “ruses” (*h . iyal*), but what did he mean by this word? Hartwig Hirschfeld translated *h . iyal* as “witchcraft” but an examination of its seventeen occurrences in the *Kuzari* reveals that although it is often connected with magic, its core meaning is that of deceptive stratagems.<sup>46</sup> *H* 

*. iyal* often appears in the context of illusion and deception. Four instances of the word involve a contrast with the real and powerful miracles performed by God during the Exodus from Egypt. When Moses promised his people that God would bring them out of Egypt through great 

*[p. 23]* ^pg36

miracles, they feared “ruses” performed through astrological knowledge, which, like all deception, cannot stand up to examination (1.49). Hearing the story of the Exodus, the Khazar king states that no one would think that it resulted from “magic and ruses and fantasy” (1.84). It was caused directly by God, and “not by accident and not by means of human ruses and astral spirits and stars and angels and demons” (3.73). In contrast with Moses, the Egyptian magicians used incantations to perform actions that were mere “ruses” (5.21). Although *h . iyal* just as often has no association with magic, such as spiders using “ruses” to catch their prey, we can generalize that in the case of the Indians of the *Kuzari*, the word *h . iyal* connotes deception through magic, in contrast with the real miracles of God.<sup>47</sup>

The ruses in Ha-Levi’s picture of Indians thus reflect a number of Muslim reports about Indian magic. Ibn Khurradadhbih claims that the people of al-Hind use enchantments and conjure delusions to the bewilderment even of intelligent men, and Marwazi included magic in his list of the arts known by Indians, saying, “They pretend that by this means they obtain what they want.” As examples of magic, he lists the use of “imagination and thought” to work wonders, sorcery that baffles the minds of experts, and “wonderful talismans.”<sup>48</sup> Nadim in the *Fihrist*, summarizing “another account,” wrote that “the art of illusion is a specialty of India”; they use talismans based on astrology to “perform wonderful things.”<sup>49</sup>

A third theme likely derived from Muslim travel writings provokes Ha-Levi’s entire discussion of Indian religion, igniting his angry tone. Indians are reported to assert that their “remains and buildings” were built thousands and thousands of years in the past. I could not find this exact motif in Arabic writings about India, but there is no reason to think Ha-Levi invented it, since this motif, more than any other, undercuts the claims he is making about the truth of the Torah. We do, however, find a reflection in Indian claims about the vast age of a famous statue of a god located in the pilgrimage city of Multan. Biruni wrote, “It is said to have been made in the last Kritayuga \[Satayuga\]. If one supposes it were made at the very end of the Kritayuga, its age would be 2,164,132 years.” Ram Kumar Chaube notes that “other Muslim writers also said its age is incalculable.”<sup>50</sup>

*The Book of Nabatean Agriculture*

The one book that Ha-Levi associates with India is *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* (*Kitab al-Filaha al-Nabatiya*). Indians, he writes, “possess only a few books … such as some books by astrologers who put dates in them of tens of thousands of years, like the Book of Nabataean Agriculture, in which are mentioned the names of Yanbushad, Sagrit, and Duani. It is claimed that they lived before Adam, and that Yanbushad was the teacher of Adam.”

This book has survived to our time, continuing to fascinate and puzzle modern scholars. In *The Last Pagans of Iraq: Ibn Wahshiyya and his Nabatean Agriculture*, Jaakko Hameen-Anttila summarizes the history of 

*[p. 24]* ^pg37

European study of this book and various theories about its nature and origin. He expounds his own argument that it was composed in Syriac in the 6th century in Iraq and contains local ideas and practices of that region as well as a layer of late Hellenistic rites and Neoplatonic philosophy, before being translated into Arabic by Ibn Washiyya (d. 930).<sup>51</sup>

*The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* does indeed speak of great spans of time, as in this passage on the subject of its own composition:

Concerning the one who began \[writing\] this \[book\], it is said that he was a man who appeared in the seventh millennium of the seven thousand years of (the rule of) Saturn. That is the millennium in which Saturn was in partnership with the Moon. His name was Sagrith. The one who added other things to that was a man who appeared in the end of these millennia and his name was Yanbushad, and the third who made it complete was a man who appeared after four thousand years had elapsed. … I counted the interval between the two times and it came out to be 21,000 years.<sup>52</sup>

These enormous time spans contradict the Bible’s date of Creation; indeed, the *Nabatean Agriculture* assumes an eternal world formed from movements of the eternal celestial bodies.<sup>53</sup>

Two of the names appearing in Ha-Levi’s statement about the book, Sagrith and Yanbushad, appear in the quotation above; Ha-Levi’s third name, “Duani,” should have appeared first in his list. Duani, or Dawanay, was, according to *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture*, the first man in human history, called “the lord of mankind” and “the merciful father.” He “believed that all things come to be through the actions of the celestial bodies,” and he wrote books on the science of the spheres, stars, and the elements, and “taught people some talismans.”<sup>54</sup> *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* considers Dawanay and Adam to have been prophets who received inspiration through dreams. Contrary to the Bible, the Adam of *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* was the progenitor only of the Nabateans and some other peoples of Mesopotamia. A prophet-sage, law-giver, and traveler, Adam brought mathematics, medical cures, and agriculture to the world.<sup>55</sup>

It is doubtful that Ha-Levi knew much about *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture*, a long book filled with layers of diverse opinions and traditions. Prophecy appears in it, contradicting Ha-Levi’s claim that Indians reject prophecy. Nor was Yanbushad the teacher of Adam, but was born many years after Adam’s time and even rejected his prophecy.<sup>56</sup>

Ha-Levi thinks that astrologers wrote *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture*, and indeed the book speaks extensively about the influence of celestial forces upon human life and especially on the life of agricultural plants, as in these two sentences: “The Seven \[celestial bodies\] rule over all things and are the agents of all actions. They share with each other their influence 

*[p. 25]* ^pg38

on all animals, plants, and minerals.” These celestial bodies are often portrayed as gods. For example, a prayer attributed to Sagrith speaks of the Moon revealing agricultural wisdom to his idol, which then taught it to Sagrith.<sup>57</sup> In addition, the *Nabatean Agriculture* contains numerous examples of sympathetic magic, but it offers only a few instructions for preparing talismans. A talisman that made palm trees thrive safely involved drawing a picture of a human being on a plate and smearing it with honey.<sup>58</sup>

Why would Ha-Levi have connected *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* with Indian beliefs? First, the book does contain very ancient dates, and Ha-Levi could have connected this with claims he thought Indians were making for the origin of their pre-biblical temples. Furthermore, as we saw, many Muslim writers, including Ibn Hazm and Andalusi, categorized Indians as a type of “Sabian,” and Sabians were thought to venerate celestial bodies by worshiping images representing them. *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* clearly exhibits such theology, as well as guidance from sages who could be called astrologers. In addition, from the end of the 9th century, the people of Harran, who had been called Nabateans, were appearing under the name of “Sabians of Harran,”<sup>59</sup> so that identifying *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* with the theology held by Sabians elsewhere would have been an easy inference. Finally, India had acquired a reputation for remarkable astronomers and astrologers, and astrology appears prominently in the book.

This analysis of the Muslim sources and parallels for Ha-Levi’s view of India has enabled us to discover a range and texture of meanings that Ha-Levi probably associated with the specific elements of his image of India. The rejection of revelation had a history and implications associated with Barahima, who had become associated with India. Indian idols, talismans, and reliance on astrologers evoked Sabians, who venerated celestial objects to gain from their power. *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* implied Indian belief in astral beings and astrological powers and in pre-biblical history. Internal analysis of *h . iyal* and Arabic reports about Indians practicing magic reveal a specific meaning of the “ruses” that Ha-Levi ascribed to Indians.

We also see more clearly the logic behind Ha-Levi’s attack on Indian claims. His argument had two intertwined parts. He contended, first, that Indian sources of knowledge are unreliable because their books were composed by a few fallible astrologers rather than a line of inspired prophets, they cannot agree on a common authoritative scripture for all Indians (unlike Jews, who all agree on the truth of the Torah), and no thoughtful person would believe the few books in their possession because they consist of absurd astrological works like *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* (which ridiculously names men living before Adam). Second, the people making such claims about ancient knowledge are themselves unreliable witnesses of poor character. This is evidenced by their inability to agree in matters of doctrine, their outrageous doctrines and offensive idols 

*[p. 26]* ^pg39

and talismans, their use of ruses to deceive people, and their faith in preposterous astrology books.

Maimonides: Indians and astral magic

We can further clarify the implications of the talismans and astrology in Ha-Levi’s picture of Indians by turning now to the famous Jewish philosopher, legist, and physician, Moses Maimonides (1135–1204), who wrote extensively about Sabians in his major philosophical work, the *Guide of the Perplexed*, composed in the same general Islamicate setting as the *Kuzari* but more than half a century later (finished around 1200 in Fostat). Maimonides made only two brief statements about Indian thought, both appearing in the *Guide*: Indians are “Sabians” and they do not slaughter oxen. But these two statements imply much more when understood in the context of his ideas about Sabians, who appear in thirteen chapters of the Guide and most prominently in Chapters 29 and 37 of the book’s third section.

In the *Mishneh Torah*, his earlier book codifying Jewish laws, Maimonides formulates an abstract history of the rise of idolatry which, although never mentioning Sabians, reflects ideas about Sabians found in the writings of Andalusi, Ibn Hazm, and other Muslim writers. Recall that Andalusi wrote that all seven nations of the world were once Sabian in that they worshiped celestial powers by means of statues placed in temples. The Arabian Sabians, he recounted, had claimed that worshiping statues could bring them closer to God. Ibn Hazm also conceived of Sabian beliefs as the world’s first religion, which was originally purely monotheistic, worshiping God directly, but which became distorted when people began worshiping planets by means of statues representing them, until Abraham brought the Sabians back to pure monotheism.

Maimonides’ account in *Mishneh Torah* of early human worship shows striking parallels. Human beings were originally pure monotheists, he writes, but began to err in the third generation. Their mistake was an intellectual one: thinking that God wanted to be honored through honor given to His servants, the stars and spheres that God had created to control the world. This mental error then led to erroneous practices. People built temples to the stars, bowed down to them, and offered sacrifices – all the while imagining they were fulfilling God’s will. This modest first step, worshiping God through intermediaries, was, in Maimonides’ opinion, already the essence of idolatry (*\`avodat kokhavim*).

Maimonides’ story of the gradual degeneration of monotheism continues. In its second stage, prophets arose claiming that God had instructed them on how to venerate the stars – not only by building temples and offering sacrifices but by fashioning images in specific forms representing the sun, moon, and five planets, and worshiping them in certain ways, including some “strange” and “distorted” practices, that had been revealed to them. The priests (*kohanim*) promised that such worship would result in progeny 

*[p. 27]* ^pg40

and success. Then, in the final stage, people forgot about God altogether; the sages believed that the astral entities were the ultimate gods, and “the common people” believed that the statues made of wood or stone were themselves gods.<sup>60</sup> This is full idolatry.

Maimonides’ version of the history of human beliefs resembles Ibn Hazm’s idea that primordial monotheism was distorted by star-veneration and Andalusi’s idea that all human beings once worshiped the stars. Maimonides is warning that even the slightest notion of mediating the divine inevitably leads to idolatry.

In the *Guide*, when Maimonides uses the word “Sabian,” he is referring to the third stage of his history of idolatry, in which God is forgotten. But unlike the idolaters of the *Mishneh Torah*, the Sabians of the *Guide* clearly engage in a type of magic that scholars today call “astral magic.” Dov Schwartz, in the introduction to his *Studies on Astral Magic in Medieval Jewish Thought*, defines astral magic as a practice “predicated on the assumption that individuals can utilize celestial elements for their benefit and advantage.” Astral magic further assumes that the planets and constellations emanate a power called (in Arabic) *ruhaniyyat* in a form and force determined by the influencing planets and their location in the sky. Through certain actions, practitioners of astral magic can “draw down” this emanation and use it for practical purposes such as changing natural events and healing the sick. These actions “involve the creation or procuring, at a specific time, of an image, an effigy, or an amulet that symbolizes the emanating source.”<sup>61</sup>

Maimonides points to eight Arabic-language books describing astral magic and theurgy, which he identified as Sabian books. The book he cites most often (seven times) is one we are already familiar with –*The Book of Nabatean Agriculture*; the other seven titles include two books about talismans and a long book by Ishaq al-Sabi “concerning the laws (*nawa* *mis*) of the Sabians, the details of their *din* \[their laws or doctrines\], their festivals, their sacrifices, their prayers.”<sup>62</sup> From these books Maimonides draws a complex picture of the Sabians as star-worshipers holding specific doctrines, believing certain historical narratives, and following a set of detailed astral practices. The reason Sabians are important to him is that he finds in the exact details of their doctrines and practice an explanation for most of the mysterious details of the ritual laws commanded in the Torah.

Sabians believed, according to Maimonides, that the heavens are eternal and the stars are the gods of this world, with the sun as the greatest god.<sup>63</sup> “They believed in spirits (*ruhaniyyat*)” that could be compelled to descend, and “they made talismans.”<sup>64</sup> They thought that *ruhaniyyat* “flow over from the Sphere toward idols and certain trees” and cause them to convey revelations about “what is useful and what is harmful.”<sup>65</sup> Sabians claimed that Adam was not the first man (as we already saw in *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture*), that he was born in India and told stories of a miraculous tree growing there, and that he worshiped the moon and received revelations 

*[p. 28]* ^pg41

from it. Sabian practice consisted of fashioning statues of the seven main luminaries, made of specific metals associated with each one, which were placed in temples. Sabians also planted trees in such a way as to attract *ruhaniyyat* from certain luminaries. They made “disgusting” offerings to the Gods, such as a combination of beetles, mice, and rats.<sup>66</sup> And Sabians engaged in a variety of magical actions related particularly to agriculture. Some of these actions were believed to drive away lions and snakes, others to stop hail or make young trees produce fruit more quickly. One “disgraceful” practice was to engage in sexual intercourse while grafting a limb of one tree onto another.<sup>67</sup>

Sabians thought that agriculture pleased the gods, and Maimonides described many of their agricultural practices in the third part of the *Guide*. One example has to do with India. He thought Sabians held oxen in great esteem because the gods were pleased with their strength and role in cultivating the soil, which is why Sabians forbade the killing of oxen. “Hence, you will find that up to our time, Indians (*al-hanud*) do not slaughter oxen.”<sup>68</sup> (So Sabian beliefs survive “to our time” among the Indians of today.)

Maimonides uses harsh words about Sabian beliefs and practices. He calls their beliefs (in Shlomo Pines’ translation) “untrue,” “unhealthy,” “crazy,” “absurd,” and “useless.” Sabian concepts are “ravings” (*hadhayan*), a term found in the writings of Maimonides and Muslim authors of his time. Sarah Stroumsa analyzed Maimonides’ use of this term, concluding that he employed the word “for superstitious, nonscientific or pseudo-scientific discourse that presumes to offer a coherent system” and falsely pretends to be scientific knowledge. He associated “ravings” particularly with “the sciences of the occult.”<sup>69</sup> Of Sabian claims about history, Maimonides says they are “remote from philosophy” and “impossible in nature,” which proves that the Sabians were deficient of intellect, unphilosophical, and devoid of all science. Just as bad were the magical practices of the Sabians. These he calls “useless,” “disgusting,” “disgraceful,” and “absurd.” They were a great oppressive burden to people, “fatiguing,” and “a waste of lives.”<sup>70</sup>

Maimonides’ concept of the history of human beliefs centers on the Sabians. For many years in the past, he writes, “in those dark times,” except for a few exceptions, everyone was Sabian.<sup>71</sup> Astral magic was performed by the Canaanites, Babylonians, and Egyptians, which marked these peoples as Sabian. The Egyptians, because they were Sabians, worshiped the constellation of Aries, and therefore prohibited the slaughtering of sheep.<sup>72</sup> Abraham, however, discovered the truth about God and converted a small number of people to monotheism, but in the time of Moses, nearly the whole world was still Sabian and even the Israelites were falling back into idolatry while living in Egypt.<sup>73</sup>

But Maimonides thinks that in his own time, the “darkness” of Sabian beliefs has died out and is “remote from us today,” for nearly the whole world has been blessed by the monotheistic teachings of Abraham – except 

*[p. 29]* ^pg42

for three peoples who still hold on to Sabian beliefs. One group is the Magians of “the lands of the East,”<sup>74</sup> and the other two Sabian peoples live “in the extremities of the earth, as for instance the infidels among the Turks in the extreme North \[of Asia\] and the Indians (*al-hanud*) in the extreme south. These are the remnants of the religious community of the Sabians (*millah al-tzavah*), for this was a *millah* that extended over the whole earth.”<sup>75</sup>

It is possible that Maimonides knew more about Indians than merely that they refrain from slaughtering oxen. He had contact with Jewish merchants who had traveled to India, and he could have read Muslim geographies. His writings make several ethnological and historical claims about India. In his “Letter on Astrology,” for example, Maimonides contrasts the falsehoods of astrology taught by Chaldeans, Canaanites, and Egyptians with the “exceedingly glorious science” of astronomy on which the scholars of Greece, Persia, and India wrote books.<sup>76</sup>

But the only ethnological item about Indian beliefs that appears in the *Guide* concerns oxen. From this alone, Maimonides could have drawn the inference that Indians were obeying gods who prohibited the slaughter of oxen, and he could then have reasoned that these gods were the same ones appearing in the *Nabatean Book of Agriculture* in which they expressed approval of agriculture and of animals involved in agriculture. It could then follow logically that Indians practiced the Sabian rituals of astral worship and magic. Likewise, everything else that Maimonides had learned about Sabian beliefs and practices applied as well to Indians. Their beliefs had to be, by simple implication, also untrue and crazy; their rituals disgusting, absurd, and oppressive, and their thinking unscientific and unphilosophical. The Indians of his time, like the ancient Sabians who appeared in the *Nabatean Agriculture*, continued to try to draw down *ruhaniyyat* through talismans and astrological preparations, and they continued to venerate oxen because the gods loved oxen.

Maimonides’ picture of Indian belief, therefore, is not so different from Ha-Levi’s. Both imagine Indians as people who follow teachings in *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture*, use talismans, and consult astrologers. But Ha-Levi, for reasons particular to his argument, raised the question of whether Indians accepted prophecy and followed a reliable tradition of knowledge. Maimonides certainly considered *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture* untrue but focused his attention on the irrational, unscientific, and harmful nature of Sabian “ravings” and practices, which would also apply to those of India’s Sabians in his own time.

Thus, in relation to Ha-Levi’s picture of Indian belief and practice, Maimonides’ portrayal of Sabians fills out two motifs. Since Maimonides identified talismans with astral magic, and did so through *The Book of Nabatean Agriculture,* which indeed contains astral veneration and theurgy, we could reasonably infer that Ha-Levi’s vague use of the word “talismans” together with his reference to the *The Book of Nabatean* 

*[p. 30]* ^pg43

*Agriculture* likely intended similar beliefs and practices. Ha-Levi seems to have ascribed to Indians a set of astral beliefs and practices similar to those that Maimonides later discussed in far greater detail. This inference about Ha-Levi’s view of Indians finds further support in Andalusi’s idea that Indian Sabians “obtain the power of each star and use it in the lower world in accordance with their wishes.” They were engaging in astral magic.

Ha-Levi’s history of religions

In this section, I will begin asking one of the main questions of my book: how did Jewish thinkers “compare religions?” Here and in future chapters when I ask this question, I will use the term “religion” in both “redescriptive” and “descriptive” ways (etic and emic): I will be interposing a foreign concept, religion, upon writings that do not carry that exact concept, but I will also identify approximations and intersections in the way Jewish thinkers themselves thought about and linguistically expressed certain entities that reflect the modern meaning of religion. I begin using the word “religion” in an indefinite provisional sense and then will work to identify specific elements that Ha-Levi would have connected with the word.

Indian beliefs are unique in the *Kuzari* in their claim to ancient dates of temples and human history, and no other ancient beliefs are associated with the actual rejection of prophecy (although they all lacked true prophecy of the Israelite kind). But elsewhere in the book Ha-Levi does discuss elements of Indian beliefs and practices that he identified among other nations – namely, astrology, veneration of images, use of talismans, and doctrinal discord, all of which, he believes, were widespread in ancient times. An examination of Ha-Levi’s history of what I will call the “ancient pagan religions” will provide further perspective on his picture of India’s religion and will introduce Ha-Levi’s framework for comparing religions. (I use the word “pagan” for non-monotheistic religions.)

Ha-Levi discusses ancient pre-Israelite religions in several contexts in the *Kuzari*, focusing on varying features of these religions relevant to the specific topics of his argument. The underlying question for him, however, is how human beings should approach God. In writing about this, Ha-Levi usually uses a technical term that refers to the aspect of God that human beings can know and approach, namely, *al-amr al-ilahi*, literally, the Divine Word or Command. As Barry Kogan writes, this is a “multivalent term for diverse aspects of divine immanence,” and in general, “signifies the experienced aspect of divinity in nature and history.” According to Diana Lobel, Ha-Levi used the term in “a fluid way … to describe God’s interaction with creation.”<sup>77</sup>

Ha-Levi’s simplest categorization of ancient religions, in the first section of Part 4 of *Kuzari*, divides them between those of ignorance about God and those of misconception. People were either unaware of God and worshiped the forces of nature, calling each one a god (*‘eloha* in Hebrew), or 

*[p. 31]* ^pg44

they acknowledged God but imagined he was too remote to be interested in human beings, so they turned to one of the forces that God had created, such as the sun, the moon, the Sphere, a constellation or star, fire, air, or spiritual angels. Ha-Levi asserts that the Hebrew term, ‘*elohim*, refers to these many forces and objects that ancient people considered gods.

Ha-Levi’s other discussions of ancient “religions” tell a more complex story that explains how the world’s original monotheism degenerated into error. His first discussion asserts that people “were seduced by natural and astral laws,” thinking that these were the forces that brought practical benefits. They “forgot” God, who controls these forces, and passed from one set of laws (*namus*) to another, from one venerated object to another, or they venerated many objects at one time, not realizing that what really helps or harms people is the *amr ilahi* alone. Here what I am calling “religion” is a matter of human theorizing that leads to a set of laws of practice, called *namus* in Arabic (1.79).

In 1.97 Ha-Levi adds the theme of image-veneration to his history of ancient error. Explaining why Israelites waiting for Moses at Mount Sinai fashioned the statue of a golden calf, he writes that some of them needed to imitate other nations (*‘awam*) by constructing a tangible object to mediate their belief in God. Ha-Levi observes that this was a widespread way of thinking at this time when most nations worshiped images of gods because the common people needed tangible images to worship, even though philosophers had proven the existence of an abstract God. So philosophers told their people that the divine *amr* was conjoined with the image (*tzurat*), giving it a wondrous power. But people began to argue over what made the images wondrous; some said it was connected with God, and others imagined a connection with astral spirits from the stars or a constellation or with a talisman. A great division of practices arose, characterizing ancient idol-worship. In this analysis, Ha-Levi is still describing a history of human thought, but he adds the phenomenon of worship. “Religion” here consists of theories and consequent forms of worship (*‘ibada*), and Ha-Levi calls each one a *shar\`a*.

Ha-Levi tells the same story but with a different emphasis later in the *Kuzari*. In 2.54 he distinguishes five doctrinal sects (*aqwam*) among ancient peoples. All of them lived in “blindness and error” during the period when God had been forgotten. No one could believe in a transcendent reality beyond nature, so they instead tried to discover which object in this natural world was most worthy of veneration. One group decided on the universe as a whole because they discerned no separate creator; another group venerated the eternal Sphere above (which they considered the cause of all changes in this world); another venerated fire as the essence of light and power; another sect venerated various astral objects: the sun, the moon, the stars, or the animals of the zodiac; and the last group worshiped great human kings and sages.

In this reconstruction of the rise of idolatry, we notice a story not so different from the history imagined by Maimonides in his *Mishneh Torah*. 

*[p. 32]* ^pg45

Human beings once knew God but gradually turned away from God, worshiping intermediaries that they thought connected them to God, or worshiping just the powers they experienced in the world around them. Maimonides’ theory is more abstract and shows a progressive distancing of humanity from God; Ha-Levi’s theory is more complex and nuanced but is likewise based on the idea of “forgetting” God over time and using tangible images as either intermediaries or substitutes for God.

Unlike Maimonides, however, Ha-Levi does not characterize all ancient religions as Sabian astral magic, but astral magic still features prominently in his history of religions, as well as in his view of contemporary heresies. One example appears in Ha-Levi’s depiction of the idolatry practiced by the Judean kings Manasseh and Zedekiah. They were influenced by the magic practices of the surrounding nations, who used talismans to try to draw down astral spirits in order to obtain practical benefits from them. Manasseh and Zedekiah, while still worshiping God, turned to astral magic to gain victory and amass wealth (4.23). Earlier in the *Kuzari*, in 1.79, Ha-Levi had already explained that many ancient peoples had conjectured that religious images worked their benefits through stellar spirits (*ruhaniyyat*) or talismanic action. Astrology books were written to prescribe how to do this.

Surprisingly, however, Ha-Levi asserts that these flawed practices sometimes had the purpose of “receiving divine traces,” that is, emanations of the divine *amr* into this world. This claim complicates his picture of pagan religions. This group of religions and seekers know of God, at least vaguely, but misunderstand God and how to reach him. In 1.98 the Khazar king summarizes the sage’s lesson by stating, “One can reach the divine *amr* only through the divine *amr*,” meaning that only through God’s precise instructions can one approach God. Then Ha-Levi has the king say that if this were not so, most people who strive to do so without the divine instructions would succeed, and he lists five groups who strive to approach the divine *amr*: astrologers, users of talismans, worshipers of fire or the sun, and dualists.<sup>78</sup>

In another passage (3.53) connecting astral magic with God, Ha-Levi discusses at some length the people who seek “divine traces” of the *amr* by means of alchemy, astrology, *ruhaniyyat*, and incantations. Spirit summoners (who draw down *ruhaniyyat*) “heard that the appearance of the divine traces … was gained by sacrifices.” Lobel points to Ha-Levi’s use of the Arabic word *qiyas*, meaning in this context, human logic, to explain the failure of spirit-summoners and other practitioners of magic. The spirit-summoners, continues Ha-Levi, composed astrological books and tried to “determine sacrifices to be offered up at certain times and astrological opportunities, accompanied by ceremonies and burning of incense, which their *qiyas* prescribed.”<sup>79</sup> Likewise, people who chant magical names, hearing that Hebrew prophets received divine utterances and experienced miracles, imagined that they could duplicate these phenomena 

*[p. 33]* ^pg46

through human acts of chanting. And similarly alchemists: “Who has it in his power to determine the actions upon which divine action will rest, except God alone? This is the error committed by alchemists and spirit summoners.”<sup>80</sup> The essential mistake of all practitioners of magic who seek God is trying to approach him through their fallible human thoughts (*qiyas*) and devices instead of God’s revealed instructions. Later Ha-Levi asserts that the Jews of his own time are also seduced by the fantasies of astrology, incantation, and charms, which are “far from nature as also from the Torah.”<sup>81</sup>

Ha-Levi, therefore, and unlike Maimonides, allowed that some practitioners of astral magic are people who believe in God and strive to approach the divine *amr* or bring down its emanations. They always fail because their knowledge of God and ways of approaching him are fundamentally flawed.

Thus, taken together, we find in Ha-Levi’s picture of the world’s religions thirteen types of doctrine and practice. The essential doctrines assert (1) that the universe is eternal and uncreated, (2) that the Sphere alone is eternal, (3) that the main powers and creators are celestial entities such as stars, (4) that the main powers are natural forces in the world, (5) that only two main powers control the world (dualism), (6) that an eternal creating God exists but does not intervene in this world, and (7) the divine *amr* may be reached through magical practices. The essential practices consist of (8) venerating tangible images of objects in this world, (9) venerating tangible images of God, (10) summoning *ruhaniyyat* from the stars, (11) engaging in rituals such as sacrifice that reflect astrological views of the power of the stars, (12) chanting magical names, and (13) practicing alchemy.

Ha-Levi’s picture of India includes three of these general categories of belief and practice: veneration of images, use of talismans (astral magic), and belief in astrological powers. If we assume that Ha-Levi’s interpretation of these practices followed by other peoples also applies to Indians, we may infer that Indians too must venerate statues because they depend on tangible images to represent higher realities, and Indians use talismans and follow books of astrology because they believe that practical power in the world comes from the stars. It is unclear, however, whether Ha-Levi thought Indians, like some other practitioners of astral magic, were seeking God in some way.

In summary, we find in the *Kuzari* a picture of the world’s religions in which they all fail to reach God. They err in many ways. Some know nothing of God and wander in utter blindness, some think God is too distant and they therefore seek help from the natural and astral forces they see in the world, some seek God but do so through useless human devices such as images, astrology, and incantations. Israelites who added to God’s commandments while still worshiping God, such as Jeroboam who altered the direction of prayer or the Israelites who worshiped the Golden Calf as a talisman, also failed. Even Muslims fail to reach God, according to Ha-Levi. They did abolish idolatry, but they changed the direction of prayer, altered 

*[p. 34]* ^pg47

the ritualistic commandments of the Torah, and “sought the divine *amr* where it is not found” – all of which took them away from God.<sup>82</sup> In the refrain of the *Kuzari*: approaching God is only possible through the exact commandments of God. The main question Ha-Levi asked in comparing and evaluating the religions of the world was whether they succeeded in reaching a relationship with God.

Ha-Levi’s vocabulary of “Religion” and 

whether Indians possess “*din*”

In Ha-Levi’s accounts of early religions, they begin with thinking and theorizing about the nature of the world. Ha-Levi portrays ancient people “saying,” “claiming,” “contending,” “arguing,” “conjecturing,” and “imagining.” These ideas then lead to practices of worship or magic. We saw the same mental process in Maimonides’ analysis of the Sabians. Their idolatry began with an intellectual error that grew into a whole system of fantasies and false assertions, and these then produced errors of worship and magic.

In addition to this language of thinking and belief, Ha-Levi uses specific Arabic terminology: *namus, shari’a, ‘ibada,* and *din.* In regard to *namus*, he writes that ancient seekers who had forgotten God passed from one *namus* to another (1.97). Ha-Levi uses this term to refer to laws or a system of laws, and these laws can be natural or ethical laws discovered through reason (2.48), but also a system of laws revealed by God, *al-namus min Allah* (1.81). Thus, Ha-Levi writes that the Indian, Persian, and Greek nations (*‘awam*) each followed their own *namus* because they had not received the true *namus* (2.20). The words *shar’a* and *shari’a* likewise refer to laws or a system of laws and practices. For example, Ha-Levi asserts that the many theories of the ancient seekers led to a division of *shar’a* (1.97). He also speaks of *shar’a al-Islam* and of Judaism as *shari’a*, and Torah as the divine *shari’a*.<sup>83</sup> Another component of religion is ‘*ibada*, worship, as in the worship of images or the forces of nature (2.54, 4.1), or the worship of God. This term refers to organized worship, a cultus.

Finally, the term *din* appears in Ha-Levi’s discussion of why the Israelites fashioned the golden calf, having been influenced by the *adyan* (plural of *din*) of the surrounding nations (4.23). *Din* refers here specifically to the worship practices (*‘ibada*) of idolatrous nations, but may also imply their beliefs. The term also appears prominently in the following sentences that Ha-Levi places in the mouth of a philosopher: “Do not be concerned about the laws (*shar’a*) of your *din* or the word or language or actions you employ. You may even choose a *din* in the way of humility, worship, and benediction … or fashion your *din* according to the laws (*nawamis*) of reason set up by philosophers” (1.1). Here *din* seems to be a more inclusive category than either *shar’a* or *namus*; it includes both attitudes and worship. Notice, too, that *din* in both 4.23 and 1.1 refers to a human construction of ideas 

*[p. 35]* ^pg48

and practices rather than divinely revealed truths and laws. A statement in Book 3, however, offers the strongest evidence that Ha-Levi can use the term in reference to thoughts and behavior that include but go beyond law, when he applies it to the whole life of a figure whom he calls “the good one” (*al-khayr*), his ideal representative of Judaic piety. This pious person not only follows laws of Torah but seeks nearness to God through selflessness and continual consciousness of God; then “he gathers the fruit of his *din* in this world and the next.”<sup>84</sup> I may generalize, therefore, that although Ha-Levi’s use of *din* has a range of meanings, it most often approximates a complex phenomenon composed of beliefs, laws, and worship, whether divinely revealed or based on human thought.<sup>85</sup>

Interestingly, the word *din* appears in Ha-Levi’s polemic against Indians in 1.61. He accuses them of angering the people of *din (ahl al-adyan)* with their claims to prehistoric buildings and their idols, talismans, and ruses. Does Ha-Levi’s phrase, “they anger people of *din*,” imply that he thinks Indians lack *din*? This is the interpretation of Hans-Michael Haussig, who thinks this phrase indicates a lack of *din* among Indians since, in Haussig’s words, “they fail to base their claims on divine revelation.”<sup>86</sup> Indian lack of divine revelation is clear, but as we saw, Ha-Levi uses the term *din* for the idolatrous religions of biblical times, as also for religions built on human reason; hence, *din* in Ha-Levi’s usage need not be divinely revealed, as Haussig himself points out in regard to the *din* of the Khazar people.<sup>87</sup> Then why would Indians boasting about the benefits of idols and talismans anger people of *din*? Or if the phrase means “other people of *din*,” the question remains, because other idolatrous peoples with *din* employ idols and talismans too. Perhaps Ha-Levi’s hostility toward Indian claims for prehistoric buildings leads him to deny *din* to Indians even in its sense of unrevealed belief and worship. If so, he would be saying that their beliefs are so chaotic and their practices so useless that these cannot be dignified with the word “*din*.”

Apparently however, Ha-Levi portrays Indians differently in 2.20, where they appear as a people equal to Persians and Greeks in possessing unrevealed *namus*, a system of law. These two images of the Indian people – possessing *namus* and failing to possess a coherent *din* – seem to conflict, probably because the two images serve opposing polemical functions in the two contexts where they appear.

Two differences between Judaism and the pagan religions I argued that the *Kuzari* pictures the world’s seekers and religions, all except Judaism, failing to reach God. Ha-Levi saw Judaism differing from the pagan religions and groups of seekers in a fundamental and unbridgeable way. I want now to offer two examples of what this difference would mean in the life of pious Jews, in a relationship with God that was unavailable to all other peoples.

*[p. 36]* ^pg49

The first example comes from Ha-Levi’s long discussion of the life of ideal pious Jews found in 3.11–21 of his book. He demonstrates how the commandments and prayers of the Torah and Talmud train Jews in a life of piety and closeness to God in which they will experience the divine *amr*.

Ha-Levi thought that only the complex divinely revealed commands of Torah, exactly designed according to time, place, and quantity, as learned from God and not human speculation, enables attachment to the *amr* (1.99). In the past, in the time of the pagan religions, when the Jerusalem Temple still stood, the *amr* “rested” on all its ceremonies, and the exact performance of a sacrificial ritual would bring about a new spirit and visions of heavenly fire and wondrous signs, as a result of “the mighty *amr* with which you came into contact and reached it” (3.53). In Ha-Levi’s time, however, after the loss of the Temple, Jews must rely entirely on the Torah’s non-sacrificial laws and the rituals transmitted by inspired rabbis, but he thought that pious Jews could still reach a state of piety only one level lower than prophecy (3.11).

Pious Jews, writes Ha-Levi, have numerous divine commandments to obey, such as circumcision, the Sabbath, the festivals, dietary and purity laws and forbidden mixtures, phylacteries and fringes, and many more (3.11). “These are difficult to perform,” he writes, “and yet are performed with the utmost zeal and love.”<sup>88</sup> And it is in such actions, performed with a pure mind, that the divine *amr* is be encountered (2.56). Jews also obey the social and ethical laws such as honoring parents, loving the stranger, and not swearing falsely in God’s name. All these laws instill an awareness in pious Jews of living constantly in God’s judgment, which leads to both fear of sin and the joy of “enduring hardships in obedience to God.” Then the daily round of prayers and blessings train their minds in gratitude and repeated awareness of God’s guidance and daily miracles. A pious Jew “connects his mind with the divine *amr* by various means, some of which are prescribed in the Written Torah and others in tradition.”<sup>89</sup> For example, voicing the prayer, in pure thought and intention, about God’s redemption of the Israelites from Egypt makes one “a true Israelite and worthy of aspiring to the divine *amr*.” Through this continual consciousness of God’s gifts, pious Jews experience the nearness of the *shekhinah*, God’s presence (3.11). Jews praying as a pure community gain connection with the divine *amr* “as a smooth mirror connects with the light.” (3.17) Moreover, Jewish prayers teach Jews to respond to suffering with patience and trust in God. They accept tribulations as God’s justice, and they endure their own sufferings lightly as well as those of the Jewish people, looking forward instead to “contact with the divine *amr* in this world” and reward in the afterlife (3.11) The result, says Ha-Levi at the end of his discussion, is that pious Jews live in holiness and purity, untouched by the ritual and moral pollutions of the world, caring nothing for their material livelihood, their whole lives devoted to serving the Lord (3.21). But for the pagan religions, 

*[p. 37]* ^pg50

muddled in misconception and lacking the guidance of Torah, the pious Jew’s attachment to God was unavailable.

My second example of a fundamental difference between Judaism and the pagan religions revolves around the different names they used for God. Recall that in Part 4 of *Kuzari* Ha-Levi pictures ancient religions worshiping the forces of nature as gods. *‘Elohim*, says Ha-Levi, is the Bible’s word for these natural forces that were considered divine. Then he adds that the root cause of these ancient errors was *istidlal*, demonstration and inference (related to *qiyas*, mentioned earlier). *Istidlal* leads astray and “from it arises heresy and destructive ways of thought”<sup>90</sup> (4.1). It led people to worship fire or the sun, and led materialists to believe the world is its own cause. In its most abstract form, the *istidlal* of ancient sects became Aristotelian philosophy, which teaches the notion of an eternal world and a God who neither knows of individual prayers nor intervenes in the world. This idea of God, too, is a form of ‘*Elohim*. It represents the most that the unaided human mind can know about the real God but is merely a distant reflection of the truth that “all creative forces depend on God, who arranges and guides them” (4.1). Ancient peoples related to these forces, the *‘Elohim*, through veneration and seeking power from them.

But there is another name for God and a different kind of relationship, asserts Ha-Levi, which only Jews know: “the Ineffable Name, written with \[the letters\] *yud heh vav heh*, may He be blessed and exalted.” This name, also known in Greek as the Tetragrammaton, is often abbreviated in Hebrew usage, as usually by Ha-Levi, as simply “the Name,” and is spoken as *‘Adonai*, translated in English as “the Lord.” It is the particular name of God that differentiates him from all the forces called *‘Elohim*. Ha-Levi points out that the Egyptians knew *‘Elohim*, but, as Pharaoh says in [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^5-2|Exodus 5:2]], they did not know the Ineffable Name. This higher name of God indicates an essentially different reality and way of relating to human beings. As the Name, God enters actively into human life, revealing the truth about creation and watching over individuals, knowing their every action, to reward and punish them. The Name raises the lowly and humbles the mighty, and judges and rules history, and guides and protects the Israelites (4.4).

The human response to God known by this Ineffable Name is fear and love, which comes to Jews naturally and remains in their hearts for their whole lives (4.5). “It is thus that man becomes a servant, passionately in love with the object of his worship, and ready to perish for His sake, because of the bliss of this attachment (*ittisal*), and the pain and suffering of being apart from Him”<sup>91</sup> (4.15). The Khazar king then summarizes what the sage has told him: “People yearn for the Name with a yearning for taste \[experience\] and witness. But they tend toward *‘Elohim* only by deduction (*qiyas*). That taste brings one who attains it to be consumed in love for Him, and to die for Him”<sup>92</sup> (4.16). Ha-Levi is identifying the essential human response to a direct experience of the Name as a self-negating love and unifying 

*[p. 38]* ^pg51

attachment which seeks only to know and serve the Name rather than gain power in the world.<sup>93</sup>

In Ha-Levi’s view, then, Judaism differed from the pagan religions in radical ways. Judaism had been formed by a special revelation of instructions for living a pious life of closeness to God, which promotes contact with the divine *amr* and seeks only to serve God in purity. And this revelation further included an understanding of God that allowed Jews to experience the true God of the Ineffable Name directly, responding with a mystical self-negating love. These differences (among several others) placed Judaism far beyond what the pagan seekers understood or attained through mere human reason and effort. This of course applies fully to Indian beliefs and practices, were Ha-Levi to compare them explicitly with Judaism.

Why two Indias? Why four?

Besides Ha-Levi’s main images of India as vexing idolaters in 1.60–62 and as good citizens with a good king in 1.109 and 1.119, Ha-Levi mentions India briefly in two unconnected sentences that portray India in additional ways. One sentence reflects India’s reputation for its high level of science and mathematics. Andalusi, we recall, praised Indians for their “advancement in all the branches of knowledge,” writing specifically about their achievements in astronomy and mathematics. Ha-Levi did not disagree but explained Indian science, like Greek science, as derivative and dependent on the ancient sciences introduced into the world by King Solomon: “Assisted by divine, intellectual, and inborn abilities, Solomon had spoken on all the sciences” and gained such fame that “the inhabitants of the earth would come to him to copy his science for the benefit of the nations, from as far as *al-Hind*.” In Ha-Levi’s view, Jews had originated the foundations of all the sciences and then transmitted them not only to the Indians but also to the Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, and Romans (2.66).

The other sentence about India, which I mentioned before, names India, along with Persia and Greece, as nations who, despite having different sets of laws (*namus*) and beliefs – not having received the true law (*namus al-h . aq*) of Israel – spent great sums of money to have sacrifices performed and prayers said for their benefit at the Temple in Jerusalem. They did this out of recognition of the inherent and unique power of the Temple and its location, unavailable to them through their own laws and locations (2.20).

In these two sentences, Indians are a people possessing science, though derivative, and a people possessing a set of laws, though inferior to Israel’s laws. The point of both sentences was to show India’s inferiority. How do these sentences relate to Ha-Levi’s two main images of India? India’s science fits poorly with Ha-Levi’s image of Indians as weak-minded magicians and astrologers, but the derivative nature of this science shows another weakness of the Indians. India’s set of laws fits Ha-Levi’s parables about a righteous kingdom, but these laws are inferior and unrevealed, which does 

*[p. 39]* ^pg52

not fit the parables. So we end up with four different pictures of India in the *Kuzari*, all of them inconsistent with each other to some degree.

How should we explain Ha-Levi’s contradictory images of India? Abraham Melamed suggested that Ha-Levi, like other medieval Jews, felt ambivalent about India, and couldn’t make up his mind whether India was a land of barbarians or mysterious wisdom.<sup>94</sup> I think, however, that a simpler answer is that Ha-Levi was using each of the four images of India for a different polemical purpose integral to specific arguments in which the images appeared, and that he did not mind the inconsistency. In addition, I would argue for giving much greater importance to Ha-Levi’s harsh attack on Indian beliefs and practices in 1.60–62, the paragraphs to which I have devoted the most attention in this chapter. As I earlier said, the abstract tales of the Good India function only to illustrate theological points and are presented as provisional supposition, whereas the polemic against lying idolatrous Indians is presented as a maddening fact and includes many details, as I have demonstrated, that reflect central elements of India’s image in Ha-Levi’s culture and Ha-Levi’s understanding of the pagan religions. Toward this India with its claim to actual pre-biblical temples and a world history that challenged the Torah’s accuracy, Ha-Levi was not ambivalent.

Ha-Levi’s picture of Indian religion introduces several motifs that will reappear in later chapters of this book. Those are the absence of revelation, the presence of idolatry, magic, and astrology, the antiquity of Indian culture, and the derivative nature of whatever valuable knowledge India was seen to possess (in this case, science). We turn next to Abraham ibn Ezra, a younger colleague of Ha-Levi, who viewed Indian culture and thought in surprisingly different ways. India was reflected in his mind as a land of admirable scientists who believed in an eternal universe, one God, and astrology.

### 2. Abraham Ibn Ezra and the Aristotelian astrologers

The Indians we shall meet in this chapter practice astrology and deny prophecy like those in the *Kuzari*, but do this for very different reasons – so different that one wonders at how two Jewish thinkers imbibing the same Islamicate culture of medieval Spain, colleagues who conversed with each other, could reach such opposing images of India.

Abraham ibn Ezra (1089–1164) referred to India fourteen times in his commentaries on the Bible, and many more times in his books of science. In the 19th century, a few scholars read these reports about India and wondered whether Ibn Ezra had gained his information by traveling there – a possibility which the great bibliographer, Moritz Steinschneider (d. 1907), quickly dismissed because Ibn Ezra’s information appeared either wrong or derived from well-known Arabic writings.<sup>1</sup> Since that brief debate, no one has discussed Ibn Ezra’s ideas about India or analyzed them. This is understandable: not only are Ibn Ezra’s references to India brief, unsystematic, and fragmented, appearing in the form of ethnology, philosophy, and science, but one must try to understand them in the framework of his own rather complex theology that combined Neoplatonic, astrological, and traditional Jewish ideas.

Abraham ibn Ezra is best known for his commentaries on the Bible, appreciated for their crisp style and perceptive reading of the literal meaning of the text, often based on grammatical analyses and expanded by a range of anthropological and scientific information. His commentaries also contain fascinating essays on a variety of topics such as the structure of the cosmos, astrology, the nature of God, the calendar, and the Golden Calf. Ibn Ezra was the last great poet of the golden age of Andalusian literature, and wrote hundreds of poems during his life, which ranged from secular poems about his family, personal misfortunes, and patrons, to liturgical poems to be read during synagogue worship, to poems mourning Israel’s exile. He was born in Tudela, in northern Spain, where he acquired an education in traditional Jewish texts together with Spanish and Islamicate poetry, science, philosophy, and history. Ibn Ezra lived during the same politically turbulent years as Ha-Levi. Eventually, along with a number of other Jews at that time, Ibn Ezra left Spain altogether. Beginning in 1140, at the age of 50, he lived first 

*[p. 48]* ^pg61

in several towns of Italy and then in French towns, never anywhere for long, and wrote the commentaries, poems, and the numerous scientific and philosophical works that have survived to today. He wrote these works to transmit to Jews living in Christian lands the science, philosophy, and culture of Andalusian Jews, to teach students, and to thank the wealthy patrons who fed and sheltered him. In the history of European scientific thought, he served as an important link, through translations of his writings, between Greco-Arabic science and Latin Europe.

We begin now a circuitous journey through the varied worlds of Ibn Ezra’s thought. To reach his full view of Indian culture, we shall visit obscure customs recounted in biblical verses, medieval debates about the creation of the universe and God’s knowledge, the three levels in Ibn Ezra’s cosmos, and the medieval science of lunar mansions, solar years, and horoscopes to predict rain and religious faith.<sup>2</sup>

The ethnology of India: Civil morality, customs, and diet Nahum Sarna pointed out that Ibn Ezra, in explaining biblical verses, often made use of his knowledge of astronomy, astrology, mathematics, and the complexities of the Hebrew calendar. This type of exegesis, Sarna observed, “testifies to a conviction that the Hebrew Bible cannot be understood without recourse to a wide spectrum of varied branches of human knowledge.”<sup>3</sup> In this regard, Ibn Ezra sometimes used a cross-cultural perspective to explain obscure language and behavior or even laws in the Bible. As an example of explaining laws, consider this rather daring, seemingly relativistic, interpretation of civil laws in the Torah, an interpretation typical of Ibn Ezra’s interest in non-Jewish cultures.

*[[Torah 18 - Mishpatim#^23-20|Exod. 23:20]] (Short Commentary)* They \[the laws of Torah\] are not given except to straighten the crooked, for if these \[people\] live in a place where there are no oxen, there is no need for the laws about an ox who gores. And if they live in tents like Kedarites, there is no need for \[laws about\] a parapet and doorpost. And if in the land of India, there is no need for a law about theft and murder and oppression and false witness.<sup>4</sup>

Ibn Ezra observes that the civil laws of the Torah are designed for a specific society that needs them. The Israelites needed laws about oxen that gore, about parapets and doorposts, and about theft, murder, and oppression, whereas other societies did not need these exact laws. Indians, for example, had no need for laws about theft, murder, and “oppression” (the essential example of which is, for Ibn Ezra, cheating in weights and measures<sup>5</sup>).

What is Ibn Ezra saying about Indian society? He could not be saying that it is chaotic and violent, because then it would indeed need this particular type of law. He must have relied on Muslim reports, examined in 

*[p. 49]* ^pg62

the last chapter, about the innate honesty and good habits of Indian people and the civility of Indian society. Abu Abdullah Muhammad al-Idrisi even writes,

They administer justice among themselves without any recourse to a judge or an arbitrator. They do all this naturally, by force of habit and character, in which they were brought up and to which they are accustomed.<sup>6</sup>

In Idisi’s report, Indians do not need civil laws or courts because they settle disputes among themselves as a matter of good character and habit. Ibn Ezra’s comment on [[Torah 18 - Mishpatim#^23-20|Exod. 23:20]] assumes a similar view of Indian character. His argument even implies that had God given the Torah to Indians, it would not have included laws prohibiting theft, murder, and dishonesty!

Ibn Ezra’s biblical commentaries sometimes took an ethnological direction, drawing attention to similarities between ancient biblical customs and present-day customs in Muslim lands, often in contrast to Christian practices. For example, explaining [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-22|Exod. 3:22]], he points out to his Jewish readers living in Christian lands that, like ancient Egyptian women, women “in the land of the Ishmaelites” (Muslims) today follow a custom (*min* *hag*) of borrowing jewelry from their neighbors to adorn their children, but this is not the custom “in the land of the uncircumcised” (Christians).<sup>7</sup> Ibn Ezra’s several references to Indian customs extend such anthropological comparisons to a faraway society that was neither Muslim nor Christian.

He proposes that Indian customs explain three puzzling gestures in the Bible.

*[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^24-2|Gen. 24:2]]* It seems to me that the practice in those days was for a man to place his hand under the thigh of whoever had authority over him …. And the master sits and the thigh is upon the hand, meaning: behold, my hand is under your authority to do your will. And this is still the custom in the land of India (‘*eretz hodu*).

*[[Psalms 002#^2-12|Psalms 2:12]]* A practice among the nations of the world is to place their hands under the hand of the king like the brothers of Solomon, or the servant under the thigh of his master, or to kiss the king. And this is the custom to this day in the land of India.

The problem for Ibn Ezra and his readers was that these gestures, which seemed to express submission to authority, were not practiced in modern-day Europe and therefore appeared strange. Ibn Ezra’s reply is that the gestures were common “in those days” “among the nations of the world.” He wants his readers to imagine a different time and society with different customs. Proof of the widespread practice of these gestures and their meaning comes from India, where these gestures survive to the present time.

*[p. 50]* ^pg63

Ibn Ezra uses another aspect of Indian culture to learn the meaning of a biblical word about food.

*[[Daniel 01#^1-15|Daniel 1:15]]* They \[Daniel and his three friends\] ate rice (*‘orez*), called in Arabic *‘aroz*, and it is highly valued and more palatable than wheat, and it makes the blood pure. This is the food of the Indians (*‘anshei hodu*), who have no wheat, and they make \[various\] dishes from it, and this rice expands when it is boiled in milk. The main thing is that \[even\] a little of it is satiating, and it is heavy on the scales. Its grains are very thin and not round, and its nature is warm and moist.

The word in question, *zer\`onim*, appears in the Book of Daniel twice in its first chapter, when Daniel asks his guard to allow him and his three friends to eat *zer\`onim* instead of the impure food allotted by the king (verses 12 and 16). The usual meaning of this word, and the one used by other medieval exegetes, is pulse, that is, the leguminous crops that include lentils, chickpeas, and vetch. Ibn Ezra begins his interpretation of this word with Sa\`adiah Gaon’s statement that young men eating mere *zer\`onim* should not have looked, in the Bible’s words, “better and healthier” than the other youth, so this was a miracle. Not necessarily, replied Ibn Ezra, because *zer\`onim* actually refers to rice, *‘orez* in Hebrew, and for two reasons. One is the similarity of the sound of the two words, especially if we consider that the “n” in *zer\`onim* is superfluous, as happens in other Hebrew words,<sup>8</sup> thereby leaving only the consonants “r” and “z” (as in *‘orez*). The second reason is the highly nutritious nature of rice itself, and Ibn Ezra offers a long description of rice as it was prepared in India: “more palatable than wheat … makes the blood pure …. satiating.” Hence, the good health of Daniel and his friends in the court of the king is not miraculous, but a natural result of eating rice, the “food of the Indians.” Again, an ethnological datum from distant India served Ibn Ezra as a means for understanding behavior described in the Bible. In the process, he has replaced a miraculous interpretation with a scientific one.

But why does he need to describe rice so fully to his readers? Don’t they already know that “its grains are very thin and not round”? If Ibn Ezra had been writing for the Jews of Spain, he wouldn’t need to describe rice, because Muslims had brought rice to Spain in the 8th century, and it was being cultivated in the east near Valencia.<sup>9</sup> But Ibn Ezra wrote his second commentary on the Book of Daniel for readers living in France.<sup>10</sup> Historians are still uncertain about when or how rice reached these regions, but it does not seem to have been widely known north of Spain and Italy before the 13th century.<sup>11</sup>

Customs of the descendants of Ham

In regard to general diet, however, Ibn Ezra saw similarity not between India and biblical Israel, but India and Egypt.

*[p. 51]* ^pg64

*[[Torah 11 - Vayigash#^46-34|Gen. 46:34]]* In those days the Egyptians did not eat meat. They would not allow anyone to kill sheep, just as the Indians do today. Whoever is a shepherd is an abomination, for he drinks the milk. And the Indians to this day will not eat or drink anything that is derived from sentient life.

*[[Torah 14 - Vaera#^8-22|Exod. 8:22]]* And in my view the Egyptians in the days of Moses were of the same opinion as the Indians, for they are \[were?\] more than half the world, all of them descendants of Ham, and they do not eat meat to this day, nor blood, milk, fish, or eggs, or in general, anything derived from a living creature, and they abhor anyone who eats them. … To this day they will not allow in their land any person who eats meat, and if one of them comes into a foreign land, he must flee from any place where they eat meat and not eat anything that a meat-eater has touched, and utensils are impure in his eyes. … There is no reason to ask why they \[Egyptians\] had cattle, for so have the men of India (*‘anshei hodu*), for the horses and donkeys and camels are for carrying burdens and riding, and the cattle for plowing, and the flock for shearing.

*[[Torah 17 - Yitro#^19-9|Exod. 19:9]]* Know that Egypt and India are of the descendants of Ham, and these rely on those. The people of India do not eat meat, and so would the Egyptians behave, and their custom has not changed from this, except because the kingdom of Ishmael overpowered them and they turned to his belief.

These passages address a problem associated with a particular behavior attributed to Egyptians by the Bible. [[Torah 11 - Vayigash#^46-34|Gen. 46:34]] and [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^8-22|Exod. 8:22]] both use the phrase, *to\`avat mitzrayim*, in a puzzling way. In Genesis Joseph advises his family to live by itself in Goshen because “all shepherds are abhorrent to Egyptians (*to\`avat mitzrayim*).” In Exodus Moses tries to persuade Pharaoh to allow his people to leave Egypt to sacrifice animals because “what we sacrifice to the Lord our God is abhorrent to the Egyptians (*to\`avat mitz* *rayim*).” But why were shepherds and sacrifices abhorrent to Egyptians? Medieval exegetes inferred that the Israelites planned to sacrifice sheep in the wilderness and suggested that Egyptians may have worshiped sheep or that they looked down on shepherds and their herds or that they used milk and wool from sheep. But Ibn Ezra proposed a different solution: Egyptians abhorred the eating of meat from any animal at all and even the drinking of milk. Since this is never stated explicitly (or at all) in the Bible, Ibn Ezra seems to have inferred this idea from his concept of ethno-cultural origins as framed by what is called the “table of nations” in Genesis 10 and [[I Chronicles 01#^1-1|1 Chronicles 1]]. These chapters derive all the nations of the world from Noah’s three sons, Shem, Ham, and Yafet. Ham’s sons were Cush, Mitzrayim, Put, and Canaan, whose own children originated more nations. In the Bible’s telling, the peoples born of Ham thus stretched from the Canaanites and Philistines in the north to the Egyptians in the middle and to the Cushites
