## About

Martin Buber's (1878–1965) book on two basic attitudes toward the world, which he names by the primary words I–Thou and I–It. The German title, *Ich und Du*, means literally "I and You".

- **1923:** published at Leipzig by Insel Verlag (SEP). The IEP says Buber first drafted it in 1916 and revised it in 1919; the SEP says it began as the prolegomena to a planned five-volume work that he abandoned.
- **Main ideas:** in I–It the other is an object to classify, measure or use; in I–Thou it is met whole, not reduced to an object, and the stress falls on what lies between the two. Buber does not reject I–It, which makes science possible (IEP). I–Thou relations occur with people, with nature, and with spirit or God, and all of them point toward the eternal Thou (Britannica, IEP, Wikipedia).
- **English translations:** Ronald Gregor Smith (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1937) and Walter Kaufmann (New York: Scribner, 1970). Kaufmann added a prologue, "I and You", and the IEP reports his complaint that the book's language is obscure and romantic.
- **Influence:** the IEP says Protestant theologians took it up widely; the SEP names Reinhold and H. Richard Niebuhr and Will Herberg. Martin Luther King Jr. drew on it in his *Letter from Birmingham Jail* (1963). In psychotherapy it touched Hans Trüb, Viktor von Weizsäcker, Ludwig Binswanger and Carl Rogers; in their public dialogue of 1957 Buber stressed the unequal footing of therapist and client (Wikipedia). Michael Theunissen (1965) read it as an alternative to Heidegger and Sartre (SEP).
- **Criticism:** the IEP notes objections to the dialogue with nature and spirit, to the romantic style, and to the book's low view of philosophical and scientific knowledge.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says it has had a lasting effect on modern thought, especially psychology. Britannica describes it as a theological doctrine of direct, mutual relation between beings, purest between humans and God.
