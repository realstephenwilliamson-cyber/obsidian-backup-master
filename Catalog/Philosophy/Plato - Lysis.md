## About

A short dialogue by Plato on friendship or love (*philia*). Socrates questions two boys, Lysis and Menexenus, at a wrestling school, while Hippothales, who is in love with Lysis, listens hidden among the onlookers.

- **People:** Socrates; Lysis, son of Democrates of Aexone, in his early teens; his friend Menexenus, son of Demophon; Hippothales; and Ctesippus, Menexenus's cousin, who also appears in the *Euthydemus*.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** a newly built wrestling school. The SEP (Nails and Monoson) dates the conversation to 409 BC; Wikipedia gives no date.
- **Argument:** Socrates tests whether like is friend to like (after Homer), whether opposites attract (after Hesiod), and whether what is neither good nor bad loves the good because of the presence of evil. Each view fails, and the search points to a "first friend" (*prōton philon*, 219d) loved for its own sake and not for anything beyond it. It ends in aporia, though Socrates still calls the boys friends.
- **Composition and authenticity:** no date is known. The IEP lists it among the early dialogues, written after 399 and before 387 BC. The SEP entry on friendship says most scholars put the "erotic" dialogues in the order *Lysis*, *Symposium*, *Phaedrus*. None of the sources consulted questions Plato's authorship.
- **Readings:** Wikipedia, citing Hoerber, says the neither-good-nor-bad view is probably Plato's own, consistent with the *Symposium*. The SEP (C. D. C. Reeve) reads the dialogue as Socrates showing Hippothales how to talk with a beloved: by questioning, not praise.
- **Why it matters:** with the *Symposium* and *Phaedrus* it is one of Plato's three dialogues on love (SEP). Wikipedia notes later echoes: Jacques d'Adelswärd-Fersen named his Capri house Villa Lysis after it, and Mary Renault made Lysis a major character in *The Last of the Wine*.
