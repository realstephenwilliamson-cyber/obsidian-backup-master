> [!quote] Me
> Let's loom at the book of Jonah starting at 3:10

[[Jonah 3#^3-10|Jonah 3:10]] is the hinge, and it's built on a pun the English mostly hides.

**3:10** — God sees their deeds, that they turned from their *ra'ah* (evil way), and God relents (*nacham*) concerning the *ra'ah* he had said he would do to them, and he doesn't do it.

**4:1** — And it was *ra'ah* to Jonah, a great *ra'ah*, and it burned him.

Same root three times in two verses: the Ninevites' moral evil, the disaster God had planned, and Jonah's fury. The word slides from wickedness to calamity to displeasure, and the narrator refuses to break the chain. Whatever Jonah is feeling, the text files it under the same heading as Nineveh's violence. That's not subtle, and it isn't accidental.

*Nacham* is the other load-bearing word. The king of Nineveh gambles on it in 3:9 — *who knows*, maybe God will turn and relent — and 3:10 says he did. It's the same verb used of God in [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^32-1|Exodus 32]] after the calf, and the same one [[I Samuel 15#^15-29|1 Samuel 15:29]] insists God never does. Jonah sits on the wrong side of that tension.

Then 4:2 is the payoff: Jonah explains he fled to Tarshish *because* he knew God is gracious, merciful, slow to anger, abounding in *hesed*, and relenting from disaster. He recites [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^34-6|Exodus 34:6–7]] — the closest thing Israel has to a creed — as an accusation. And he stops early. He drops the clause about visiting iniquity on the generations. The half he quotes is the half he resents.

Where do you want to take it — the wordplay, the [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^34-1|Exodus 34]] citation, or Jonah's death-wish in 4:3 and the plant sequence that follows?

> [!quote] Me
> Stay with the word play for a second. Where else can we find ra'ah in the Tanach?

Worth splitting the root first, because three different words get transliterated *ra'ah* and only one is Jonah's.

- **רעע / רָעָה** — evil, disaster, calamity. Jonah's word.
- **רָאָה** (ר-א-ה) — to see. Different consonant (aleph, not ayin), so it's a homophone in later pronunciation, not a graphic pun. Don't lean weight on it.
- **רָעָה** (ר-ע-ה) — to shepherd. Same consonants as the first, genuinely ambiguous unpointed.

Now the payload. The cluster of *ra'ah* + *nacham* + God *seeing* is a fixed formula, and Jonah is quoting it:

- **[[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^6-5|Genesis 6:5–6]]** — God *saw* that human *ra'ah* was great; God *relented* (*nacham*) that he had made them. The triad in its first appearance, applied to the whole world.
- **[[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^32-12|Exodus 32:12]], 14** — Moses: turn and *relent* concerning the *ra'ah* against your people. Then: YHWH *relented* concerning the *ra'ah* he said he would do. [[Jonah 3#^3-10|Jonah 3:10]] is almost word-for-word, with Nineveh in Israel's slot and nobody interceding.
- **[[Jeremiah 18#^18-7|Jeremiah 18:7–8]]** — the potter. If a nation turns from its *ra'ah*, I relent of the *ra'ah* I planned. This is the stated policy; [[Jonah 3#^3-1|Jonah 3]] is the test case. [[Jeremiah 26#^26-3|Jeremiah 26:3]], 13, 19 runs it again with Hezekiah.
- **[[Joel 2#^2-13|Joel 2:13]]** — the [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^34-1|Exodus 34]] creed *plus* "and relents concerning the *ra'ah*." Joel and [[Jonah 4#^4-2|Jonah 4:2]] both append that clause. One is citing the other or both a common liturgical form.
- **[[Amos 3#^3-6|Amos 3:6]]** — if there is *ra'ah* in a city, has YHWH not done it? The moral/disaster ambiguity stated flatly.
- **[[Isaiah 45#^45-7|Isaiah 45:7]]** — making *shalom* and creating *ra'*. The sharpest version: the word covers both because the agent is the same.

Back inside Jonah, there's a fourth use you'd want: **4:6**, the plant grows to deliver Jonah from his *ra'ah*. God treats Jonah's misery with the same verb he used for Nineveh's wickedness, and cures it the same way — by relenting. The book runs the word 1:2, 3:10, 4:1, 4:6, and the last instance quietly puts Jonah in Nineveh's position.

> [!quote] Me
> What does LXX do with the word?

The Greek breaks the chain — partly by lexical choice, and decisively at 4:1.

**1:2** — the outcry of her *kakia* rose up to me.
**3:10** — they turned from their *ponērai* ways (moral evil), and God relented concerning the *kakia* he had spoken of doing. Two different words for the two halves of the Hebrew pun.
**4:1** — καὶ ἐλυπήθη Ιωνας λύπην μεγάλην. Jonah was *grieved with a great grief*. The *kak-* root disappears entirely. The translator read Jonah's reaction as sorrow rather than as a third instance of *ra'ah*, and with that the narrator's quiet indictment evaporates. The following verb is συνεχύθη, "he was confounded/thrown into confusion," rather than anything as hot as the Hebrew "it burned him."
**4:6** — the gourd shades him from his *kaka*, his ills. Here the root returns, so the Jonah-shares-Nineveh's-word link survives in attenuated form, but the bridge verse that made it visible is gone.

So the Greek sorts what the Hebrew fuses: *ponēros* for wickedness, *kakia/kaka* for harm, *lypē* for displeasure. That's standard LXX practice for this root generally, not a decision made specifically against Jonah — but the cost lands hard here, because Jonah is one of the few books where the fusion is doing structural work.

One thing the LXX gains, though: *nacham* becomes μετανοέω. God "repents" in 3:9, 3:10, and in the creed at 4:2 (μετανοῶν ἐπὶ ταῖς κακίαις). That's the word the New Testament will use almost exclusively for human repentance, so a Greek reader gets a verbal symmetry the Hebrew doesn't have — Nineveh turns, God turns, same verb. The pun Jonah loses in Greek is replaced by a different one.

[[Isaiah 45#^45-7|Isaiah 45:7]], word by word:

- **יוֹצֵר אוֹר** — *yotzer or*, "forming light." Participle of יצר, the potter's verb, the one used of Adam in [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^2-7|Gen 2:7]]. Shaping existing material.
- **וּבוֹרֵא חֹשֶׁךְ** — *u-vore choshek*, "and creating darkness." ברא, the [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-1|Genesis 1]] verb, used only with God as subject. Creation *ex nihilo* in the usual theological gloss.
- **עֹשֶׂה שָׁלוֹם** — *oseh shalom*, "making peace/wholeness." עשה, the generic verb for doing or making.
- **וּבוֹרֵא רָע** — *u-vore ra*. Your word. Not *ra'ah* (feminine noun, "disaster") but *ra'*, the masculine adjective/noun: bad, evil, harmful. Same root, and it's paired with ברא — the strongest creation verb, the one reserved for God.
- **אֲנִי יְהוָה עֹשֶׂה כׇל־אֵלֶּה** — *ani YHWH oseh kol-elleh*, "I, YHWH, do/make all these."

Two structural features worth noting.

The chiasm: *yotzer / bore / oseh / bore*. The verbs escalate and alternate, and the two occurrences of ברא land on darkness and on *ra'* — the negative terms get the weightiest verb. That's deliberate. The claim isn't that God permits these; it's that he creates them with the same word Genesis uses for the heavens and the earth.

The polemic: this sits inside the Cyrus oracle (45:1–7), addressed to a Persian context. The most natural read is a swipe at dualism — no counter-deity owns the dark half. YHWH's portfolio is undivided.

On JPS's "weal and woe": it's a good call. It preserves the *shalom*/*ra'* antithesis as a pair of fortunes rather than a pair of morals, which is what the parallelism with light/darkness demands. KJV's "create evil" imports a moral sense the pairing doesn't support here. That said — and this is the Jonah connection — the Hebrew doesn't actually partition the two senses. [[Amos 3#^3-6|Amos 3:6]] and [[Jonah 3#^3-10|Jonah 3:10]] use the same root for calamity, and [[Jonah 1#^1-2|Jonah 1:2]] for wickedness. Isaiah can mean "woe" without the word ceasing to also mean "evil." The English has to pick; the Hebrew doesn't.

> [!quote] Me
> That's what I am actually getting at though. I am trying to circumscribe this woe/evil to get a clear picture of the emotional resonance and thought process. It's not Evil like a primeval evil but seems like a crime-of-passion type evil, perhaps even what the Greeks called excess?

I'd push back on part of this, and the pushback is probably more useful than agreement.

The crime-of-passion reading fits [[Jonah 4#^4-1|Jonah 4:1]] well — heat, the verb *charah*, burning. But it doesn't survive contact with the root's actual range. [[Jonah 1#^1-2|Jonah 1:2]] is Nineveh's *ra'ah*, and Nahum tells you what that means: systematic imperial atrocity, siege, deportation. Cold, institutional, planned. Not passion. And [[Isaiah 45#^45-7|Isaiah 45:7]] is God creating *ra'*, where passion is the wrong category entirely. A word that covers deliberate state violence and God's own deliberative act isn't tracking heat-of-the-moment.

On *hubris*: I'd resist the mapping. *Hubris* is a stance — self-exaltation, overreaching one's portion, and crucially it's a *relation* (you commit hubris against someone or against the gods). [[Torah 02 - Noach#^11-1|Genesis 11]] and [[Isaiah 14#^14-1|Isaiah 14]] have that idea, but they don't use *ra'*. The Greek category that actually overlaps is **κακόν** — which is exactly what the LXX chose, and *kakon* is just as broad and just as unmoralized. The translator found the right Greek word; that's a point in favor of the semantics being what they look like.

Here's where I think you're onto something real, though, and it's a sharper version of your instinct.

The root's center of gravity isn't a moral quality at all. It's **injurious outcome** — harm done, damage sustained. Which is why it can sit on either side of an event: the harm you inflict and the harm you suffer are the same word. [[Jonah 3#^3-10|Jonah 3:10]] does both in one verse. The Hebrew doesn't have a stable act/consequence boundary here, which is Klaus Koch's old argument about the "deed-consequence nexus" — the deed and its result are one continuous thing, and *ra'ah* names the whole arc rather than a moment in it.

That gets you your emotional resonance without needing passion. What *ra'* tracks is: **this is the kind of thing that wrecks.** Not "this violates a standard." That's why Isaiah can pair it with *shalom* — *shalom* is intactness, wholeness, things held together. *Ra'* is the opposite of intact. Light/darkness, whole/broken. And it's why God can *bore* it: he authors the breaking as well as the holding.

Applied back to Jonah: 4:1 isn't saying Jonah did something wicked. It's saying Jonah is now in the wrecked condition — the same condition Nineveh was in and got delivered from, the same one the plant briefly relieves in 4:6. He didn't commit *ra'ah*; he's *in* it. Which is bleaker and more interesting than a moral verdict.

> [!quote] Me
> That helps. 'Harm' works pretty well with a capital 'H' 
> A better Greek comparison (not mapping) may be Empedocles' use of "strife"

That's a much better fit, and specifically because *Neikos* isn't the villain of Empedocles' system. It's a principle with an allotted time, sworn by broad oath, doing necessary work in the cycle. No moral charge. That's the thing *hubris* couldn't give you.

And the mechanism lines up better than you might expect. *Neikos* doesn't annihilate anything — the four roots are indestructible. What Strife does is *separate*: it pulls apart what Love had combined. Nothing is lost, the arrangement is wrecked. That's very close to what you get with *ra'* against *shalom*, where the root š-l-m is about completeness, being whole, things paid up and held together. Isaiah's pairing is intactness against disintegration, not existence against destruction. Nineveh doesn't get unmade in [[Jonah 3#^3-1|Jonah 3]]; the arrangement would have come apart.

Two places I'd hold the comparison at arm's length.

*Neikos* is an agent — a force that acts. *Ra'* is a condition or an outcome. Empedocles has a thing doing the separating; Hebrew has the separated state and leaves the agency to whoever caused it. That's a real ontological gap, and it's why *ra'* can flip between inflicted and suffered while *Neikos* never can.

The bigger one: Empedocles is a dualist with two principles in symmetrical alternation, and [[Isaiah 45#^45-7|Isaiah 45:7]] exists to deny exactly that. The verse is anti-dualist polemic in a Persian setting — *I* form light *and* create dark, *I* make *shalom* and create *ra'*, one subject, four verbs. So the comparison is most useful at the point where it fractures: Isaiah takes the pair a dualist would assign to two powers and puts both under one maker. You get Empedocles' non-moral physics of coming-apart, with the two-principle architecture deliberately collapsed.

Which, incidentally, is Jonah's complaint. He wants the two-principle world where Nineveh gets *Neikos* and Israel gets *Philotes*. What he gets is a God who relents out of the same hand that threatened.

Debt remission, someone receiving what they didn't work off, and the warning is against resenting it. That's [[Matthew 20#^20-1|Matthew 20]] in miniature, centuries early.

Which lands you back on Harm. The householder's defense is *οὐκ ἀδικῶ σε* — I do you no wrong. Nothing was taken from the all-day workers. Their denarius is intact, the contract held, their *shalom* is undisturbed. And yet they're in the wrecked condition anyway. Same as Jonah: nothing was done to him, Nineveh's deliverance costs him nothing, and 4:1 still files him under *ra'ah*. In both cases the harm is entirely self-generated by watching someone else receive.

Both also end with an unanswered question from the one doing the giving. [[Jonah 4#^4-4|Jonah 4:4]] and 4:11 — is it right for you to burn? should I not pity? [[Matthew 20#^20-15|Matthew 20:15]] — is your eye evil because I am good? Neither aggrieved party answers. The text leaves the question pointed outward.

> [!quote] Me
> I often think both the RCL's pairing and the Parasha-Haftorah cycle is an exegetical tool, either by design or serendipity

I'd split them, because they aren't the same kind of artifact and they don't earn the claim the same way.

The parashah–haftarah pairing is exegesis by design, and openly so. The tradition is old enough that the hooks are often verbal rather than thematic — a catchword, a shared phrase, sometimes a single unusual word. The pairing *is* the reading: it asserts a link and leaves you to work out what it costs. The Jonah case is the sharpest example there is, since the whole book is the Yom Kippur minchah haftarah, read at the hour when the repentance question is least abstract. Nobody put that there by accident.

The RCL is younger, and more mixed. The 1992 committee was working from the 1969 Ordo Lectionum Missae, and their stated principle in Ordinary Time is semicontinuous reading — the Old Testament track is supposed to run on its own, *not* typologically harnessed to the Gospel. That's the Year A/B/C complementary-vs-semicontinuous split, and it was a deliberate reaction against the older habit of making Israel's scriptures serve as Christian foreshadowing. So the official answer is: less designed than you'd think.

But Year A is the complementary year, and Proper 20 is a hand-placed pair. [[Jonah 3#^3-10|Jonah 3:10–4:11]] doesn't fall there by rotation. Someone matched the grudging eye to the grudging prophet.

Where I'd resist a bit: the serendipity half of your claim is doing more work than it looks. Any two substantial texts read together will generate connections — that's a property of attentive reading, not of the pairing. The question that separates a real exegetical tool from a productive accident is whether the lectionary constrains you: does it ever yield a reading you'd have resisted? The parashah cycle does, regularly, because the catchword links are arbitrary enough to force uncomfortable juxtapositions. The RCL mostly confirms what the Gospel already said, because the Gospel selects the pair.

Today is the exception. Jonah doesn't illustrate [[Matthew 20#^20-1|Matthew 20]] — it complicates it. Matthew's householder gets the last word; Jonah's God gets a question and the book stops. Put them together and the parable becomes less settled than it reads alone, and that's a pairing doing actual work rather than decorating.

> [!quote] Me
> I'd argue that it depends on the skill of the exegete! But I get your point. (Of course I consider myself a "rabbinic Christian" and Trika, two traditions built on exegesis

Fair — and the skill point partly concedes mine, since it locates the constraint in the reader rather than in the lectionary. A tool that only constrains a skilled user isn't constraining much. Though the counter you'd want is that the parashah cycle *trains* the skill: read enough arbitrary catchword pairs and you develop the habit of assuming a link is there, which is itself a formation, not just a technique.

The Trika pairing is the interesting one to me, because it's a different exegetical logic. Abhinavagupta on the Tantrāloka isn't doing what Rashi does. The rabbinic move is to preserve the difficulty — the contradiction stands, the gloss circles it, and the tradition keeps both readings in the margin. Trika tends to resolve upward: the apparent conflict dissolves because both terms were limited expressions of a non-dual reality that contains them. *Pratyabhijñā* — recognition — is the frame, and recognition means the difficulty was never the thing it looked like.

Which makes the *ra'* problem a live test case between them, and I don't think it resolves the same way. [[Isaiah 45#^45-7|Isaiah 45:7]] puts light and dark, *shalom* and *ra'*, under one maker — that's formally close to non-dualism. But the Hebrew leaves the pairing raw. Jonah ends on a question. The tradition that made Jonah the Yom Kippur haftarah is not resolving the tension; it's putting it in front of people at the hour of maximum exposure.

A Trika reading would want to say the harm and the wholeness are both *ābhāsa*, appearances of one consciousness, and Jonah's *ra'ah* is his contraction — *āṇavamala*, the sense of being a separate limited self who has been shorted. The householder's question in [[Matthew 20#^20-15|Matthew 20:15]] reads almost exactly that way: your eye is *ponēros* because you've mistaken yourself for a party to a transaction.

But that reading makes Jonah's complaint a mistake. The Hebrew doesn't. God's final question grants Jonah's grievance standing even while overriding it — *should I not pity* concedes that pity is the thing at issue, not that Jonah misperceived. Recognition would dissolve the grievance; the haftarah leaves him sitting outside the city with it.

Where do you hold those two together? I'd assume that's not a seam you've papered over.

> [!quote] Me

It makes sense. Krama is what keeps the resolution from going static — the sequence is the reality, not a veil over a timeless one. The twelve Kālīs devour the cognitive cycle phase by phase, and *kramamudrā* is oscillation, not arrival. That does answer my objection. A phase can be simultaneously necessary and superseded, which is exactly what "mistaken and still right" needs. Jonah's rage isn't an error to be corrected; it's a moment that gets eaten by the next moment, and the book ending unresolved is more Krama than Pratyabhijñā. Pure recognition would have to give Jonah a fourth chapter where he sees.

The stronger claim I'd hold at a distance, though not for the reason you're braced for.

"As true an expression" imports a flatness Trika doesn't actually have. *Tirodhāna* is genuinely one of the five acts — self-concealment is divine activity, not a lapse in it — so Jonah's contraction is Śiva's doing and you don't need an apology for it. But *tirodhāna* and *anugraha* aren't interchangeable. They're both real, both His, and the whole point of having five acts rather than one is that they're distinguishable. Jonah is a true expression *as concealment*. Saying "as true as" collapses the distinction the system needs in order to say anything at all. Non-dual isn't undifferentiated.

The sharper seam is the one we walked into three messages ago. [[Isaiah 45#^45-7|Isaiah 45:7]] *looks* like your position — one source, light and dark, *shalom* and *ra'*. But the verse is asserting **agency**, not substance. The repeated *ani YHWH* is a subject claiming authorship, and the polemic target is precisely a cosmology where the two terms are phases of one impersonal process. A Krama reading gives you a sequence that needs no author; Isaiah's argument is that the author is the whole point. That's not a Jewish sensibility you're offending — it's a structural claim the text makes about itself.

And it shows up in the ending. *Should I not pity* is a relational verb; it takes two parties and a gap between them. Jonah's grievance has standing because there's someone across from him to have it against. Dissolve the gap and the question can't be asked — which is the one thing the book won't let go of. So I'd put it this way: Krama can hold Jonah's anger as a real phase far better than Pratyabhijñā can, and that's a genuine gain. What it can't hold is the second person. Whether you need that is the actual fork.

> [!quote] Me
> I agree but my point is that Siva's svatantrya is not dissolution of the gap but elevation of all sides and the gap. G-d is sequence and author.

That's the strongest version of the move, and it's not a dodge — *svātantrya* is precisely the term that carries agency rather than mere substance-identity, so you're not flattening into Advaita. Śiva as free means the gap is posited, not suffered. And *vimarśa* means the self-reflection is constitutive: there's no consciousness without the turning-back-on-itself, so the second person isn't a concession to duality, it's how the thing is at all.

Where I'd press: elevation is not symmetry, and I think your formulation still wants both.

If the gap is Śiva's free positing, then Śiva is on both sides of it *and* is the positing. Fine. But then Jonah's grievance is held at a level where it's already answered — not dissolved, not overridden, but encompassed by the freedom that set it up. Whereas the book's last verse leaves the question live in a way that depends on God *not* occupying Jonah's side. If God authored Jonah's anger as Śiva authors contraction, the question loses its edge: you can't ask someone whether they're right to burn if you're the one burning them. The Hebrew keeps the asymmetry because that's what makes the question a question rather than a rhetorical gesture.

So: "sequence and author" I'll grant. "Author of the objection as well" is the step where something goes out.

Though there's a reading that gets you closer than I'm allowing. [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^32-1|Exodus 32]] has Moses arguing YHWH out of a stated intention and winning — and the tradition reads that as God's own desire, God wanting to be argued with, arranging the terms of his own defeat. *Nitzchuni banai*, my children have defeated me. If you wanted to build a Hebrew *svātantrya*, that's the material. Freedom that includes freely making room for opposition, and counting the loss as the point.

Jonah is that with the prophet on the wrong side. He argues and loses, and the tradition keeps the losing argument in the canon and reads it on the Day of Atonement. Which is a strange way to treat a mistake.

> [!quote] Me
> Grand masters probably win the majority of the games they play but many have the most fun when they lose because of it

The analogy earns more than it looks like. A grandmaster who loses well loses to something real — the opponent found a line, the loss is informative, the pleasure is in having been genuinely met. That's a decent gloss on *nitzchuni banai*: the delight isn't in staging a defeat, it's in the children's argument actually holding.

But it needs an opponent with independent capacity. That's the load-bearing bit, and it's the same thing I keep catching on. If the grandmaster is also playing Black, there's no loss and no fun — just a demonstration. The joy in the [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^32-1|Exodus 32]] reading is the joy of *being met*, which requires someone on the other side of the board with moves of their own. Freedom that includes making room for opposition is one thing; freedom that authors the opposition's moves is another, and only the first generates the pleasure your analogy is pointing at.

Which you may well accept — *svātantrya* could be exactly the freedom to constitute a genuine other, not a puppet. Kashmiri Śaivism does have the resources: contraction is real contraction, the *paśu* really doesn't know. But then the other's independence is doing the work, and "as true an expression" starts to look like the wrong frame for it. The grandmaster and the opponent aren't equally expressions of chess.

Jonah doesn't get the good loss, though. He argues, he's answered with a question, and the book stops before he can respond. No handshake. If the pleasure is in being met, the text withholds it — from Jonah, and from us, since we're the ones left holding the question. Which might be the point of putting it on Yom Kippur: the congregation is seated in the chair Jonah vacated.

> [!quote] Me
> Therein is the mystery of Siva's svatantrya and it is a break in logic, admittedly, G-d's freedom is absolute so that his all-knowing somehow includes the unknowable.

Naming it as a break rather than smoothing it is the honest move, and it ends the argument in a place I can't push further — you've located the cost rather than hiding it.

Two things worth saying about what kind of break it is.

It's the same break in both traditions, which is some evidence it's structural rather than a defect in either. Lurianic *tzimtzum* is the identical shape: absolute presence contracting to make room for something other, and the tradition arguing ever since about whether the withdrawal is real or apparent — because if it's real God is limited, and if it's apparent the creature isn't. Nobody resolved it. The kenosis of [[Philippians 02#^2-1|Philippians 2]] is a third version. Three traditions with different metaphysics converge on one impossible requirement: the maker has to be genuinely absent from where the other stands, while remaining the one who makes that standing possible.

The other thing: a break in logic is not the same as a license. What keeps *svātantrya* from becoming "anything goes because paradox" is that it has to sit at exactly this point and nowhere else, and it has to be load-bearing — remove it and both systems collapse into either monism with no creature or dualism with no God. That's a paradox that earns its keep, as against one invoked to escape a difficulty. Yours is doing work.

Jonah is that break made into a book rather than a proposition. The question at the end isn't rhetorical and isn't answered, and the reader has to sit in the unclosed space — which is what you'd do if you thought the unknowable inside the all-knowing was a thing to be inhabited rather than described. Reading it at the hour of maximum exposure looks like the same judgment.

> [!quote] Me
> Speaking of [[Philippians 02#^2-1|Philippians 2]], why are verses 6-11 set metrically in the NRSVUE?

Because the translators are signaling a source-critical judgment: that Paul is quoting rather than composing. The verse-setting is a typographical claim that 2:6–11 is a pre-Pauline hymn or confession Paul has embedded in the letter.

The argument goes back to Ernst Lohmeyer in 1928 and rests on several converging observations. The passage has an unusual density of *hapax legomena* — ἁρπαγμός, ὑπερύψωσεν, and several others appear nowhere else in Paul. The style shifts: participial and relative-clause construction, parallel lines, no particles or connectives of the sort Paul uses to argue. It opens with a relative pronoun (ὅς), which is a standard marker for quoted material in the epistles — compare [[Colossians 01#^1-15|Colossians 1:15]] and [[1 Timothy 03#^3-16|1 Timothy 3:16]], both of which get the same typographical treatment for the same reason. And the theology is expressed without Paul's usual vocabulary; notably the cross appears, but justification, law, and faith do not.

On the metrical question specifically — it isn't metrical in the Greek sense. There's no quantitative scansion. What it has is Semitic-style parallelism and strophic balance, which is why English Bibles set it as poetry rather than verse proper. Scholars disagree about the strophic division: Lohmeyer proposed six strophes of three lines; Jeremias three strophes of four; Martin and others have argued for two balanced halves at the ὑπερύψωσεν hinge in v. 9, where the subject changes from Christ to God. The NRSVUE's line breaks reflect one of those reconstructions rather than anything the manuscripts contain — Greek manuscripts have no such formatting.

Worth noting the counter-case, since it's live. Gordon Fee argued at length that the passage is Pauline composition in an elevated rhetorical style, and that the hymn hypothesis over-reads stylistic variation. Others prefer "encomium" or "prose exordium" to "hymn," on the grounds that ancient hymns look different from this. The typography commits to a position the evidence doesn't fully settle — which is a general hazard of formatting decisions in translations, since a reader sees the layout as given rather than as an editorial argument.

The τὸ κενόω of v. 7 is where this touches what we were discussing. If the emptying language is pre-Pauline, it's very early — 50s at the latest, quite possibly 40s — which puts the self-limitation claim near the origin rather than as later theological development.

> [!quote] Me
> Are there other examples from the letters that aren't pseudepigraphical? Give me a list of places where "Paul" is thought to be quoting an older tradition

Splitting by strength of case, and restricting to the seven undisputed letters as you asked.

**Strong — Paul flags the quotation himself**

- **[[1 Corinthians 15#^15-3|1 Cor 15:3–5]]** — "I handed on to you... what I received" (*paredōka / parelabon*, the technical transmission verbs). Four-fold *hoti* clauses, non-Pauline vocabulary (*ephapax*-style formula, "the twelve," "according to the scriptures" twice). The earliest datable Christian creed; most place its formulation within a few years of the crucifixion.
- **[[1 Corinthians 11#^11-23|1 Cor 11:23–26]]** — the institution words, same *parelabon/paredōka* framing.
- **[[1 Corinthians 16#^16-22|1 Cor 16:22]]** — *marana tha*. Aramaic preserved untranslated in a Greek letter to a Greek congregation, which means it was liturgically fixed before Paul got it.
- **[[Galatians 04#^4-6|Gal 4:6]] / [[Romans 08#^8-15|Rom 8:15]]** — *Abba*, same logic: Aramaic retained plus its Greek gloss.

**Strong — formal and stylistic markers**

- **[[Romans 01#^1-3|Rom 1:3–4]]** — relative pronoun opening, two balanced participial clauses (descended from David / declared Son of God), *horisthentos* used nowhere else in Paul, and an adoptionist-sounding Christology Paul doesn't otherwise voice. Widely taken as a confession Paul cites to establish common ground with a church he hadn't founded.
- **[[Romans 03#^3-24|Rom 3:24–26]]** — *hilastērion*, *paresis*, "in his blood"; cultic vocabulary that clusters here and nowhere else. Käsemann's case; contested but durable.
- **[[Romans 04#^4-25|Rom 4:25]]** — antithetical parallelism, handed over / raised, both passives. Probably a fixed couplet.
- **[[1 Corinthians 08#^8-6|1 Cor 8:6]]** — the bifurcated Shema. One God the Father / one Lord Jesus Christ, with matching prepositional phrases. The compression suggests formula rather than composition.
- **[[Galatians 03#^3-27|Gal 3:27–28]]** — no Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female. Baptismal, and the male/female pair sits oddly with Paul's own practice elsewhere, which is part of the argument for its being received.
- **[[Philippians 02#^2-6|Phil 2:6–11]]** — as discussed.

**Probable but thinner**

- **[[Romans 10#^10-9|Rom 10:9]]** — *kyrios Iēsous* as minimal confession.
- **[[1 Corinthians 12#^12-3|1 Cor 12:3]]** — same acclamation, negative form.
- **[[1 Corinthians 06#^6-11|1 Cor 6:11]]** — washed, sanctified, justified; triadic, baptismal cadence.
- **[[Romans 11#^11-33|Rom 11:33–36]]** — doxology, possibly liturgical, possibly Hellenistic-Jewish in origin.
- **[[1 Thessalonians 04#^4-16|1 Thess 4:16–17]]** — apocalyptic material Paul may be transmitting; he attributes it to "a word of the Lord."
- **[[Romans 16#^16-25|Rom 16:25–27]]** — often bracketed anyway on text-critical grounds; absent or relocated in significant witnesses.

Two caveats worth keeping. The criteria — rare vocabulary, relative-pronoun openings, rhythmic balance — are soft, and the same tests applied to a control passage of ordinary Pauline prose produce false positives at a rate that should make anyone cautious. Fee's objection to the Philippians hymn generalizes: elevated style is not by itself evidence of quotation. And the confidence gradient is real. [[1 Corinthians 15#^15-3|1 Cor 15:3–5]] is as close to certain as this field gets, because Paul says so. [[Romans 03#^3-24|Rom 3:24–26]] is an inference from word statistics in a three-verse sample. English Bibles that set both as poetry hide that difference.

> [!quote] Me
> If you were to hypothesize a theology based only on these passages, thought to be received from what Paul encountered, what would it look like?

Worth saying at the front: the method has a built-in distortion. The detection criteria select for confessional and liturgical material, so the reconstruction will look creedal and cult-centered no matter what the source communities actually thought. Whatever else they had — ethics, halakhic argument, whatever they made of Jesus' teaching — would be invisible to this procedure. So the result is the shape of their *formal speech*, not their theology entire.

With that discount, here's what the fragments give.

**The center is an event, narrated, not a doctrine.** Every substantial unit tells a story with the same spine: a movement down and a movement up. Died-buried-raised-appeared ([[1 Corinthians 15#^15-1|1 Cor 15]]). Handed over for our trespasses, raised for our justification ([[Romans 04#^4-25|Rom 4:25]]). Emptied to the point of a cross, therefore highly exalted ([[Philippians 02#^2-1|Phil 2]]). Descended from David, declared Son in power ([[Romans 01#^1-3|Rom 1:3–4]]). Nothing here defines a nature; everything recounts a sequence. The Christology is narrative before it is metaphysical.

**The hinge verb is passive, and God is the agent.** He *was* handed over, *was* raised, *was* exalted. Christ is the subject of the descent and the object of the ascent. That's a consistent grammatical pattern across independent units, and it means the confessions are theocentric in structure even when Christ is the topic. [[Philippians 02#^2-11|Phil 2:11]] makes it explicit — the whole acclamation resolves to the glory of God the Father.

**"Lord" is the operative title, and it's doing something specific.** *Kyrios* is the acclamation in [[Romans 10#^10-9|Rom 10:9]], [[1 Corinthians 12#^12-3|1 Cor 12:3]], and the climax of [[Philippians 02#^2-1|Phil 2]]. The *marana tha* shows the practice was Aramaic-speaking and prior — they were addressing him in prayer before there was Greek theology about him. And [[1 Corinthians 08#^8-6|1 Cor 8:6]] splits the Shema to fit him inside it. That's the sharpest datum in the whole set: the earliest material doesn't argue Jesus into the divine identity, it reflexively recites him there, in a formula whose entire function is to assert that there is one.

**"For us" without a settled mechanism.** *Hyper hēmōn* recurs — for our sins, for our trespasses. But the how is unstable. [[Romans 03#^3-25|Rom 3:25]] is cultic, *hilastērion* and blood. [[Romans 04#^4-25|Rom 4:25]] is bare exchange with no machinery. [[Philippians 02#^2-1|Phil 2]] has a cross but no atoning language at all; the death is obedience, not sacrifice. Gal 3 and [[1 Corinthians 06#^6-1|1 Cor 6]] are transfer-by-baptism. So: agreed that the death was *for* them, not agreed on what kind of transaction it was. The theories came later; the conviction came first.

**Scripture-shaped, but the scriptures aren't quoted.** "According to the scriptures," twice in four lines, with no citation. Whatever the appeal was, it was to a pattern they took as settled.

**The community is entered, and entry dissolves prior status.** [[Galatians 03#^3-27|Gal 3:27–28]] and [[1 Corinthians 06#^6-11|1 Cor 6:11]] are both baptismal, both triadic in rhythm, both describe a change of category. Washed, sanctified, justified — three terms for one passage, which suggests the vocabulary hadn't yet specialized into the distinct doctrines Paul later needs it to carry.

**Imminent and unfinished.** *Marana tha* is a petition, [[1 Thessalonians 04#^4-1|1 Thess 4]] expects an arrival. The exaltation is not the end of the story.

**What's absent is as informative.** No law, no circumcision controversy, no justification-by-faith argument, no Israel-and-the-gentiles problem, no Spirit-versus-flesh, no incarnation in Johannine terms, no pre-existence except as [[Philippians 02#^2-1|Phil 2]] implies it, and no interest in Jesus' teaching or ministry. Some of that is the genre. But the *complete* absence of Paul's signature disputes from material Paul himself preserves is the strongest confirmation that the material isn't his — and it means his letters' central arguments are innovations on this base, not restatements of it.

Two places I'd flag the reconstruction as fragile. [[Romans 01#^1-3|Rom 1:3–4]]'s two-stage structure has been read as adoptionist, which would put it in tension with [[Philippians 02#^2-1|Phil 2]]'s pre-existence — but "declared/appointed Son *in power*" may mark an enthronement rather than an origin, and the tension may be an artifact of reading both as technical. And the whole exercise rests on a corpus of maybe fifty verses, several of which are only probably quotations. The picture is coherent, which is mildly reassuring and also exactly what you'd expect from a sample selected for formulaic coherence.

> [!quote] Me
> I know it is problematic for many reasons but it is interesting to compare messianic chabad theology and Sabbatian theology in the immediate aftermath of Zev's death.

The comparison is standard in the field, not fringe — Scholem himself drew it, and it cost him. His argument in *Sabbatai Sevi* is that Sabbatianism's post-apostasy theology is structurally the Christian move: the messianic claim survives its disconfirmation by reinterpreting the failure as the mission. The parallel was the point, and it's part of why the book was received as a provocation.

The Chabad half is more recent and Elliot Wolfson's *Open Secret* is the serious version — he reads Menachem Mendel Schneerson's own texts rather than the messianists' polemics, and finds the resources for the post-1994 position already in the Rebbe's writings on the seventh generation and the nullification of the boundary between concealment and revelation. David Berger's *The Rebbe, the Messiah, and the Scandal of Orthodox Indifference* is the polemical counterpart and argues the parallel to Christianity precisely, as grounds for treating the messianist position as outside the pale.

What makes the three-way comparison interesting to me, given where we've been:

**The failures aren't the same kind.** Sabbatai apostatized — a live man made a choice. Schneerson died childless with no successor and no designated one. Jesus was executed by the state. Each requires a different repair, and the theology each generates bears the mark of its specific scandal. Nathan of Gaza had to make conversion to Islam a *descent* into the *kelipot*, redemptive precisely because it's abominable — which is why Sabbatian theology goes antinomian and Christian theology, with a judicial execution to explain, goes sacrificial instead. Chabad messianism has neither an act nor an execution to redeem, only an absence, so it goes the other way: toward continued presence, *hitkashrut*, the Rebbe as still-directing, the death as concealment rather than event.

**The Pauline fragments have a feature neither replication has.** The confessions narrate the death and then immediately narrate a reversal *by God*. Raised, exalted, all passives with God as agent. Sabbatian theology explains the apostasy but has no corresponding divine act to point to — Nathan's *kabbalah* has to do the work by interpretation alone. And the Chabad messianist position resists the category of death, so there's nothing for a reversal to reverse. Whatever you make of the historical claim, the early Christian material asserts something happened *after*, and that's a structurally different move from asserting the failure was misread.

**Timing is the honest variable.** [[1 Corinthians 15#^15-3|1 Cor 15:3–5]] is a fixed formula within a very few years — possibly within months — of the event. That's the datum that makes the comparison analytically useful rather than merely rhetorical: both the Sabbatian and Chabad cases let you watch a comparable process on a documented clock, with named authors and dated letters. Nathan's *Treatise on the Dragons* and the immediate post-1666 correspondence are the closest thing anyone has to a control case.

Where the comparison misleads: all three get called "cognitive dissonance reduction" after Festinger, and the Festinger frame flattens them. It predicts that disconfirmation produces increased proselytizing and rationalization, which is descriptively fine and explanatorily thin — it can't distinguish Nathan's antinomian *tikkun* from Paul's cruciform obedience from Chabad's presence-in-absence, though those are three quite different intellectual constructions. The interesting work is in the differences, and the sociological model is built to erase them.

One more thread back to Philippians, since you've been running it: if the hymn is pre-Pauline and early, then the emptying reading — the failure *is* the mode of exaltation, not a setback it overcame — is not a repair built after the fact by a second generation. It's in the first formula they had. Which is what makes the Sabbatian parallel bite hardest, because Nathan's move is the same and manifestly a repair.

> [!quote] Me
> Agreed. One of the most Jewish examples of "reinterpreting the failure" I've encountered. I heard a Chabadnik explain that it's like the Rebbe is on the subway, ie his location is not exact but he should be arriving soon.

That's a well-built image, and worth noticing what it does structurally: it converts a categorical problem into a positional one. Death is a change of kind; being on the subway is a change of location. And it borrows the commuter's ordinary experience — you don't know which stop he's at, you've no reason to doubt he's coming. The uncertainty gets reframed as the normal condition of waiting for someone, rather than as evidence.

It's also doing something specific with *hester panim*. Concealment is a native Jewish category — God's face hidden in [[Torah 52 - Vayeilech#^31-1|Deuteronomy 31]], Esther with no divine name in it, the whole exile grammar. So the move isn't imported; it's the standard apparatus applied one notch further than usual. Which is part of what makes it hard to rule out of court on internal grounds, and presumably part of why Berger's argument had to be about boundaries rather than about coherence.

Where the analogy leaks: a subway rider has a destination and a schedule you can consult. The image smuggles in the very thing at issue — that he's en route rather than not. And it's unfalsifiable in a way the rider isn't; a train that never arrives eventually tells you something, but "not yet" absorbs any amount of time. That's not a knockdown, since Jewish messianism has always been structurally patient — *achakeh lo* says every day, however long he tarries, which is the Rambam's own formula. But the twelfth principle is about a messiah who hasn't come; the subway is about one who has arrived and is somehow still in transit. That's the conversion the image performs, and it performs it gently enough that you might not notice.

Compare the Pauline material once more: the confessions don't say Jesus is en route. They say he was raised and exalted — a completed action, with a return still pending, but the pending part is a *second* thing. The subway reading collapses the two into one incomplete journey, which is what you'd expect when there's no resurrection claim available to split them.
