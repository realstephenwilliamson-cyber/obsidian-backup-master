If we look back at the very first sentence spoken by the Buddhist,” and one from the very end of the last chapter, ? we can see that these three qualifiers of cognition are all picking up on claims that the Buddhist has made. He stands with Buddhism against the Brāhmanṇical realist traditions in denying a Self beyond cognition. Rāmakantha jokes here that he never said Buddhist acceptance was a necessary requirement of his position. Rāmakaṇtha has claimed that the Self is established by direct experience (anubhavasiddha), and the Buddhist has claimed that momentary cognition is established by direct experience. So the urgent task is to focus on that language-defying, pre-conceptual experience (anubhava) which is the same for everyone, whether Śaiva, Buddhist, wise person or fool. The point behind Rāmakantha’s joke is that he can go straight to an examination of anubhava to test his conclusion without the Buddhist way-laying him. Refuting Buddhist arguments or convincing Buddhists of Śaiva arguments are insignificant compared to that examination.

Inferences and refutations will recede into the background for a while and direct experience of ourselves, svānubhava, which is the same for all of us, appearing to us before we impose our own culturally conditioned concepts on to it, will be examined. For the build-up so far (the first chapter and this chapter up to here) has established that it is on this point and this point only that Rāma-kaṇtha disagrees with Buddhism, namely whether this pre-conceptual cognition is stable (sthira) or momentary (ksanika).

When cognition features in Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika arguments for the existence of the Self, it is used as the starting point from which to prove the ātman. This is done in a variety of ways: cognition is said to be an action that therefore requires an agent (jñātr); or to be a process (bhāva) that therefore requires a bearer of that process (bhavitr);, or to be a quality (guna) that therefore requires a substrate or possessor of that quality (ãśraya, gunin);, or the linguistic usage of the word cognition is seen as pointing to a further entity that is its subject (‘X cognizes Y’, ‘The thing which cognizes ...°); or the Self is postulated as some kind of cause of cognition.” Rāmakantha’s discussion is thus unusual in that once cognition has been proved, he does not have to prove anything beyond it: rather he simply has to prove that it is non- 23
