## About

A dialogue by Plato. Socrates meets Phaedrus on the outskirts of Athens, hears a speech on love by Lysias, and answers it. The talk turns from love and the soul to the art of rhetoric and the worth of writing.

- **People:** Socrates; Phaedrus, who also appears in the *Symposium*; and Lysias, the speechwriter, who is present only through his speech, read out by Phaedrus.
- **Dramatic date:** uncertain. Debra Nails (*The People of Plato*, 2002) settles on 418–416 BC, as AskPhilosophers reports. The 2022 Loeb edition says only that it must fall before 404 BC (Polemarchus is spoken of as alive), perhaps in the last decade of the fifth century, and that no exact date may have been intended. Nehamas and Woodruff (1995) hold that its characters were never in Athens at the same time.
- **Composition:** there is no direct evidence (Loeb). Wikipedia puts it about 370 BC, near the *Republic* and *Symposium*. The Loeb puts it in the late middle period, the 370s. The IEP places it at the end of the middle period or later, c. 360–355 BC.
- **Shape (Stephanus pages):** Lysias's speech 230e–235e; Socrates' first speech 237a–241d; his second speech 244a–257b, with the four kinds of divine madness and the chariot allegory of the soul; the tale of Theuth and Thamus on writing 274e–275b.
- **1578:** the page numbers the vault's notes use come from the three-volume Plato published by Henri Estienne (Stephanus) at Geneva, with Jean de Serres's Latin translation. The *Phaedrus* runs 227a–279c. Modern line numbers usually follow Burnet's Oxford text, which is the Greek the vault holds.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls the chariot allegory famous. The IEP says the *Phaedrus* and the *Symposium* set out Plato's theory of erôs, whose images kept recurring in Western culture.
