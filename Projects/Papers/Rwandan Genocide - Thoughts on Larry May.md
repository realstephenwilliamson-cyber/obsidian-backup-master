*Give to the departed eternal rest, let light perpetual shine upon them*

Rwandan “Genocide” and Rwandan Genocide:

Thoughts on Larry May’s *Genocide a Normative Account*

Near the beginning of his introduction May writes “I am aware that the dispassionate writing style I will adopt might risk insulting those who are the victims, or family members of the victims, of genocide” and then offers a “heartfelt acknowledgement that we should always keep in mind that the crime of genocide begins with the perpetration of terrible acts.” I would like to start off with the same acknowledgement. Genocide is a difficult subject considering the amount of pain associated with it throughout history and it is impossible to investigate it without seeming unduly cold to someone at some point, for this I apologize.

This paper will be dived into three parts, each addressing a prevalent question on the topic: 1) “what is genocide?” 2) “what can be done about it?” and 3) “how does one have the capacity for it?” Part one will ask what is genocide beginning with Berel Lang’s[^1] critique of May[^2]’s nominalist answer, before exploring the U.N. Convention on the subject and ending with an alternative view. Part two will consider the proposals May refers to as “modest changes” in international law and then present a few suggestions of my own regarding what can be done about it. Finally, part three will look at the question of capacity, essentially arguing that genocide must be “engineered” ideologically as well as politically.

Part 1: What is Genocide?

Lang writes in his article:

Larry May’s critique of the concept of genocide is both…systematic and fruitful. Whether or not one agrees with his positive proposals, he clearly identifies central issues related to that concept, thus providing a valuable checklist for reconsidering both genocide and “genocide.” *But*—a very large “but”—an odd issue unsettles his critique, beginning with the question of whether the phenomenon of genocide is even *possible.* Not because of gaps in the received definition, but because May’s “nominalist” premise repeatedly asserts that only individuals, not groups, *really* exist, and what then, one asks, happens to the *genos* in genocide? The nominalist presupposition here formally resembles Nichanian’s assertion that “genocide is not a fact”: both statements have the status of stipulations; both are critical of the “received” view of genocide, rejecting in it methodological elements arguably necessary for *any* historical analysis—and both present their own versions of the atrocity of “genocide” without, however, substantiating their central assumptions. May recognizes his own *aporia*: How can “genocide” be a credible concept if groups—to which “genocide” refers etymologically as “group murder”—do not “*really”* exist?[^3]

I agree that May’s nominalism presents “an odd issue” here, *but* I think that it’s an existential problem that, by no means, “unsettles his critique.” May himself realizes the apparent contradiction and seems to struggle with it throughout his book, but he clearly defines groups for the purposes of his discussion arguing from a previous work that groups are: 1) composed of individual human persons; 2) who are related to each other by organizational structure; 3) and who are identifiable both to the members and to those who observe the members, by characteristic features.[^4] Elsewhere he writes “Groups are nonetheless abstract names that can be talked about, and indeed perhaps must be mentioned in various accounts of phenomena”[^5] and “from a nominalist standpoint these bonds themselves are not really existing things.”[^6] Lang accuses May of missing the point of the U.N. Convention when he points out the straw man fallacy in the discussion of genocide as “the worst of crimes,”[^7] but he misses that same point with regards to what May is trying to do, that is, provide a normative account of genocide. And it is my view that he has provided sufficient definitions for groups to achieve this goal without the need to consider his existential views on the subject (regardless of whether he offered them or not.)

Lang continues:

This nominalist premise of May’s has a history, and he cites Ockham and Hobbes as predecessors who argued against theories of universals that had populated the philosophical world since Plato. But he describes little of the basis for that conflict, and to assume the one side here without recognizing its alternative is in effect to stipulate, not to argue. Yet—consistently or not—May then raises substantive issues and proposals about genocide and “genocide” that warrant attention; why he would do this, given his doubts about the status of groups, seems to call for an explanation rather than a reason.[^8]

And

May’s metaphysical nominalism, on the other hand, has substantive implications for the concept of genocide, and although at times he qualifies this commitment, it remains the key to his effort to limit the concept of genocide, with the basic line of argument clear: If “groups don’t really exist,” allegations of “group murder” are factitious. Indeed, the nominalist view of any aspect of group-identity asserts its reducibility to features of individuals (thus, for example, the nominalist view of “group rights”—immediately relevant to genocide—as entirely functions of individual rights). But May ignores the arguments that have been given for group “reality” (and rights); his effort at a “normative” understanding of genocide and “genocide” appears then *as if* (a phrase he himself uses) its reference—groups—were real, in effect suspending (if only for the moment) the claims of nominalism.[^9]

For our purposes the insight that we should take from the nominalist approach,[^10] is the realization that it cannot provide an adequate answer to the question “what is genocide” because genocide itself is a categorical abstraction to which nominalism would deny existence. Perhaps it could be argued from a slightly less extreme version (of nominalism) that token instances of genocide are real (i.e. Rwanda, the Holocaust, etc.) but that their likenesses of each other are not (and that’s just silly, but it does seem to be the view Lang attributes to Nichanian.[^11]) Lang points out a further objection to the nominalist answer when he discusses the one-sidedness with which May presents the history of the debate Ockham and Hobbes were participating in, a debate that still thrives[^12] despite the air of finality in which May discusses the issue.

All of that being said the simplest and most universal answer to the question, “what is genocide?” (not to mention the one that is legally binding) is laid out in the definition established by Article II of the U.N. Convention on the subject which states that genocide is: “...any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such: a) Killing members of the group; (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; (c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.” Of course, Larry May and countless others have thoroughly demonstrated that there are many, and indeed some say too many, issues with this received definition. As Lang writes:

This progression of the concept of genocide to a normative role in jurisprudence and public discourse has not, however, been uncontested. In the U.N. itself, before the Genocide Convention’s adoption, the definition of genocide provoked sharp disagreement, and that basic issue has persisted, intensified by the Convention’s own spare formulation, with gaps in it—ambiguity, arbitrariness, even inconsistency—acknowledged by both defenders and critics. Despite such reservations, the Genocide convention has been generally deferred to, partly as the seminal historical statement on genocide, but also for its broadly understood applicability. To be sure, its characterization of *particular* events has been disputed (as in accounts of the fate of the Turkish-Armenian populace in 1915–17), but such disagreements have focused not on the *concept* of genocide but on whether a particular occurrence met the convention’s criteria.[^13]

And:

This balance has changed recently, however, with a growing number and intensity of objections to “genocide.” Such objections have varied emphases, although it should be noted that “genocide-denial” has virtually no place among them. the common question they raise is whether purported “genocides” would be more adequately—precisely, consistently, fully—subsumed under another, probably broader category (for example, “crimes against humanity”), or alternately, whether the category of genocide could be preserved, but with substantive revisions.[^14]

I, for one, am uncomfortable, to say the least, with the idea that genocide continues to be prosecuted in light of the “ambiguity, arbitrariness, and inconsistency” that the experts seem to all recognize but lack the ability to repair. This is especially important considering the precedent set in *Prosecutor v. Jean-Paul Akayesu* which effectively adds rape to the list covered by Article II of the Convention. I am not only concerned with due process for the accused but more so worried that the law’s language makes the indictment of genocide a small net with many holes. In fact, earlier in the course of my studies I questioned whether a legal definition of genocide was even possible. I, therefore, considered myself on the radical side of the debate to which Lang is referring. I now realize, however, that I am not actually participating in the same argument because I believe that the designation of genocide is unnecessary, regardless of how we define it. I will conceptualize this idea more completely at the end of the next section when I offer suggestions regarding what can be done.

Part 2: What can be done about it?

*For the dead and the living, we must bear witness.*

*― Elie Wiesel*

Lang again:

May’s critique addresses four main problems in the received concept of genocide: (1) how to identify the groups subject to genocide; (2) what actions suffice for the charge of genocide; (3) what forms of intention (*mens rea*) genocide presupposes and how such intentions can be determined; and (4) what the justifiable and/or obligatory responses to genocide are.[^15]

May writes with the precision of a lawyer and the depth of a philosopher, a combination that may make for a dry read at times but one that is thoughtful and very thorough. In fact, he is so thorough that much of what he says leaves no room for further discussion, or at least, in my opinion, little need for it. Although that may be the case, in this section I would like to take a look at the proposals made by May in the first three of the four areas distinguished in the excerpt above. As far as the fourth area, that is, what the justifiable and/or obligatory responses to genocide are, I do not think a satisfactory answer has been found in either May’s original argument, Lang’s critique, or my own commentary. I will, therefore, leave this discussion for a future date. First I will lay out May’s recommendations and then I will discuss a few concerns I have for the implications that I fear are inherent in their applications.

*How to identify the groups subject to genocide.*

May advocates that we change the language of the law from “genocide means any of the following acts committed with the intent to destroy in whole or in the part a national, ethnical...” to “genocide means any of the following acts committed with the intent to destroy in whole or in part **a publicly recognized group that is relatively stable and significant for the identity of its members, such as** a national, ethnical...[^16]” Clearly May’s suggested alterations would do little to correct the “ambiguity, arbitrariness, inconsistency” that we discussed in the previous section, they may in fact be counterproductive in that regard. On the other hand, they would do much to close some of the holes in the net of genocide prosecution as well as lengthen its width and breadth.

The thing that concerns me about this proposed definition goes back to the question of whether the distinction “crime of genocide” is necessary. As someone who has experienced firsthand the chaos, terror, intoxication, guilt, and pain of combat, I find that safe people often categorize death in order to justify it, to give it meaning, to offer theodicy. I think that this tendency is part of humanity’s genocide problem, the idea that violence can be categorized leads to the view that it can also be justified. In other words, the primary recognized group that is stable and significant to everyone’s identity is *human being* and killing but one is destruction in part. This is the fact that should be the basis of international law. I do not deny that the dehumanization characteristic of genocide is especially heinous nor that the victims of genocide experience incomparable horrors but I do deny any need of a special intent for these atrocities to occur. It would seem however that courts do not agree with me given the prevalence of intensifiers for bias-motivated crimes such as hate crimes throughout the world. Considering this fact, I support May’s proposals.

*What actions suffice for the charge of genocide?*

Currently Article III of the Genocide Convention lists the acts punishable under the ‘heading’ of genocide as: (a) Genocide; (b) Conspiracy to commit genocide; (c) Direct and public incitement to commit genocide; (d) Attempt to commit genocide; and (e) Complicity in genocide. To this list May proposes the addition of: (f) Prohibiting the use of the language of the group in daily intercourse or in schools; (g) Prohibiting the printing and circulation of publications in the language of the group; and (h) Forcing the migration of civilian members of a significant subsection of the group.[^17] Again May does nothing to ease the furthering of complexity where the prosecution of genocide is concerned but once more his suggestions attempt to close some of the “cracks” that those suspected of genocidal acts may “fall through” in terms of prosecution.

Here my concerns are of a more pragmatic nature. First of all, (f) and (g) above have occurred countless times throughout history without the malice, oppression, and dehumanization that is generally associated with genocide so I am not sure they are acts of the same caliber as those covered under (a)-(e). Granted if May is correct then genocide is not morally unique and this point is absurd, but that does not make it *feel* right. Secondly, if it is complicated to achieve the proof of intent necessary for a conviction in cases of (a)-(e) then it will be exponentially more difficult to prove it in cases involving the actions in May’s proposals. Finally, we must be careful here to avoid a slippery slope. I would argue that every modern country is guilty of the acts May seeks to vilify during a particular stage of its cultural development. If the ICC ever seeks to hold the powers that be accountable for past transgressions and these suggestions are implemented guidelines would have to be established to keep the proceedings limited else the whole institution dissolve into absurdity.

*What forms of intentions does genocide presuppose and how such intentions can be determined.*

As far as the *mens rea* element of the crime of genocide May lays out three separate intentions that are involved in genocide cases: (a) The intent to initiate a concerted effort to destroy a given group; (b) The intent to perform a particular act of killing or assaulting, etc. a member of a given group; and (c) The intent that by killing or assaulting, etc. one contributes to a concerted effort that is known to intend to destroy a given group.[^18] May goes on to say that in order to satisfy the *mens rea* requirement one must either manifest intent (a) or both (b) and (c).

Elsewhere he lays this out in the form of a coherent (though again ambiguous) proposal:

Putting the pieces together, I propose that the chapeau that sets the *mens rea* elements of the crime of genocide, which currently says: *any of the following acts committed with the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, religious, racial or religious group*[^19] be changed to the following: *any of the following acts committed with the intent, **either by directing others to act in a coordinated endeavor or through intending to participate in a coordinated endeavor that is known to intend**, to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, religious, racial or religious group.*

Out of all that May suggests this to me seems wisest. With the possible manifestations of the required intent clearly defined (i.e. directing others to act or intending to participate) we have at least a more quantifiable way to illustrate instances of it. With the proper application these reforms could perhaps solve some of the problems faced in both the Rwandan Tribunal’s cases and the *gacaca* proceedings involving complicity in genocide. And this is not to mention the effect such reforms would have on future genocide cases of which we can only speculate.

Part 3: The Question of Capacity

*-I will do such things,-- What they are, yet I know not: but they shall be/The terrors of the earth.*

*--King Lear*

In this section I will consider the question of capacity. The typical formation of this question would be along the lines of “how could somebody do that to another human being?” This sentiment is a common reaction often associated with a visceral response to genocide. I think our collective bewilderment here stems from the fact that the capacity for genocide is not a mental pathway that occurs naturally, but rather one that must be in a sense “engineered.”

I believe that this engineering can incorporate the use of propaganda, institutional racism, bigotry and intolerance as well as countless other tools to further the achievement of one of two distinct states which I consider necessary for genocidal capacity, each described by great philosophers. The first state being what Kierkegaard calls the Teleological Suspension of the Ethical[^20] and the second, what Hakim Bey calls the Temporary Autonomous Zone.[^21] I will not attempt to fully flesh out these states here as a brief description of them should suffice.

The Teleological Suspension of the Ethical occurs when an individual acts contrary to their own ethics in what they perceive as a means to an end, an end that is seen as conveying greater significance than them and their actions. The paradigmatic example here would be a Nazi that truly believes their genocidal actions are for the ultimate good of German race, despite the apparent barbarism of them. Of course, the famous example Kierkegaard used was the Akedah, which is a much better choice considering the meaning and connotation the history of philosophy grants to the concept of teleological. However, for our purposes (and indeed Kierkegaard’s as well upon careful examination) we need not necessarily equate the telos with the divine, but rather the universal. He writes:

It is the right of the subjective will that it should regard as good what it recognizes as authoritative. It is the individual's right, too, that an act, as outer realization of an end, should be counted right or wrong, good or evil, lawful or unlawful, according to his knowledge of the worth it has when objectively realized. (...) Right of insight into the good is different from right of insight with regard to action as such. The right of objectivity means that the act must be a change in the actual world, be recognized there, and in general be adequate to what has validity there. Whoso will act in this actual world has thereby submitted to its laws, and recognized the right of objectivity. Similarly, in the state, which is the objectivity of the conception of reason, legal responsibility does not adapt itself to what any one person holds to be reasonable or unreasonable. It does not adhere to subjective insight into right or wrong, good or evil, or to the claims which an individual makes for the satisfaction of his conviction. In this objective field, the right of insight is reckoned as insight into what is legal or illegal, or the actual law. It limits itself to its simplest meaning, namely, knowledge of or acquaintance with what is lawful and binding.[^22]

It is the “right of insight” that the situation of genocide obscures for the participants.

\[*In a later version of this paper there was more here to flesh out the points presented in this section, i.e. a wrap up of the “right of insight” discussion as well as a more detailed introduction of the Temporary Autonomous Zone. Unfortunately I am unable to locate that version. Hopefully a careful search of my archive will locate it soon.\]*

The Rwandan genocide would likewise be the paradigm for modeling the Temporary Autonomous Zone, a situation characterized by a type of anarchy dominated on some level by mass, possibly violent hysteria, in this case by the systematic extermination of the Tutsi. (The case of the Yana Indians also come to mind when I consider this model.) In both cases, and indeed I would argue, most cases of genocide, we find a deeply rooted xenophobia within a system that is stratified along the lines of any of the groups protected under the current definitions of genocide. (A fact that possibly offers yet more insight into our previous discussion.) In support of my argument I would like to look at the history of the ethnic discord in Rwanda.

Ethnic conflict is as old as humanity, but what exactly do we mean by ethnicity? In his book, *The Cauldron of Ethnicity in the Modern World* Manning Nash lays out five factors that he calls “the building blocks of ethnicity.”

1.  The body (a biological component expressed as blood, genes, bone, flesh, or other common “substance” shared among group members)

2.  A language (some spoken and written forms of communication, meanings, essences, and stylistic elements unavailable in other languages)

3.  A shared history and origin (this gives the sense of shared struggles, shared fate, common purpose, and the implication that personal and group fate are one and the same thing, with personal fate being itself dependent on group survival)

4.  Religion (the set of beliefs and practices that purport to relate a group in a special way to the supernatural…)

5.  Nationality (this is the right to a territory, an equality with the most powerful other nations and all the symbolic and political accoutrements of a sovereign and independent people)[^23]

Indeed throughout recorded history these five factors have separated peoples and fueled tensions between groups opening the door for the dehumanization and demonization of the Other. However we can see from these constituents of ethnic identity that the so called tribal groups in Rwanda must be divided by biological and historical factors as the Hutu and Tutsi share the same language, religion, and nationality.[^24] In order to, therefore, understand the *mens rea* elements we have seen established as necessary for genocide to occur, we must look at the two remaining distinctions, the “body” and the shared history.

Before we begin our examination of the historical factor let me first make a few comments about the biological. My choice to focus on the history between the two peoples rather than the biology rests on a variety of reasons. (The primary one being my rejection of the idea that genetics can determine behavior except in the most basic of ways.) This is not to say that I do not believe that some biological factors are important in the case of Rwanda’s ethnic identity. Differences in appearance, for example, are obviously important (especially as reinforced by first the German and then the Belgian colonizers) but to trace genocide to these or other genetic distinctions is overly simplistic. (For instance, the ethnicities involved in the genocide in former Yugoslavia are so genetically similar that the differences are insubstantial.)[^25] Furthermore, studies of the genotype expressions in the adult Rwandan population, though showing that there is indeed a genetic distinction between the Hutus and Tutsis, have indicated “centuries of intermarriage”[^26] suggesting that there is no such thing as a “pure-blooded” Hutu or Tutsi and that there hasn’t been for a long time.

From Settlement to Occupation

The precolonial history of Rwanda has been misrepresented by both sides of the ethno-political conflict. Indeed the malleability of a history recorded exclusively by oral tradition is at the heart of the issue, as the truth has been hijacked by the extremists on either side. The complication was summed up best by political thinker and academic Mahmood Mamdani when he wrote: “That much of what passed as historical fact in academic circles has to be considered as tentative-if not outright fictional-is becoming clear as the post-genocidal sobriety compels a growing number of historians to take seriously the political uses to which their writings have been put, and their readers to question the certainty with which many a claim has been advanced.”[^27]

That being said, there are some facts we are able to discern. The archeological record shows that the earliest inhabitants of the region making up modern day Rwanda (and modern neighbor Burundi) were ancestors of the aboriginal Batwa people, the smallest ethnic group that today makes up less than 1% of the total population. Traditionally hunter-gathers and forest dwellers, they occupied the territory as early as 2000 BCE.[^28] Hutu farmers began to arrive in the region around 900 CE as part of what historians call the “Bantu Expansion,” a century long migration in which the Bantu population centered in present day Cameroon expanded into the Great Lakes sub-region. (The Hutu are a Bantu subgroup.)…¶

The Tutsi also began arriving around this time but they were few in number until around 1400 CE. The immigration was gradual and peaceful. There is evidence that some Hutu lineages had emerged as states prior to the arrival of the Tutsi but the majority of the administrative institutions were products of Hutu/Tutsi cooperation.[^29] The most apparent and “normative” distinction between the two peoples was that the Tutsi were herders and the Hutu were farmers, a distinction in no way without exception.

Over the next 400 years the Hutus and Tutsis amalgamated to the point that they “spoke the same language, practiced the same religion, intermarried, and lived intermingled, without territorial distinctions…sharing the same social and political culture… Because of all this mixing, ethnographers and historians have lately come to agree that Hutus and Tutsis cannot properly be called distinct ethnic groups.”[^30] During this time the various clans slowly unified but it was not until the second half of the nineteenth century that a mwami (or king) emerged capable of bringing the entire country together.[^31]

It was under Mwami Kigeri IV Rwabugiri, a Tutsi who ruled during the late 1800s, that the characteristics that distinguish modern Rwanda began to become apparent. He established a powerful state authority that was able to provide firm direction to and control over an elaborate series of subordinate social institutions. Through this infrastructure Rwabugiri was able to consolidate political power and form a unified kingdom.[^32]

Someone Else’s Lies: The Colonial Era

At the time of the Berlin West Africa Conference in 1885 no European had ever set foot in Rwanda, but nonetheless it and neighboring Burundi became German East Africa with the stroke of a pen.[^33] The colonial era had begun, and with it came Roman Catholic missionaries with teachings inspired by the overtly racist theories of 19th century Europe, what experts now refer to as “scientific racism,” a destructive ideology with ramifications that resonate across the history of the world.[^34] And let’s not forget that since the missionaries controlled the only schools during the colonial-era, these pernicious values were disseminated to several generations of Rwandans. Generally, I believe that this intergenerational transmission is, if not a necessary condition for most genocides, at least a salient feature of them. I will have more to say on this front when examining the conclusions I have reached in this section.

The theory was partly based on the appearance of many Tutsi, as they were taller and thinner than most Hutu, and partly based on the European disbelief of the fact that Africans could, by themselves, that is without white people, create the sophisticated kingdom that was first discovered upon European arrival in Rwanda. Known as the Hamitic hypothesis, the fundamental doctrine of scientific racism posited that “the Tutsi had sprung from a superior Caucasoid race from the Nile Valley, and probably even had Christian origins. On the evolutionary scale, then all the rage in Europe, the Tutsi could be seen as approaching, very painstakingly, to be sure, the exalted level of white people. They were considered more intelligent, more reliable, harder working, and more like whites than the “Bantu” Hutu majority.[^35]”

From 1895 to 1916, Rwanda was a German colony. In 1916, during the First World War, Germany was forced to give up its east African territories. In Rwanda and Burundi the German control was replaced by Belgium. For the next 45 years, the Belgians would control Rwanda, Burundi, and the Congo. Both Hutu and Tutsi agree that first Germany, then Belgium, organized the colony in such a way as to maintain the ethnic lines that Mwami Rwabugiri had drawn, though the Europeans made those lines inflexible and absolute.

It served the colonizers to recognize the King and the Tutsi aristocracy surrounding him and to extend to them significant –but subservient – political power and administrative functions. With the classic colonial system of indirect rule, a small handful of Belgians were able to do what they saw fit in Rwanda, whatever they deemed most beneficial to imperial interests. Slowly the colonizers made sure that the local elite shared an interest in endorsing the racist notions about the Tutsi and the Hutu that had been preached by missionaries.

In the early 1930’s the Belgians enacted a series of administrative measures that institutionalized the divisions between the two races (race being the language of the time, this was before the notion of ethnicity was introduced.) These measures culminated in the issuance of identity cards to every Rwandan, declaring each to be either Hutu or Tutsi. The tribal amalgamation was over. The identity card system was maintained by the colonizers for over 60 years. The tragic irony is, that the cards would become the key that would enable Hutu killers to identify the Tutsis during the genocide, Tutsis who were the system’s original beneficiaries.[^36]

Of course the fact was that not all Rwandans could be so easily distinguished so arbitrariness and foolishness found their way into the identification exercise. For example anyone who owned 10 cows was automatically designated a Tutsi, while the rest were deemed to be Hutu. Another account insists that the Belgians asked each Rwandan to declare for himself or herself, with 15 per cent identifying themselves as Tutsi, 84 per cent as Hutu, and one per cent as Twa. Whichever way ethnic identity was assigned, it became the basis for determining the allocation of many of the resources of the country such as schools, civil service jobs, and other public infrastructure.[^37]

The colonizer’s spiritual partner, the Roman Catholic Church of Rwanda, supported and reinforced the racist ideology and any other measures deemed important to the imperialism of Rwanda’s masters. Although Catholic missionaries had arrived before the Belgians (and Germans) it was not until government initiatives during the late 1920’s that large-scale conversions to Catholicism took place.

Hundreds of thousands of Rwandans converted, making the church the country's main social institution. When the King demonstrated an unacceptable determination to keep alive Rwandan traditions and customs and to resist the will of the administrators and missionaries, they united to depose him in favor of his son, who had been educated in mission schools and was likely to accept Christianity. With the population's conversion, Belgium's interests were largely satisfied. They had created the Rwanda they wanted: centralized, easy to control, efficient, intolerant of nonconformity, and Catholic… It is not possible to write about Rwanda without writing about the role of the Catholic Church, which, since the arrival of the Belgians, has functioned virtually as the country's state church. That role, as evident during the genocide as it was in the colonial period, is one about which it would be hard to feel proud at any time…Much of the elaborate Hamitic ideology was simply invented by the Catholic White Fathers, missionaries who wrote what later became the established version of Rwandan history to conform to their essentially racist views. Because they controlled all schooling in the colony, the White Fathers were able, with the full endorsement of the Belgians, to indoctrinate generations of school children, both Hutu and Tutsi, with the pernicious Hamitic notions. Whatever else they learned, no student could have failed to absorb the lessons of ethnic cleavage and racial ranking…Together, the Belgians and the Catholic Church were guilty of what some call “ethnogenesis” – the institutionalization of rigid ethnic identities for political purposes. The proposition that it was legitimate to politicize and polarize society through ethnic cleavages – to play the 'ethnic card' for political advantage, as a later generation would describe the tactic – became integral to Rwandan public life. Ethnogenesis was by no means unknown in other African colonies and, destructive as it has been everywhere, no other genocide has occurred. But it was everywhere a force of great potential consequence and, in Rwanda, it combined with other factors with ultimately devastating consequences…Until the end of the colonial period, Rwandan society resembled a steep, clearly defined pyramid. At the very top of the hierarchy were the whites, known locally as Bazungu; a tiny cluster of Belgian administrators; and Catholic missionaries whose power and control were undisputed. Below them were their chosen intermediaries, a very small group of Tutsi drawn mainly from two clans who monopolized most of the opportunities provided by indirect rule. Wherever the Belgians gave this group the latitude to exert control, they did so stringently, almost always leaving animosity behind in their wake…The fact that just two Tutsi clans among many were privileged by colonial rule points to a central truth of Rwanda: It has never been valid to imply that a homogeneous Tutsi or Hutu community existed at any time.[^38]”

In 1931 the last bastions of Rwanda’s traditional system were eradicated when Bishop Classe, head of the Roman Catholic Church in Rwanda, and General Tilkens, Governor General of the Belgian Congo, took steps to “destroy the ancient monarchy with its political and ideological foundations and replace it with a Christian, pro-colonial monarchy.”[^39]

The Colonial Shift

During the late 1950’s the wave of colonialism finally ebbed with the war for Algerian liberation and the independence of Ghana. Like many other European powers, the Belgians were desperate for an African exit strategy that would allow them to retain their economic control in the region. Soon it became clear that independence was inevitable and the Belgians decided it to be in their self-interest to shift support from the Tutsi minority to the Hutu majority.

In 1957 a Hutu intellectual by the name of Gregoire Kayibanda, with the Church’s help, produced and distributed a document known as the Hutu Manifesto. The manifesto’s main point was that the Hutus must take over control of the country prior to independence in order to end the “monopoly of the Tutsi.” Using the same fabricated propaganda taught by the colonizers and missionaries, Kayibanda reiterated the “double colonization, one by Hamites (Tutsi) and another by Europeans” calling it a “violent conquest.” He claimed this “bitterness of over 900 years of Tutsi domination” justified the use of force against the Tutsi.[^40]

In late 1959, members of PARMEHUTU (a pro-Hutu political party) heeded Kayibanda’s claims leading a revolt that resulted in widespread ethnic clashes and the mysterious death of King Kigri V. By the early 1960’s, these conflicts and other Hutu attacks had caused thousands of Tutsi deaths. Additionally over 130,000 Tutsi refugees fled to neighboring countries and their land and cattle were quickly claimed by Hutu horticulturalists. Nonetheless the trail leading to an independent Rwanda continued to be blazed. In 1961 Gregoire Kayibanda became Rwanda’s first president-elect through a national election conducted under UN supervision. Finally Rwanda was declared independent on 1 July 1962 but the country was far from unified.[^41]

From Freedom to Atrocity

Refugees supported by the Tutsi led government in Burundi, started launching unsuccessful attacks into Rwanda. Typically these skirmishes would serve as catalysts for brutal reprisals carried out by Hutu against local Tutsi. In 1963 Rwanda’s Hutu government used a failed invasion as the justification to launch a new series of retaliation. It is estimated that between December 1963 and January 1964, these actions resulted in over 10,000 Tutsi deaths, including those of any Tutsi politicians still living in Rwanda.[^42]

In July 1973, Major Juvénal Habyarimana, a Hutu from the north, overthrew southern Kayibanda declaring himself president of the Second Republic. Throughout the next few years, Habyarmana’s security forces would set plans into motion for the destruction of any serious political opposition be it Hutu or Tutsi. This plot would go on to include the death of former president Kayibanda and many of the high-ranking aristocracy that supported him. Next Habyarimana further entrenched his power by appointing his kin and other loyal supporters to high level positions in both the government and security forces. Soon the president’s relatives (or that of wife) dominated the army, civil services and, most especially, the Presidential Guard. Military regulations were put in place to prohibit members of the armed forces from marrying Tutsi. Under Habyarimana’s rule Rwanda became a dictatorship in all but name. The new “administration” maintained the “ethnic” identity card program and the Tutsi were restricted to the private sector.[^43]

The number of Rwandan refugees in neighboring countries continued to rise and by the mid-1980’s, it had surpassed one-half million not to mention the thousands more that had fled all the way to Europe and North America. Habyarimana’s regime refused to allow them to return, insisting that Rwanda was “already too crowded and had too little land, jobs, and food for them.” The surrounding countries were also poor and struggling to provide for their own citizens let alone an ever growing refugee population.

Tutsi refugees in Uganda, together with some Hutu refugees, formed a new paramilitary party called the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) and committed themselves to a single cause, returning to Rwanda. During the early 1990’s RPF troops conducted a number of strikes from Uganda in various unsuccessful attempts to seize power. This renewal of violence caused a new wave of displacement, forcing hundreds of thousands of people from their homes. In response Habyarimana further increased the internal repression and oppression of the Tutsi with an estimated 2,000 dying at the hands of Hutu ultra-nationalists in this new round of violence.[^44]

Despite strong opposition within the growing Hutu Power movement, Habyarimana’s government (possibly in attempt to quell the violence) signed a series of agreements with the RPF that would come to be known as the Arusha Accords. The Accord focused on three main points: the establishment of a power-sharing government with the Tutsi, a return of the Tutsi refugees, and the integration of Tutsi into the armed forces. Under the new agreement the RPF was to constitute 40% of the enlisted military forces and 50% of its officer corps. For Habyarimana and his administration the Accords amounted to political (or perhaps in a way, actual) suicide for if the Accords were implemented, many Hutu in the government and military would see their privileged positions in the hands of hated Tutsi. Among those fated to lose significant power were the Hutu related to or allied with the powerful lineage of Habyarimana’s wife. The Hutu Power leaders cried treason and but days after the signing, Radio Milles Collines, a new, private station, began broadcasting anti-Accord, anti-Tutsi rhetoric.

On April 6, 1994, Habyarimana’s private jet was shot down as it neared the Kigali International Airport killing the president and all aboard. Although the circumstances of the crash were unclear and the identity of alleged assassins unknown, the Hutu Power movement immediately used its control of the media to blame the RPF and its leader Paul Kagame. Meanwhile the RPF blamed extremists within Habyarimana’s own government. (In 2006 a French investigation concluded that Kagame was responsible for the crash but a more recent probe concluded in its January 2012 report that the missile responsible for the crash could not have come from the suspected military base, apparently exonerating Kagame.[^45]) Within an hour following the crash, and <u>prior to its official announcement</u>, members of the Interahamwe (Hutu militiamen) had begun to set up road-blocks thus beginning the “preventable” genocide.

Conclusions

As we can see the complexity of the ethnic tensions that led to the Rwandan genocide was built upon decades of propaganda, segregation, and abuse. It would be easy to point to the foreign involvement (i.e. colonization and conversion) when attempting to lay blame for the genocide, but I think it would be a mistake. Many other states, indeed most of Africa, suffered from the yoke of colonialism without such atrocities marring their futures. So we must ask ourselves what is different about Rwanda and in order to find an answer we must take insight from other genocide cases. In my opinion two related factors are to blame: the fact that Rwanda’s pre-colonial culture was all but eradicated and the fact that the racist ideology was reinforced by religious authority. It is the combination of these two elements as well as their intergenerational transmission that led to the formation of the moral Temporary Autonomous Zone that I have deemed necessary for this type of genocide to occur. In the words of Emmanuel Levinas, “the justification of the neighbor’s pain is certainly the source of all immorality.”[^46]
