## About

A dialogue of Plato in which Socrates and Meno, a young visitor from Thessaly, ask whether virtue (*aretē*) can be taught. It contains Meno's paradox about inquiry, the claim that learning is recollection, shown with a slave boy and a geometry problem, and the distinction between true belief and knowledge.

- **People:** Socrates; Meno, a student of the sophist Gorgias; one of Meno's slaves; and Anytus, a politician friendly with Meno who later joined in prosecuting Socrates.
- **Dramatic date:** about 402 BC (Wikipedia). The SEP's chronology in "Socrates" also places it in 402, and warns that dramatic dates rest largely on the dialogues themselves.
- **Composition and authenticity:** Wikipedia gives about 385 BC. The IEP treats it as authentic and places it in the early-transitional group (c. 387–380 BC), with the *Cratylus* and *Menexenus*.
- **Argument:** Socrates insists on asking what virtue is before asking whether it is teachable. Meno's paradox holds that one cannot inquire into what one knows or into what one does not know. Socrates answers with immortal souls that learned everything before birth, and by questioning the slave boy brings him to find that the square on the diagonal has double the area. They then test the hypothesis that virtue is knowledge.
- **Ending:** true beliefs must be tied down by reasoned explanation (*aitias logismos*), which Socrates equates with recollection. The dialogue closes by suggesting that virtue comes by divine inspiration, which most modern readers take as ironic.
- **Pages:** Stephanus 70a–100b.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says it is often seen as the first statement of the "Meno problem," why knowledge is worth more than true belief, and says its distinction underlies the definition of knowledge as justified true belief, though Burnyeat and others read *aitias logismos* differently. It reaches the opposite conclusion from the *Protagoras*, where virtue can be taught.
