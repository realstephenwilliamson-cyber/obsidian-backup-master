## PART II: The Indian history of a biblical verse: Partial revelation 17th and 20th centuries

### 6. Genesis 25:6, Menasseh ben Israel, and the east

Current Orthodox interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]

[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] – “But to the sons of the concubines whom Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts and sent them away from his son Isaac while he still lived, eastward to the land of the east.” This is the biblical verse that I heard cited several times by religious Jews in Jerusalem when I asked them about the relationship between Judaism and Asian religions. They meant the verse to explain that Hinduism and Buddhism derive from gifts of knowledge which Abraham gave to children whom he sent east, and east means India.

The same claim appears on more than a dozen Jewish websites. On the widely used Aish Ha-Torah website, for example, the anonymous authors of “Ask the Rabbi” assert that the “gifts” of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] refer to “the mystical traditions of Abraham. Hence, the ancient eastern religions have their roots with Abraham.”<sup>1</sup> The same website includes an article called “Harry Potter and the Children of Ketura.” (Ketura stands for the “concubines” of the verse.) The article’s author claims that “the Talmud and Zohar present a consistent connection between Jewish teachings and Eastern mysticism, brought east by the children of Ketura.”<sup>2</sup> Similar claims appear on other Jewish and non-Jewish websites, sometimes pointing out the similarity between the names Abraham and Brahman.<sup>3</sup>

Some recent Orthodox books also promulgate this Indian interpretation of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]. Probably the most influential statement of it appears in a book by Rabbi Aryeh Kaplan, a prominent Orthodox teacher of Qabbalah (also spelled “Kabbalah”). Writing in 1990, he explained that Abraham’s gifts to his sons consisted of “occult mysteries, which then spread in eastern Asia.”<sup>4</sup> In 2002, influenced by Kaplan, Audi Gozlan composed a book that takes its title from [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] – *Gifts of Abraham: the untold story of Brahman*. Gozlan claims that the phrase “land of the east” in the verse is “the biblical reference to India.” Abraham hoped that by sending his sons to India, they would “greatly contribute to the spiritual development of this eastern land.”<sup>5</sup> He sent techniques for gaining knowledge about the one God of the Torah, but Hindus instead turned to many gods and idolatrous practices.<sup>6</sup>

*[p. 140]* ^pg153

Rabbi Akiva Tatz, respected British author of seven books of Jewish outreach and medical ethics, employs the Indian interpretation of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] to evaluate Buddhism. In *Letters to a Buddhist Jew*, Rabbi Tatz portrays Abraham as “a great iconoclastic ‘enlightened one’ long before Buddhism began.” He sent eastward through his sons elements of his spiritual work and rudiments of esoteric wisdom. That is why “our sources clearly indicate that the wisdom of the East is built on principles of spiritual teaching that Abraham sent to those parts.”<sup>7</sup> But Buddhist wisdom, though based on Judaic sources, is merely human and therefore inferior to Judaic revelation.

Buddhism … is a later reflection of a Torah that is cosmic in its depth and scope. Buddhism may carry its loyal disciple to the outer limits of the Universe in consciousness; Torah carries the Jew beyond the Universe to its Source, to that which dissolves the very notion of limits.<sup>8</sup>

Buddhism offers, according to Tatz, human wisdom about the physical universe, whereas Judaism offers revealed knowledge, transcendent reality, and joy now and eternally after death.<sup>9</sup>

But these Indian interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] raise questions because the verse, understood solely in its biblical context and early Aramaic translations, speaks of neither India nor gifts of knowledge. Nor do all medieval Jewish commentators define Abraham’s gifts as knowledge. Rashi (Rabbi Shelomo Yitzh. aqi), the influential 11th-century commentator, cites an interpretation that has Abraham giving his sons the material gifts that he himself received when he married Sarah. Similarly, Abraham ibn Ezra thought Abraham had given gifts of money and sent his sons somewhere vaguely east of the Land of Israel. Then how did this verse become a contemporary statement about the relation of Jewish to Indian spirituality? And what ideas do the new interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] express about the nature of Hinduism?

This chapter will function as a bridge between the medieval ideas about Hinduism discussed in the previous chapters and the ideas of the 19th-century Jews to be discussed in the following chapters. I will not analyze all Jewish ideas about Hinduism during those intermediate centuries, or all interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], but will focus on the ideas of Menasseh ben Israel, living in 17th century Amsterdam, whose writings brought together various intellectual sources and tendencies, from outside and within Jewish literature. He was the first Jewish author I know of who connected [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] to India, taking the verse in a new direction that reflected the great European explorations of his time as well as Christian interpretations of the verse that circulated in Europe. Beginning in the 17th century and continuing among some Orthodox Jews today, [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] became one significant vehicle for thinking about Hinduism.<sup>10</sup>

[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] in context and in Talmudic 

and medieval commentary

In its biblical context, this verse differentiates Abraham’s relationship with Isaac from that with the children he begot through his concubines. In the previous verse Abraham had given “all that he had” to Isaac, the son of Sarah. Now, in [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], he gives merely “gifts” to the sons of his “concubines.” There is some confusion about the meaning of “concubines” (*ha-pilagshim*), since a few verses earlier Abraham had “taken” a wife named Keturah, not a concubine, and she bore him six sons, whose names are then listed. The plural form of the word, “concubines,” also confuses. Jon Levenson suggests that the term means “concubinage” as an institution.<sup>11</sup> The Book of Chronicles resolved the problem by identifying Keturah as a concubine instead of a wife ([[I Chronicles 01#^1-32|1 Chron. 1:32]]).<sup>12</sup>

[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] also establishes a spatial distinction: Isaac remains in the land promised to Abraham, whereas the later children live “east” of it. “East” signifies the lesser importance and locale where the lesser relatives live. Where was the “land of the east”? Scripture locates the Midianites, descendants of Keturah’s son Midian, in the Sinai Peninsula during the time of Moses and near the land of Moab in later times, east of the Jordan River. In the rest of Scripture, “the children of the East,” *benei qedem*, which I am translating as “the Easterners,” were nations who lived in an area generally to the immediate east of the Israelite territories, and perhaps as far as Aram-Naharaim to the northeast.<sup>13</sup>

In the Babylonian Talmud, “the children of Keturah” appear as cunning competitors for the ownership of the land of Israel (b. [[Sanhedrin 91#91a|Sanh. 91a]]) and as a taunting name for ignorant Jews (b. Zevah. im 62a-b). When the question arises of what Abraham gave to Keturah’s sons, Rabbi Jeremiah bar Abba, on the basis of the faulty way in the which the word *matanot*, “gifts,” is spelled in the biblical text, and to belittle this inheritance, infers that the gifts were faulty. “This teaches,” he said, “that he passed to them a name of impurity” (*shem tum’ah*), which Rashi later explains as a name to be used for “sorcery and \[dealings with\] demons” (b. [[Sanhedrin 91#91a|Sanh. 91a]]). This interpretation then becomes the major current of meanings surrounding [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]].

Medieval commentators later addressed questions about the impurity of Abraham’s gifts. Hezekiah ben Manoah. , writing in the mid-13th century, asks in his comment on [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] how such a saint as Abraham could transmit a holy name to “wicked ones” (as Keturah’s sons are now called). Reading the Talmudic phrase as “a name in impurity,” *shem betum’ah*, Hezekiah replies that Abraham gave them merely a name to conjure demons through lower unholy powers, even when the sons were in an impure bodily state. Hezekiah identifies the “east” of the verse as the land of Aram, related to Uz where Job lived among “the Easterners” ([[Job 01#^1-3|Job 1:3]]). The *Tosafot*, commentators living in the 13th and 14th centuries (in *Da\`at* 

*[p. 142]* ^pg155

*Zeqanim*, comment on [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]), express the same view, and as examples, they point to Arab experts in impure magic. So far in its history of interpretation, the verse has nothing to do with India or spiritual wisdom.

The Zohar: Evil magic and dangerous ideas

The Zohar (13th century, Spain), the central text of Jewish mysticism (Qabbalah), moves the interpretation of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] to another stage by connecting the children of Keturah with the “wisdom of all the Easterners” mentioned in [[I Kings 05#^5-10|1 Kings 5:10]], which says, “And Solomon’s wisdom was greater than the wisdom of all the Easterners and all the wisdom of Egypt.” Three passages in the Zohar employ [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] to prove that these Easterners inherited their wisdom ultimately from Abraham.

Two of them connect the Easterners with sorcery and evil, their main traits in the Zohar. The first passage ([[Zohar 2.04 - Parashat Hayyei Sarah#^z1-133b|Zohar 1:133b]]) asserts that Abraham gave Isaac supernal faith, *mehemnuta \`al’ah* – that is, insight into the mystery of the divine emanations (called *sefirot*), whereas he gave the sons of Keturah “names of aspects of impure spirit,” which bring temptation and destruction upon the world, and he sent them eastward.<sup>14</sup> Because *qedem*, “east,” appears also in [[Torah 40 - Balak#^23-7|Num. 23:7]] in relation to the sorcerer Balaam, whose home was “the mountains of the east,” the Zohar infers that “the descendants of the children of Abraham’s concubine” became sorcerers who inhabit “the mountains of the east,” where they “instruct human beings in sorcery” (*h. arshin*). “From that land of the east issued … all sorcerers,” concludes the Zohar.

A second passage (1:223a–b) interprets Abraham’s legacy to his sons as two types of wisdom – a higher “supernal wisdom” for Isaac, attained through knowing the holy name of God, and the much lower gifts of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] given to the sons of Keturah, which consisted of wisdom based on “knowledge of the lower crowns,” that is, the emanations of the *sitra ‘ah. ra*, “the Other Side,” the domain of dark and demonic powers and the source of the “impure spirit.” Abraham’s gifts to Keturah’s sons thus consist of demonic knowledge enabling them to practice sorcery – basically what Rashi had said in his comment on the verse, but now gaining mythic dimensions in a great conflict between good and evil.

But a third and much longer passage containing [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] (99b–100b) offers a different view of the Easterners and their wisdom, related perhaps to a more favorable image of Easterners found in rabbinic Midrash.<sup>15</sup> Rabbi Abba, one of the main teachers appearing in the Zohar, speaks with initial approval of teachings he personally heard from them.

Once I happened to be in a certain town inhabited by the Easterners, and they told me some of their wisdom that they knew from ancient days. They also possessed books of their wisdom, and they brought me one book in which it was written that according to the aspiration 

*[p. 143]* ^pg156

that a human being intends in this world, so there is drawn to him a spirit from on high. If he focuses on a high and holy entity, he draws that entity to himself from above, and if he aspires to cleave to the *sitra* *‘ah. ra* \[the Evil Side\], focusing there, he brings that down upon himself below. They said that it essentially depends on the words, deeds, and the aspiration to which one cleaves, for the side to which one cleaves is drawn down from above.

The Easterners apply the same principle of cosmic correspondence to the afterlife: “As a human being is drawn in this world, so is he drawn when departing this world. Whatever he cleaves to and is drawn to in this world, so will he be drawn to in that world: if holiness, holiness, and if defilement, defilement” (*‘i bequdsha bequdsha ‘i bimsa’ava bimsa’ava*). Hence, if people cleave to holiness in this life, they will minister to God among the angels, and if they cling to evil and impurity, the *sitra ‘ah. ra*, they will be dragged to Gehinom to be punished by impure demons and then become such demons themselves.

Easterners thus understand the difference between holy and impure and how these categories structure the world, know a law of consequences operating in the universe, and believe in an afterlife with reward and punishment. Like Jews, they possess an ancient knowledge written in books. Rabbi Abba even calls these Eastern doctrines “close to the words of the Torah.”

But in the end these books hold a serious danger to Jews. For Rabbi Abba also found written in them “rites for the worship of stars and constellations.” His final response to Eastern wisdom takes the following form:

My children, this is close to the words of the Torah, but you should keep far away from these books lest your hearts stray after these rites toward all those sides (*sitrin*) just mentioned. Be on your guard lest, God forbid, you turn aside from the rites of the Holy and Blessed One, for all these books lead people astray.

Rabbi Abba then explains this wisdom as ultimately Jewish wisdom (Abraham’s) gone bad: “For the Easterners possessed a wisdom which they inherited from Abraham, who transmitted it to the sons of the concubine,” as told by [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]. “But later they were drawn by that wisdom in many \[wrong\] directions.” Isaac, in contrast, received “all that Abraham possessed” ([[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-5|Genesis 25:5]]), meaning a “holy heritage of faith,” which, presumably because it was a fuller inheritance (Abraham’s “all” in that verse), prevented Isaac’s descendants, the Jews, from distorting it. Finally, [[Psalms 024#^24-1|Psalm 24]] is cited to suggest that the Easterners worship man-made images, turn their hearts to the *sitra ah. ra*, and defile their bodies sexually.

This interpretation of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] offers a more complex view of the Easterners than the passages connecting them with sorcery. Easterners are 

*[p. 144]* ^pg157

portrayed here with appealing doctrines and commendable ethics, founded in a qabbalistic theory of correspondence and reciprocity and the distinction between holy and profane forces in the world. Their “wisdom” derives from the same source as Jewish wisdom, Abraham. Yet their theology is judged wrong because it does not worship the God of Israel, engaging instead in idolatry, and so stands ultimately aligned with the evil of the *sitra* *‘ah. ra*. Indeed, its sharpest danger consists of its hidden mixture of truth and falsehood, since the elements of truth in its confused teachings work an attraction upon the innocent soul.

It is tempting to identify these Easterners and their wisdom with a Zoharic image of Asian religious practices, as Aryeh Kaplan has done. He wrote, “This resemblance” between Jewish and Eastern techniques of meditation “was first noted by the Zohar, which recognized the merit of the Eastern systems, but warned against their use.”<sup>16</sup> Kaplan could only be thinking of Rabbi Abba’s statement about the wisdom of the Easterners. Several Jewish writers continue today to link these Easterners with Asians and to identify the “east” of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] in this passage as Asia. Bruce Krulwich copies the passage about Rabbi Abba and then concludes with the sentence I quoted earlier: “Whatever books the Zohar is referring to, we see the Talmud and Zohar presenting a consistent connection between Jewish teachings and Eastern mysticism, brought to the east by the children of Ketura.”<sup>17</sup>

However, there is no explicit basis in the text for drawing this connection, and several reasons not to. In fact, Gershom Scholem, the great 20th-century researcher into Qabbalah, warned against believing most of the Zohar’s claims about other books, qabbalistic or otherwise. “The whole book,” he wrote about the Zohar, “is full of fictitious quotations and other bogus references to imaginary writings.”<sup>18</sup> If, however, we were to persist in trying to identify the Easterners whose “books of wisdom” Rabbi Abba perused, I would start with writings associated with Middle Eastern peoples at the time of the Zohar’s composition in the 13th century, such as the *Book of Nabatean Agriculture*, supposedly originating in the area of Harran. More likely would be a book combining astral magic with Neoplatonic theories of cosmic sympathy coming out of the Hermetic tradition. One of these, the 11th-century *Aim of the Sage*, contains a chapter on the planetary doctrines of the Sabians. “Underpinning the entire work,” writes Tamara Green, “is the central notion of a pervasive cosmic sympathy: as above, so below,” explaining the theory behind Sabian rites of theurgy.<sup>19</sup> Along a similar line, Yehudah Leibes argued that the Zohar’s description of the content of the Eastern “books of wisdom” exhibits remarkable parallels to a passage written by the Syrian philosopher Iamblichus (3rd and 4th centuries), although there is no evidence that his writings circulated in Spain at the time of the Zohar’s composition. Leibes calls Iamblichus “the foremost representative of Hermeticism and theurgy within the Neo-Platonic philosophical school.”<sup>20</sup>

Isaac Abravanel and the origin of the sciences

The exegetical history of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] now changes abruptly. The meanings which Isaac Abravanel and Menasseh ben Israel will discover in the verse reflect not Qabbalistic mythology, but classical literature and new historical and geographical perspectives.

The great Portuguese-Spanish statesman and theologian, Isaac Abravanel (1437–1508), lived through Spain’s expulsion of its Jewish population and spent his last years in Naples and Venice, where he completed his *Commentary on the Torah* around 1505. He cites [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] in his discussion of the descendants of the three sons of Noah.<sup>21</sup> The nations descended from Ham, he writes, lack political life and the ability to reason, whereas the descendants of Yafet, namely, Greece and Rome, are beautiful in their manners, bravery, and political life. “But among the descendants of Shem … are to be found the investigative sciences (*he-h. okhmot ha-meh. qariyot*) in their entirety, for the Indians, Babylonians, and Assyrians are founders of mathematics and astrology, people who first investigated the natural and divine sciences.”<sup>22</sup> This is the image of Indians as great mathematicians and astrologers, similar to Ibn Ezra’s view of them (whom Abravanel quotes frequently). Also like Ibn Ezra, Abravanel believed in the efficacy of astrology, even though he considered it inferior to prophecy.<sup>23</sup>

Rabbinic writings had imagined the early biblical figures Shem and Eber, who were ancestors of Abraham, establishing academies of learning (*batei midrash*) to study Torah and decide legal rulings.<sup>24</sup> Abravanel reworks this motif into the study of science at these schools. These studies were then passed down to Abraham and from him to the Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans.

Long ago our rabbis, may their memory be for a blessing, investigated the development of science from the Academy of Shem to the Academy of Eber and from there to Abraham our Father. And from Abraham to the children of Ishmael and the children of Keturah came the science of magic (*h. okhmat ha-kishuf*) and the hidden elements and astrology (*ha-’itztag* *ninut*) and the rest of the investigative sciences. They are the ones who brought these sciences to Egypt. According to the sages, “To the sons of the concubines whom Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts” \[[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]\] means that he passed to them a name in impurity, for by means of these names they wanted to acquire all the science and knowledge that do not come through the paths of divine prophecy cleansed from any dross and error. Yet the children of Esau were the ones who brought the sciences to the Romans and Greeks, the children of Yafet. And over all of them, like the height of the heavens over the earth, the wisdom \[or “science”\] of the children of Israel was raised high. And the glory of God shone on them and in its light they saw the light of the sciences and their attainments, and they \[Israel\] were all holy descendants praising God.<sup>25</sup>

*[p. 146]* ^pg159

In contrast to earlier interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], Abravanel sees nothing evil in the magic transmitted by the children of Keturah; it is clearly a valid “investigative science” like astrology. Abravanel believed that magic possessed true power derived from demons, which he considered real and active forces in the world.<sup>26</sup>

Abraham had learned the investigative sciences from the ancient Jewish academies and had transmitted this knowledge to the Egyptians by sending Keturah’s sons there, and to the Greeks and Romans by sending Esau’s sons there. Perhaps Abravanel imagined the ancestors of the Indians, Babylonians, and Assyrians also studying at the academies of Shem, or perhaps, as Shem’s descendants, they had later discovered scientific knowledge on their own.

However, Abravanel also drew a sharp distinction between this human knowledge and divine revelation. He thought that all the sciences, having been gained by human investigation, were inherently fallible, contaminated with impure “dross and error,” whereas the knowledge held by the Jews since the revelation at Sinai, came from prophetic revelation and was therefore complete and perfect. Through the light of this revelation, Jews “saw the light of the sciences and their attainments” – meaning that they understood science in a superior way.

For Abravanel, all the secular knowledge and sciences of Europe originated from the Jewish people. In this contention, as Benzion Netanyahu and Moshe Idel explain, Abravanel was repeating a claim made by important Christian, Muslim, and Jewish thinkers before him.<sup>27</sup> Justyn Martyr, for example, thought that Plato had borrowed his ideas from Moses and the Prophets, and Clement of Alexandria asserted “the plagiarizing of the dogmas of the \[Greek\] philosophers from the Hebrews,”<sup>28</sup> a thesis appearing later in Augustine’s *The City of God* (18.37). Roger Bacon believed that Prometheus, Atlas, and Apollo had studied with Abraham, and that Pythagoras, Plato, and Aristotle had taken their philosophies from Solomon; and also that the nations of the world obtained their sciences from the Hebrews, who were especially skillful in astronomy.<sup>29</sup> Al Ghazali made the same claim about Greek philosophy, and Muhammad Ibn Rushd asserted the origin of all the sciences from the Israelites and “their perfection in the sciences.”<sup>30</sup> The Jewish author who stated this theory most forcefully, and in a form close to Abravanel’s idea, was Judah Ha-Levi in the 12th century, in a passage from the *Kuzari* (summarized in the first chapter of this book).

The sage said, And what do you think about the wisdom of Solomon, who has already spoken on all the sciences with divine, intellectual, and inherent assistance? People would seek his presence in order to copy his wisdom for the benefit of the nations as far as India. And all the sciences, their foundations and principles, were copied only from us \[the Jews\], going to the Chaldeans first, and afterwards to the Persians and Medians, and afterwards to Greece, and afterwards to the Romans.<sup>31</sup>

*[p. 147]* ^pg160

To Abravanel likewise, Jewish priority and preeminence in the realm of secular knowledge were a long- and widely acknowledged fact of history. And Abraham’s children had a key role in spreading this knowledge to the non-Jewish world.

Thus, in the history of Jewish interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], Abravanel’s interpretation still functioned to distinguish a higher and a lower form of knowledge, but he construed the lower form of knowledge as secular knowledge achieved by human effort rather than knowledge of an evil and dangerous magic. This knowledge was still “impure,” reflecting the Talmud’s term, but impure now in the sense only of flawed secular knowledge. This interpretation thus ascribed new value to the gifts given to Abraham’s sons born from Keturah.

As much as Abravanel acknowledged the achievements of Indians in astronomy and mathematics, he also decried their “strange” concept of cosmic renewal in contrast with the Jewish doctrine of resurrection.

The scientists of the ancient nations who engaged in the science of astronomy, who had not seen the light of the Torah, thought that the action for resurrecting the dead was the position of the stars, for they would be aligned in the times of the configurations and circuits of the stars and constellations, when natural existence must cease totally, and they would return to their existence as it was at its beginning. The scientists of India spoke \[even\] stranger opinions (*dei\`ot zarot*) about this. They set the days of the world as four million and three hundred and twenty thousand years …. And then the world would be renewed as before.<sup>32</sup>

Ibn Ezra had merely summarized this Indian concept of time without commenting on it. But Abravanel set the Indian and Jewish concepts into the same category for comparison and judgment. The Indian concept of time was cyclical and eternal, and therefore mistaken, whereas Jewish time moved in the direction of a culminating resurrection.

[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] in Menasseh ben Israel’s 

*Nishmat H. ayyim* (1652)

Menasseh ben Israel (1604–1657), living in Amsterdam a century and a half later, often quoted Abravanel’s writings. Unsurprisingly, then, the theme of the Jewish origin of the world’s knowledge reappears in Menasseh’s two references to [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] in his book, *Nishmat H. ayyim* (“the soul of life,” 1652). Keturah’s sons migrate to India, bringing with them two different gifts: the doctrine of transmigration and oracles powered by demons. For the first time in the Jewish history of this verse’s interpretation, the words “land of the east” point to India, and Abraham becomes linked to Brahmans by the similar sound of the two names.

*[p. 148]* ^pg161

Menasseh’s first citing of the verse appears near the end of a series of chapters in *Nishmat H. ayyim* about demons. Chapter 17 in Part 3 of the book deals with oracles, which he calls *terafim* in Hebrew, the term in [[Torah 07 - Vayetzei#^31-19|Genesis 31:19]] for the objects that Laban consulted, according to Menasseh, to learn about the future. He defines *terafim* as “forms and engraved images” that contain an “impure spirit” and “speak through the power of a demon.” Menasseh asserts that these oracles have real power to foretell the future, although they are evil and sometimes lie, and he cites many classical and contemporary authors in support. Near the end of the chapter, Menasseh gives two examples of demonic oracles in lands beyond Europe. In his first example, people in the West Indies ingest peyote when they want to gain information from Satan. Then “the demon enters them” and they answer questions about the future. The second example comes from India.

Historians also stated that when the oppressed \[forced Jewish converts, *conversos*\] of Portugal arrived in India, they heard that the matter of their arrival to the land had already spread among them \[Indians\] because the demons had told them, and they \[Indians\] did not deny that the Name \[God\] had raised over them a nation from afar from the ends of the earth who would trouble their peace and trample them with their feet.<sup>33</sup> I will answer you that the truth is that even there the demons and oracles speak. For thus said our rabbis about the verse, To Abraham’s sons by the concubine he gave gifts and sent them away from Isaac his son while he was still alive eastward to the land of the east \[[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]\], for he transmitted to them a name of impurity and sent them to the land of the east, which is India, and they still retain the gift of their father until Everlasting Righteousness<sup>34</sup> \[the messianic era\] comes. About this Isaiah said, “The idols (*ha-’elilim*) will utterly pass away.” And Zechariah, “And the spirit of impurity I will remove from the land.”<sup>35</sup> For in every place of the settlements of Israel \[the Jews\], in Asia, Europe, and Egypt, to which spread the letter of the divine prophecy and Torah, their \[idolaters\] deceit darkened of itself and never went forth again to the light.<sup>36</sup>

From unnamed sources, Menasseh has learned that Indians used oracles to learn about the future arrival of *conversos* in India along with Portuguese soldiers. Menasseh claims that demons really “had told them,” and the Indians accepted the arrival of the Portuguese conquerors as the will of God (“the Name”). Menasseh thinks this event shows that even in far-off India, people rightly believe in the power of demons to foretell the future, which he considered additional evidence for the claim he makes in this part of his book, namely, that demons are real and active in the world.

But how did Indians learn the art of conjuring demons? [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] answers this question. Following the Talmud and medieval commentators, Menasseh interprets Abraham’s gifts to Keturah’s sons as a “name 

*[p. 149]* ^pg162

in impurity,” that is, words to be used in conjuring demons. In this case, Indians learn about the future from demons, and the knowledge of how to do this came from Abraham. Elsewhere in *Nishmat H. ayyim*, Menasseh condemned trafficking with demons as sinful and dangerous, and he denounced the practice of witchcraft, but he thought that demonic oracles were often accurate.

The end of the passage translated above, with its phrases from Isaiah and Zechariah, may imply that Menasseh associated idolatry and impurity with Indians, but that these would pass away with the coming of the Messiah, an event that Menasseh considered imminent.

Menasseh’s second reference to [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] appears in Part 4 of his book, most of which is devoted to proving the reality of the transmigration of souls (*gilgul*) – the belief that after the death of the body, the soul is reborn into a new body. The Zohar had already assumed the phenomenon of *gilgul*, and the concept spread to other Jewish literature and was a commonplace of European Jewish belief by the 16th century.<sup>37</sup> In Chapter 21 of Part 4, Menasseh claims that *gilgul* is acknowledged over most of the world, including Japan and India, and is evidenced particularly in the books of great classical writers like Plato, Virgil, and Plotinus, and the Church Fathers. Menasseh, however, also seeks to show that the doctrine of rebirth originated with Abraham even though it was commonly associated with Pythagoras: “For the whole world believed that souls disappear and ‘a man is no better than a beast’ ([[Ecclesiastes 03#^3-19|Eccl. 3:19]]), until Abraham our father came and spread in the world the subject of \[the soul’s\] continuation and transmigration (*ha-hisha’arut veha-gilgul*).” He taught the doctrine of transmigration to the Egyptians before Pythagoras professed the doctrine. And Pythagoras either learned it from Ezekiel or was himself a Jew, so that “all that he \[Pythagoras\] compiled, he stole and took over from our holy Torah and true Qabbalah.”<sup>38</sup>

Menasseh’s Europe was receiving new reports about India and its beliefs, including the doctrine of rebirth. Menasseh, advocating the Jewish origin of this doctrine, needed to argue that it had reached India not through Pythagoreans but through Jews. He turned again to [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] for support.

The sons of the concubine whom he had, he sent away from his son Isaac while he still lived, eastward to the land of the east \[[[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]\], which is India. They too spread this belief. You see that the Abrahamites (‘*avrahaminim*), who today are called Brahmanites (*bra* *haminim*), are the children of Abraham our father. They were the first in the land of India who spread this belief, as Apollonius of Tyana testified, who spoke face to face with them and with Iarchus the king. He verifies and maintains the belief in transmigration, in saying that they were the ancient priests and sages who taught them this principle. And they spoke the truth because from the sons of Abraham our father this knowledge was newly established there and from them, it extended to all the land of India, as is known to all the historians.<sup>39</sup>

*[p. 150]* ^pg163

Menasseh’s interpretation of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] follows from his new understanding (probably learned from Christian writers, as we shall see) that “eastward to the east country” referred to India. Abraham had sent his sons there, transmitting the gift of knowledge about rebirth. Menasseh uses the linguistic similarity of the words ‘*abrahamini* and *brahamini* to prove that the descendants of Abraham are now Brahmans teaching rebirth.<sup>40</sup> Menasseh intends Philostratus’ *Life of Apollonius of Tyana* as indirect 

corroboration. The book portrays a 1st-century Greek named Apollonius who adheres to monastic rules prescribed by Pythagoras. He journeys to India where he observes Brahmans who live at a mysterious high castle and instruct kings on how to rule their kingdoms.<sup>41</sup> He also converses at length with a King Iarchus about rebirth. Although nothing appears in the book about a man named Abraham, Iarchus does claim that the Egyptians knew the doctrine of rebirth before Pythagoras did.<sup>42</sup> Menasseh’s entire picture of India in this chapter comes from this 3rd-century source, which has joined the Pythagorean doctrine of rebirth to the Hellenistic image of Brahmans and gymnosophists.

Menasseh’s next chapter extends the concept of rebirth to animals – meaning that a human soul can be reborn into the body of an animal. Menasseh constructs an argument for the truth of this doctrine again on the basis of consensus, and his evidence comes again from classical writers such as Pythagoras, Homer, Plato, and Empedocles, but a large segment of the chapter comprises information taken from a contemporary travel report about Indian customs.

And thus it is said of Pythagoras that because of this \[transmigration to animals\], he prohibited the eating of animals and would feed and sustain himself from every seed-bearing plant and every tree that has seed-bearing fruit<sup>43</sup> for eating, and this because he thought of himself as a murderer if he killed the soul that is in it, a living speaking soul. And even today Indians living between the Gih. on and the Indus Rivers, believing in transmigration, act according to his \[Pythagoras’\] custom. And they show great compassion for animals. Some of them walk to the streets of the city and purchase birds from their captors and send them away free, and some of them, when a bull mates with a cow, it is their custom to devote a great expenditure \[in celebration\], as Pedro Teixiera testifies. And in their hour of death, they take in their hands the tail of the cow to which they have fed vetch, in the thought that they would immediately enter inside it \[when they die\]. And in Cambay there are buildings full of all good things which will cure every wounded and sick animal, all this in their thought that they would not only perhaps help an animal but also the soul of a human being perhaps reborn there. And thus they say that according to the merits and sins of a man, so is he reborn into an animal of good and healthy strength or thin and bad, wounded from the afflictions of God. 

*[p. 151]* ^pg164

In the Kingdom of Gujarat, the men called among them Banians do not eat any animal at all. And among them are pious ones and men of good deeds who put a mask on their faces because they fear to kill with their breath the small flying creatures which for their smallness cannot be seen by the eye. And thus almost all the people of India believe in the transmigration of animals.<sup>44</sup>

At the end of this chapter, Menasseh concludes, “We have sufficiently demonstrated that also among the nations the matter of the transmigration of animals is accepted.”

The Pedro Teixeira cited by Menasseh was a Portuguese traveler who visited South Asia and the Middle East in the late 16th century and recorded his observations on Indian customs as an aside in a book on Persia published in 1610. Teixeira characterizes Indians as “Pythagoreans” holding to “the immortality of the soul” which “receives reward or punishment in its metempsychosis or transmigration.” He also describes charity for animals, celebration when a cow and bull mate, the animal hospital, the idea of behavior determining rebirth, and abstention from meat by certain sects in Cambay. Teixeira expresses only disdain, however, for the religious practices he observed, calling them absurdities, follies and superstitions, and diabolical ceremonies, and saying of Yogis, “What pains they take to go to hell,”<sup>45</sup> whereas Menasseh records Indian practices with approval because they attest to an underlying doctrine which he considers universally true.

The role of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] in Menasseh’s 

defense of the immortality of the soul

Menasseh ben Israel uses [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] to support three ideas important to him – the existence of demons, the transmigration of souls, and the Jewish origin of the knowledge of such truths. These ideas are central to *Nishmat H. ayyim*’s response to contemporary theological and scientific challenges to his beliefs.

Menasseh lived in an era of conflict and change. He was born at the beginning of the 17th century in Portugal with the name Manoel in a *converso* family persecuted by the Inquisition. After his parents escaped, they settled in the more tolerant society of Amsterdam, where he studied Talmud, Qabbalah, and Jewish philosophy while gaining a broad knowledge of non-Jewish philosophy and theology, including Greek philosophers, Church fathers, and writers from the Renaissance and his own century. This training prepared him for his life’s work: helping *conversos* return to Judaism and representing a middle-ground Judaic theology to Christian intellectuals. He wrote five books in Latin and Spanish on topics related to the afterlife, resurrection, the immortality of the soul, and the problem of human sinfulness, and in his last books, the imminence of messianic redemption. As Henry Mechoulan has shown, these books intended to 

*[p. 152]* ^pg165

position Judaism strategically amid the clashing Christian doctrines and messianic hopes of the day.<sup>46</sup> Menasseh also intended to educate *conversos* returning to Judaism about Jewish doctrines and to defend traditional theology against skepticism from some members of the *converso* community. His books, together with his widespread friendships and correspondence with sympathetic Christian intellectuals, made Menasseh the most famous Jew of Europe in the middle of the 17th century.

During this time the old ways of understanding the world were waning. The medieval Aristotelian worldview was being challenged by the mechanistic theories of philosophers such as Descartes and Hobbes and the empirical methodologies of scientists such as Copernicus, Galileo, Bacon, and Harvey. Theological doctrine underwent criticism from the biblical critics Richard Simon, La Peyrere, and Spinoza, from the mystic Jacob Boehme, and from radical skeptics such as Francis Veron. At the same time, European soldiers and explorers were encountering foreign cultures in Asia and the Americas, which raised questions of religious truth and origins.<sup>47</sup>

Sara Wolf-Bank has argued that Menasseh and the Amsterdam Jewish community were aware of many of these currents of thought, in general, if not the specifics, and that in writing *Nishmat H. ayyim*, Menasseh was responding not only to internal Jewish debates but to the intellectual ferment in European science, philosophy, and theology.<sup>48</sup> Within the Jewish community, one of the foremost issues of debate was the question of the afterlife. Steven Nadler, in the last chapter of his *Spinoza’s Heresy*, explores the deep interest that Amsterdam’s Jews took in this topic. Several *con* *verso* intellectuals, including Uriel da Costa, Daniel Ribera, and very likely Baruch Spinoza, defied the majority of the community in maintaining that souls die when their bodies die.<sup>49</sup> Community leaders wrote a series of books rebutting this view, and *Nishmat H. ayyim*, published in 1652, was one of them.<sup>50</sup> Menasseh’s only book written in Hebrew, it was probably addressed not to the *conversos* of his own community, who lacked sufficient Hebrew facility to read it, but to Jewish leaders elsewhere in Europe who faced the same questions, from Jewish skeptics as well as from the wider European intellectual milieu.

Menasseh begins his book with a personal story of his sleep being disturbed by anxious questions: how do human beings differ from the animals? how could he demonstrate to the world “the greatness of man, precious and beautiful, because of his soul.”<sup>51</sup> The first section of his book examines passages from the Pentateuch to refute claims that it contains no evidence for the continuing existence of the soul (*hisha’arut ha-nefesh*) after death. The main part of the book, however, consists of three further sections that use evidence and reasoning outside the Torah to address questions about the nature of this immortal soul and demonstrate its eternal existence. Menasseh explains the crucial doctrinal significance of the soul’s eternity several times in the book, as for example:

*[p. 153]* ^pg166

Upon the continuing existence of the soul depend, learned reader, the major points and principles of belief (*dat*), namely, the existence of God, the divine origin of the Torah, and reward and punishment. For if you say that the soul ceases and disappears with the body, there can no longer be reward and punishment, and if there is no reward and punishment and providence over created beings, there can no longer be the existence of God, and if there is no existence of God or reward and punishment, for what purpose are the Torah from Heaven and the toil of maintaining the commandments.<sup>52</sup>

This is the central aim of *Nishmat H. ayyim*, to provide Jews with arguments and proofs for the immortality of the soul, upon which stands the entire edifice of Jewish faith. (Menasseh’s use of the term *dat* here implies correct belief.)

Sections 3 and 4 of the book, however, do not actually deal directly with the soul’s immortality, but instead with what might be called the realm of the occult and supernatural. In the words of Joseph Dan, “It seems that Rabbi Menasseh regarded any doubt concerning the veracity of even one supernatural or psychic phenomenon as a threat to the theological integrity of his thesis concerning the eternity of the soul.”<sup>53</sup> Section 3 discusses angels, the various types of demons, demons entering human bodies, demons having sex with women, magic of many forms, witches and sorcerers, shape-shifting and casting spells, amulets, and the powers of both holy names and impure names. Menasseh even recounts his own encounter with the soul of a thief who entered into a woman in Ferrara. He first interrogated the soul and then the woman after the soul had left her, in order to learn about the afterlife.<sup>54</sup> Section 4 of *Nishmat H. ayyim* discusses the transmigration of souls into new human beings, proved from logic and from numerous Jewish and non-Jewish texts, transmigration proved through events reported and witnessed, transmigration of human souls into birds, animals, and plants, dead souls possessing living beings (*ibbur*, called *dybbuk* in Yiddish), and exorcism.

Menasseh reveals the logic of these examples in his introduction to Chapter 19 of Section 3:

From the preceding I have proved the existence of demons by means of empirical evidence, received tradition, and logic. Now I shall speak about additional actions proceeding from them in order to further strengthen your belief in the continued existence of the soul.<sup>55</sup>

That is, if demons and transmigration are real and occur independent of bodies, then the human soul is real and survives the body’s death. But if demons and reborn souls are not real, the eternity of the soul falls with them.

Of the three types of proof that Menasseh delineates in his book – namely, received tradition, logic, and experience<sup>56</sup> – his numerous accounts of demons, witches, exorcisms, and other occult occurrences 

*[p. 154]* ^pg167

function as the third type. Such accounts emerge chapter after chapter in numerous forms and locales, corroborated by an aggregate of authorities and witnesses from many nations and eras. The point was to create the impression of an undeniable mass of empirical evidence for the independent existence of the human soul. Yossi Chajes, in his book *Between Worlds*, calls this type of argument “a rhetoric of experience” and a “polemic of demonology.”<sup>57</sup> Chajes and Wolf-Blank situate *Nishmat H. ayyim* within a larger genre of non-Jewish books being written in the middle of the 17th century that based their proofs for the existence of an immortal soul upon accounts of “spirits, witches, and supernatural operations,” to quote from the title of Meric Casaubon’s book of 1672 that made the same argument.<sup>58</sup>

Thus, the reports in *Nishmat H. ayyim* about Indians consulting demonic oracles and believing in the transmigration of human souls as well as their rebirth into cows, functions as further empirical evidence, and from faraway lands, that almost the entire world believes in demons and the transmigration of souls, “for so this belief was disseminated in all the world.”<sup>59</sup> Menasseh has discovered in Japan the same belief in transmigration,<sup>60</sup> and sees the Indian ritual of *sati* as further proof of belief in the soul’s immortality since the wife who throws herself into fire at her husband’s death does so in the expectation that she will join her husband “in the world of souls.”<sup>61</sup>

Menasseh’s use of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], however, adds an additional claim important to his view of the world and his message to the Jews of his time. Indians could not have learned these truths without the gifts of Abraham in the form of the knowledge of reincarnation and techniques for consulting demons. Like Abravanel before him, Menasseh identified all the deepest knowledge of the world with knowledge revealed to Jews “from our holy Torah and true Qabbalah.” Menasseh simply extended Abravanel’s idea to the customs and beliefs being discovered in India and Japan.

We can recognize the encouragement that this notion offered to a European Jew living in the middle of the 17th century. *Nishmat H. ayyim* was written in Hebrew for a Jewish audience at a time when the Inquisition in both the Old and New Worlds was still torturing New Christians and burning them at the stake, and Cossacks had massacred a terrifying number of Jews in the Ukraine. England and much of Western Europe still excluded Jews from residence, while the churches excluded them from salvation, and millenarian-inspired Christians in England and Amsterdam were increasing their proselytizing efforts. To demonstrate, then, particularly from Greek and Christian writings and from observations recorded by world explorers, that nearly the whole world agrees with the most fundamental Jewish doctrines; that Jews originated these doctrines and spread them to the world; that in every land to which Jews migrated, false beliefs were fleeing before the light of the Torah; and hence, that Jews have a great and splendid role in world history, reaching even to India and Japan – is 

*[p. 155]* ^pg168

to reassure discouraged Jews and sustain their sense of worth in a hostile world, even as Menasseh in his other books pointed to signs of their imminent messianic redemption.<sup>62</sup>

We should, finally, notice in 4.21 and 4.22 of *Nishmat H. ayyim* the underlying tolerance in Menasseh’s approach to other religious communities. What essentially concerns him in his picture of India is not rituals such as *sati*, and not the divergent scriptures that he would infer to exist, but a doctrine, an understanding of the human soul. Although he did not think that non-Jews had acquired the whole truth, as he states in concluding 4.22, and he knew that many non-Jews around the world still engaged in impure idolatry, they nevertheless had been given a very important truth, that of transmigration. This view accords with the tolerance appearing in another form in Menasseh’s *Piedra gloriosa*, written mainly for a non-Jewish audience. According to Henry Mechoulan and Gerard Nahon, he thought it right and reasonable that all good people of every nation would partake of the World To Come. “The non-Jew,” he wrote, “who is virtuous and has the law fresh in his mind, will not fail to gain his reward.” By “law” he meant a universal natural law of morality, the demands of which he defined in the following manner: “Live with fairness and justice, do wrong to no one, do not encroach on the good of another … behave charitably to others, live soberly.”<sup>63</sup> Thus, a place in the World To Come is gained through high moral standards rather than any ritual or doctrine limited to Jews.<sup>64</sup> If Menasseh applied this principle universally, he would think that Indians too, despite any incorrect beliefs and rituals they followed, so long as they behaved morally, would attain a place in the World To Come and be saved from Gehinom.

If, however, Menasseh’s view of Indian thought exhibited tolerance, it was a tolerance shaped by [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]]. For him, Indians possessed a deep truth about reincarnation, and oracles for learning about the future, but these were not ideas native to India that Indians could have discovered on their own, but ideas that had been borrowed from the Jews. That has always been the implication of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] when applied to foreign traditions of knowledge.

Menasseh and Christian interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] In the history of Jewish interpretations of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], Menasseh ben Israel played a pivotal role. First, he maintained the verse’s older connection to impure names for invoking demons. That meaning came out of the Talmud through an exegetical twist given by medieval commentary and the Zohar. But he turned the verse’s meaning in a direction new to Jewish thinking when he explicitly connected the “east” in the verse to India and linked Abraham with the Brahmans. These meanings continue to be asserted by a number of Orthodox thinkers today. How did Menasseh come upon these new meanings?

*[p. 156]* ^pg169

He was not the first writer in Europe to associate [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] with India or Abraham with Brahmans. In fact, the association was already a commonplace among Europeans who wrote about India in the 17th century. They apparently learned this idea from the brilliant 16th-century linguist and visionary, Guillaume Postel (1510–1581). As expounded by his modern interpreter, William Bouwsma, Postel’s philosophy centered around the concept of unity. He envisioned a perfect unity within God in the primordial past that had broken apart into today’s universal discord, but he foresaw a reunification that would soon emerge, re-integrating the human soul and human society and ultimately the cosmos within God. An enlarged Christianity assimilating all the wisdom and religions of the peoples of the world would mediate this coming unity. Postel had found his model for this vision in the Zohar. He saw human history as the story of a perfect stream of wisdom revealed to Adam, which had divided into various branches after the Flood and in the time of Abraham, and now dwelt as valid but incomplete expressions of truth among various peoples of the world and especially in Asia. The world’s original language, too, which was Hebrew, issuing directly from God, had devolved into the current multiplicity of human languages, but Postel hoped to spread the knowledge of Hebrew as preparation for the return of Christ. Meanwhile, he employed the Hebrew language as an analytical tool for learning deeper truths in other languages of the world. From travel reports and the Alexander Romance, he had gained some knowledge of the Brahmans of India and the Japanese, and he found genuine gems of wisdom and truth in their religions. How had they gained this truth? On the one hand, Postel asserted a basic rationality among all human beings, which he considered especially strong in the East. On the other hand, they had received some of Abraham’s own knowledge.<sup>65</sup> Postel turned to [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] as proof and explication of this transmitted wisdom.

In his book about human languages, *De originibus …* (1553 version), Postel interprets the verse in relation to two types of gift: Isaac’s is a general understanding of the Spirit of Christ (*spiritus Christi*), whereas through the sons of the concubine, Abraham “sent a gift of other books of heavenly things into the entire East.” This gift consisted of “ritual books and knowledge of celestial and terrestrial spirits which are impure and are called so.”<sup>66</sup> *Spiritus Christi*, the knowledge given to Isaac, is the highest mediating principle in Postel’s cosmology, the cosmic principle of authority, which he also identifies as *animus mundi* and the Word of God.<sup>67</sup> The second type of knowledge, in contrast, depends on human interaction with lower spirits, and is therefore not pure like the *spiritus Christi*. In his French work of around the same time, *Des merveilles du monde*, Postel defines this knowledge bequeathed to India as “divine doctrine (*Doctrine Diuine*) with their magic or astrology, of which even today they \[Brahmans\] retain the trace, along with a very great knowledge of astrology superior to all the world.”<sup>68</sup> For Postel astrology was a sublime science, a pathway to knowledge about God. “The heavens,” writes Bouwsma, “were to him a window through 

*[p. 157]* ^pg170

which man may read the wisdom of God,” and Postel traced the origins of astrology “back to the Egyptian lore of Abraham.”<sup>69</sup> He thought that contemporary Brahmans continued to excel in the esoteric science of astrology bequeathed to them by Abraham.

This, then, divine doctrine, astrology, and magic, was the knowledge that Abraham’s children brought to India, founding the Gymnosophists and Brahmans.

For the Greeks called them Gymnosophists because of their nudity and voluntary poverty: others, as the priests today in Guzarati Narsignae and especially in the kingdom of India, call them Abrahmanes, or Brahmanas, or Brahmanos, after Abraham himself, the originator of their teaching, which shows the token of its truth not only in the prophecies of Balaam but especially in the Magi, who, with the star leading in a clear sky and with the knowledge that Abraham had taught, came to adore Christ before the Jews themselves.<sup>70</sup>

For Postel, language reflects history. Because of linguistic similarity, therefore, the name “Brahman” revealed Abraham as the source of Brahmanic teachings. Based on the word “east” associated in Scripture with Balaam and the Magi, Postel saw them too as Easterners who had inherited Abraham’s secret knowledge sent eastward with his sons.

Using the same technique of linguistic analogy, Postel drew further connections between Indians and Jews. “Hudi, or Hundi, or Indi, alternative names, are as if indicators for Judeans, or those who praise.” Postel thought the Hebrew word *Yehudi, “*Judean,” was rooted in the Hebrew verb *hodah* (praised). When the letter *yud* in *Yehudi*, being inessential, was dropped, the deeper correlation of *Yehudi* with Hundi and Indi was revealed. Hence, he concluded, “Hundia, or India, is as though the Judea of the East.”<sup>71</sup>

Such is the gist of Postel’s idea about Abraham and the Brahmans. His theory, much simplified, went on to influence later European thinking about India’s Brahmans, especially in the 17th century. Ines G. Zupanov wrote, “By the beginning of the 17th century, identifying Brahmans as the descendants of Abraham was quite common.”<sup>72</sup> For example, the French author Pierre d’Avity composed a world geography around 1613 with its title beginning “The estates, empires, & principallities of the world.” Combining the Alexander Romance, Postel’s theories, and contemporary Portuguese reports, he described Brahmans in this way:

Among these Sages, were also the Brachmans, who desired nothing but what nature required, and did live of that which the earth did willingly bring forth. They hold that these men were descended from the children of Abraham’s concubines, who sent them into the East, as we read in the holie writ, where it is said that they carried certaine gifts from 

*[p. 158]* ^pg171

him. These gifts of Abraham, besides gold and clothes, are the arts and sciences; especially Astrologie, and natural Magicke, wherein they have not only excelled, but also are at this day very skillful, if we shall believe the Portugals, who speake of their owne knowledge.<sup>73</sup>

At about the same time the Portuguese missionary Pero Francisco reported a theory he had heard “that Brahmans come from Abraham and are their descendants according to the flesh.” Although Francisco found this claim “difficult to believe,” many important Jesuit missionaries a century later treated the theory as fact.<sup>74</sup>

Then in 1630 in a book on Indians and Parsees, Henry Lord, a “sometimes resident in East India and preacher to the Hoble Company of Merchants,” recounted the same theory, emphasizing the linguistic parallel and attributing the idea to Postel.

Postellus, lib. d. Origin. Cap. 13. & 15. affirmeth them \[Bramanes\] to bee descended from Abraham by Cheturah, who seated themselues in India, and that so they were called Abrahmanes; the word suffering a Syncope, they in the tract of time, for breuity of pronunciation became called by the name of Bramanes.

Mr. Lord, however, doubted this etymology and upon asking some Brahmans themselves, he discovered that “neither haue they heard of Abraham, but \[they\] affirme they receiue this name of Bramanes from Brammon, which was the first that euer exercised their Priestly function.”<sup>75</sup>

Postel’s theory gradually lost credibility but was still maintained by Isaac Newton in the early 18th century,<sup>76</sup> and by various Christian thinkers into the 19th century, and can be found on the internet today.

Menasseh ben Israel understood Latin and read widely in non-Jewish writings. The most likely source, then, of his own linking of Abraham linguistically with the Brahmans, and of “east” in [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] with India, was Postel’s theory, in original form or as rewritten and simplified by later authors. This means that the crucial link between this verse and India, between Judaism and Indian thought by way of this verse, entered Jewish discourse through Christian speculations on the subject.

What Menasseh did, however, in adopting a non-Jewish idea about the meaning of the verse, was to maintain alongside it the verse’s traditional Jewish association with impure names and Abravanel’s interpretation of the gifts as beneficial forms of knowledge. Menasseh altered the content of this knowledge, making it the profound truth of transmigration. Neither Abravanel nor Postel and other Christian writers had defined Abraham’s gifts in this way. That was the novelty of Menasseh’s interpretation, which he would have inferred after learning from Christian writers 

*[p. 159]* ^pg172

that “east” meant India and from Philostratus and Teixeira that Indians believed in transmigration, a doctrine he saw as originating in Judaism and fundamental to it.

Matityahu Glazerson (Jerusalem, 1990): 

Seeds of truth borrowed from Judaism

I conclude this Jewish history of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] with a book published in 1990 that opens with a (deficient) English translation of sentences from *Nishmat H. ayyim* that tell of Abraham’s sons bringing gifts of his knowledge to India.<sup>77</sup> This book, *From Hinduism back to Judaism*, was written by Rabbi Matityahu Glazerson, an Israeli who directs much of his teaching efforts toward *ba\`alei teshuvah*, Jews converting to Orthodoxy from a secular life or from other religions.<sup>78</sup> (I follow Glazerson’s use of “Hinduism” and “Judaism.”) In the book’s introduction, he speaks of Jews returning to “staunch observance of the Jewish faith after encounters with … Eastern schools of spiritual practice” and the failure of these Jews to attain “the total bliss promised to them by their mystic teachers.” Glazerson places the main thesis of his book into his paraphrase of what Jews returning from Hinduism learned: “We never knew that the Torah deals with all matters found in the Eastern teachings … and not only this, but it is our view now that Judaism is the source of the wisdom of the East.”<sup>79</sup>

The first part of this thesis, that Judaism possesses the resources to address all the issues that Asian religions address, occupies most of the book. Glazerson shows – with topics like absolute bliss, karma, self-discipline, higher consciousness, divine illumination, and inner contentment – that the same issues are addressed by Torah, Qabbalah, or (his main method of argument) the very form of Hebrew letters and the structure of its words, and that Judaism often teaches the same answer as Hinduism. Happiness and joy are the most important goals that he discusses. Defining the relationship between the two traditions, Glazerson uses the phrases, “same philosophy,” “similar to,” “comparable with,” “also found in,” and “common to both.” But this similarity lies for Glazerson in comparable goals and ideas rather than in the means of attaining them. Since Jews possess an innately different soul from that of non-Jews, “the Jewish soul … can attain happiness only through allegiance to the whole Torah and the 613 commandments.”<sup>80</sup> This is why meditation and other Eastern disciplines, though effective for non-Jews, cannot bring happiness to Jews.

Within certain chapters Glazerson uses this theory to portray Hinduism and Judaism as merely different means to similar goals, suited to different types of people. But when demonstrating the second part of his thesis, Hinduism’s dependence on Judaism, we learn that this deep distinction between Jews and other human beings corresponds to Glazerson’s view of 

*[p. 160]* ^pg173

a general and critical difference between the two religious traditions. Here [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] enters his argument. He speaks of the children of Keturah six times in the book. For example,

Avraham transmitted to his sons, from his wife Keturah, keys to understanding creation and the spiritual forces which are at work within the framework of nature.

… Avraham presented the sons of Keturah with wisdom in a form which could be used within the framework of nature and which was appropriate for their spiritual level.

… It is true that eastern religions attempt to bring man to a state of harmonious balance with the forces of nature, thus enabling him to promote the good in himself and in others. This method was bequeathed to Avraham’s sons by \[sic\] Keturah.

… Hindu concepts consist of those less advanced methods of implementation which Avraham communicated to Keturah’s sons in order that their binds on the material world be lessened.<sup>81</sup>

In all four of these examples, Abraham’s gifts to Indians were a “less advanced” knowledge or a meditative technique suited to a lower spiritual level limited to “the framework of nature.” These statements show Glazerson’s ultimate theory of how the two religious traditions differ. “While the Hindu disciple is taught to identify with the flow of nature to achieve innocence, a Jew does so only by elevating himself above nature through keeping the Torah and *mitzvot* \[commandments\] …. The laws governing the Torah and the Jewish soul totally contradict the logical and natural flow of events.”<sup>82</sup> Glazerson believes that Hinduism focuses on gaining happiness through moral behavior and harmony with nature, whereas Judaism focuses on higher worlds “above nature” and seeks to influence the state of the universe as a whole (as understood by Qabbalah) rather than directly helping other human beings.

Like Menasseh and Postel, Glazerson uses the linguistic similarity between “Abraham” and “Brahman” to support his claim of Jewish influence, but Glazerson extends this linguistic argument to dozens of Sanskrit terms. For example, he thinks the word “Veda” resembles the word *yada\`*, knowledge, in Hebrew, and that the Sanskrit word *tamas*, impurity, resembles the Hebrew word of comparable meaning, *tame\`*.<sup>83</sup> In a more complicated example, Glazerson links the Sanskrit word *Prana* to the divine emanation (*sefirah*) called *Tiferet* in Qabbalah, and defines *Tiferet* as the force that “enables man to harness the hidden upper powers.” Based on this linguistic word play (the “p” and “r” in both *Prana* and *Pa’ar*, another form of *Tiferet*), Glazerson concludes that “the attribute of *Pa’ar* in creation was confided by Abraham to his sons by Keturah, who \[the sons\] called it *Prana*.”<sup>84</sup> As he writes elsewhere, he believes that “Hebrew is the source of all languages.”<sup>85</sup>

*[p. 161]* ^pg174

Glazerson asserts repeatedly that various Hindu concepts and names “have their source in,” “are derived from,” “stem from,” or are “based on” Judaism. Then extending this claim to all the world’s philosophies, he asserts, “We have attempted to isolate certain details that shed light on the wisdom of Judaism as the well from which other cultures of the world draw their ideologies” – cultures which “only have the seeds of truth which were taken from Judaism.”<sup>86</sup> Glazerson’s idea implies that non-Jewish societies possess valid truths but these are derivative and deficient.

His final view appears at book’s end as a statement of Judaism’s absolute superiority: “While both Judaism and Hinduism maintain the importance of closeness to G-d, only the path of G-d’s Torah reveals to humanity the true and therefore the best way to come near to him.”<sup>87</sup> This is Glazerson’s fundamental comparison between Hinduism and Judaism, based on effectiveness in attaining closeness to God.

But [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] did not originally carry the meanings that Glazerson has given it. This chapter has shown that it took on its Indian connection only in rather recent times and with Christian influence. The original meaning of the verse, in its biblical context, had nothing to do with India or Brahmans. Nor did the medieval interpretations of the verse, which understood Abraham’s gifts to Keturah’s sons as powers of demonic magic. The Zohar in the 13th century gave this magic a more sinister and powerful quality, while also interpreting Abraham’s gifts as an alluring but distorted wisdom that had turned idolatrous. Abravanel in the early 16th-century reinterpreted [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] to give value to Abraham’s gifts, which were the secular sciences – valuable but imperfect. Finally, in the middle of the 17th-century Menasseh, influenced by Postel or other Christian authors, decided that the destination of Abraham’s sons was India, that the gifts were magical techniques and the truth of transmigration, and that the Brahmans had learned this from Abraham’s children. This meaning of [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] is certainly not the dominant interpretation among Jews today, but it has become one significant vehicle through which numerous Orthodox Jews conceptualize Judaism’s relationship with the Hindu and Buddhist traditions. It construes Hindu ideas and spirituality as partially valid but ultimately derivative and incomplete. In Glazerson’s case, the verse supports his claim for Hinduism’s dependence upon Judaism and for Hinduism’s inferior knowledge and practices, since Abraham’s gifts to Keturah’s sons were inferior to his gift to Isaac (the Jews).

Menasseh’s view of Indian beliefs combines the themes of magic and philosophy seen earlier in this book – magical techniques for controlling demons and the important doctrine of reincarnation. His Indians still lack direct revelation but through Abraham’s children they now receive certain sparks of it. Glazerson imagines a truncated form of Jewish revelation that provided the entire structure of Hindu philosophy and practice. Ha-Levi’s idea of the derivative nature of Indian science becomes in this chapter the derivative nature of Indian philosophy.
