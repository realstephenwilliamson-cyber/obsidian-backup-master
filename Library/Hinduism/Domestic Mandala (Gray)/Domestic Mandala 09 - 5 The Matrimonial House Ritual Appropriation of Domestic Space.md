## 5 The Matrimonial House: Ritual Appropriation of Domestic Space

‘After all, life was right and the architect wrong.’ 

Attributed to Le Corbusier in P. B. Jones, *An Anthropological View of Architecture* 

Kholagaun Chhetris build their houses as mandalas through a mixture of intentional design and conventional social and ritual conduct. Room layout and building practices incorporating what they consider to be ‘vastu rules’ orient the house and the worship room to the cardinal directions and ritual performances accompanying each stage of house are deliberate actions aimed at transforming the domestic compound into an auspicious yantra. Everyday domestic activities, such as the handling and cooking of rice to maintain its purity, customary spatial prohibitions limiting the access of non-household members into increasingly interior spaces, and the planned location of the kitchen in a place inaccessible visually and physically to the impurity and danger of lower caste people, witches and disembodied ghosts, together construct the domestic compound as a concentric mandala with the kitchen as its pure centre. Not just the result of intentional and conventional conduct, domestic spaces shape and give meaning to that conduct. They are auspicious places for carrying out everyday domestic chores, social relations and rituals so that they are in harmony with the cosmos and portend prosperity to the household group. They are also maps and microcosms of the cosmos that serve as instruments for knowledge as Kholagaun Chhetris move through them in the conduct of everyday social life. This multifaceted nature of domestic space is indicative of the more general point that the relation between space, activities and experience in Kholagaun Chhetri social life is internal and mutually constitutive rather than linear and causal. 

This chapter adds another nuance to this multifaceted relation by highlighting the way in which the performance of ritual appropriates the mandalic configuration of domestic space in achieving ritual projects and engendering experiences of those projects that link them to the ideas of attachment and detachment. In developing this argument, I build upon themes from the previous two chapters. In Chapter 3, I focused on the rituals that accompany the construction of houses and described how, in the performance of them, Kholagaun Chhetris configure their houses as auspicious yantras. Here, to reiterate the subtitle of that chapter, there is a ‘ritual creation of domestic space’. I now show the converse relation between ritual and domestic space: that is, I describe how the configuration and meaning of domestic 

*[p. 114]* ^pg131

space as a mandala—in both the yantric and mandalic modes—is incorporated into ritual performances shaping and enhancing their transformative effects. 

One of the main themes of the previous chapter was Kholagaun Chhetris’ use of spatial inclusions and exclusions to protect their food and their bodies from impurity and danger and how these spatial prohibitions build their compounds into a concentric mandala. These protective strategies are aimed at outsiders who are not members of the household group and whose bodily corruption, as manifest in their impurity and danger, cannot be controlled or neutralized by household members. In the following, I continue with Kholagaun Chhetris’ concern with the access of outside people to the increasingly pure and protected interior of their houses, but now in relation to wives of the men who form the patrilineal core (santan) of the household group. Kholagaun Chhetri men usually marry women who are outsiders: they come from other household groups of other Chhetri lineages residing in other villages. Upon marriage, they become members of their husbands’ lineages, take up residence in their husbands’ houses and gain access to the pure and vulnerable centre of their domestic mandalas. The ritual performances that bring about these social and spatial transformations of marriage take place in and assimilate the domestic mandala. 

Marriage 

Marriage is Janus-faced in different ways for the groom and bride. From the perspective of the groom and his household group, a wife’s sexual and procreative power (shakti) enables the continuation of her husband’s patriline through the sons she bears. At the same time, in-marrying women begin their married lives as outsiders to the husband’s household group. Experience shaped by cultural stereotypes suggest to the husband’s household group that the shakti of these outsider wives can also potentially be used to bring misfortune when it is exercised maliciously to cause illness or selfishly to cause the dissolution of the household group (see Gray 1982, 1995; Bennett 1983:182-83). So, their loyalty to their husband’s household group is something that needs to be established. 

From the perspective of the bride, it is only in marriage that she can realize her selfhood and gain prestige as a mother and senior member of a household group (see Bennett 1983). Unlike Chhetri men who move into the Householder stage of life through Bartaman, a rite of passage specifically performed for that purpose, women become Householders through marriage, that is, by becoming the spouse of a Householder (cf. Kondos 2004:7). In addition, the village of the husband is usually some distance away, so that a married woman leaves her natal home where she is a pure and divine sister (chelibeti) and joins the household of her husband and resides in his house where she is a subordinate wife subjected to the authority, surveillance and criticism of her mother-in-law. 

As a result, marriage for the bride and groom as well as their families is a joyous occasion tinged with sadness and danger. It involves the principal 

*[p. 115]* ^pg132

transformation of the man and woman from unmarried to married as well as three other entailed transformations: from equality to hierarchy; from ascetic abstinence to active reproductive sexuality of the bride; from the bride’s membership in her natal household group and lineage to her incorporation into her husband’s lineage and household group. The change of residence and physical movement of the bride from her natal house to her husband’s house is simultaneously a consequence and the major symbol of marriage and its complex of transformations. As an important part of the marriage performance, movement from the bride’s to the groom’s house is an embodied spatial experience through which marriage and its consequences for the bride and groom are known and understood. 

*From Equality to Hierarchy* 

The majority of marriages that take place among Kholagaun Chhetris are ‘arranged’. I am including here both the ‘traditional’ form of arranged marriages in which the parents of the bride and groom have searched for and mutually found suitable spouses for their child prior to their meeting and what might be called a contemporary modified form in which the boy and girl have found each other and the parents then arrange the marriage *post facto*.<sup>1</sup> The formal arrangement of marriage is an explicit recognition by both families that bride and groom are of the same caste and that there is no danger of the permanent impurity. 

When they discussed arranging marriages for their children, Kholagaun Chhetris usually stated a ‘quasi-juridical rule’ (Bourdieu 1990a:60) of lineage exogamy: spouses should not be members of each other’s father’s, mother’s or father’s mother’s patrilines. Prior to marriage there should be no traceable kinship links between the Kholagaun Chhetri lineage and that of the spouse. This is important because kinship relations always entail hierarchy. Marriage creates affinal relations in which the wife-giving lineage becomes subordinate to the wife-receiving lineage. This form of hierarchy is not based upon differential purity but upon the obligation to respect (*mānnu pārne*).<sup>2</sup> In these terms, Chhetri marriage is a process in which relations of equality as unrelated caste members are transformed into relations of hierarchy between kinfolk (*nāto*) (see Gray 1980). 

Such asymmetry and obligations to pay respect have a dramatic effect on relations between the members of the two household groups. During the first years after marriage, a woman and her husband make frequent visits to her natal home where she remains a divine daughter (Kondos 2004:13-14), where she gets some respite from the constant chores and disciplining authority of her mother-in-law and where she is pampered by her father, mother, brothers and their wives with her favourite foods and comfortable social relations. Her husband, however, is treated with great respect and formality as an honoured guest. Special food is prepared and gifts are offered to him and for him to take back to his father and mother. Throughout the visit there is a general air of formality and deference because of the presence of the husband and a sense of relaxation and relief for the wife as she enjoys the easy company of her natal family. The wife’s kin rarely, if ever, visit 

*[p. 116]* ^pg133

their daughter in her conjugal home since this would cause discomfort and embarrassment by confounding the honour and respect due to a guest and their subordinate status as wife-giving affines. 

*From Sexual Asceticism to Sexual Passion* 

Marriage also entails a transformation in and transference of a woman’s procreative power (shakti). Prior to marriage, while living in her natal household and a member of her father’s patrilineage, a woman is expected to be virgin, a state of ascetic detachment in which her shakti is ascetic and asexual. To her father and brothers, she is a ‘chelibeti’, a pure, divine and beneficent being: ‘chelibeti are equivalent to gods and so are deserving of worship’. Her shakti participates in the continuance of her natal lineage through the ritual of *Bhāi Tikā* that occurs on the fifth and last day of the festival of *Tihār* celebrated in the month of *Kārtik* (October/November) (see Kondos 2004:9). Married sisters return to their natal houses and join their unmarried sisters in blessing their brother\[s\]. In performing Bhai Tika, a walnut, the idol of the Yama, the god of death, is placed on the sill of the main entrance. Sisters then construct a protective ring—to ward off Yama—around their brothers with water poured through sacred grass (*kus* or *darbha*) and flowers, both of which have an everlasting quality suggesting immortality. Kholagaun Chhetri men told me that for them the most important part of the ensuing ritual is the blessing by their sister: it consists of placing an ornate tika on her brother’s forehead and bestowing upon him the following blessing: 

May you \[my brother\] live as long as these flowers do. As long as the sun and moon remain in the sky, as long as the earth and the oceans remain, as long as truth and religion prevail, and as long as Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva remain, may you earn fame and may your line of descendants \[santan\] never break. 

After marriage, a woman becomes a member of her husband’s patrilineage and her shakti becomes sexual and passionate with ambivalent potential. On the one hand, Kholagaun Chhetris say that it is a wife’s duty to discipline and dedicate her sexuality to the reproduction of her husband’s patrilineage through the birth of sons. On the other hand, older women in Kholagaun often told me how young wives use their sexuality to entice the loyalties of their husbands away from their mothers and the joint family household group and more exclusively towards their wives and children. Blaming the wife for such sexual treachery is the most common explanation given in Kholagaun for the sometimes acrimonious break-up of household groups.<sup>3</sup> Wives’ undisciplined sexuality is thought to drain their husbands of their strength; and their shakti can be diverted into a more dangerous form because it can provide the power to curse and cause harm, particularly to their husbands and/or sons. Several women in Kholagaun, who had borne sons and who now had daughters-in-law living in their houses, told me that one of the functions of the scrupulous observance of practices of deference and submission in carrying 

*[p. 117]* ^pg134

out chores demanded of a new wife in her husband’s household is to discipline her public social relations with members of the household. This is based on the hope that such public restraint will also discipline her private sexual relations with her husband so that her shakti is controlled and channelled for the benefit of his household group and patrilineage of which she is now a member. 

*From Natal Home to Husband’s House* 

Accompanying the changes in kinship and sexuality, marriage entails a spatial transformation. A woman relocates from the house where she was born and raised to her husband’s house in another village.<sup>4</sup> This is transformation of movement as well as membership. In the performance of the wedding rituals, the groom travels from his domestic mandala to the bride’s where the nuptials take place; then he returns with his bride to his domestic mandala as a member of his household group. The change of residence is particularly moving for the bride who is usually in tears during the rites of departure from her house. The physical movement over the intervening space and relocation of the bride to her husband’s house is not just the culmination of the other transformations—from a divine, auspicious, asexual and cherished daughter to a subordinate, sexual and potentially dangerous wife—and the mixed emotions entailed by them, it also encapsulates and epitomizes them for the bride, groom and their respective household groups. Marriage is more than a rite of figurative passage between social states. It is also a rite of literal passage between domestic spaces in which bride and groom experience their change of social state through their bodies’ spatial movement and entrance into the pure and vulnerable centres of their respective domestic mandalas. 

Sacred Enclosure: A Mandala within a Mandala 

Before I proceed to the marriage rituals, there is one further aspect of the way in which the bride’s and groom’s domestic mandalas are spatially configured for marriage rites. In the courtyard of both the bride’s and groom’s compounds, a sacred enclosure called a *jagya* (Figure 5.1) is erected; in the centre of the jagya is the fire pit where a sacrificial rite called *hom* is performed. 

For high caste Hindus in Nepal, a person’s life on earth progresses through a number of transitions from conception to marriage. Kholagaun Chhetris recognize ten transitions (*das karma*), each of which marks a phase in a person’s physical and moral development and is accompanied by rites of passage called *samskāra*. In enumerating the stages of life, most villagers included a core set of transitions and their accompanying rites—name giving, first rice eating, shaving of the head, investiture of the sacred thread, engagement and marriage.<sup>5</sup> The common element of every samskara is hom, a sacrificial rite marking and providing the ritual power for the transformation (see Gray 1979). The sacrifice consists of an oblation to the gods by immolation on a sacred fire (*Agni*), said to be the ‘mouth of the gods’.<sup>6</sup> 

*[p. 118]* ^pg135

The ritual process of marriage is punctuated by three hom sacrifices. The first, *Grahasanti* hom, is for the auspicious and peaceful configuration of nine planets (*graha*) during the marriage. It is performed in the groom’s jagya at an auspicious time, usually several hours prior to his departure from the house. The second takes place in the bride’s jagya just prior to the nuptials performed in the bride’s kitchen. The third also takes place in the bride’s jagya and accompanies the post-nuptial rites. 

While constructed within the domestic mandala, the jagya is iterative and homologous to it in a number of ways. First, the spatial arrangements of ritual objects and sacred diagrams form a mandala. Like the yantric configuration of a house, the jagya is a four-sided polygon; but in this case it is a square—the appropriate shape for sacred space as opposed to a rectangle appropriate for mundane space—created and defined by four branches of a cherry tree (*painyu*)<sup>7</sup> placed upright in the ground forming its corners. A fifth branch placed along the eastern side functions to orient the whole jagya auspiciously to the cardinal directions (see Figure 5.1). Like the concentric configuration of a house, the powerful centre point of the jagya is a fire understood as a materialization of the god Agni.<sup>8</sup> There is a material homology between the kitchen fire and the sacrificial fire, and by extension between the domestic mandala and the jagya mandala, that is made in the way the sacrificial fire is lit. The officiating priest lights the hom fire in the jagya with a flame taken from the kitchen fire. In the concentric space surrounding the fire are the ritual paraphernalia: a sacred water 

Figure 5.1 Marriage jagya

named and located in each of the eight directions; idols of Dio, Ganesh and Kalash who are worshipped in preliminary rites; a branch of the sweet basil plant (tulasi) for Vishnu, sacred grass for Brahma; other leaf plates with rice, coins, fruits and flowers as offerings (*bheti*) to the gods; vessels containing the ghee, *charu* (a mixture of rice, barley and black sesamum) and leaves from five sacred trees (*panchapalab*) that are the sacrificial offerings to the hom fire. 

Finally, like the domestic mandala, the spatial relations and performative movement of ritual objects between the concentric zone surrounding the fire and the fire itself iterate the principles of the cosmos. Recall that in mandala spatiality the concentric space surrounding the centre represents the force of creation of the diversity of people and things in the world. The concentric zone of the jagya that surrounds and is juxtaposed to divine Agni contains ritual paraphernalia made from a diversity of everyday objects and prosperity: staple grains (rice, barley and sesame), money, cloth, water, flowers, fruits, leaves, grasses, brass vessels and plates, vessels made from leaves that both represent and provide a place for the planets and deities of the cosmos to inhabit during the ritual. The two kinds of offerings that are made to the hom fire are themselves metonyms of this diversity of objects that characterize the world. Charu is made with the product of human labour; its contains the staple of the diet—rice; and having enough rice for one’s family is a measure of worldly prosperity. Ghee is the product of the cow, whose fertility is the embodiment of prosperity and a manifestation of the Goddess in one of her diverse forms. In performing hom sacrifice, charu and ghee are moved from this outer zone inward toward Agni. By offering them to the fire, the outer form of the grains and ghee are destroyed in an act of transcendence of earthly form. The diversity that characterizes the world merges in the fire into a single existence beyond form. In this respect hom sacrifice is an iteration of the nature of religious knowledge that the mandala itself encodes and fosters through meditation on its form: movement inward to the centre point where diversity dissolves into a single unity. 

Describing the layout and meaning of the jagya and hom sacrifice is an extended preface to the analysis of the marriage rites. It serves to draw attention to the house–jagya homology and to re-state the importance of the mandala as a microcosm of and an instrument for harnessing the powers of the cosmos for worldly purposes—in this case the transformations of marriage to which I now turn.

Marriage: Rites, Space and Movement 

Chhetri marriage consists of a long and complex series of rites. The most important are carried out over two days, alternating between the bride’s and the groom’s houses. There are a number of excellent and detailed descriptions of marriage rites among high caste Hindus of Nepal (see, for example, Campbell 1976, Bennett 1983 and Daryn 2002). Their primary focus is on interpreting the symbolic meaning of the ritual paraphernalia and actions that produce the transformations of marriage. In the following, I take a different approach to ritual. Rather than ‘the modern conception of ritual as enacted symbol’ (Asad 1993:59), as a type of signifying or expressive behaviour to be interpreted, I treat marriage rituals as disciplined and scripted practices, ‘apt performances’, or ‘techniques of the body’ through which performers develop dispositions, virtues (Asad 1993:62) and knowledge. ‘In this conception, there could be no radical distinction between … activities that are expressive and those that are technical \[shape experience, develop dispositions, produce knowledge\]’ (Asad 1993:63, brackets added). As a result, in the following analysis of Kholagaun Chhetri marriage ritual, I highlight the performative dimensions of the rites, particularly the scripted pattern of spatial movements between the bride’s and groom’s domestic compounds, how they contribute to the transformations of marriage and how they develop and encapsulate Kholagaun Chhetris’ experience and tacit understanding of them. 

During the two days of the marriage celebrations, the participants move over structured space punctuated by carrying out a series of rites. In saying this I am highlighting three aspects of performance—rites, space, and movement—that are integrated in the ritual process (Figure 5.2). 

*Rites* 

The whole series of marriage rituals proceeds through the three stages of rites of passage: separation, margin or limen, aggregation (van Gennep 1960; Turner 1967). Moreover, there is a spatial dimension to this temporal process: the rites of separation, the rites of transformation during the liminal phase and the rites of aggregation which structure the temporal flow of marriage are all carried out in the centre of a mandala. 

*Purbāñga* \[1\] is a series of preliminary rites performed by the unmarried man and woman in their respective kitchens and they mark the formal beginning of marriage. (Numbers in brackets refer to the number rites punctuating the dashed lines in Figure 5.2.) They are rites of separation; they configure the place and the time as out of the ordinary. One of the most important of these rites is the installation of the seven Mother Goddesses and the ancestors in the kitchen. As a result, the kitchen’s everyday character as a place for cooking and eating becomes a place set apart for the worship of sacred beings and the performance of the nuptials. The ritual time following purbanga is also rendered out of the ordinary because the bride and groom are purified to such an extent that the normal 

*[p. 123]* ^pg140

obligation to postpone performing rituals in the event of pollution resulting from birth and death within their lineages is suspended. 

The rites performed by the new wife upon her arrival at her new husband’s house are rites of aggregation \[16\]. She consummates her transformation from a non-kin outsider to membership of her husband’s household group and lineage by worshipping his ancestors and handling rice in the kitchen: the food that is most vulnerable in the place that is most vulnerable to the impurity and danger from outsiders. 

In between, the bride and groom are in a liminal—no longer single but not yet married—and vulnerable state as evident by the concern of purbanga with ensuring a supra-normal level of purity to protect the bride and groom against any ensuing impurity of birth and death in their respective lineages. In this state, they perform three major transformative rites in the homologous centres of the bride’s kitchen and jagya: the nuptial rite of the ‘gift of the virgin’ \[7a\] that transforms them into married Householders takes place in the bride’s kitchen; the rite of ‘washing the feet’ \[7b\], through which the bride’s and groom’s lineage acquire the hierarchical disposition appropriate to their new affinal relation, also takes place in the bride’s kitchen; and the rite of the groom ‘placing vermilion in the bride’s hair’ \[8b\], by which he activates and takes possession of the bride’s procreativity and sexuality (shakti), takes place in the jagya in the bride’s courtyard. 

*Space* 

The marriage space within which these rites are performed reiterates the tripartite temporal structure of marriage time (see Figure 5.2). It consists of the two domestic mandalas of the groom and bride located in different villages and separated by an intervening space. This in-between space has a liminoid character. Relative to the highly structured domestic spaces that frame it, it is only rudimentarily structured. Outside the compounds of the bride and groom are their villages but, more importantly, between their villages are tracts of relatively undifferentiated agricultural land (see Figure 5.3). 

As we saw in the previous chapter, this in-between space outside the boundary of the house is populated by impure and dangerous beings whose bodily corruptions are indicative of excessive attachment to the world. As such, it is juxtaposed to and constitutive of the increasing pure and vulnerable concentric spaces of the domestic mandala. During marriage, this in-between space is ambiguous: one the one hand, it spatially separates the domestic mandalas of the two groups who are, prior to marriage, socially distant because there are no traceable kinship links between them; on the other hand, because participants travel across it as part of the marriage process, it is the spatial medium for connecting them socially. 

*[p. 124]* ^pg141

*Movement* 

Throughout the two days of performances, the participants move over this marriage space. They begin in the centre of the bride’s and groom’s respective domestic mandalas and end in the kitchen of the groom’s. (Refer to the dashed lines indicating movement over mandalic spaces in Figure 5.2.) In between, the groom and his wedding party move successively outward through the concentric zone of the groom’s domestic mandala, across the intervening space, successively inward then outward through the concentric zones of the bride’s domestic mandala, returning over the intervening space with the bride and then successively inward through the concentric zones of the groom’s domestic mandala to its centre for the final rites. Within this space of marriage there are two vectors of movement—outward departures and inward arrivals—relative to and punctuated by a third movement (or should I say a non-movement), the still centres of the groom’s and bride’s domestic mandalas. These spatial movements are not merely a neutral backdrop to the marriage rites; they are a performative assimilation of the domestic mandala into the rites. 

In Centre of the Domestic Mandalas 

Marriage begins in the kitchens of the bride’s and groom’s domestic mandalas with separate astrologically determined performances of the preliminary rites, Purbanga \[1\].<sup>10</sup> There are three features of the rites and ritual paraphernalia that are especially important to the transformative themes and performative movement of the marriage ritual: the worship of the seven Mother Goddesses, the worship of the ancestors and the presence of a sacred water vessel and a bundle of auspicious items. 

*Worship of the Mother Goddesses* (*Matrika Puja)* 

One of the major rites of purbanga is the worshipping the seven Mother Goddesses (*sapta matrika*) materialized in seven blobs of pure mud (*chokho māto*) or cow dung (*gobar*) stuck on the wall of the kitchen. The seven Mother Goddesses are a collective form of the Universal Goddess who Kholagaun Chhetri call ‘Devi’ and who for them encompasses the numerous individual female deities that are the diverse manifestations of shakti. Beside the myriad individual forms of the Goddess, there are two collective forms that together manifest the tantric duality of major deities in Nepal. One of these is a collective of virgins embodying the ascetic and pacific power of shakti. Collectives of virgins consist of an odd number of young girls<sup>11</sup> who usually lead processions, often involving circumambulations that demarcate a pure, sacred and auspicious space. We saw this earlier in the rituals inaugurating a new house. The opening rite is a procession, led by a calf (another worldly manifestation of Devi) and three or five virgin girls that circle and enter 

*[p. 125]* ^pg142

the house for the first time. The other collective materialization of Devi consists of Mother Goddesses (matrika)—the title indicating that they manifest the active and sexual power of shakti. Collectives of Mother Goddesses appear in contexts of guardianship and protection. The most well known in Nepal, particularly among the Newars of the Kathmandu Valley, are the *Asta Matrika*, the eight Mother Goddesses—the number eight linking them directly to the cardinal and intermediate directions, the sacred geography of the mandala and the creation of terrestrial space. The eight Mother Goddesses are used throughout the Kathmandu Valley to encircle, define and guard mandalas—whether cities, towns or temples—from the cardinal directions. The most spectacular example of such guardianship of mandalic space by the Asta Matrika is the series of three concentric rings of eight Mother Goddesses around Kathmandu (Gutschow and Bajracharya 1977 in Gellner 1992:190). There are similar protective encompassments by the Asta Matrika around the city of Bhaktapur (Levy 1990:155) and the Kathmandu Valley (Slusser 1982:344). 

Among Kholagaun Chhetris, the sapta matrika, the seven Mother Goddesses, is the more common collective manifestation of Devi appearing in rituals. Rather than space, the seven Mother Goddesses protect time, particularly the genealogical time marked by the succession of generations of agnates that constitute Chhetri lineages (*khalak*). The reasons it is seven Mother Goddesses who protect a lineage are: (1) the number seven is generally auspicious and (2) seven is the number of generations of descendants that Kholagaun Chhetri say define a lineage. For example, a principal theme of the boy’s initiation rite is entering the Householder stage of life when a man marries and begets children, the conditions of founding a lineage. The rite is punctuated by a number of hom sacrifices, one of which is explicitly about the role of deities in protecting a lineage. As explained to me by a presiding priest, the sacrificial offering to Agni by the initiate is a ‘request to the gods to take care of \[the boy’s\] khalak, the *seven* generations which will follow from him’.<sup>12</sup> 

Returning now to purbanga, worshipping the seven Mother Goddesses is a similar appeal for the protection of the lineages of the groom and bride as well as the lineage that the groom will found through the children born to him by his wife. 

*Worshipping the Ancestors* (*Pitri Puja*) 

Purbanga also includes a separate rite of commemoration and worship of the ancestors (pitri). The recent agnatic ancestors linking the groom and bride to their lineage—father, grandfather and great grandfather—are represented by three small leaf bowls containing a plait of everlasting kus grass. The worship, which consists of making offerings of leaf plates containing the essential contents of a meal (*sidha*), is said ‘to make the ancestors happy’, so that ‘no bad luck or harm befalls the young groom or bride’ during the wedding ceremonies.<sup>13</sup> The groom and bride are also inviting their ancestors to witness the important marriage rites, particularly those that will take place in the kitchens of their respective houses—the nuptials, 

*[p. 126]* ^pg143

the separation of the bride from her natal household group and lineage, and the incorporation of the bride into her husband’s household group and lineage which she will continue through the birth of future sons. 

*Sagun* 

The ritual set up for purbanga also includes a sacred water vessel and a bundle of auspicious items (*bhār*) consisting of sugar cane stalks, a bamboo basket filled with dried fish, spinach greens and sacred leaves of the *sal* tree (*perungo*), and a clay gourd (*théki*) of curd. They are important to purbanga because they are ‘for *sāgun*’ (‘omen’ or ‘portent’), that is, they suffuse the occasion with auspiciousness and portend a beneficent outcome. As we will see shortly, the water vessel and bundle of auspicious items are carried by members of the groom’s procession from the centre of the groom’s domestic mandala across the intervening non-mandalic space to and from the bride’s house; the corresponding water vessel and bundle of auspicious items (kalash/bhar) from the bride’s purbanga are carried outside her domestic mandala in the ritual of greeting the arrival of the groom’s procession.<sup>14</sup> 

Departures and Outward from the Kitchen 

In performing the series of marriage rites, outward movements occur in the two departures from the centres of the groom’s and bride’s domestic mandalas. Both involve a corresponding series of successive steps through the concentric zones—kitchen, verandah and courtyard—and into the external and dangerous in-between space for the journey. 

*The Groom’s Departure* 

The first departure is the groom’s \[rites 1-4 in Figure 5.2\]. It begins when he performs purbanga in his kitchen \[1\]. It continues the following day when he emerges dressed in new clothes from the interior of his house through the main entrance with Ganesh, the remover of obstacles, on his auspicious right-hand side. On the verandah, he receives a garland and a tika of blessing from his mother \[2\] for the auspiciousness of his journey and his marriage. He then moves into the courtyard where a band, his father and his male agnates have gathered to participate in the procession (*janti*) that accompanies him to the bride’s house. Prominent in the courtyard is a jagya where earlier in the day a fire sacrifice was performed by the household priests to ensure the planets are in a peaceful and auspicious state for the wedding. Now in the centre of the jagya is the palanquin in which the groom will be carried to the bride’s house. As he circumambulates the jagya \[3\], he is preceded by two men carrying the kalash/bhar from the previous day’s purbanga. After the circumambulation, he moves to the centre of the jagya, 

*[p. 127]* ^pg144

takes his seat in the palanquin where his virgin sisters offer him another form of sagun, curd and flowers, for the beneficence of the marriage. 

With a band playing in the lead, the groom is carried outward through the jagya and his domestic mandala crossing the boundary to the outside through the gate of the courtyard. He is accompanied by a procession consisting of several lower caste men from Kholagaun carrying gifts (*sāypati*) of clothes in a trunk for the bride, plates of spices and sweets for her family, the kalash/bhar followed by the groom’s father and men of his lineage. Because for the groom and his lineage marriage has the sense of ‘winning a bride from another village’, there is a triumphant mood to the journey and the accompanying music as the procession travels across an intervening space between his compound and the bride’s, which is usually located in another village \[4\] (see Figure 5.3).

The second outward movement involves the more sombre and poignant departure of the bride from her natal home with her new husband for the return journey to his house where the couple will take up residence \[rites 8-12 in figure 5.2\]. 

After the post-nuptial rites \[8\], the bride and groom return to the kitchen where they worship the ancestors and the Mother Goddesses established in her kitchen during her purbanga \[9\]. In this rite the bride bids farewell to her ancestors and her natal household group. In a ritual process homologous to that performed by the

In each of these steps outward, the participants are entering spaces of escalating risk of impurity from lower caste people, curses of witches and attacks of disembodied ghosts—embodiments of attachment—culminating in a space that might be called ‘non-mandala land’, that is, the space between the groom’s and bride’s houses. Each successive movement outward is accompanied by protective auspicious actions and objects: blessing with tika and garland by the parents at the main entrance \[2 and 10\]; propitious circling of the jagya in the courtyard accompanied by the kalash/bhar from the host’s purbanga \[3 and 11\]; departing from the domestic compound in a procession, again accompanied by the groom’s kalash/bhar \[4 and 12\]. As highly auspicious items, they are carried ‘to protect the travelers from any bad things or evil en route’.<sup>15</sup> In both departures it is the groom’s kalash/bhar that accompany the procession across the space between the two domestic mandalas because they are providing protection to the couple and the groom’s kin who make up the members of the procession.

Arrivals and Inward to the Kitchen 

As the processions approach their destinations, the two departures and their outward movements become two arrivals of corresponding successive steps inward through the concentric zones of the domestic mandala and into the pure centre where only household members are allowed, especially during the preparation and consumption of meals.<sup>16</sup>

*The Groom’s Arrival at the Bride’s House* 

Upon arrival in the bride’s village, the procession stops at a convenient place outside the bride’s domestic mandala, close to but out of direct sight of the bride’s courtyard \[5\]. In relation to the bride’s domestic mandala, this external location—outside the boundary of her compound but within the village—is the place of potentially dangerous outsiders: people who are not members of her household group and beings who might cause impurity and danger. 

The men who carried the gifts of clothes, spices and sweets walk to the bride’s house, enter the courtyard (a place that they can legitimately enter as emissaries of the groom) where the bride’s father is waiting \[5a\]. They place the gifts of clothes and food—items of prosperity and attachment—in the outer zone of the jagya surrounding the centre. Then, a small procession representing the bride’s family—led by a few close lineage mates, followed by lower caste men carrying the kalash/bhar from the purbanga performed the previous day in the kitchen of the bride’s house, the household priest and finally the bride’s father—exits the courtyard to the place where the groom and his procession are waiting. The bride’s father’s procession circumambulates the groom in the auspicious clockwise direction in an act of encompassment by his protective kalash/bhar. The bride’s father reciprocates the groom’s gift by formally and respectfully greeting (janti or *dulaha parsine*) the groom \[5b\]: he puts a tika of red paste on the groom’s forehead and sprinkles him with red powder. Now no longer outsiders, the bride’s father invites the groom and his procession into the courtyard of his domestic mandala where, on the morning of the wedding, a jagya was constructed.<sup>17</sup> 

The groom and his procession enter the courtyard where the Welcoming (*Barani*) is performed \[6\]. The kalash/bhar from the groom’s purbanga are placed in the jagya where they remain until the couple leave on the return journey to the husband’s house. Standing within the sacred space of the jagya, the bride’s father formally welcomes the groom to his into domestic compound. He purifies the groom’s body with a nectar of five pure liquids (*panchāmrit*) from a sacred yoni-shaped vessel (*argha*) by touching his stomach, hands, chest, shoulders and head, puts a tika on his forehead and offers him a flower garland and foods. These ritual acts replicate those used in worshipping deities which Kholagaun Chhetris liken to beckoning and hosting an honoured guest. After the formal welcome, he invites the groom onto the verandah, and, if his house is of the traditional style, offers him a seat on the raised platform reserved for guests who are close friends and kin. The groom is given food while members of the procession are fed in the courtyard. After Barani and the meal, there is a pause in the ritual action until the auspicious time (*lagan*) for the nuptials which take place in the kitchen. 

*The Bride’s Arrival and Entry into the Kitchen*<sup>18</sup> 

The bride’s arrival is both an emotionally and corporeally moving performance that begins in the courtyard—the zone of the domestic mandala where household 

*[p. 131]* ^pg148

members can visually inspect visitors before inviting them any further inward—when the procession arrives at the groom’s house. The bride, still veiled, is carried into the jagya where she dismounts and the kalash from her palanquin is placed in the jagya area. Then the groom dismounts and the bhar from his palanquin is put in the jagya area. The couple then sit in the jagya area for the *arti syāl*, the rite of welcoming the bride and groom \[13\]. The groom’s pure, auspicious unmarried virgin sisters start the transformation of the bride from outsider to household member by unveiling the bride, beholding her face, welcoming her with a tika of respect on her forehead, placing a flower garland around her neck and sprinkling her with puffed rice (*lāwā*) \[13\]. I was told that the meaning of the puffed rice derives from the size of the kernels. Since rice is botha measure and sign of prosperity, the exaggerated size of puffed rice kernels is an iconic portent of the fecundity of the bride and the concomitant prosperity she will bring to the household group. 

The bride then moves inward by stepping onto the verandah and approaching the main entrance to the house where a brief rite of ‘blocking the entrance’ takes place \[14\]. The groom’s sisters demand a promise of gifts to allow her to enter. Upon making the promise, the sisters step aside allowing the bride to enter the interior of the house. Both the arrivals involve people who begin the spatial inward movement as social ‘outsiders’, that is, non-members of a household group, and end up as social insiders. The groom moves through the concentric zones of the bride’s house to her kitchen where the formal rite of marriage takes place and he becomes a respected kin of the bride’s household group; after marriage at her house, the bride moves inward through the concentric zones of her husband’s house to his kitchen where she is incorporated into his household group. The rites that accompany these inward movements treat the groom and bride as increasingly socially close: from outsider to honoured guest to kin who are free to enter the kitchen where the transformations take place. The two movements of decreasing spatial and social distance are enhance by rites involving gift giving—the groom offering clothes and food to the bride’s family and the bride promising gifts to her husband’s sisters when the block her entry into his house. 

In his usual incisive style, Sahlins captures the role of gifts in not just expressing existing relationships but in actively creating them in a way that decreases the social distance between individuals or groups: ‘If friends make gifts, gifts make friends’ (1972:186). With the arrival of the bride, her promise of gifts to her husband’s sisters at the entrance to the house coordinates the spatial movement of the rites with her social movement towards closer relations with her husband’s kin. The same could be said to be happening with the arrival of the groom. He starts in a zone of potentially dangerous beings outside the bride’s compound, a location whose spatial distance from the centre of the compound iterates the social distance between the groom’s and the bride’s household groups and lineages. With 

*[p. 133]* ^pg150

each successive rite of gift giving, greeting and welcoming, the groom is transformed—through auspicious and purifying actions upon his body by the bride’s father—from outsider, to friend, to honoured guest and future husband of a daughter of the household group. In performing these rites, the groom enters zones of a decreasing mandalic distance from the centre—outside the compound, to courtyard, to verandah. And again this movement in mandalic space has a double significance: the decreasing social distance between the groom and his kin on the one hand and the bride and her kin on the other is at once depicted and experienced. At the end of this social and spatial progression, the groom is fit to enter the bride’s kitchen for the nuptial. 

In the Centre of the Domestic Mandala 

*In the Bride’s Centres* 

The nuptials take place in the pure kitchen of the bride’s house \[7\] where they are witnessed by the bride’s ancestors materialized in the balls of cow dung installed during the previous day’s purbanga. The first is the ‘gift of the virgin’ (kanya dan) when the woman is given in marriage to the groom by her father and mother. In attendance are the household priests of the bride and groom as well as their parents. The bride and groom sit on a bed-like platform with the bride on the groom’s right indicative of her auspiciousness as a pure and divine virgin. With the bride and her father together holding a leaf plate with sacred grass and water and with the groom cupping his hands beneath, the bride’s mother pours sacred water over her husband’s and daughter’s clasped hands and plate so that it flows into the cupped hands of the groom. The sensual experience of the rite’s meaning is striking: the custody, care and shakti of the woman flows from her father to her husband’s hands. Except for the chanting of the officiating priests, nothing more is or needs to be said. Kanya dan has two consequences: first the woman becomes a wife and member of the groom’s lineage; and second, as a result, her father’s lineage becomes subordinate to the groom’s lineage—a status transformation marked immediately afterwards in a rite called ‘washing of the feet’ (*gonra dhune*) in which members of the bride’s lineage enact their subordination by washing the feet—the most impure part of the body—of the bride and groom and sipping the water. 

A short time after the nuptial, the ritual action moves to the jagya in the courtyard, a mandalic space which I argued earlier is homologous to the kitchen. The groom and bride, dressed in vibrant red, the colour of active sexuality, sit sideby-side on the southern side of the jagya where a large crowd has assembled to watch the newly-married couple perform a series of post-nuptial rites—some playful some serious—accompanied by hom offerings.<sup>20</sup> In these rites, the bride becomes a sexual woman and a subordinate wife \[8\]. 

*[p. 134]* ^pg151

The change in the bride’s status is marked by the change in her position relative to her husband. At the beginning of these post-nuptial rites, she sits to her husband’s right, the relative bodily orientation indicative of her purity, auspiciousness and high status. The transformation begins with Sila puja \[8a\] in which the couple circle the jagya to the northern side where a grinding stone (*silāuto* or *silā*) and stone rolling pin (*lohoro*) have been placed, perform puja to the grinding stone, the bride places her right toe on the grinding stone and the groom touches it in an acknowledgement of her pre-marital purity as a virgin. The next rite called ‘placing the vermilion’ \[8b\], is the turning point: the groom puts some red powder in the part of her hair. In Nepal this is the sign that a woman is married. In one marriage I attended, the bride began weeping during this ceremony because, according to a woman bystander, by putting the powder in her hair she became a subordinate wife.<sup>21</sup> Immediately afterwards, the brides sits to the groom’s left, the relative bodily orientation of inauspiciousness and subordination. The final rite is the most dramatic expression of the bride’s change of status. She eats the impure leftover food from the groom’s plate in a rite called ‘eating impurity’ \[*jutho khuāune*\] \[8c\]. Here the bride embodies her husband’s impurity and her consequent subordination in an act that will be repeated almost everyday of their married life: a wife usually waits until after her husband has finished to eat her own meal; if there are any leftovers, she mixes them with her food. 

The groom is then instructed to give his wife a personal name ‘so that they would be able to call her by using that name’. Naming is more than nominalism. Soon after birth, Chhetris are given two types of names (*nām*). One is bestowed upon the child on the eleventh day after birth in a rite called nwaran or *namakarma*. For this rite, the household priest determines the place of the child in the cosmic order by constructing an astrological chart based upon the position of the planets at the time of birth. This chart foretells the child's dharma, personality and life-trajectory and indicates which letter of the alphabet is appropriate as the first letter of the child’s name. On this basis, the priest bestows a formal personal name on the child which reflects its fate. This name is referred to as the child's ‘nwaran nam’ and is normally used only for official purposes and for official documents. Most Chhetris also have what was known as a ‘calling name’ (‘*bolaunu nam’*) which is given to them by their parents a few weeks after birth. The delay allows the parents to select a name that reflects some characteristic that they detect in the child. Personal names reflect the person’s being either derived ontologically from the astrological position of the planets at the time of birth or socially from the nature of the intimate relation between the child and its parents. Both the formal name and the calling name are given to the child in his or her natal household, are based on events and relations occurring there, and are thus intimately associated with the identity of the person as a member of the natal household. Bestowing a new name on the bride is proactive rather than reflective because it is an act of subordinating her will (see Parkin 1984:361), another step in the subsumption of her identity in her husband, her life-trajectory in her husband’s 

*[p. 135]* ^pg152

household group and her concomitant separation from her natal household group who use the name that her parents gave her at birth.<sup>22</sup> 

*In the Groom’s Kitchen* 

Upon arrival in the centre of her husband’s domestic mandala, the bride is incorporated into his household group and lineage, and concomitantly gains access to the kitchen, through the performance of four rites. She worships the deities and matrikas invoked in the groom’s purbanga \[16a\]. After this, daughter-in-law (*buwāri*) and mother-in-law (*sāsu*) perform a playful rite in which the new daughter-in-law displays her skills in handling rice—the archetypical domestic activity indicative of membership \[16b\]. She and her mother-in-law then entwine their hair \[16c\] which is, as Kholagaun Chhetri women explained to me, a sensual expression and practice of one of the most important features of the sasu-buwari relation: the cooperation and unity of these women in carrying out household chores typified by the preparation and consumption of food in the kitchen. The bride’s incorporation into her husband’s household group culminates in the rite of ‘seeing her face’ (*mukh herne*) when the new mother-in-law lifts the veil of her daughter-in-law who in return performs *khutta dhog*—the sensual practice of subordination and acceptance of her social place within the household group—by touching her forehead, the purest part of her body, to the mother-in-law’s feet, the most impure part of her body \[16d\]. 

Babb (1981) provides a more extended interpretation of this ritual interchange of seeing the face and touching the forehead to the feet. 

Our main concern is not with feet but with eyes. But to understand divine eyes, we must understand the feet—or rather what they symbolize—as well. It is not a matter of feet in themselves, but of the ideas they powerfully evoke in the Hindu world. How is it that the gods come to look kindly on their human worshippers? The answer seems to be that it is above all surrender that invokes the ‘glance of compassion’. Therefore, it is at, under, and even within the lord’s feet that shelter is to be found, and in this sense to touch his feet is to control of power of his eyes. More simply, if one wishes to be seen beneficially by a deity, one had better be below the deity \[bodily and socially\] (1981:396, words in parenthesis added). 

While his concern is relations between deities and worshippers, the meanings he describes for the gestures of seeing and touching the feet work perfectly well—substitute mother-in-law and daughter-in-law in the appropriate places in the above quotation—with the relationship that is being given form to and embodied between the daughter-in-law and mother-in-law.

Ritual Performance, Embodied Knowledge and Revelation 

The performers of these ‘rites of the centre’ engage the domestic mandala as representational (as ‘a map of the cosmos’ (Tucci 2001:23)) and as revelatory (as ‘a machine to stimulate inner visualization’ (Zimmer 1972:141)) and in their performance, the spatial and social are rendered homologous so that each ‘gives form to’ the other (Fabians 1990:11). Like the centre of a mandala, marriage is a union and transcendence of worldly diversity in a number of senses—of groom and bride, of diverse unrelated lineages, of male and female, and of passive male and active female \[shakti\] principles. The union is achieved through the ritual actions and gestures of the nuptial rite of ‘the gift of the virgin’ performed in the bride’s kitchen, the centre of her domestic mandala. Recall that in this rite, the bride’s mother pours water over the clasped hands of father and daughter into the cupped hands of the groom. The continuous stream of water not only depicts the transfer of the bride from her father’s care and joins her with the groom, but father and daughter, bride and groom also sensuously feel this transference and union as the water flows over the skin of their hands and into the cupped hands of the groom. Simultaneously, like the centre of a mandala, the union of male and female contains the energy of creation in the birth of children and the continuation of the husband’s lineage that is activated in the rite of placing the red powder in the part of the bride’s hair. This spatial positioning of the performers in the kitchen is more than just a representation of marriage; the performance brings their bodies into the centre of the mandala and they dwell in it. Performers’ bodies and performing space become a single entity, an experience that results from the confluence of giving form to the centre in embodied ritual actions and the complex of meanings the centre makes present to the performers. 

A mandala is also revelatory through movement over its organization of spaces, pictures and geometric patterns. I have identified in Kholagaun Chhetri marriage ritual two episodes of inward movements, one toward the bride’s and the other towards the groom’s kitchen. The latter that is most important because the inward movement and the rites of the bride’s incorporation into her husband’s household group culminate and encapsulate all the transformations of marriage that previous rites and movements have accomplished. Moving towards and into the kitchen on piles of rice like the goddess Laxshmi, worshipping the ancestors as her own, and handling rice with her mother-in-law are all indicative of the bride’s marriage and new status as a member of her husband’s household group with access to the vulnerable centre of the house. In this ritually orchestrated movement to the mandalic point of stillness in the centre, the bride gives form to and grasps through her body her social movement to the point of her social stillness in and union with her husband’s house where she will live out the rest of her life. 

Because the kitchen’s purity and central location in the mandala link the everyday with the cosmological, her inward movement is also her entry into the life of the Householder. The bride enters the auspicious house where she and her husband hope to prosper through discharging their sacred duties as Householders 

*[p. 137]* ^pg154

of begetting children, feeding the ascetics and performing sacrifice to the deities. Fulfilling these duties motivates a life characterized by the necessity of creation, passion, and attachment to children (who epitomize the diversity of people), to the land that produces food for subsistence and material wealth (which epitomizes the diversity of material things) to beneficent deities (who epitomize the diversity of deities that personify the diversity of qualities). Again, in this movement to the centre of the domestic mandala, the bride gives form to and experiences with her body the moral sensibility of the Householder’s enigmatic life-in-the-world: that worldly diversity is an illusion and that detachment enables revelation, dissolving the illusion and ultimately gaining release from the round of death and rebirth. ‘An experience of the body becomes a moment in an experienced (taught) body’ (Asad 1993:77). 

Finally, the kitchen as centre has a special revelation for the bride. It is her activated sexuality, and the passions it arouses in her, her husband and mother-inlaw, that can lead to the excessive attachments—wife to husband, parent to child—and that may cause the disruption of social relations and the fragmentation of the household group. The evil witch using her shakti to curse her husband or son is a portrayal of the consequences of undisciplined shakti and the depravity of excessive attachment it causes. Against this background, the bride performs the rites of her incorporation—worshipping his ancestors, handling rice with his mother, and showing her face—into her husband’s household in the cultural purity and cosmological stillness/potency of the kitchen. In doing so, active body and meaningful space, worshipper and mandalic centre, become co-present and consubstantial in the bride’s embodied revelation of the transcendence of attachment though a disciplined sexuality characterized by asceticism and detachment.

Agni forms the centre of a sacred space formed by an octagonal yantra drawn with three concentric lines in a white-red-white pattern. The octagonal yantra design surrounding the fire is formed by superimposing a square rotated ninety degrees on top of another square. According to Danielou, such an octagonal design is the Ganapati Yantra which ‘points at the identity of the macrocosm and the microcosm’ (1991:360).

14 There is perhaps another implicit dimension to the significance of the kalash. I described in Chapter 3 the core significance of a kalash is its auspiciousness and purity but it can also take on additional meaning and functions in specific ritual contexts. For example, in the rite establishing house foundations (Jag Puja) there are kalash in each corner into which the gods of the four directions are invoked. There is also some mercury in each kalash so that the vessel also functioned to ward off snakes from undermining the foundations of the house with their wriggling. In Purbanga, the kalash continues its ritual function as the material form in which deities are invoked and become manifest to humans. This time it is the protective Mother Goddesses who are thought to be embodied by the kalash (see Daryn 2002:112) for the duration of the two days of marriage rites. 

15 The bhar and kalash always accompany movement outward from the kitchen where they are established in purbānga. They accompany the groom on his journey to the bride’s house; they are placed in the jagya at the bride’s house for the duration of the rites performed in her domestic mandala; and they accompany the bride and groom on the journey from her domestic mandala to the groom’s domestic mandala. In addition, the bhār and kalash established in the bride’s house during her purbānga accompany her father outward from his domestic mandala when he goes to greet the groom and his procession outside his compound upon their arrival in the village. 

16 As I described in the previous chapter, the interior of the house is protected by both the architectural location of the kitchen far away from the main entrance to block visual access and by social prohibitions against the entry by people who are not members of the household group. 

17 The courtyard is visually and physically the most publicly accessible zone within the domestic mandala. It is the place where people of all castes can enter upon invitation for casual visiting or carrying out tasks such as tailoring, carpentry, and winnowing rice. It is the appropriate zone for the jagya because the post-nuptial rites conducted around and within it require the participation of and are eagerly viewed by a large number of people—lower caste members of the groom’s procession, the groom’s lineage mates, the bride’s father’s agnates and people of the bride’s village from a variety of castes—many of whom would not be allowed to enter the interior of the house. It is also the appropriate place for the next stage in the transformation of the groom into an honoured guest. 

18 The rites that accompany the bride’s arrival, inward movement and entry into the groom’s kitchen after their marriage are more elaborate than the groom’s entry into the bride’s kitchen prior to their marriage because the bride’s arrival culminates and encapsulates all the transformations of marriage.

22 When addressing one another, a husband and wife avoid using personal names as well as these kinship terms for spouse, husband and wife. When I talked with Kholagaun Chhetri men about the names of their wives, they said that they ‘felt ashamed’ to call their wives by their personal names; many claimed that they did not even remember it! Instead they said there are two alternatives a man uses in addressing his wife. One is the name of the village in which his wife was born, augmented by a feminine suffix. In using this form of address, the husband emphasized that the woman was by birth an outsider to the village, to the household and to its core *santan*. The other alternative form of address is the use of the teknonym ‘mother of (son’s name)’. Together the two alternative modes which Silwal men used in addressing their wives are practices in which is embedded their doubleness. They are outsiders whose loyalty to their husband’s household and *santan* was suspect because they continued to maintain important social relations with their natal households, especially with their brothers. In giving birth to sons they are essential for the fulfilment of the husband’s *dharma* and the realization of his socially constituted interests: i.e. the ideal joint family, the continuity of his *santan*, lineage and clan. Moreover, in Hindu thought a woman only fulfils her womanhood in marriage and motherhood—that is, roles which were defined in relation to her husband, his *santan*, and his *dharma*.
