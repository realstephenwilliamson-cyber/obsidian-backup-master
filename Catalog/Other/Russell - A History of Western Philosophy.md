## About

Bertrand Russell's (1872–1970) one-volume survey of Western philosophy from the Pre-Socratics to his own time, which sets each philosophy against the political and social circumstances of its age. It grew out of lectures and became a commercial success that has stayed in print.

- **Origin:** lectures on the history of philosophy at the Barnes Foundation in Philadelphia, 1941–42. Russell wrote it between 1940 and 1943, mostly in Pennsylvania; his third wife, Patricia, did much of the historical research.
- **1945–46:** published in the US by Simon & Schuster in 1945 and in Britain by George Allen & Unwin in 1946; the British editions dropped the opening "A". The SEP dates it 1945; the Nobel presentation speech cites it as 1946.
- **Contents:** three books, with chapters mostly on single philosophers, schools or periods, each major division opening with its historical background. It runs from the Pre-Socratics through the Church Fathers and Schoolmen to Kant, Hegel, Marx and Dewey, and ends with a chapter on logical analysis that states Russell's own view.
- **Later editions:** corrections went into the British first edition and the reset 1961 edition but not the American text, which still misdates Spinoza's birth. A Folio Society edition (2004) has an introduction by A. C. Grayling.
- **Reception:** mixed among academics, which dismayed Russell. George Boas (1947) said he never decided between history and polemic; Frederick Copleston found it lively but in parts inadequate and misleading; Einstein and Schrödinger praised it. Grayling (2004) said it was never a standard textbook.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says it was cited as one of the books that won Russell the 1950 Nobel Prize in Literature, and that it gave him financial security. The presentation speech names it first in a short list of his books; the SEP says Russell believed the prize was mainly for *Marriage and Morals*. The SEP counts it, with *The Problems of Philosophy*, as one of his two best-sellers.
