## About

The Mishnah tractate Avot: ethical maxims of the rabbinic sages, read in the liturgy with an added sixth chapter. The name is usually rendered "Chapters of the Fathers"; Wikipedia notes that avot can also mean fundamental principles.

- **Place in the Mishnah:** in the order Nezikin. Wikipedia calls it the second-to-last tractate of that order; the Jewish Encyclopedia (Moses Mielziner) calls it the last of the order's nine. Wikipedia describes it as the only tractate dealing solely with ethics. It has no Gemara.
- **Sages and dates:** chapter 1 opens with a chain of tradition from Moses at Sinai to the Men of the Great Assembly (the Jewish Encyclopedia: 'from Moses to the Men of the Great Synod'). The sayings run from Simon the Just (Wikipedia: 200 BCE) to shortly after Judah ha-Nasi (200 CE), redactor of the Mishnah. The Jewish Encyclopedia counts about sixty teachers in chapters 1–4, over about 450 years; chapter 5 is mostly anonymous numbered lists. Neither source gives a date of compilation.
- **Sixth chapter:** Kinyan Torah ("Acquisition of Torah"), a baraita added in the prayer book, partly so that a chapter could be read on each Sabbath of the Omer, between Passover and Shavuot.
- **Liturgical use:** Wikipedia traces the weekly reading to at least Saadia Gaon (10th century); today it is commonly read on summer Sabbath afternoons, from Passover to Rosh Hashanah.
- **Commentaries:** Maimonides (his introduction is known as the Eight Chapters), Yonah Gerondi and Samson Raphael Hirsch; the minor tractate Avot de-Rabbi Natan expands it.
- **Taylor's edition:** *Sayings of the Jewish Fathers*, Hebrew and English with notes and excursuses, by Charles Taylor, Master of St John's College, Cambridge. First published 1877; the second edition (Cambridge University Press) reprints it with additional notes, and its title page reads 1897, with the preface dated 13 August 1897. Wikipedia also gives 1897; the Jewish Encyclopedia cites the second edition as Cambridge, 1899.
