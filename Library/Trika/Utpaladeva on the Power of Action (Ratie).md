The following pages contain the edition, translation and study of a hitherto unknown chapter of a Sanskrit text that has only come down to us in fragments, namely, the detailed commentary (*Vivṛti*) written by the Kashmirian Utpaladeva (c. 925–975 CE) so as to explain one of his own philosophical treatises, a major Śaiva nondualistic work entitled the *Recognition of the Lord* (*Īśvarapratyabhijñā*). Three chapters of this *Vivṛti* have recently come to light; the present book focuses on the first and shortest among them, chapter 2.1, which is devoted to the examination of the power of action (*kriyāśakti*) that the Śaivas ascribe to every conscious being. The chapter’s main point is to show that even though the core of consciousness, i.e., its power to become aware of itself or to grasp itself, is the subtlest form of action, sequence (*krama*), whether temporal or spatial, only belongs to lower forms of action, so that Śiva—understood as an infinite, omniscient and omnipotent consciousness—has a dynamic essence without being in any way spatially or temporally limited. The text therefore discusses the nature of ordinary actions performed by transmigrating individuals as well as that of Śiva’s cosmic creation, while engaging in debates on the nature of space and time. The first chapter of the book discusses the place of Utpaladeva’s *Vivṛti* within the *Īśvarapratyabhijñā* corpus and relates how its known fragments were discovered. Chapter 2 describes the manuscript sources used for the edition. Chapter 3 provides a brief overview of Utpaladeva’s system and of the philosophical context in which he wrote his treatise. Chapters 4 to 10 contain a detailed analysis of the text, the translation of which is given in chapter 11; the Sanskrit original is edited in chapter 12. 

The recent discovery of parts of the detailed commentary that Utpaladeva composed to explain his own magnum opus has amply confirmed that his system is one of the most refined and original in the history of Indian philosophy; that he was the main innovator as far as Śaiva nondualistic philosophy is concerned; and that the *Vivṛti* was the most important textual layer of the *Īśvarapratyabhijñā* treatise. But the text edited here is not easy to 

*[p. xiv]* ^pg15

understand. This is so for several reasons, not least of which are its polemical yet very elliptical character and the fact that despite years of painstaking (some might say: obsessive) efforts to gather fragments of this text from various annotations scribbled in the margins of manuscripts, I have failed to fill several remaining lacunae in the edition. Besides, we are fortunate enough to have at our disposal two brilliant commentaries on the *Īśvarapratyabhijñā* treatise by Abhinavagupta (c. 975–1025 CE), one of which is devoted to the *Vivṛti*; alas, however, Abhinavagupta does not comment on every aspect of Utpaladeva’s text—far from that. For all these reasons, as well as, of course, my own shortcomings as a translator and historian, I still have numerous doubts about the translation and interpretations provided below, and I am painfully aware that they must still contain many errors. Hopefully these, once published, can be corrected by others more learned than me; and I will certainly have occasion for offering additional corrections in the forthcoming study of another, longer chapter of the *Vivṛti* that I am currently editing and translating.
