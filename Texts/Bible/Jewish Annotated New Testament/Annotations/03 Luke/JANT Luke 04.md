### 4.1–13: The temptation

*[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-1|Text]], p. 118* · ([[Matthew 04#^4-1|Mt 4.1–11]]; [[Mark 01#^1-12|Mk 1.12–13]]). ^4-1

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-1|1]]:** *Holy Spirit*, see 1.15n. ^4-1-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-2|2]]:** *Forty days* in the *wilderness* (v 1), recalls Israel’s testing ([[Torah 46 - Eikev#^8-2|Deut 8.2]]; Ps 106). *Tempted*, or “tested” (11.16). *Ate nothing*, [[Torah 46 - Eikev#^9-9|Deut 9.9]]; [[I Kings 19#^19-8|1 Kings 19.8]]; Jesus enacts the roles of Moses ([[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^34-28|Ex 34.28]]; [[Torah 46 - Eikev#^9-9|Deut 9.9]],[[Torah 46 - Eikev#^9-18|18]]) and Elijah ([[I Kings 19#^19-8|1 Kings 19.8]]). ^4-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-3|3]]:** *Devil*, Satan; in Jewish thought (e.g., [[Zechariah 03#^3-1|Zech 3.1–2]] and Job 1–2 \[the “Satan,” i.e., the “Accuser”\]) is variously a member of the heavenly court whose role is to test the righteous, and an evil being who opposes God (e.g., [[I Chronicles 21#^21-1|1 Chr 21.1]]). ^4-3

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-4|4]]:** *By bread alone*, see [[Torah 46 - Eikev#^8-3|Deut 8.3]]. Jesus counters Satan’s challenges by quoting Deuteronomy (the citations are closer to the LXX than the MT). ^4-4

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-5|5]]:** Compare [[Torah 54 - V'Zot HaBerachah#^34-1|Deut 34.1–4]], Moses overlooking the promised land. ^4-5

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-6|6]]:** *Given over to me*, suggests Satan presently rules the world. ^4-6

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-8|8]]:** *It is written*, see [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^6-13|Deut 6.13]], see also 10.20. *Lord your God* here can subtly refer to Jesus (see 1.17n.). ^4-8

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-9|9]]:** *Jerusalem*, see 2.22n. Matthew places this temptation second and ends with the temptation to universal rule. *Pinnacle*, Josephus (*Ant.* 15.412), speaking of the Temple, notes, while the valley was very deep, and its bottom could not be seen . . . \[this\] high elevation of the cloister stood upon that height, insomuch that if anyone looked down from the top of the battlements, or down both those altitudes, he would be giddy, while his sight could not reach to such an immense depth. Eusebius (*Hist. Eccles.* 2.23.11), claims that James the brother of Jesus was thrown to his death from the pinnacle of the temple. ^4-9

> [!note] Jesus’ Synagogue Sermon (Lk 4.16–30) *(p. 118)*
> Replacing Mark’s account ([[Mark 06#^6-2|Mk 6.2–6]]) of Jesus’ rejection in Nazareth, this depiction of Jesus’ preaching in the local synagogue is generally recognized as the Gospel’s signature story: Jesus announces he fulfills Isaiah’s predictions about the one “anointed” (4.18; Gk *echrisen* is related to the term “Christ”); the Gentile mission is suggested; and the Jews in the synagogue violently reject him. Although the congregation initially welcomes his message (4.22), this narrative begins the process of turning approval into rejection by citing two precedents to his ministry: the prophets Elijah and Elisha performed miracles not for Jews but for Gentiles. Luke then describes how the congregation was “filled with rage,” “drove \[Jesus\] out of the town,” and sought to “hurl him off the cliff” (4.28–29). Christian sermons occasionally explain this murderous reaction by claiming that the Jews, as not only ethnocentric but also xenophobic, wanted to reserve the messianic benefits for themselves; therefore, they seek to kill Jesus because he has a positive message for the Gentiles. Such conclusions misread Jewish history. Jews in general had positive relations with Gentiles, as witnessed by the Court of the Gentiles in the Jerusalem Temple, Gentiles as patrons of synagogues (7.1–10), and Gentiles as God-fearers (Acts 10). They also expected the redemption of righteous Gentiles, who would come streaming to Zion, as [[Zechariah 08#^8-23|Zech 8.23]] states, “In those days ten men from nations of every language shall take hold of a Jew, grasping his garment and saying, ‘Let us go with you, for we have heard that God is with you.’” (See “Jewish Views of Gentiles,” p. 640.) The rejection of Jesus is not prompted by xenophobia; it is prompted instead by Jesus’ refusal to provide his hometown with messianic blessings.

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-10|10–11]]:** *It is written*, see [[Psalms 091#^91-11|Ps 91.11–12]]. Ironically, the Psalm was used as a protection against Satan (11QPsª; *Tg* Ps 91; *y. Shabb*. 8b \[“a song against demons”\]). ^4-10

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-12|12]]:** *It is said*, see [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^6-16|Deut 6.16]]. *Lord your God*, see v 8n. ^4-12

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-13|13]]:** *Opportune time*, see 22.3,28. ^4-13

### 4.14–15: Teaching in Galilee

*[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-14|Text]], p. 119* · ([[Matthew 04#^4-12|Mt 4.12–17]]; [[Mark 01#^1-14|Mk 1.14–15]]). ^4-14

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-14|14]]:** *Spirit*, see 1.15n. ^4-14-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-15|15]]:** *Synagogues*, locations of Jewish worship, teaching, and community gathering; see 4.44; see also “The Synagogue,” p. 662. *Praised by everyone*, initial Jewish reception is unequivocally positive. ^4-15

### 4.16–30: Rejection in Nazareth

*[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-16|Text]], p. 119* · See [[Matthew 13#^13-53|Mt 13.53–58]]; [[Mark 06#^6-1|Mk 6.1–6]]; a scene unique to Luke (perhaps re-writing [[Mark 06#^6-1|Mk 6.1–6]]; cf. [[Matthew 13#^13-53|Mt 13.53–58]]). See “Jesus’ Synagogue Sermon,” p. 118. ^4-16

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-16|16]]:** *As was his custom*, referring to v 15. *To read*, most people in antiquity were illiterate. ^4-16-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-17|17]]:** *Scroll of the prophet Isaiah*, whether Nazareth had a separate synagogue building or was prosperous enough to own an Isaiah scroll remains debated. (The fully preserved Isaiah scroll from Qumran (1QIsaa) is 734 cm high and has fifty four columns.) Jesus would be reading the “haftarah,” a passage from the Prophets (“Neviʾim”) complementing the weekly Torah portion. The earliest rabbinic reference to this practice is *t. Meg*. 4 (3).1; however, Lk 4; [[Acts 13#^13-15|Acts 13.15]] suggest it, and some scholars have argued that Philo knows a haftarah cycle from 17 Tammuz to the Day of Atonement. The text Jesus reads does not appear among any Jewish haftarah listing used today. ^4-17

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-18|18–19]]:** *Spirit . . . is upon me*, see 3.22. *Good news to the poor*. . . *year of the Lord’s favor*, see [[Isaiah 61#^61-1|Isa 61.1–2]]; see also [[Isaiah 58#^58-6|Isa 58.6]] and the Jubilee tradition of Lev 25 (see *b. Sanh*. [[Sanhedrin 102#102a|102a]]). For messianic interpretations of Isa 61, see 1QH 18.14; 11QMelch 1.18. *Release to the captives*, ironic, given John’s imprisonment (3.19–20). Luke lacks Isaiah’s reference to “the day of vengeance” (61.2). ^4-18

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-22|22]]:** *Joseph’s son*, see 2.33,48; 3.23. ^4-22

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-23|23]]:** *Doctor, cure yourself*, a similar proverb appears in *Gen. Rab*. 23.4. *You did at Capernaum*, Luke does not describe these events; see 4.31–41. *Capernaum* (from “Kefar- Nahum,” Nahum’s village), fishing center on the Sea of Galilee. ^4-23

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-24|24]]:** *Truly* (Gk/Heb “amen,” lit., “so be it”; the only Heb word Luke uses), a term usually found at the end of prayers, but Jesus’ usage is not unique (e.g., [[Jeremiah 28#^28-6|Jer 28.6]]). ^4-24

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-25|25–26]]:** See [[I Kings 17#^17-8|1 Kings 17.8–16]]. ^4-25

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-27|27]]:** See [[II Kings 05#^5-1|2 Kings 5.1–4]]. ^4-27

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-28|28]]:** *Filled with rage*, not because of beneficence shown to Gentiles but to Jesus’ withholding his powers from them. ^4-28

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-29|29]]:** *Cliff*, Nazareth is not built on a cliff. ^4-29

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-30|30]]:** *Passed through* may suggest supernatural abilities. ^4-30

### 4.31–37: Capernaum healing

*[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-31|Text]], p. 120* · ([[Matthew 04#^4-13|Mt 4.13]]; [[Matthew 07#^7-28|7.28–29]]; [[Mark 01#^1-21|Mk 1.21–28]]). The first of five Sabbath healings (4.31,38; 6.6; 13.10; 14.1). The people take no offense; Jesus violates no Sabbath commandment. ^4-31

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-31|31]]:** *Capernaum*, see v 23n. *Teaching*, as a place of assembly, the synagogue would have been an appropriate location for teaching, and not only of biblically related material. ^4-31-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-32|32]]:** *With authority* (also v 36), perhaps indicating teaching without citing tradition (see e.g., *m. Avot* 1.1); see [[Matthew 07#^7-29|Mt 7.29]]. ^4-32

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-36|36]]:** *Commands the unclean spirits*, see [[LXX Tobit 03#^3-17|Tob 3.17]]; [[Acts 19#^19-13|Acts 19.13]]; 1QapGen 20.16–29; Josephus (*J.W*. 7.185; *Ant*. 8.45–49), and other sources attest Jewish exorcists. According to [[Zechariah 13#^13-2|Zech 13.2]], the “unclean spirit” is removed in an eschatological context. ^4-36

### 4.38–39: Simon Peter’s mother-in-law

*[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-38|Text]], p. 120* · ([[Matthew 08#^8-14|Mt 8.14–15]]; [[Mark 01#^1-29|Mk 1.29–31]]). The second Sabbath healing (see 4.31–37n.). ^4-38

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-38|38]]:** *Simon*, see 5.1–11; 6.13–14. *Mother-in-law*, the Gospels do not mention the wife (but see [[1 Corinthians 09#^9-5|1 Cor 9.5]]). ^4-38-2

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-39|39]]:** *Rebuked*, suggesting that a demon caused the fever (4.35). *Serve* (Gk “diakonei”), or “minister”; origin of the term “deacon” (see 8.3; 10.40; 22.26–27). ^4-39

### 4.40–41: Evening healings

*[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-40|Text]], p. 120* · ([[Matthew 08#^8-16|Mt 8.16–17]]; [[Mark 01#^1-32|Mk 1.32–34]]). ^4-40

**[[NRSVue Luke 04#^4-40|40]]:** *Sun was setting*, concluding the Sabbath. *Laid his hands*, for healing and blessing (see [[Torah 12 - Vayechi#^48-14|Gen 48.14]]), as well as for consecration (e.g., Ex 29) and commission (e.g., [[Torah 40 - Balak#^24-23|Num 24.23]]). ^4-40-2
