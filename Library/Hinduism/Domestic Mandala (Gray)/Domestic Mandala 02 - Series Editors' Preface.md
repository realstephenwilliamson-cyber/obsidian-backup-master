## Series Editors’ Preface

Andrew Strathern and Pamela J. Stewart

We are very happy to include John Gray’s excellent ethnographic exposition on *Domestic Mandala: Architecture of Lifeworlds in Nepal* in our series on Anthropology and Cultural History in Asia and the Indo-Pacific. Dr. Gray is a noted scholar whose work spans Lowland Scotland and the Highland reaches of the Kathmandu Valley in Nepal. This book also creates bridges between architecture and anthropology, since it is a sustained discussion of the cosmological significance of houses in Kholagaun hamlet in the village of Banaspati in Kathmandu. Specifically, Dr. Gray argues that the house is seen imaginatively as a mandala or mystic diagram that acts to bring the meanings of the sacred order or cosmos in people’s lives literally ‘close to home’. Unifying the analysis is the concept of homology and mystical connection between different domains of existence, seen as macrocosm and microcosm. In this way the house can be seen to be a part of and also to replicate the wider cosmos; while the human body itself may be seen as a further microcosmic version of a mandala. As Dr. Gray points out, these correspondences are not just a matter of abstract cognition. They are embodied and practical, guiding people in terms of rituals they need to perform in order to ensure their health and fertility. At the same time, people are enjoined by their cosmological notions not to become too attached to worldly concerns, so that they can see through the impediments of illusion and grasp the unity of the cosmos as something beyond themselves. It might seem a tall order for the architecture of houses to express all these levels and contradictions of cosmological thought, but Dr. Gray points out that architecture in general produces embodied meanings and creates meaningful space; or, as we might put it, meaningful places that encapsulate both practical concerns and cosmological values. In the case of Kholagaun, Dr. Gray argues that there are actually two spatial modalities at work in houses: a mandalic mode, which emphasizes concentric spaces around a center and leads people to understand the unity of the cosmos; and a yantric mode, which deals with cardinal directions and the ritual ways of achieving auspiciousness and success in the world. Both persons and houses are intersections of these two modes, and both are also related to the foundational myth of the cosmic being Purusha, from whose self-sacrificial body came everything in the world, and a kind of sacred geometry associated with the construction of circles and with central points.

All this is extremely elaborate and also appears to be a part of the conscious models of the people themselves rather than simply a construct pieced together by the anthropologist. The general idea, moreover, that houses and bodies may be seen 

*[p. xii]* ^pg13

as deeply bound up with the cosmos has considerable comparative applicability. Dr. Gray himself quotes early on a remark from Carsten and Hugh-Jones’ edited book *About the House*, which in turn took its inspiration from Lévi-Strauss’s observations on ‘house societies’. Interestingly enough, these included societies of Indonesia where houses were not only an important locus of social structure but were also integral to ideas of a gendered cosmos in which houses were modes of connections between sets of kin linked by marital alliances and the flow of valuables (for an example see McKinnon 1991, discussed in Strathern and Stewart 2000: 33-4). Another locus of such a description of houses familiar in ethnography is Pierre Bourdieu’s exposition of the interior spaces within the Kabyle house (Bourdieu 1977: 90-91). Bourdieu refers in this context to a ‘dialectic of objectifiction and embodiment’, by which he means the interplay between human experience and the material structures that humans make or interpret/give meaning to. Houses themselves contain a number of gendered oppositions, while the house as a whole stands in a similar gendered way to the world outside. Body space is thus integrated progressively with cosmic space (Bourdieu 1977: 91). In the island of New Guinea, gendered divisions of houses are also often significant. In the highlands region this division may take various forms. In one form, found traditionally in the Mount Hagen area, a round central men’s house, shared by a man and his male children above the age of about ten years, sometimes along with other men, was surrounded by oblong houses occupied by the man’s wife or wives and the wives of his sons, if these were already married. The men’s house was associated with collective projects and leadership in ceremonial exchange activities and was sometimes built at the head of a long ceremonial ground used for exchanges; in which case, immediately in front of it was placed a ceremonial tub in which cordyline shrubs were typically planted, along with magical stones and other substances held to bring fertility and success in exchange to the men’s house and its occupants. Women’s houses were the centers of cooking, bringing up of young children, and feeding domestic pigs, which might be stalled at night in one end of the house with its separate entrance, the pigs being let out to forage in fallow land each morning. Men’s houses were thus the locus of political reproduction, women’s houses the locus of economic and familial reproduction, the whole seen as a set of complementary functions relating to a total cosmology of success and prosperity (Stewart and Strathern 2002). In the second pattern, found among the Duna people, a single house structure was divided in two, one half being for a husband, the other half for his wife, again with separate entrances. The wife cooked for the husband and brought it to his side for him to consume and share, as appropriate, with other males. Communal men’s houses were also built in the past, with separate hearths for particular men, and not linked with women’s houses as in Hagen. This pattern was modified through colonial influence from the 1960s onward, although it was also revived in the 1990s. 

In both the Hagen and the Duna cases, the clearly marked and gendered division of houses indicated the importance of such ‘separations’ in social life. But the fact that the genders were also linked together was marked with equal clarity, a point that becomes obvious when we consider the Duna house, a single encompassing

Our general point here is that, although these practices were not supported by so elaborate a set of notions as in Nepal, New Guinea houses were important indicators of overall cosmological values just as they are in Gray’s present case study.

Perhaps even more clearly there are parallels between the Nepalese idea of sacred geometry as it relates to houses, and the Chinese practice of geomancy or ‘feng shui’. In Taiwan, on a particular University campus, we interviewed in 2003 and 2004 a student who was troubled by apparitions of unquiet ghosts, and part of the reason for this, she suggested, was that the architects who planned the student dormitories had not conducted a proper ‘feng shui’ survey to determine how this accommodation should be auspiciously situated so as to block the possible entry of such ghostly entities. Disembodied ghosts are also significant among the people of Kholagaun, and are thought of as haunting liminal spaces, namely crossroads in the vicinity of compounds. They are seen as the ghosts of people who died inauspicious deaths, including deaths from witchcraft. Such ghosts are said to be unhappy because of the broken-off, unfinished business of their lives, and so they remain inappropriately attached to the world of the living, making harmful attachments to people and possessing them by making them sick. These ghosts are thought of as discontented also because no proper mortuary rites are performed for those whose death is inauspicious. Like witchcraft itself, they are a problem in the system; and witchcraft produces a succession of problems, since deaths caused by it produce these discontented ghosts. This phenomenon of witchcraft does not form a large part of Dr. Gray’s exposition. But he does mention that in-marrying wives are at risk of being accused of being witches, partly for structural reasons, because they are thought to possess sexual power which can enable them to influence their husbands, draining them of their semen and entrapping them in too great an attachment to themselves: a pattern of thought and practice which again meets with its parallels in the life-worlds of some New Guinea peoples. In addition we might suggest that rumor and gossip, classically used against outsiders, may be the means of generating conflict and jealousy in households, thus leading to witchcraft accusations (see Stewart and Strathern 2004 on this pattern). Also, boundaries are clearly important in Nepalese thinking, including the boundaries of the house. Outside women entering the house are a necessity for its reproduction; but they also represent a potential threat to it. They are likely to be seen as dangerous, and are therefore likely also to be the subjects of gossip.

At the beginning and end of his book, Dr. Gray stresses a kind of phenomenological background to his whole analysis, appealing to writers such as Edward Casey, Michel de Certeau, and Martin Heidegger, especially Heidegger’s concept of ‘dwelling’, referring to the basic embodied relationship between people and the things they use in ordinary sequences of life. Heidegger’s idea of dwelling seems to be founded on a concept of practice, and is therefore similar to Pierre Bourdieu’s theory of practice; and both are related philosophically to the work of theorists such as Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Edmund Husserl, and Alfred Schutz. In the Nepalese case this kind of stress on embodied action takes on another dimension, because the body itself is seen as a 

*[p. xiv]* ^pg15

part of a wider cosmos. Ideas of purity and impurity, centered on the body, also relate the body to the world in ways that are partly consciously expressed and are also in part tacit and unexpressed, but are geared to a deeper set of truths. In approaching this latter point in his concluding chapter, Dr. Gray draws further on Heidegger’s use, or borrowing, of the ancient Greek word ‘techne’, which is usually translated as a form of ‘craft’ or ‘technique’ of dong things. For Heidegger the word gathers a further meaning of creativity or revealing something, which Heidegger linked to the notion of truth, translating the Greek word ‘aletheia’ as ‘unconcealing’ or ‘revealing’ (another meaning is ‘remembering’, which might possibly have Orphic connections). Regardless of the etymology of the word here, Heidegger’s point is that truth is something that human action and thought reveal, or bring into being. Dr. Gray’s analysis implies the deeper significance of Kholagaun houses as residing in this same realm of truth, which in this case is the paradoxical combination of attachment to, and detachment from the world. Houses, therefore, seen in this light, reveal a deep philosophy.

It is a mark of John Gray’s achievement in this book that at the end of his exposition, a statement of this kind is perfectly comprehensible.
