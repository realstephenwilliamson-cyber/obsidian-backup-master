## Chapter 9

## Violence, the Fundamental, and the Cross

Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion is not a theologically neutral enterprise. Like Kant and Spinoza, Ricoeur makes philosophical moves that have theological consequences. Some of these philosophical moves also create problematic theological ambiguities—something we discussed in the last chapter regarding divine transcendence and immanence. In this final chapter we will see that there are also theological consequences to Ricoeur’s revisionary interpretations of central Christian ideas of sin, the cross, resurrection, judgment, and forgiveness. Revisionary interpretation can be a good thing, but the question is whether the revisions create new problems for the overall coherence and cogency of Ricoeur’s hermeneutic of Christian religion.

Ricoeur’s theological non-neutrality becomes increasingly evident in his late writings. At times he writes that he is no longer as scrupulous about the divide between philosophy and theology, and even ventures into what he calls “philosophical theology or theological philosophy.”<sup>1</sup> Nevertheless, he continues to refuse the label “Christian philosopher,” describing his status instead as a philosopher who is a Christian. He defines his own Christian commitment as “someone who professes a primordial adhesion to the life, the words, the death of Jesus” (LD 69). Yet the content of this adhesion—that is, Ricoeur’s hermeneutic of the Christian kerygma—is based on a rather selective reading of the biblical texts and the Christian tradition.

At times Ricoeur seems intent on living up to H. Richard Niebuhr’s famous description of liberal Protestantism: “A God without wrath brought men without sin into a kingdom without judgment through the ministrations of a Christ without a cross.”<sup>2</sup> The exception is that Ricoeur still appeals to the cross, but he offers a revisionary take on the meaning of Jesus’ crucifixion as self-giving love, stripped of biblical motifs like judgment and justification, sacrifice and atonement. As we will see, Ricoeur’s interpretation of the cross 

*[p. 184]* ^pg201

is related to his interpretation of forgiveness, insofar as both fail to reckon sufficiently with the need for judgment if justice is to be done. There is a tension between love and justice in Ricoeur’s account of forgiveness, but this tension is ultimately more problematic than fruitful. I will suggest an alternate interpretation in which love and justice find their unity in the cross.

These reflections open a larger consideration of nonviolence as a desideratum in Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion. We saw this concern with nonviolence in his interpretation of revelation, and it is evident in his nonviolent interpretation of the cross. In several works from the 1990s, Ricoeur turns his attention to religious violence, seeking a model of revelation that would be nonviolent in its reception and proclamation. On this account, all religions are responses to what he calls the “fundamental,” and religious violence arises from a desire to capture and contain the fundamental, in rivalry with the responses of other religions. In order to overcome this rivalry, Ricoeur proposes an ethic of interconfessional hospitality based on a philosophy of translation. This raises further questions—once again, regarding the personal and impersonal, transcendence and immanence, as well as questions of religious plurality and truth. While some ambiguity remains in this area, Ricoeur shows that religious plurality calls for neither violent exclusivism nor reductive pluralism, but a nonviolent conflict of interpretations.

### Ricoeur’s Nonviolent, Nonsacrificial Interpretation of the Cross

Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of the cross is based on the paradoxical logic of Jesus’ teachings about the self and self-giving. For Ricoeur, the programmatic statement is Jesus’ saying: “Whoever would save their life will lose it, and whoever loses their life for my sake will find it” ([[Matthew 16#^16-25|Mt. 16:25]]) (FS 284). To take up one’s cross and follow after Jesus is the pathway of true life ([[Luke 09#^9-23|Luke 9:23–24]]). This paradox becomes a basic principle of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of the self, which holds that self-understanding only comes through self-divestment, when the self loses itself as self-positing *cogito*.<sup>3</sup> 

Ricoeur also finds this paradoxical logic in the parables of Jesus. Whereas human logic is a logic of equal exchange, the logic of Jesus is one of extravagance, excess, hyperbole, and *superabundance*. The logic of superabundance is pivotal in Paul’s epistle to the Romans, which describes the history of salvation in these terms: “For if, by the fault of one, many died, *how much more* the grace of God and the gift conferred by the grace of one man Jesus Christ have abounded” ([[Romans 05#^5-15|Rom 5:15]]—see also 5:9–10 and 5:17) (FS 279). Ricoeur appeals to this logic of superabundance on numerous occasions, but he does not elaborate on how this gift is conferred by Christ. For the apostle, this 

*[p. 185]* ^pg202

grace is not a generally available possibility but is instead uniquely grounded in the historical events of Jesus’ death and resurrection. Ricoeur’s interpretation of the cross, by contrast, seems to suggest that Jesus’ death is revelatory as an example of a fundamental ontological possibility of self-giving.

This theme of self-giving is central to Ricoeur’s interpretation of the cross. What happened at the cross? What does Jesus’ death mean? Ricoeur is keen to reject several of the themes traditionally associated with the cross, such as sacrifice, punishment, and divine wrath. “Since the time of the New Testament a sacrificial theology has circulated, in which the accent falls on the substitutionary aspect of the Paschal victim in response to the punitive requirement for retribution pronounced by a vengeful God” (RB 32). Ricoeur wants to demythologize this sacrificial theology, along with the ideas of judgment and sin insofar as they suggest punishment for infractions that separate us from God. He deems this demythologization necessary because a contemporary hermeneutics of faith must translate classical theological categories of atonement into the existential concerns of our present day. In his estimation, for late modern people the most urgent questions do not concern the categories of sin and judgment but rather *sense* and *nonsense* (LD 46; CC 166). Thus the biblical language of justification needs to be reoriented as a question of existential meaning, rather than God rectifying or setting things right in the world, and between the world and himself. 

How does Ricoeur reinterpret the cross after discarding traditional categories of sacrifice, judgment, punishment, sin, and divine wrath? Jesus’ death is “exemplary, inasmuch as the *idea of service* turns the meaning of an imminent death toward the future of the survivors” (LD 51). On this reading, Jesus is not looking ahead to a “material, corporeal resurrection,” but instead pours himself out in a “*gift-service* that engenders a community” (LD 53, 54). The cross is a victory over death because of the “quality” of Jesus’ death (CC 152), which can be transformative because it exemplifies a “positive ethics of detachment,” a “transfer of every one of my vital expectations to the *other who is my life after afterlife*” (LD 52). The death of Jesus is an event of self-giving love—a “pure offering” (RB 32).

This interpretation of the cross is oddly ahistorical, calling to mind those portraits of the crucifixion in which Jesus has a clean porcelain body and a faint look of detachment on his face. That kind of sentimental, disembodied depiction lacks the horror of crucifixion and its devastating effect on the disciples. The meaning Ricoeur discerns in the event may be edifying on some level, but it is hard to see how a mere example of self-giving love could account for the radical change in the disciples, giving a scattered band of devastated followers such a clear sense of mission and a readiness to die rather than stop proclaiming the risen Christ. It is hard to see how the quality of Jesus’ death alone—and not his resurrection—could motivate such a reversal.

*[p. 186]* ^pg203

Ricoeur’s proposal is also unclear on why Jesus had to die in the first place. Why is a “pure offering” required? How is it *for* the disciples and the Christian community? Ricoeur does not answer these questions, but he does “strongly support the liberation of the theology of the cross from its sacrificial interpretation,” and likewise the victory of the gift over the “vindictive idea that blood was the necessary price” to satisfy an angry God (CC 152, 160). Instead, Ricoeur opts for a selective reading of the biblical texts (quite unlike what we find in an earlier text like *The Symbolism of Evil*) that does not account for the complex symbolic network of biblical images associated with the crucifixion. As Fleming Rutledge observes, there are two main groups of biblical motifs for what is happening at the cross: (1) atonement for sin and guilt, including the symbolism of sacrifice, blood, sin offering, expiation, and substitution; (2) deliverance from slavery, bondage, and oppression by sin as an alien power.<sup>4</sup> 

It is unfortunate that Ricoeur never gave a fuller hermeneutical study of these images, comparable to what he provided in his essay “Original Sin: A Study in Meaning” (CI 269–86). Whatever one makes of his attempt to mediate Augustine and Kant, in that essay Ricoeur carefully treats the symbolism of original sin so as to discard its distorted aspects and rehabilitate those aspects that are genuinely meaningful. He exposes the distortions that result when one tries to rationalize and systematize the symbolism of original sin as a rationally transparent concept. Something very similar is true of the symbolism of atonement. The battlefield metaphors of triumph and victory, the legal metaphors of judgment and justification, and the priestly metaphors of blood and sacrifice are susceptible to misuse, especially when they are rationalized or systematized as “theories of atonement.”<sup>5</sup> The question we need to consider is whether Ricoeur offers a more compelling alternative. In the face of their more grotesque distortions, Ricoeur opts for an abrupt dismissal. A more compelling approach would be a hermeneutical exercise in recovering the semantic core of the symbolism of atonement, which cannot be extracted from Christian faith without creating a whole new set of distortions.

Ricoeur is not alone in his aversion to the motifs or symbolism of atonement. Our modern sensibilities often find this imagery primitive and cruel, as it suggests a God who is not loving but vindictive, and possibly even guilty of divine child abuse. For similar reasons, Ricoeur rejects the atonement motif and calls for a “nonsacrificial interpretation” of the cross, which he derives from René Girard. According to Girard, the cross puts an end to the cycle of sacrificial violence. Sacrificial rituals are foundational to religion, but they are not founded by divine command. Instead, they arise as a release valve for mimetic rivalry. According to Girard, all desire is mimetic, or imitative: I desire what the other desires. Mimetic desire is often a good thing, since it 

*[p. 187]* ^pg204

makes culture possible, but it also gives rise to envy, resentment, and rivalry.<sup>6</sup> This conflict poses a serious threat, since it will destroy everyone unless it is resolved. Enter the scapegoat. The only way out of this mimetic crisis is for the group to redirect its hostility toward a scapegoat, who is charged with being the source of the conflict. By murdering the scapegoat, the group achieves peace and reconciliation and is released from the escalating crisis of mimetic rivalry. The scapegoat mechanism is thus foundational to both culture and religion. Like Levinas, Girard does not see the sacred as a realm of benign enchantment, but as violent at its very roots.

According to Girard, the trouble with the traditional scapegoat mechanism is that the scapegoat is in fact innocent, not guilty. He argues that this is where the Christian message is truly liberating. The crucifixion of Jesus reveals the innocence of the victim, and by unmasking the scapegoat mechanism, strips it of its power. This is how the cross saves us: God himself “assumes the role of the single victim” of the crowd and thereby frees us from the mystifying power of sacrifice.<sup>7</sup> The cross is the sacrifice to end all sacrifices, because it shows that while human beings want sacrifice, God does not.

Girard’s nonsacrificial interpretation of the cross correctly focuses on the biblical motif of deliverance. But deliverance from *what*? And deliverance by what means? Girard takes as central the biblical theme of the principalities and powers that enslave, bind, and oppress. These are the *stoicheia*, the elementary principles of the world, and they are forms of “false transcendence”<sup>8</sup> that organize and weaponize our mimetic rivalries: “rivalries that are political, economic, athletic, sexual, artistic, intellectual .  .  . and even religious.”<sup>9</sup> Girard takes this biblical imagery more seriously than thinkers like Kant, who reads this cosmic conflict as a metaphor of moral struggle within the self, and Bultmann who seeks to demythologize it as part of an outmoded cosmology. Here Ricoeur is closer to Kant and Bultmann, as he does not attend to this imagery of cosmic conflict in any detail. In this regard Girard could expand Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of evil. The benefit of attending to this motif is an expansion of our understanding of the binding power of sin, which operates not only on an individual but also on a social and even cosmic level. An additional benefit is that this imagery corresponds to Ricoeur’s efforts to go beyond the individualism of existential theologies and show how hope has a social and cosmic horizon.<sup>10</sup>

This cosmic horizon is the focal point of the biblical genre of apocalyptic. We are captive not merely to ourselves and our past misdeeds, but also to powers of Sin, Death, and Law that “rule over the *kosmos* as semi-autonomous Powers.”<sup>11</sup> We can’t simply overcome these powers by making better choices. This is the focal point of the *Christus victor* theory of atonement, which reads the cross as the triumph of Christ over the principalities and powers.<sup>12</sup> Girard assures us that this imagery does not commit 

*[p. 188]* ^pg205

the triumphal*ism* so despised by the progressive, because the triumph is not the military victory of a victorious general, for “rather than inflicting violence on others, Christ submits to it.”<sup>13</sup> This metaphor of triumph is indeed borrowed from the battlefield, but the triumph comes through weakness rather than military might. The key passage for this triumph is [[Colossians 02#^2-13|Colossians 2:13–15]]:<sup>14</sup>

And when you were dead in trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, God made you alive together with him, when he forgave us all our trespasses, erasing the record that stood against us with its legal demands. He set this aside, nailing it to the cross. He disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public example of them, triumphing over them in it. 

On Girard’s reading, it is by making a “public example” of the principalities and powers that Jesus triumphs over them. The cross reveals the scapegoat mechanism for what it is, revealing that the victim is in fact innocent. As for the legal claim against us, this too is nailed to the cross. We no longer stand condemned, because the whole sacrificial system has come down. 

According to Girard, this unmasking and liberation is also the interpretive key to understanding the resurrection accounts. As Ricoeur writes: “Girard interprets Easter, after the moment of the disciples’ complicity in the collective lynching, as the proclamation of innocence that dissipates the collective illusion of the guilty victim.” In this exemplary death, then, the liberating power is semantic: Resurrection becomes the renewal of “the religious content of the Gospels” (RB 33). Jean Greisch has observed that for Girard, “the meaning of the cross exhausts itself in a knowledge, a Gnosis: the truth hidden since the foundation of the world bursts out in the eyes of all, the principle of violence is definitively unmasked, humanity is now supposed to know that it remains without sacrificial protection.”<sup>15</sup> Greisch’s question for Girard is also our question for Ricoeur: Does this account exhaust the meaning of Christ’s death and resurrection?

### The Cross as the Unity of Love and Justice

As we have seen, Ricoeur follows Girard in calling for a nonviolent, nonsacrificial interpretation of the cross. According to Girard, the “essential theme” of Jesus’ preaching is that

reconciliation with God can take place unreservedly and with no sacrificial intermediary through the rules of the kingdom. This reconciliation allows God to reveal himself as he is, for the first time in human history. Thus mankind 

*[p. 189]* ^pg206

no longer has to base harmonious relationships on bloody sacrifices, ridiculous fables of a violent deity, and the whole range of mythological cultural formations.<sup>16</sup>

Both Girard and Ricoeur are correct that there are ill-conceived theories of atonement that should be subjected to critique and in some cases rejected outright. But bad interpretation calls for better interpretation, and there are some significant difficulties that arise from their reading of the cross.

The first problem is that their reading does not show how the cross is God’s judgment on sin, by means of which God sets things right in the world. Girard’s insights regarding the scapegoat mechanism are significant, but a crucial gap in his account is that it does not confront the fact of our own sin and injustice. It’s not just that the victim is innocent; it’s also that we are guilty. Girard highlights the biblical figure of Satan as the accuser, arguing that Jesus cancels this accusation, nailing it to the cross, by revealing his own innocence and thus the “falsity” of the accusation: “The Cross of Christ restores all the victims of the single victim mechanism, whether it goes under the label of legal accusation, Satan, or principalities and powers.”<sup>17</sup> This is good news for innocent victims, but what about those who are actually guilty? One may be a victim in one situation and a perpetrator in another. Exposing the scapegoat mechanism does not rectify that which is wrong. Justice is left undone, and we are not truly set free from the bondage of sin and injustice. 

One might wonder why anyone has to die in the first place. Why can’t God just forgive? For Girard and Ricoeur, the “rules of the kingdom” and the logic of superabundance point to a grace that does not require sacrifice, nor entail divine judgment. But this undermines the assurance that God is just and takes sin, injustice, and suffering seriously. As Miroslav Volf writes: “Suspend justice and truth, and you cannot redeem the world; you must leave it as it is.” If grace is simply acting “as if” sin had not occurred, “the world will remain forever awry, the blood of the innocent will eternally cry out to heaven. There can be no redemption unless the truth about the world is told and justice is done.”<sup>18</sup> This is the hermeneutical key to understanding talk about divine wrath. Contrary to distortions from across the theological spectrum, both liberal and conservative, divine wrath is not the passionate, out-of-control fury of a God out for vengeance. This is true even for Anselm, who is so often blamed for leading Western theories of atonement astray. Anselm’s critics often assume he is offering an anthropomorphic picture of God as, in the words of Colin Gunton, a “rather testy monarch punishing offences against his personal honour.”<sup>19</sup> But for Anselm, sin is not merely a moral infraction that any reasonable person could forgive and forget; it mars, corrupts, destroys creation as it is meant to be.<sup>20</sup> 

*[p. 190]* ^pg207

God’s wrath is therefore not an emotional outburst, but rather the just response of a loving God who is intimately involved with his creation, to the extent that he is willing to enter into creation to set things right. As Rutledge puts it, “God’s righteousness leads him to all lengths to oppose what will destroy what he loves.” God is not acting from an offended sense of honor, but out of concern “*for the creature.*” “There was never a time when God was against us; even in his wrath he is for us.”<sup>21</sup> God’s wrath is therefore an expression of his love, which is set against anything that seeks to destroy the goodness of his creation.

The cross, then, is not about a vengeful God who must somehow vent his anger, and decides to take it out on his son rather than his creatures. Ricoeur is right to oppose this view. The cross does not bring about a change in God’s attitude toward us, because God is for us even in our separation from God. Nor is the cross about the Father abusively sacrificing his Son, since the Father and the Son are acting “*together, with a single will.*”<sup>22</sup> As Rutledge puts it, this action is undertaken from within the three persons of the Trinity, and so “the crucifixion reveals God placing himself under his own sentence. The wrath of God has lodged in God’s own self.”<sup>23</sup> By assuming humanity in the Incarnation, by taking divine judgment on himself at the cross, and by overcoming sin, death, and the hostile powers in the resurrection, God redeems a moribund creation in revolt. God does not change himself but instead changes the relation between himself and humanity, and his creation as a whole. This is the meaning of the term *atonement*: reconciliation, an overcoming of the separation between God and his creatures.

Ricoeur wants to move beyond the idea of sin as separation from God, but his alternative interpretation does not do justice to the destructive consequences of sin. Ricoeur is right in claiming that we can hardly understand the symbolism of sin anymore. To modern ears it rings like an outdated moralism, and has very little effect in stirring our imagination or desire. Why would God be concerned with our personal peccadillos? It seems awfully petty. Yet at the same time we feel outrage over horror, trauma, and atrocity: Where is God? In those moments we want justice, and it appears God is uninterested, unjust, or arbitrary. These extremes coexist in our late modern sensibilities, and we struggle to see how a God who is at once loving and just has anything to say to either situation. But maybe the fault is in our sensibilities—or rather our *in*sensibility to the real significance of sin. Recall Ricoeur’s point in *The Symbolism of Evil*, that there is a realism to sin that is irreducible to its “subjective measure” (SE 81–83). If that is true, then maybe the better response is not to discard the symbol but to rehabilitate it so it recovers some of its semantic shock.

Ricoeur wagers that we have an originary goodness deep down, deeper than radical evil, and that this goodness can be rehabilitated by religious 

*[p. 191]* ^pg208

symbols and narratives, restoring our capability for the good. But this does not really take our situation seriously enough. The path of deliverance from the bondage of radical evil is not to locate and mobilize a remnant of goodness that lies deeper than fault. Nor does it help to discover that the scapegoat was actually innocent, because *we* are not. What we need is a divine grace that confronts the reality of our situation and makes things right. What we need is neither vengeance nor violence<sup>24</sup> but, as Volf puts it, a “divine grace that restores the violator.”<sup>25</sup> This is a divine hospitality in which God himself restores communion with the world,<sup>26</sup> not by ignoring the demands of justice but by fulfilling them himself. In this way the cross is the unity of love and justice—not as an abstract speculative principle or idea, or a poetic or existential possibility, but in concrete historical actuality. The cross does not set love or mercy in tension with justice, but instead shows a divine love that does justice. This hermeneutics of the cross also allows for a more coherent understanding of forgiveness—even difficult forgiveness, which is possible not in spite of justice, but because of a God whose love leads him to the cross in order to ensure that justice is done. That is the topic of our next section.

### Difficult Forgiveness

A hermeneutics of the Christian kerygma needs to reckon with the cross as the unity, not the tension, of love and justice. This unity in turn provides a hermeneutical basis for understanding the possibility of forgiveness. The danger of downplaying the divine concern for judgment and justification is that it undermines the radical concept of forgiveness that Ricoeur proposes.

Ricoeur gives a beautiful description of the liberating power of forgiveness in the Epilogue to *Memory, History, Forgetting.* There, in one of Ricoeur’s last works, he returns to a theme from his earliest works: the theme of fault as paralyzing our power to act, and the “possible lifting of this existential incapacity” in forgiveness (MHF 457). Where fault binds the self to his or her past action, forgiveness unbinds and liberates the capability of the self. “Under the sign of forgiveness, the guilty person is to be considered capable of something other than his offenses and his faults. He is held to be restored to his capacity for acting, and action restored to its capacity for continuing. . . . And finally, this restored capacity is enlisted by promising as it projects action toward the future” (MHF 493). Forgiveness unbinds the self from past action, opening a future with new possibilities of living with and for others. 

This unbinding works through the paradoxical logic of superabundance, over and against the economic logic of exchange. The act of forgiveness is an act of faith that dissociates the agent from the act; it is “a credit addressed to the resources of self-regeneration” (MHF 490). The capable human being 

*[p. 192]* ^pg209

has an immanent ground of self-renewal that is activated by the speech act, “I forgive you.” This is the word that liberates and regenerates the self. Alterity activates the resources of ipseity.

Ricoeur’s goal is a hermeneutics of the paradox of forgiveness as “it is inscribed in our historical condition: under the various figures of unbinding the relation of the agent to his act” (MHF 491). Ricoeur offers a philosophical approximation—a “secular parable,” to use Barth’s phrase—of the logic of superabundance in Jesus’ parables and Pauline justification by faith. This is evident in Ricoeur’s distinction between the person and the work: “Everything, finally, hangs on the possibility of separating the agent from the action” (MHF 490). Ricoeur proposes a radical picture of unconditional grace, and yet he stops short of drawing the fully radical conclusion when he sums up the liberating word of forgiveness: “You are better than your actions” (MHF 493). Can this appeal to capability be a sufficient grounding for forgiveness?

When the external word of forgiveness—“you are better than your actions”—is internalized, it becomes an attestation of the self: “I am better than my actions.” If this is an attestation of my own capability, then it means: “*I can* do better.” But if the gift of forgiveness means I can do better, then it is still based on the economy of exchange, not the logic of the gift. My recognition is based on my being-able, and as such is a conditional rather than unconditional word. The word opens a new future, to be sure<sup>27</sup>—I have a free, undeserved opportunity to do better in the future. This may be a logic of abundance, but it is not the true logic of *super*abundance.

Eberhard Jüngel makes this point regarding capability and recognition, writing that “\[i\]n dealing with the person who is not yet capable of, or no longer capable of, achieving anything, we have a *criterion* for the dignity of our society.” This includes children, the elderly, the disabled, and those incarcerated for their past actions. These people should by all means be encouraged in their efforts to become capable. But on what basis? If they (or we, or anyone) justify their existence on the basis of their capability, then they are still not truly free and this conditional recognition actually threatens to undermine their becoming capable. Jüngel argues that “only when a society recognizes the dignity of the person who is not capable of achieving anything, and thus recognizes the truly human being in the person distinguished from his or her works, is a light cast from this kind of respectable humanity onto our conduct, our activity, and the institutions which it regulates—a light which makes it possible to go on to speak of *human agency*, of *human work* and even of *human institutions*.”<sup>28</sup> True humanity depends on the recognition of God, who loves persons unconditionally, not *because of* their varying degrees of capability. If the self is going to be reborn as capable, it needs to hear a word that is truly liberating—a word in which the self receives a justifying love prior to any affirmation of capability.

*[p. 193]* ^pg210

Again, capability is an exceedingly good thing and should be nurtured and defended. But to borrow terms from Bonhoeffer, it is a penultimate rather than ultimate good<sup>29</sup>—ultimate in the sense that God’s final word regarding the self is one of unconditional love and forgiveness, not a conditional judgment based on one’s acts or powers. Capability is the *result* of this recognition, not its basis. Otherwise our recognition and dignity are still conditional and we are stuck in the economy of exchange. This is where Ricoeur might draw more deeply from the biblical figures of forgiveness. To recall the passage we discussed above, Colossians 2, we see the idea of forgiveness grounded in the cross. Consider verses 13–14, which Girard quotes: When we were “dead in trespasses,” God made us “alive together with him, when he forgave us all our trespasses, erasing the record that stood against us with its legal demands. He set this aside, nailing it to the cross.” God’s forgiveness is not based on our capability, since we were dead—utterly incapable—in our sins.

It may be a bit surprising to criticize Ricoeur’s account of forgiveness for being conditional, since it seems to be so radically unconditional—even irresponsibly so. Ricoeur recognizes the difficulties in this kind of unconditional forgiveness, which can often appear to be deeply unjust. He cites Nicolai Hartmann, who argues that “if forgiveness were possible .  .  . it would constitute a moral evil” (MHF 466). Likewise, Charles Reagan admits to having 

difficulty with Ricoeur’s claim that forgiveness must be unconditional. He does not mention in his discussion of guilt and forgiveness the ideas of sorrow, repentance, absolution, and penance that play such an important role in traditional Christian theology. Here, forgiveness depends on sorrow, one being sorry for one’s evil act. Repentance means the resolve to not do the same act in the future. Absolution is the theological concept that God’s forgiveness is requested and granted. And penance is the punishment one must pay as retribution . . . to make up for the consequences of the evil act.<sup>30</sup>

Reagan suggests (correctly) that this radical picture of forgiveness is based on Ricoeur’s Protestant presuppositions, which do not make forgiveness dependent upon penance. Yet, as we have seen, Ricoeur’s interpretation of the cross is also a departure from his Protestant presuppositions. Ricoeur finds a paradox in forgiveness—a tension between love and justice. But this unconditional forgiveness is unstable because it is not grounded in the conviction that there is justice, that we can forgive because God has and will set things right. This is not justice and forgiveness grounded in an impersonal *il y a*, but in the cross. The cross can be read as God’s answer to Jankélévitch’s despairing admission, which Ricoeur cites: “Forgiveness is as strong as evil, but evil is as strong as forgiveness” (MHF 474). I am loath to trivialize the 

*[p. 194]* ^pg211

testimony of any who have suffered horrors in the course of human history. We need to hear these testimonies. The cross is not an intellectual resolution to the problem of evil, but the intervention by which God sets things right. This conviction is a hope, a wager that ultimately evil and forgiveness are not equally strong. The forgiveness that overcomes evil is neither cheap nor facile. In the words of Desmond Tutu: “It is costly. Reconciliation is not an easy option. It cost God the death of his Son.”<sup>31</sup> A deeper hermeneutic of the cross could show that forgiveness is based on the cross as the unity of love and justice, rather than a paradoxical tension between the two.

### Religion, Violence, and the Fundamental

Violence is a problem within the interpretation of particular religious traditions. It is also a problem between religious traditions. What are the sources of religious violence? Religious apologists often contend that acts of religious violence are clearly a deviation from the true heart of religion, which is essentially peaceful. This defense is a little too easy and does not convince Ricoeur, who thinks that the potential for violence runs deeper than religious apologists will often admit. The hermeneutical task is to understand the source of this potential violence. How is it possible that the “religious impulse” can be such a source of good, yet be “deflected” into damage and destruction? As Ricoeur puts the question: “What is the turning point, the point at which the religious impulse is perverted?” (WMT, 265, 269).

Ricoeur’s recurring explanation is that religion is constituted by a word that is anterior and superior to the self. It comes from outside. Violence arises with the attempt to capture and control this word, to master and possess it in an exclusionary or authoritarian way. His answer is to appeal to the transcendent source of this word, which we cannot control, and instead to point to a hermeneutical orientation in which we recognize the finitude and fragility of our interpretations.

One of the earliest versions of this account of religious violence is Ricoeur’s 1950s essay “Truth and Falsehood,” where he writes that Christianity is founded by an “absolute event,” the revelation of the Truth as the Word of God that is attested in scripture, preaching, and theology. This Truth has authority over the individual believer and the community, and it thus calls for both subordination and self-transcendence. This authority is originally a call to obedience, not an opportunity to commandeer that authority for oneself, but in wielding this authority there is a temptation for religious institutions to practice falsehood in order to control and protect the truth (HT 180). In contrast to legitimate ecclesial authority, Ricoeur describes this religious authoritarianism in terms of “clerical” power (HT 177), lamenting the way 

*[p. 195]* ^pg212

“clerical violence perverts the non-coercive authority of the Word” and pretends to “recapitulate truth at all its levels” (RJPR 641). Instead, the Christian should desire “the rupturing of this violent unity of truth” (HT 181), while at the same time desiring a genuine recapitulation in which all truth is united. This recapitulation is neither a historical reality nor even possibility, but an eschatological hope. “Eschatology is the cure of clericalism” (HT 191). This eschatological unity is deferred until the end of all things, which means that in our pre-eschatological existence we encounter truth in the conflict of interpretations. This condition of conflict is not tragic, but simply part of our hermeneutical situation as finite creatures. This eschatological horizon also motivates the hope that there is ultimately a unity of truth and meaning, rather than reality being ultimately tragic or absurd.

Ricoeur makes some important additions to this account in the 1960s with his essay “Violence and Language,” which examines the relation between these two terms in politics, in philosophy, and poetry. The suggestion that there is violence in *poetry* might come as a surprise, especially since Ricoeur takes cues from Heidegger, for whom “poetic language emerges from a certain opening that allows some aspect of Being to appear.” The poet gives himself “up to meaning,” in an act of obedience that is not mere submission but a “following,” a “letting-be.”<sup>32</sup> This obedience is precisely why “the poetic verb” is the “most advanced element” in nonviolent discourse. In poetry most of all, language is not an instrument at our disposal; instead, language has us at its disposal (VL 36).

How, then, can poetic discourse be violent? According to Ricoeur, there is a “violent particularity” in the way the poet brings Being to the word, establishing “a being in its Being” and preserving its openness. This openness is “a capture,” even a “poetic abduction.” This violence is subtle rather than overt, but the poet is nonetheless “the violent man who forces things to speak” in a violent particularity (VL 37). This account of linguistic violence might sound more like Levinas or Derrida than Ricoeur, who typically has a more positive estimation of finitude and the particularity of meaning than either of those thinkers.<sup>33</sup> Ricoeur is not, however, arguing that particularity is inherently violent. That would make the mistake of confusing finitude with fault, a distinction Ricoeur draws carefully and clearly. The finitude and particularity of discourse is not equivalent to fault, nor is it essentially fallen or complicit in violence. We might, however, say that it is *fallible* and easily inclines toward violence. This is even true of poetry, which might strike many modern readers as the least violent mode of discourse. What Ricoeur proposes, in response to the problem of language and violence, is a discourse characterized by the effort for mutual recognition and moral responsibility for the other. This also means giving up the violent claim that one single mode of discourse “exhausts the realm of speech.” In Ricoeur’s words: “To be 

*[p. 196]* ^pg213

non-violent in discourse is to respect the plurality and diversity of languages” (VL 40–41). This posture allows us to glimpse the possibility of conflict without violence, that is, a nonviolent conflict of interpretations.

Ricoeur puts this account of nonviolent language to work in the 1990s in his writings on religious plurality and violence. This is an apt extension given Ricoeur’s point that different religions are like different languages (ELRD 130). The potential for religious violence is analogous to the potential for poetic violence, insofar as religion seeks to capture and contain the fundamental within a finite discourse. As the poet is to Being, so religion is to the fundamental*.* The fundamental, the reader will recall, is Ricoeur’s term for the *Abgrund*, the groundless ground that is the very “source of life itself,” a source that “all receive, but that no one can encompass.” This source is “undivided in its outflow,” but it is “divided in its receptacles” (RB 34–35). This division gives rise to mimetic rivalry, to a jealous attempt to capture the fundamental source and contain it, to lock it up. If religious particularity is not inherently violent, there is a latent potential for violence: a violation of the revelation itself in our attempt to capture and control it, as well as a violence in relation to other religions and their understanding of the fundamental.

Here Ricoeur’s early theme of fallibility returns, as he draws on the ontology of disproportion from *Fallible Man*. The fundamental is infinite, but the human capacity is finite. Thus when the fundamental reveals itself, religion seeks to capture and contain it in creeds, canons, and institutions; but the fundamental exceeds any capacity for reception and overflows every attempt to capture or contain it (WMT 269; RSV 4, 8). The fundamental “passes through” the various religious languages, but it also remains beyond them (WMT 273). The potential for violence emerges when these religious languages seek closure. Ricoeur associates this “phenomenon with the condition of finitude, by which the \[capable human being\], for whom religion is intended, imposes his limited capacity on the unlimited one that visits him” (WMT 269). Ricoeur puts it in darker, more dramatic terms in “Religion and Symbolic Violence,” where he describes the excess of the fundamental as “menacing.” This menace incites a violent attempt to protect oneself against the fundamental by containing it (RSV 4, 8).

Ricoeur thus detects an inclination toward violence “at the very heart of religious conviction,”<sup>34</sup> which is why religion requires self-critique, to purge our religious traditions of violence from within. The risk of violence lies “at the very heart of strong convictions.”<sup>35</sup> Religions have a “fundamental mission to speak a Word that is beyond them,” and this conviction very easily gives rise to the drive to dominate and impose one’s beliefs on others. This can take the form of violent exclusion as well as violent inclusion, working to keep people out or to “compel them to come in” (RB 35–36). There is a bitter irony in the fact that religion has the capacity to regenerate human beings, and 

*[p. 197]* ^pg214

yet also has such a capacity for destruction. As Ricoeur observes, the “height of hope” often coincides with the “height of violence” (CC 154–55).

### A Hermeneutical Response to Religious Violence

In addition to sober self-critique, Ricoeur also prescribes the practice of “interconfessional hospitality” between religious traditions (ELRD 146). This hospitality consists in projects of translation with the goal of achieving mutual understanding and recognition between religions.<sup>36</sup> Ricoeur is not proposing a superficial comparativism in which everyone discovers he or she really believes the same thing—a view he rejects much like he rejects the Enlightenment ideal of a pure rational Religion. Rather, he wagers that “truth resides in depth,” so if different religious traditions go deep into the imaginative resources of their own respective traditions, they will gain deeper understanding than if they only seek surface-level agreement. At one point Ricoeur calls for “mutual recognition of the best aspects” of the world’s religions: “My hope is that each religion might renounce any claim to possess the truth and express instead its wish to be part of the truth—in this way recognizing that outside of itself exists another part of the truth” (WMT 272).

This renunciation goes along with the cultivation of religious toleration, which has successive levels. At the first level, the believer disapproves of the other’s beliefs, but tolerates them for lack of power to prevent them. There is still a potentially violent rivalry lurking here. At the second level, the believer is convinced of the truth of his or her own belief and the falsity of the other’s, but recognizes the other’s right to believe it. At the third level, the believer adopts a perspectivism, admitting the other may also have “part of the truth,” though it is unclear what that might be. At the highest level, the believer recognizes that his or her conviction is grounded in a source he or she does not control, and which “exceeds \[her\] capacity for reception and comprehension.” The other therefore may have access to aspects of the fundamental that I do not see. This is a difficult belief to maintain, since it means remaining “anchored” in the soil of one’s own religious convictions, on a crest where the believer is also “open laterally to other beliefs, other convictions” (RB 39).

Ricoeur notes that one difficulty with this belief is that one could fall from the crest of conviction down the slope into skepticism, in which religious differences are indifferent and all beliefs become worthless (RB 39). Ricoeur does not want that. But what about the opposite move: negate religious differences and affirm them all in a pluralist vision in which every religion is leading down the same path? This is a common view, and Ricoeur seems to support it by writing that “the idea of a source of life that fragments according 

*[p. 198]* ^pg215

to the receptive capacity of the containers . . . evokes something like a fundamental religion” (RB 36). He also observes that religious phenomena like the feeling of absolute dependence (Schleiermacher), the “anteriority of a creative energy with respect to my desire,” and “unconditional trust” (Tillich) all seem to translate easily between religious languages. Nevertheless, Ricoeur maintains that “it is necessary to give up the dream of a super-religion” (RB 36).

Likewise, Ricoeur is wary of attempts to unify the various religions in a universal ethic. He makes this point in dialogue with Hans Küng regarding the “Manifesto Toward a Planetary Ethic” that arose from the 1993 Parliament of World Religions. Ricoeur’s concern is that the only way toward such an ethic is through the distinctive path of each religion’s texts and traditions; otherwise any moral consensus is going to be abstract and possibly empty. The different religions can agree on the prohibition against killing, for instance, but have different sources for this conviction.<sup>37</sup> 

Ricoeur is thus not arguing that all religions are essentially saying the same thing in different language. As he puts it in his conversation with Küng, there is something “absolutely unique” in the Christian kerygma of Incarnation, Cross, and Resurrection.<sup>38</sup> Similarly, Ricoeur states elsewhere that he has

the gravest doubts about the nature, even the identity, that could be posited outside of Scripture of that God who would be Allah here and Yahveh elsewhere; for I believe that the naming of God is itself implied in the constitution of each of the “languages” mentioned, so that the word “God” is a sort of suspended word and one that designates perhaps something that others would not call God. (CC 146)

Ricoeur’s point here is well taken. Consider, for example, the difference between divine unity in Hinduism and Judaism. In a vivid passage from chapter 11 of the *Bhagavad-Gita*, Krishna reveals his “immortal Self” to Arjuna: “a million divine forms, with an infinite variety of color and shape,” “an infinite number of faces,” and “all the manifold forms of the universe united as one” (11:5–15). Krishna is one reality, encompassing “all the gods and every kind of living creature” within itself as one divine totality. Contrast this with the idea of divine unity in Judaism, expressed most pointedly in [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^6-4|Deuteronomy 6:4]], the *Shema*: “Hear O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is One.” Following as it does the Ten Commandments in chapter 5, this notion of divine unity clearly emphasizes the singular unity of God, to the exclusion of all other gods and images. 

Two points are worth noting here. First, these descriptions are mutually exclusive. Suppose we consider Krishna’s all-encompassing reality to contain the Jewish idea of God: This rules out the truth-claims of Jewish monotheism—and vice versa. Second, if Krishna and YHWH are mutually exclusive, it is not sufficient to attribute this difference to the recipients of the revelation. 

*[p. 199]* ^pg216

Both Krishna and Yahweh reveal themselves as *this* rather than *that*. These are not generic experiences of the fundamental that are subsequently interpreted in doctrinal specifics. The revelations themselves place limits on the way in which they can be received, resulting in a mutually exclusive particularity. If we attribute this limiting solely to the recipients of the revelation, then we volatilize the idea of revelation by subordinating it to our preconceptions of the divine—in this case, by supposing the groundless ground, the source of life, is *really* the ultimate truth about the divine nature.

Is that what Ricoeur is saying? In “Difficult Belief” Ricoeur appears to be endorsing Schelling’s vision of the groundless ground, the source of life, yet he also adds that “Schelling’s groundless ground does not establish a specific religion, but instead “remains a limit-idea for understanding” (RB 37). If this is a limit idea, could it also be a Kantian postulate for which we can hope—namely, that at the end of all things every religion will participate in this fundamental source? 

In some of his earliest essays Ricoeur considers this question regarding the philosophical plurality of philosophical systems and schools. Is it possible to reconcile these seemingly incommensurable truth claims? Ricoeur proposes a conception of truth as “a milieu,” an atmosphere of light—an expression he finds Marcel as well as the Heideggerian clearing (*Lichtung*) in which being unconceals itself. To be in the truth is to participate in being, in Ricoeur’s term to “bathe” in a milieu, to encounter a shared reality we participate in ontologically: an openness, a clearing, a *lumen natural*. Plurality is therefore not the ultimate reality of things, “nor is misunderstanding the ultimate possibility of communication” (HT 55). Plurality and misunderstanding certainly do pervade the world of philosophy, but these are penultimate rather than ultimate realities.

The temptation that follows from such a view, Ricoeur notes, is to rush ahead and try to bring about this unity of truth here and now, as a present actuality. Although the manifestation of being grounds the unity of philosophical questioning, it is not possible to contain it within a single unifying discourse or within a closed system of truth. This pretension is the great temptation of philosophical system-building, of which Hegel’s is the most ambitious example. Instead of saying “I *have* the truth,” Ricoeur proposes an ontological hope: “I hope I am *within* the bounds of Truth.” I hope my thinking participates in being, “bathes in” the ontological milieu in which truth is manifest. This hope is not merely wishful thinking. It has a basis: “Ontological hope has its *signs* and its *guarantees*.” The more one explores the depths of the great philosophies, following their logic to their deepest insights, the more one is struck by the sense of a fundamental ontological unity:

One is proportionately rewarded with the joy of hitting the essential, as if by plunging oneself into the denseness of a philosophy, along with its difficulties, 

*[p. 200]* ^pg217

its intentions and its refusals, one thereby experienced its inexpressible consonance with every other philosophy. In this way, one may reach the conviction that Plato, Descartes, and Kant are animated by the same being. This is how I understand the profound saying of Spinoza: “the more we understand individual objects, the more we understand God.” (HT 55)

“But,” Ricoeur quickly adds, “this consonance cannot become systematized or thematized.” One gets a glimpse, a promise, a basis for hope in the ultimate unity of being, but here and now this ontological hope must remain an eschatological hope.

These comments sound a lot like Ricoeur’s claim that religious truth “resides in depth.” Does he propose a similar eschatological hope for the ultimate unity of the divine, in which the plurality of religious discourse participates? It might seem so: Just as philosophical plurality participates in the unconcealment of being, we might expect that religious plurality participates in the manifestation of the fundamental. What is interesting is that the later Ricoeur shows no real interest in this question. Instead, his late thinking shifts away from questions about the eschatological hope and focuses on living up to death here and now. This hope is not directed toward the end of all things, but toward the life of those who live on after us. 

Given this shift, it is hard to avoid the conclusion that Ricoeur sees this ungrounded ground, the “mystical fount of the fundamental,”<sup>39</sup> as the deepest truth about the divine. Is this the best way to think about God? Is this a good way to resist the mimetic rivalry that motivates religious violence? Perhaps, but we might remember that religious belief is a fragile, fallible thing. Does this mean it must also be half-hearted? Ricoeur would say no, but there is a danger that this appeal to the fundamental will all too easily relativize religious conviction. At his best Ricoeur helps us to see that entering the conflict of interpretations does not require giving up one’s convictions. Weak convictions and an amorphous spirituality may seem the height of virtue in relatively benign circumstances, but in the face of real evil they are often exposed as powerless and subject to the manipulation of more malign power—whether the fiat of rulers or the coercive pressure of the crowd. True humility is not equivalent to weak convictions, but is instead grounded in strong convictions about the reality of one’s condition vis-à-vis God and other humans, and a truth that is not at one’s own disposal but instead is anterior and superior to the self. If we are to cultivate a conflict without violence, it will require deep resources internal to these religious traditions. As Ricoeur puts it, we need to find “at the heart of my conviction that which condemns and breaks the moment of violence from within these convictions.”<sup>40</sup>

What might these resources be within Ricoeur’s own Christian tradition? This is where we can recall our earlier point that the answer to mimetic 

*[p. 201]* ^pg218

violence is the cross. Not, mind you, the thinned-out Gnosis of Girard. Nor the violent interpretation that sees the cross as the sign of military victory, à la Constantine. The cross has been a symbol of violent conquest and racism, but that is a return to the meaning of the cross before Christ transformed it, when it was a symbol of violent power and oppression. It was the Romans, after all, who used crucifixion as an object lesson to demonstrate their imperial might. Christ overcame this violence by taking it upon himself. The cross is God’s decisive *no* to violence, overcoming injustice by bearing it within himself and establishing God’s justice, making things right in the world. 

The triumph of the cross is not based on my own capabilities, but is a pure act of grace and forgiveness. As such, the cross leaves me no basis on which to boast of my own *dynamis* or *energeia*. Nothing I do merits this grace. As such, it undermines any claim I might make against others, or any impulse I might have to wield the cross like a sword against others.

The cross shows us who God is: a God of unconditional, superabundant love. The cross shows us who we are: incapable of making things right between ourselves and God. The cross shows us a God who is just, who can be trusted because he has made, and will make, things right in the world. The cross also shows us a God of superabundant hospitality: arms stretched out to embrace the whole world.<sup>41</sup> This is how the cross makes us capable of forgiveness and hospitality. The actual, physical resurrection vindicates the crucified Jesus, showing that forgiveness and hospitality are not naïve acts of wishful thinking, an idealistic but ultimately futile example to follow, but that they are actually in accord with the most fundamental reality: God has reconciled the world to himself, not through military conquest but by suffering love. These are the deep sources of nonviolence within the Christian tradition. This is the basis on which we might fulfill Jesus’ command—to love our enemies, and maybe even love our neighbors as ourselves.
