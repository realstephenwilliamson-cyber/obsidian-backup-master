Interestingly, Reckwitz also works with binary codes. His criteriology primarily juxtaposes sensory and non-sensory forms of perception. They include the natural capacities of sense perception and feeling (“Empfinden”) versus formal logical thought and cognitive search for information as part of an overall package of aesthetic and non-aesthetic forms of practice. Religious Practices and Literatures as Perception-Generating 

##### 1.7.5 Religious Practices and Literatures as Perception-Generating Networks

The comparison in the chapters on medieval Europe and India has already demonstrated that in a comparable media situation, similar religious styles emerged but at the same time also marked cultural differences. For example, the separation of the inner and the outer, which has been observed in the religious discourse on late medieval Europe, never occurred in India in such a radical manner characterizing the entire culture. One thesis was that in Sanskrit Hinduism binary pairs such as orality and literacy, mythos and logos, abstraction and concretization, etc. do not represent strict dichotomies, but instead co-exist and pervade each other. Another thesis immediately associated with this one was that the phonocentricity of the sonic life-world encouraged paradigms both of formalistic abstraction and of participatory fusion. These theses can be systematized in a more differentiated manner with Reckwitz’ criteriology of aesthetic and non-aesthetic practices, attitudes and forms of “habitus.” As already outlined, the cultural sociologist Reckwitz allocates the following distinct fields to the two poles: sensory perception versus formal logical thought; experience for its own sake and pleasurable devotion to the moment of “absolute present” versus activism; conscious evocation of inner events versus cognitive search for information; freedom from purpose versus orientation on purpose; creativity versus compliance with rules; overstepping norms versus orientation on norms; orientation on affect versus minimization of affect; imagination and semioticization versus everyday realism; and the ontology of play with options versus the ontology of order.

Interestingly, Reckwitz’ dichotomy nonetheless correlates more or less with the two main modes of acoustic piety that we have elaborated. However, according to Reckwitz, only the devotional mode and the “religious reader” (in Griffith’s sense) come under the heading of “aesthetic,” while the rule-based mode of Veda recitation would be non-aesthetic. Yet this caveat is hardly tenable in view of the special sound quality that differentiates Veda recitation from other recitations. Vedic compositions show that even orientation on rules can be very aesthetic; Staal was justified in comparing them with Bach’s fugues. The primary aspect is indeed action oriented on purpose and norms, and less the search for feeling and increasing affect. Affects actually tend to be disturbing factors because of the strict rule-orientation. Alertness is important, not self-forgetfulness. On the other hand, though, the rules of phonetics

illustrate that conformity with rules and emotionality are by no means mutually exclusive and even creativity is not absent. Creativity is even especially noticeable in the field of ever-new applications, in hermeneutics and in re-codings. The Veda, as the prototype for audible texts and their power, has more to do with sensory perception than did some later forms of devotion or internalized reading techniques, and contains an extreme semioticization of reality. One should remember the idea of the Veda as the origin and foundation of the world, as the primeval principle that goes back even beyond meaning. The Veda was regarded by its actors as so sacred that Śūdras were not even allowed to come within earshot of the Veda. The Purāṇas, which forbade this, thus supported the cognitive and normative moral order prescribed by the Veda — or which they project into it, initiate and perpetuate. But interestingly they also reflect the changeability of this order, by setting themselves up (within the doctrine of the cyclical ages of the world) as a new Veda for the present day, thus taking away the Veda’s monopoly on holy hearing. In this way the Purāṇas created their own forcefield of holy hearing that no longer excluded Śūdras and, moreover, was expanded in a multi-sensory manner (through performance practices, icons, etc.). In the words of Reckwitz, this intensified and enhanced the sensory perceptive apparatus and a positive structuring of affectivities. 

However, Reckwitz paid too little attention to rites. In rites, thoughts and actions, aesthetics and the logic of usefulness are always intimately linked. Rites usually have the special feature of fusing both poles: they are rule and purpose-based physical sensory action that reflects, structures and generates symbolic orders, and at the same time they bring about situative presence, momentariness, imagination and affects. Ritual efficiency is created not least via the performativity organized for the senses. 

In India the sonic culture, as a specialized culture of performance, ensured that also aesthetic aspects in Reckwitz’ stricter sense were and remain very much present. Much of what he stated was a typically modern form of aesthetic attitudes and subjectivity and explains by means of replacement of the “bourgeois writing subject” by the digital personality (characterized above all by an ontology of play) is found in medieval India in its own culturally specific form. For example, in the Śivaitic representation of how the world emerged from Śiva’s play dīlā) there is an ontology of creative, purpose-free play that is extended to the whole of reality. For Abhinavagupta (ca. 975 to 1025 AD), the Tantric philosopher and theoretician of aesthetics, the ontological play model of autonomous freedom actually became the ideal of ultimate liberation. Interestingly, this medieval Indian thinker’s concept of aesthetics is practically identical to Reckwitz’ “modern” one, but it has both profane and religious characteristics. This interpretation was hugely powerful not only in scholarly culture and poetics. It also rubbed off on mantra techniques and Viṣṇuitic devotionalism. 

A connection between the media, subjectivizations and aesthetic styles therefore appears to be unmistakable in India, too. Phonocentricity already seemed to naturally bring about an aestheticization of reality and this was associated with certain forms of experience, emotional response and generation of affect. These are not merely subjective attitudes, but also collective ideas, and Abhinavagupta is a powerful exponent for them, but not the only one. We therefore consciously speak of “sonic culture,” but not of a sonic subject (in the singular). This is forbidden not only due to the transition from the Vedic world to the Purāṇic and Tantric one, but also due to the variability of the aesthetic subjectivizations within a common field such as the Tantra. What Reckwitz terms aesthetic and non-aesthetic attitudes and practices, exist here side by side — often with a seamless transition. 

As Tantrism in medieval India established itself as the religious mainstream, we find as a consequence extreme Tantric forms being domesticated and popularized. In addition to clan-based initiation traditions of the search for liberation there also arise bourgeois mantra rites in which the “normal personality adapted to society” (Reckwitz, “das normale, marktförmige Subjekt”) uses the mantras in a purposeful way to overcome all types of everyday difficulties. But even in this domesticated, pragmatic, everyday mantra usage — just as in early Tantra — the logic of usefulness (assimilation of the deity’s supernatural powers) and the purpose-less search for the absolute present (liberation from the world) are by no means mutually exclusive. In the early period they were often associated with conscious, in some cases extreme, overstepping of norms, whereas in the later period adaptation to the norms is the general rule. One can see a creative re-shaping and at the same time rule-based continuation of the old, culturally typical notion of the salvatic power and efficacy of sacred sounds. #### 1.8 Literati in India and Europe

The third space — sonic culture — produced its own aesthetic styles, subjectivizations and forms of rationalization, as the following comparison of the literati in India and Europe will show. Antiquity and the Middle Ages, the heydays of Sanskrit production, will provide the primary timeframe. Indian scientific culture demonstrates how much the two poles characterized briefly as abstraction and fusion, or emancipation and participation, can overlap. 

Rationality and science formed an important topic in the orality-literacy debate over India. Goody had claimed that as formations of abstraction, analytical thought and rational systems are already very much present in Indian literature at a very early stage, we must be dealing with a literary culture. Yet India in fact shows how deterministic his conclusions are, which correctly triggered criticism from both Staal and Falk. In some ways Goody made incorrect assumptions and also made a forced interpretation of some of the Indian material to suit his theory, or even read something into it that was not there. This applies in particular to his treatment of the literati, who were by no means all Brahmanic priests. Certainly one cannot deny that the Brahmins had a more or less predominant position in religious literatures and dharma works, and in particular the sphere of the Veda was their special privilege. But literary and scientific work was never restricted just to them, even if it was of course largely a culture of the élite and the upper class. 

Staal’s and Falk’s objections 10 Goody’s literacy thesis, which were critical of ideology, were important. The history of science is full of examples showing Greece to be the first and only source of logic, philosophy and science, with European history as the universal yardstick. This distorted the impartial view of other cultures. All too often, Hinduism was construed as an “irrational other.” For example, in India one

*[p. 226]* ^pg250

found “Eastern mysticism” at every turn, but no philosophy — an Orientalistic distortion that Tola, Dragonetti and others rightly contested.\*® One can agree with them that it makes sense to restrict comparisons to continuities and differences in philosophical thought and argumentation up to the sixteenth century.“ But in India we find some mental figures, like those appearing in Kant or Fichte, and problems of linguistics or linguistic philosophy, like those discussed in twentieth century Europe, long before the sixteenth century. So there are many comparable points in the history of rational thought systems, going beyond historical and cultural dissonances. Furthermore, Tola and Dragonetti are right to question whether Western thought traditions were always purely rational. 

Now, one can accuse Goody’s theory of a lot of things, but certainly not of failing to recognize India’s rationality. However, the logocentric and Eurocentric “habitus” is noticeable, which even the literacy critics have perpetuated. Staal criticized Eurocentricity but argued in a logo-centric manner. So far, the discussion has been dominated by the subjects of abstract and logical thought, purposeful pragmatism and cognitively neutral propositions. In the following we will take account of the effects of aesthetic, sensory perceptive aspects in the scientific practices of the Indian literati, which go beyond the pure informational value of scientific statements and characterize it at the same time. These not only intellectual, not only purposeful aspects that automatically appear more on the scene with our focus on sonality, performance and media practice as aesthetic practice, are tremendously important for generating affect, that is, the emotional relationship to the object. They reveal a scientific “habitus” that binds intellectuality, sensory perception and emotionality far more strongly together than the Western model of science does. Their prime aim, however, was not necessarily transparency and didactics as in the West. In particular the aesthetic aspects (or the missing aesthetic aspects) illustrate significant cultural variations in the history of rationality and science. They reveal that science never produces solely neutral statement systems, and that instead scientific forms of “habitus” obey and reproduce symbolic orders.

##### 1.8.1 Audible Abstractions: Pânini's Grammar as Trendsetter of Science :

Audible Abstractions: Pāṇini’s Grammar as Trendsetter of Science 

Indian scientific practice reveals different aesthetic styles and forms of subjectivization than those in Western intellectual culture of both the past and the present. This begins with Pāṇini and continues in new formations. The Indian history of science accorded great importance to grammatical tradition. This is on the one hand because India was a global scientific pioneer in linguistics, and on the other because in India grammar was the first scientific culture that opened up fields independent of religion, even though it arose in the context of the Vedic sciences. Grammar was dedicated to the formal analysis of the natural structures of language and developed concepts that today one would call symbolic logic and linguistics. It was the paradigmatic analytical science of the Indian intellectual tradition and had a defining influence on the methodology and presentation of scientific representation. It also partly inspired the development of modern linguistics and phonetics. 

It is typical in Pāṇini, the father of science in India, that abstraction and phonocentricity are closely tied. The analytical arrangement of the Sanskrit alphabet and the technical codes (pratyāāhāra) Pāṇini developed are systematically ordered grids of sound serving to establish a phonetic basis for precise rules of word formation. Opinions may differ on whether the rules of formation set out in approximately 4000 short aphorisms (sūtras) required a system of writing or not (for the above-mentioned reasons of linguistic standardization, we believe that this was indeed the case), but this does not mean that the technical devices forming their basis also required visual notations. Strictly speaking, the “alphabet” consists not of “letters,” but of an acoustically structured series of sounds of the Sanskrit language, whose system becomes apparent via hearing and not via seeing. Pāṇini’s technical algebra-like Sūtra language, here the grammatical rules, also largely comprises material that is acoustically (rather than visually) memorable. In particular, the underlying code system, the fourteen pratyāhāras that are valid for all grammatical operations are non-semantic short formulas — a brilliant re-ordering of the sounds of the alphabet, combined with acoustic terminal signs. When recited, this especially concise tonal material leaves very specific impressions in the memory. Typically, the fourteen sound codes are learned and kept in memory by being recited vocally in a similar manner to the Veda. In Pāṇini’s grammar, the physical quality of the sound therefore has first an eminent value in scientific abstraction and, second, the aesthetic aspect is not absent from his strict, highly abstract meta-linguistic scientific system. This is shown not only by the recitation of the pratyāhāras. \[1 a stroke of genius, Pānṇini hid in his system a poetic magical, auspicious symbolism by making his 4000

As liturgical speech was perceived to be the most perfect literary form of expression, beautiful form, cryptic, complicated speech and the creation of an aesthetic numinous aura were not seen as foreign to science but, on the contrary, were celebrated. The liturgical model explains why they have special powers of persuasion and legitimacy even in the sciences that are independent of religion. The literary form of expression in the early scientific tradition in India reflects many aspects that Reckwitz regards as specifically “aesthetic” (as opposed to non-aesthetic transmission of information): not only purpose, but also aspects of purposelessness and of enjoyment, not only compliance with rules and standardization, but also poietological qualities of taking action, creative shaping, playing with language, sounds and multiple meanings, and a conscious semioticization of the actual conditions going beyond practical use. As science was conducted this way, not only did the liturgical model performatively take a profane turn, but there was also a sacralization of profane science. 

The rhetorical function of complicated, poetic and oracle-like speech is first and foremost one of showing off one’s cleverness, of legitimation and of persuasion, but at the same time it is an expression of creativity and a “technology of the self.” This aesthetic style (one can also speak of scientific subjectivization) communicates on the meta-linguistic level very much more than mere scientific statements. Since liturgical speech is regarded as the most perfect literary form of expression, the scientists speak, as it were, like a sacred text. The power of knowledge of the literati participates in the symbolic nimbus of the original Vedic text, and simultaneously asserts and strengthens its own profane autonomy and authority. This means that traditionel Indian scientists accord the presentation of their objects a symbolic value and characterize them in this way — a value going beyond the objects?’ practical use. The tendency to sacralize scientific rationality is certainly not found in equal measure throughout all scientific literatures, but almost all scientific systems do have a participatory aspect. Ranging from grammar and classical philosophy to traditional medicine, they claim to bring deliverance. Science, too, is surrounded by an aura of the sacred, of the extraordinary, non-verbal and numinous.

The popular story of how Pāṇini’s technical pratyāhāra codes arose from Śiva’s hand drum is a “telling” example. One could not express it with a better metaphor: Genius and knowledge/science have their place in the heavens, they are something “extraterrestrial” and mysterious, and at the same time the perfect order of the grammatical system reflects the cosmic order and its transcendent origin. The grammarian and linguistic philosopher Bhartrhari (5th cent. 

AD), one of India’s most brilliant scientists, elaborated this thought in a very rationally reflexive way in his linguistic metaphysical discussions on the Śabda-Brahman (literally “Word-Brahman”) that — as the language itself or the language principle — is the dynamic, communicative origin of the whole of existence. At the same time Bhartrhari developed a profane scientific theory of language and an approach for cultural studies, which still seem to be exceptionally modern even today. Looking at his overall system, one can say that he uses the concept of Brahman as a holistic metaphor. His Śabda-Brahman is not tied to any kind of religious confession, except the traditional grammarians’ statement that grammar brings deliverance, which in Bhartrhari’s writings is given a rationalistic turn that grammar and linguistics say something about the structure of reality and thought and how both depend on linguistic expression. We will come back to Bhartrhari not only in the History section, but also in this Methodology section, because he is one of those special “strategic agents” who initiated “radical aesthetic movements” and therefore formed aesthetic orientations and subjectivizations, and in this case what we call a “universal myth.” 

To summarize the observations on how science was conducted in traditional India, one can say that intellectual socialization and scientific style formation were greatly prefigured and marked by the “liturgical model.” Owing to the liturgical model, enigmatic, cryptic and convoluted speech was not avoided in Hindu India even in science. On the contrary, it was cultivated and celebrated, as it possesses a special cultural value and authority, and also ties one in emotionally. Not clear statements, but perfectly formed speech and poietological qualities of action occupy the foreground and have efficacy. As a rule, purposeless aspects are always woven into scientific statements. The concise aesthetic style combines sensory perception and experience with formal rationality, creativity with the logic of usefulness, a semioticization of reality with objective realism and an ontology of play with an ontology

*[p. 247]* ^pg271

of order. Playing with multiple meanings (almost invariably at the beginnings of the works) often ensures that something “numinous” comes into view “behind” the signs and an embedding in a larger “meaning whole” (“Sinnganzes”), and thus at the same time one sacralizes one’s own scientific activity and independent reasoning. Using these aesthetic performances, even works of natural science function as strong “networks of perception and affect-generation” (“Perzeptions- und Affektgenerierungsnetzwerke”). They never solely bring about affectively neutral assimilation of knowledge, but also authenticate the affective acquisition of the object. All of this is reinforced by the type of communication and personal transmission in the teacher-pupil relationship which, as outlined above, also reproduces the “liturgical model” of authoritative speech. Both aspects of the “liturgical model” bring about distinct styles of meaning and a generation of affect that are distinct from Western ways of teaching and conducting science. 

The following quotation reveals just how crass the discrepancy is felt to be, and not only today but also by the Muslim astronomer Al-Biruni (1030) when he traveled in India. Al-Biruni had been trained in the methodology and philosophy of the Greek mathematicians and reports on his scientific exchange with Indian colleagues as follows: 

“I began to show \[Indian scholars\] the elements on which this science rests, to point out to them some rules of logical deduction and the scientific methods of all mathematics, and then they flocked together round me from all parts, wondering, and most eager to learn from me. I can only compare their mathematical and astronomical literature, as far as I know it, to a mixture of pearl shells and sour dates, or of pearls and dung, or of costly crystals and common pebbles. Both kinds of things are equal in their eyes, since they cannot raise themselves to the methods of a strictly scientific deduction.”

The Platonic “original texts” of the Hellenistic world are of a very didactic nature. The model is maieutics (“art of midwifery”). The skills and abilities of the teacher (Socrates) consist of explaining something and using apposite questions to draw out from the partner the correct (latent) answers and insights that he had hitherto not even been aware of. This makes great demands on the teacher; it is all up to him. However, what links the two participants is the Jogos inherent in both of them. The teacher is distinguished only by the fact that the Eros of the logos is already in him — he knows that he does not know enough, while the dialog partner believes that he already knows everything. When Socrates asks his young partner what virtue is, this does not mean that he — Socrates — is especially virtuous or is otherwise especially well qualified to know a great deal about virtue. This communication situation is therefore completely different from that in the liturgical model, where all the knowledge is in the possession of the teacher, who almost embodies it. 1.8.4 

Public Scholarly Disputes in Medieval Europe and India — Church 

##### 1.8.4 Public Scholarly Disputes in Medieval Europe and India - Church and State Politics

Even ideal types undergo historical changes and this is very evident in Europe. European cultural history rests on two major foundations, the Greek and the Judaeo-Christian heritage. Ever since Christianity became widespread in the fifth century, the Christian aspect has had enormous culture-forming influence and political power. This also had an impact on the logocentric model. This becomes very clear when we consider the public scholarly disputes in twelfth/thirteenth century Europe which were seen by Angenendt as reappearance of rationality

*[p. 251]* ^pg275

within the enchanted medieval world (see above). These rule-governed disputes were indeed thoroughly considered reflections on the Christian message and extremely rationalistic in their logical argumentation — not only when compared with medieval performance culture, but also when compared with the meditation on meaning by the early church fathers. One may be inclined to find in them a reinstatement of the “logocentric model.” The ideal type of dispute consisted of verbal argument and counter-argument between theologians, the result of which was by no means already fixed at the outset. The participants were genuinely engaged in a search for the truth through dialog. And yet, such institutionalized disputations were only a continuation of the “logocentric model” to a very slight extent. It is true that “truth” was worked out in disputatious discussion. The person presenting the better arguments won the day (at least this was the ideology). However, it was only Christian theological truths that were contested — a field that was of course prescribed and demarcated by the Church. 

Free thinking in the sciences, was not possible in Europe for hundreds of years, let alone a democratic spirit open to dialog. Science had been under the control of the Church since the spread of Christianity. Philosophy had no autonomy until right into the late thirteenth century, but instead was the “maidservant of theology.” Philosophy did not establish itself as an independent science in post-antique Europe until after the Paris Condemnations of the radical Aristoteleans at the University of Paris (1277). The departure of these “heretics,” whose teachings were characterized by naturalism and pure reason, and in some cases also a decidedly anti-Christian attitude (such as the statement that theology was based on fables), effectively drove theology and philosophy apart. Other sciences did not manage to escape the straitjacket of Church control until the Renaissance. But even then the Church’s influence remained considerable. In about 1600 the Dominican monk and natural philosopher Giordano Bruno (b. 1548) was burnt at the stake for his pantheistic world-view (the universe is God and is moved by inner forces; everything is animate), which he expounded with poetic energy. The mathematician and physicist Galileo Galilei (1564—1642) had to deny before the Inquisition that the Earth orbited the sun. Yet Galileo’ s legacy shows that the power and influence of the Church had become too weak to hold out against the emerging scientific world-view. Galileo is known for discovering the law of gravity, and stands for a mechanical explanation of how the universe works and for modern rationalism. 

In a way one can speak of a European liturgical model before modern secular culture. However, it would be more correct to speak of an ecclesiastical model, since the institution of the church very much occupied

*[p. 252]* ^pg276

the foreground — which meant not only participation, but also strong demarcation. Christianity partly continued the Hellenistic heritage and partly broke with it. To be sure it was not only logocentric. The scholastic disputes are at the fringes where a predominantly participatory orientation slowly turns into a predominantly emancipatory orientation. They were still very much part of the medieval intermediate zone of oral and written expression existing in both Europe and India at that time. Such structural similarities may explain the fact that almost identical forms of public disputes arose. In India, these disputes moved away from the participatory liturgical model and forged pronounced spaces of emancipation. However, here the parallels end. The political and social conditions in India were vastly different and this had decisive impacts on the function and role of dispute culture. In elaborating this aspect the difference between ecclesiastical model and the liturgical model becomes very clear. 

In a similar — yet distinctly different - manner in India, too, political and social situation created forms almost identical to the European disputatio, i.e. forms which could not be fed from the liturgical model because of the circumstances. These were the disputes between philosophers and theologians at the courts of the Indian rājas. Of course the liturgical model had not relevance in such disputes. Here the authority of the other person was not recognized and if one did not speak clearly, there was nothing to dispute about. So however amazingly similar such disputes were to their European equivalents in terms of their historically fully developed form, their provenance and function remained radically different. 

The origins of this culture of disputation are still very much embedded in the liturgical model. In late Vedic times, rulers sought to participate in the magical spiritual power of the Brahmins - their Brahman knowledge. To this end they invited Brahmins to their courts to engage in debate in order to win a sum of prize money or goods, so the rulers would find out who had the greatest Brahman knowledge. The victor was determined in a “last man standing” procedure. The Brahmins could put curses on one another to the effect that one who spoke against his better judgment would immediately “lose his head.” The winner was the one who managed to make the others keep silent by use of this magic. This ingenious procedure was also necessary because in these early disputes there were no adjudicators or moderators. Such a role could only have been filled by the king - who was of lower social standing than the Brahmins.
