## About

A Sanskrit Purana in four parts, centred on Krishna and Radha. Krishna is the supreme reality, with Vishnu, Shiva, Brahma and Ganesha one with him; Radha is his shakti and the personified root of nature (mulaprakriti).

- **Classification:** Wikipedia counts it among the major Puranas (Mahapuranas) and calls it an important Vaishnava text; Banglapedia classes it as an Upapurana. Tradition ascribes it to Vyasa.
- **Parts:** Brahma Khanda (30 chapters), Prakriti Khanda (67, on the goddesses), Ganesha or Ganapati Khanda (46) and Krishna Khanda. Wikipedia gives 131–133 chapters for the last and 274–276 in all; Banglapedia gives 131. Tradition says 18,000 verses; Wikipedia notes that manuscripts contain more.
- **Date:** Wikipedia, citing Dalal, Rocher and Hazra, dates the extant text to the 15th–16th century. Hazra thinks a version existed by 700 CE; Rocher thinks an older version was complete by the 8th–10th century. Banglapedia gives c. 8th–16th century, with the present form reached in the 16th. Motilal Banarsidass's 2022 translation calls it substantially a Krishnaite Purana of 14th–15th-century Bengal.
- **Rewriting:** Hazra notes that 15th–16th-century digest writers quote nearly 1,500 lines, only 30 of which appear in the extant text. He also holds that most chapters on social law (mixed castes, women's duties, hells) were inserted after the 16th century.
- **Place:** the extant version was probably composed in Bengal. Banglapedia reads it as a source for early medieval Bengali society: castes, land grants and vows.
- **Translations:** Shanti Lal Nagar's Sanskrit text and English translation is published by Parimal, Delhi; one library catalogue records a 2021 edition. Motilal Banarsidass's Ancient Indian Tradition and Mythology series issued the Ganesha Khanda, by Greg Bailey, as vol. 80 (2022).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says that, with the *Bhagavata Purana*, it shaped Krishna traditions and performance arts such as the Rasa Lila. It also reports H. H. Wilson's dismissal of its claim to be a Purana, and Parmeshwaranand's reading of it as a devotional text with philosophical depth.
