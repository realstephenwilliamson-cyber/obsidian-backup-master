## Chapter 3: Shamans and Bhūta Tāntrikas: A shared genealogy?

*Michael Slouber*

### 3.1 Introduction

The Bhūta Tantras (‘Treatises on Spirits’) formed an important component of early Śaiva Tantric traditions in Sanskrit in the first millennium. They may also constitute its most enigmatic category because the original scriptures are not known to have survived to the present. However, a significant body of material drawn from the Bhūta Tantras is available in summaries and excerpts digested into various compendia from, very approximately, around the turn of the second millennium. The most informative and comprehensive source, and possibly the earliest, is a Tantra called *Kriyākālaguṇottara*. Like the other compendia, it is transparent with the fact that the material it presents originated in previous Tantric scriptures. Uniquely, though, the *Kriyākālaguṇottara* presents itself as a divine digest of earlier teachings that Śiva recapitulated himself. On the basis of a comparison with parallel passages in other sources, I found that the *Kriyākālaguṇottara* represents one of the best sources for studying the early Bhūta Tantra traditions (see Slouber 2016).

The purpose of the present chapter is to investigate what, if any, relationship might exist between the beliefs and practices of the Bhūta Tantra specialist (*bhūtatāntrika*) and the ‘shamans’ of India’s tribal communities who have long specialized in a similar sort of spirit work.<sup>1</sup> By spirit work (*bhūtakarma*)<sup>2</sup> I refer to the wide array of ritual practices for which both the *bhūtatāntrika* and shamans worldwide are famous: healing, done on behalf of a community, of illnesses perceived to be caused by spirits.<sup>3</sup> Such healing is often accomplished through ‘trance work’, which is to say, the trained practitioner entering altered states of consciousness at will, in which communication with a spirit being who has the power to effect healing is established. Often this involves exorcism for patients who are possessed, and sometimes it involves the practitioner undergoing voluntary possession by a deity or spirit.

First, I sift through and evaluate the major points of debate in the long-contested etymology of the word ‘shaman’ and then touch on shamanism in prehistory. Next, the discussion broadens to engage briefly with debates in anthropology and religious studies about the appropriateness of applying the term ‘shaman’ to non-- Siberian religious specialists. Afterward, I present recent scholarship on the 

*[p. 61]* ^pg74

origins of Tantric traditions in India that has tended to emphasize the influence of figures from non-elite communities, including numerous tribal groups in the Indian subcontinent and Monsoon Asia (including South and Southeast Asia) more broadly. Finally, I present initial findings of the probable influence of shamanic traditions of India’s tribal peoples on the Bhūta Tantras.

### 3.2 The origin of a critical term

We begin with the word ‘shaman’ itself. The well-known consensus is that the word comes into English via Russian and that Russian explorers incorporated the term from the Siberian Tungusic (specifically, Evenki) word *šaman* sometime in the 16th century (Laufer 1917: 361). Since variant spellings of the word occur in all Tungusic languages, it must have been present in the proto-language. The debate hinges on whether *šaman* is original to proto-Tungusic or derived from another language family before the differentiation of proto-Tungusic into regional languages. The most common hypothesis, which got its start already in the 18th century,<sup>4</sup> is that the Tungusic word *šaman* ultimately derives from the Sanskrit word *śramaṇa* (‘ascetic, monk’). Needless to say, an Indian origin for the term ‘shaman’ would certainly bear on the debate about its appropriateness to describe Indian practitioners of spirit work. It merits careful consideration.

It is well established that the word *śramaṇa*, in the sense of an ascetic or monk, spread widely, along with the spread of Buddhism, into China, Central Asia, Persia, and even further west into Arabic and Greek-speaking lands. The word was transmitted via the Pali spelling *samaṇa* (or, even more likely, via the Gāndhārī rendering *ṣamaṇa*) into Sogdian and Parthian as *šmn*, Tocharian A as *ṣāmaṇ*, Tocharian B as *ṣamāne*, and Chinese as *sha-men* 沙門. Given this wide transmission of the Sanskrit word to neighbouring regions to the north of the Indian subcontinent, and given the close phonological similarities of Tungusic *šaman* and the various forms of *śramaṇa* in these old central Asian languages, the hypothesis that the words might share a common origin is worth pursuing.

The most substantial linguistic arguments for and against the Sanskrit-origin hypothesis all happened around 100 years ago. Chief among the dissenters was the German-American anthropologist and Sinologist Berthold Laufer. His 1917 article titled ‘Origin of the Word Shaman’ summarizes W. Schott’s points, made already in 1842, against the shared genealogy of the words; one of Schott’s main points was that Chinese strictly distinguishes the Manchu word for shaman, *saman* (translated into Chinese as *wu* 巫), and the Sanskrit word for ascetic, *śramaṇa* (translated into Chinese as *sha-men* 沙門). Laufer (1917: 367–368) also argues on the basis of J. Németh, who purportedly established beyond doubt that the Tungusic word derives from the ancient Turkic word *kam*. The rest of the article is largely rhetorical.

In 1924, the Russian anthropologist and Tungusic specialist S. M. Shirokogoroff rebutted these arguments in an article with the Indologist N. D. Mironov. Mironov opens with a discussion of accent on the word *śramaṇa* in older Sanskrit sources 

*[p. 62]* ^pg75

and how this may have influenced the forms of the word in Tocharian B sources. Shirokogoroff then takes up discussion of the Tungusic language issues. He points out that deriving the Tungusic word from Old Turkic *kam* makes no sense in the context of Tungusic phonetics since all languages of this family have the *k*  sound and would not need to alter it when supposedly borrowing the Turkic term. He then reviews at length the various forms of this word in Tungusic languages and argues that the common root is *šama*(*n*), not *šam* as proposed by Németh and Laufer (Mironov and Shirokogoroff 1924: 115).

Laufer had also flagged the supposedly impossible gulf between the hypothesized spread of the term from Tocharian to Tungusic. But we now have evidence that the word *šaman* was also known in Old Turkic/Uighur. In a 2008 article, Peter Zieme reported his discovery of two uses of the word *šaman* in Old Turkic fragmentary manuscripts dating to around the 10th century. Zieme’s first passage is a translation of a Chinese text into Old Turkic which misreads the city name Wu as a reference to shamans, which the translator notably renders in Old Turkic as *šaman*. The second manuscript uses *šaman* in a context where it clearly refers to Buddhist monks. However, this evidence is not as important as it may appear since we are concerned with the word in proto-- Tungusic, which predates the Old Turkic evidence by many centuries. The essential question is whether the latest date for this proto-language overlaps with the earliest date for the influence of Indic religion in the area where proto-- Tungusic was spoken.<sup>5</sup>

In their 2020 article ‘The Homeland of Proto-Tungusic Inferred From Contemporary Words and Ancient Genomes’, Chuan-Chao Wang and Martine Robbeets cogently argue, on the basis of the converging results of a variety of scientific methods, that the homeland of proto-Tungusic was most likely around Lake Khanka just to the northeast of the Korean Peninsula (Wang and Robbeets 2020: 5). They evaluate the most recent theories on the era in which the proto-- language diverged and arrive at a range of 600 BCE to 700 CE. This range represents the latest stage of the proto-language and the latest possible time in which the word *šama*(*n*) could have been adopted as a loan word, if indeed that is what happened.

It is well established that Buddhism reached the northern Korean Koguryŏ kingdom in 372 CE as part of a diplomatic mission from Fu Jian, the ethnically Tibetan ruler of the Former Qin dynasty.<sup>6</sup> Gina Barnes notes that Buddhism must have been known in the kingdom even before this traditional date, as Buddhist motifs appeared in Koguryŏ by the early 4th century; indeed, she estimates that there were almost 8,500 temples and 100,000 monks across the East Asian region by the end of the 5th century.<sup>7</sup> Lake Khanka, the probable homeland of proto-- Tungusic, is less than 100 miles (160 kilometres) from the border of ancient Koguryŏ. In fact, while Wang and Robbeets make no attempt at linking historical ethnic or political units to the speakers of proto-Tungusic, the area around Lake Khanka was inhabited by the small tributary kingdom known as Yilou 挹婁 in ancient Chinese records,<sup>8</sup> which disappeared from the historical record in the 6th century; might the people of Yilou and the speakers of proto-Tungusic be one and

the same? Might the breakup of the kingdom of Yilou in the 6th century correspond to the fracturing of the proto-Tungusic language?

In their 2017 article ‘Tungus-Manchurian Component in the Ethnic History and Ethnogeny of the Korean People’, A. Alexeev, P. Vitebsky, S. Alekseyva, and V. Alexeeva discuss the ethnic ties between the early Tungus-Manchurian people and the early Korean kingdom of Koguryŏ. This shared ethnicity, combined with the geographical proximity, make it highly unlikely that the speakers of proto-Tungusic were unfamiliar with Buddhism—and basic Buddhist terms like *śramaṇa—*in the 4th century CE. Shirokogoroff concluded as much, though with less evidence, already in 1924.

The earliest known written attestation of the word shaman, discussed in an article by Gábor Kósa (2007), occurs in a 12th-century Song dynasty text called *Sanchao beimeng huibian*, where the Tungusic Jurchen form of the word (*shanman*) is glossed in Chinese as meaning a shamaness (*wuyu*). Evidently, if the proto-Tungusic speakers originally understood the term to refer to Buddhist 

*[p. 64]* ^pg77

or quasi-Buddhist renouncers, the connection to Buddhism drifted in the intervening centuries. Or perhaps the formal connection was still close in the culture of the Jurchen, but the drift toward blended shamanic and Buddhist practices resulted in the author Xu Mengxin not recognizing the figure as Buddhist.

Nineteenth and 20th-century ethnographers also often failed to recognize Buddhist elements in Tungusic shamanism. Answering them, Shirokogoroff presented various examples of Tungusic spiritual figures of foreign origin, including spirits and religious roles clearly drawn from Buddhism. Among other elements, he discusses the importance of snake symbolism, brass mirrors, and certain styles of headdresses, all of which are strongly linked with Indo-Tibetan Buddhism and Tibetan Bön. In his massive 1935 book *Psychomental Complex of the Tungus*, Shirokogoroff elaborates on the aforementioned points. Significantly, he says,

We also find no early evidence as to another important condition of shamanism, namely the idea of mastering of spirits and their introduction at will. It is not mentioned by *\[Shizong\]*, *and Tungus are unanimous in asserting that originally the art of mastering spirits did not exist among them, but was practiced among their neighbors*, e.g., the \[Jurchens\], Dahurs, Buriats and Yakut, *and especially by lamas* (emphasis mine)<sup>9</sup>. He continues to explain in a note how contemporary Tungus speakers of Manchuria regard the Dalai Lama as the real master of spirits. All of this strongly discredits Laufer’s assertion that no Buddhist missionaries ever entered Tungus-- speaking regions.<sup>10</sup> The oral history recorded by Shirokogoroff also bolsters the possibility that the role of the shaman among the proto-Tungusic speakers was integrated with the role of the *śramaṇa* in Buddhism.<sup>11</sup>

Mircea Eliade’s influential 1951 book *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* largely defers to Shirokogoroff on the origin of the term. One of the most commonly cited recent rebuttals of the Indian origin theory, that of Juha Janhunen in 1986, is unsubstantial and defers to Laufer and other predecessors whose critiques had already been rebutted by Shirokogoroff—but Janhunen does not mention Shirokogoroff’s work.

None of the evidence summarized so far in this discussion decisively ends the debate. It is possible that the word *šama*(*n*) was already in use in proto-- Tungusic before Buddhism was known in the region. In such a scenario it would have to be regarded as coincidental that the proto-Tungusic word for shaman, ‘*šama*(*n*)’, and the Sanskrit-derived word for ascetic or monk circulating in East Asia, ‘*šaman/sha-men*’, sound so similar. Such coincidences are not unheard of in historical linguistics. But given the previously discussed historical points and ethnographic evidence, it is more likely that this word was in fact unknown to the proto-Tungusic speakers until it reached them in the 4th century in the wake of a series of very charismatic early Buddhist missionaries.

### 3.3 Shamanism before Buddhism

If the proto-Tungusic language did incorporate the word *šaman* from Buddhist terminology, this begs the question, did shaman-like figures already exist among the Tungus, who simply added on elements drawn from Buddhism along with this new prestigious title? Given the ubiquity of shamanic practices across the world, and the evidence for them in prehistory, it is highly probable that they did.

The academic literature about global and prehistorical manifestations of shamanism is extensive.<sup>12</sup> The shamanic traditions of the Arctic region of North America and that of eastern Siberia are closely similar, despite the separation of these communities for thousands of years.<sup>13</sup> In an interesting article written in 2012, ‘Did Shamans Always Play the Drum? Tracking Down Prehistoric Shamanism in Central Asia’, Andrzej Rozwadowski contributes evidence of continuity between the iconography of prehistoric rock art from Central Asia with shamanic traditions from historical and contemporary times. He points out that human figures are rare in rock art from Central Asia until around 4,000 years ago, at which point instances of shaman-like theriocephalic (animal-headed) human figures begin to appear. Such figures occur far earlier elsewhere; the earliest known instance being the c. 40,000-year-old lion-headed figure from Hohlefels, Germany. But the central Asian rock art that Rozwadowski analyzes contains other features suggestive of human shamans, including feathered headdresses, protrusions from the body that strongly resemble historical shamans’ coats, and radiating dots surrounding the figures, especially their heads, that Rozwadowski links with entoptic images commonly seen by people who consume hallucinogenic substances (such as the mushroom *Amanita muscaria*, which still plays an important role in some Siberian shamanic traditions, or the enigmatic *soma* of Indo-Iranian traditions of this period). The shaman’s drum, however, is not present in prehistorical rock art of Central Asia, suggesting that it may be a more recent innovation there.

Interpretation of rock art is based on hypotheses that are, in the end, impossible to confirm. But refraining from forming hypotheses about them for this reason is an inexcusable submission to ignorance. Such unbridled scepticism may temporarily put a damper on theories about prehistorical culture, but in the long term, it loses to the curiosity that humans will always have about prehistory.

### 3.4 Who can be referred to as a shaman?

Now, regardless of the etymology and antiquity, it is necessary to shift gears, as it were, and engage briefly with a different (though related) debate about shamans and shamanism—one that has had a notable impact on anthropology and religious studies. The question is whether or not it is appropriate to speak of practitioners in various far-flung cultures as ‘shamans’. Is there common ground between the beliefs and practices of Tungus shamans, Nepalese *jhankri*, Sora *kuran*, and various other healers in far-flung regions of, for example, the Americas and Oceania? Historians of religion and mythology such as Mircea Eliade and 

*[p. 66]* ^pg79

Michael Witzel are convinced that there is. Witzel has written prolifically about his theory of shamanism’s development in his 2012 book *The Origins of the World’s Mythologies*.<sup>14</sup> Anthropologists such as Alice Kehoe, on the other hand, have in recent decades been harshly critical of the loose way scholars of religion have applied terms like ‘shaman’ and ‘shamanism’ to lump together hundreds of unique cultures (Kehoe 2000).

It is true that the words shaman and shamanism, as commonly used today, are invented, etic categories in European languages—part of the meta-language of the study of religion if you will (Pharo 2011: 15). Some prefer instead to regard each ethnic group as an isolated whole whose religious leaders should be referred to by whatever word is used in the people’s native language. Following Kehoe, several anthropologists have called for the word ‘shaman’ to be totally excised from their area of study.<sup>15</sup> I understand this as essentially a dispute over whether the indigenous spirit-healing practices of various societies are predominantly similar—the claim of the proponents of shamanism as a single global phenomenon—or predominantly different, as Kehoe and her intellectual successors would have it. What the extreme perspectives in each camp seem to miss is that it is entirely reasonable to both describe the uniqueness of individual groups *and* to recognize broad similarities among them. To deny the similarities or to reject theories of origin without offering an alternative explanation is simply deconstructive. Deconstruction is useful when an alternative constructive framework is offered. But without any alternative, it fragments our understanding of eminently comparable religious phenomena, such as spirit-- healing specialists.

Granted, the word ‘shaman’ has come to have an unwieldy range of meanings; it is essential to be clear on what we mean when we use it. Definitions of shaman range from the ultra-broad to the ultra-specific. L. K. Pharo’s 2011 article ‘A Methodology for a Deconstruction and Reconstruction of the Concepts “Shaman” and “Shamanism”’ is the most theoretically sophisticated discussion of the topic to date, however Pharo argues for a fairly limited definition of who counts as a shaman; for him, the idea of the soul-journey must be present. I prefer Shirokogoroff’s broad definition, based on his extensive work among the Tungus of Siberia, of the shaman as ‘master of spirits’, or at least master of a particular group of spirits (Shirokogoroff 1935: 271). He presents this role as not the only specialization of the shaman but as the principal one. That, at least, provides a baseline with which we can compare the beliefs, experiences, and functions of other communities which similarly rely on a master of spirits for help in diseases of spiritual origin.

While I part ways with Pharo’s agenda of limiting the meaning of ‘shaman’, I fully agree with his usage of the abstract noun ‘shamanism’, previously articulated by Eliade: shamanism is a configuration of practices within a larger religious system in a culture, not a stand-alone religion (Pharo 2011: 11; Eliade 1964 \[1951\]: 7). That accords not only with Siberian shamanism but also with the reality of the way shamanic practices interface with other beliefs and practices in most cultures worldwide.

*[p. 67]* ^pg80

One interesting sub-debate that Pharo treats concerns whether shamanic practices are only found in small-scale hunter-gatherer societies.<sup>16</sup> It is principally spirit healers in such societies whom academics call shamans. Pharo (2011: 22) mentions the work of Caroline Humphrey on the massive aristocratic state founded by the Mongols in the 12th century. She describes documents that mention shaman-like individuals within this aristocracy who ‘ascend to the sky’. Shirokogoroff (1935: 269) also refers to Manchu sources referring to shamans at the imperial court. In India, we can similarly point out how the 8th or 9th century *Netratantra* presents the royal chaplain, an individual responsible for the welfare of the king and the royal family, as a specialist in, among other subjects, managing malignant spirits and skilled in the techniques of the Bhūta Tantras.<sup>17</sup> But are these figures still ‘shamans’ when they work in the royal court of a complex and densely populated society?

I find the unwillingness of many scholars to extend the label shaman to such practitioners to be a fascinating problem of categorization and cultural bias. Let us bring the discussion closer to the present: consider the case of former US president Donald Trump inviting evangelical Christian leaders to the White House to bless him by laying on hands.<sup>18</sup> Such an event may have been staged for a variety of reasons, but as a ritual act, it is meant, on the surface, to be understood as an attempt at manipulating spirits, conceived using the idiom of ‘the Holy Spirit’ for an individual’s benefit and protection—just as shamans do when they heal or protect someone. Here we have clear similarities butting up against entrenched cultural and religious notions of difference. If we find ourselves in a privileged class in an urbanized environment, chances are good that we feel more comfortable describing such figures with other terms: priests, saints, pastors, gurus, and so forth. One should examine the root of this need to differentiate.

Particular academic disciplines are more apt to understand cross-cultural religious phenomena, such as spirit work, as expressions of common human experiences and beliefs. Psychology, for example, is based on the assumption that particular commonalities exist among human beings, regardless of culture. Be it in our experiences growing up, our desires, or our need for solutions to psycho-spiritual and mundane problems, psychologists tend to see many more similarities across cultures than differences. Sudhir Kakar, in his 1991 book *Shamans, Mystics, and Doctors*, approaches the kaleidoscopic subject of healing and healers in India from such a unifying perspective. But unlike many psychologists in Europe and the Americas, he does not agree with the characterization of psychology as an evolutionary advancement over religious worldviews. Nor does he accept the cultural relativism promoted by many anthropologists since he finds the demonological ideas of many of his subjects baffling. Instead, he works on the basis of understanding each perspective as an *idiom*—expressing their understanding of a reality that he does not share as an outsider looking in, but one which he respects as coherent *for its practitioners and patients*. Kakar’s ethnography seeks to find a common ground with his subjects, while also presenting his analysis and reflecting on his own views. 

*[p. 68]* ^pg81

I find this admirable. On the other hand, Kakar does lump together the Muslim *pir*s, Hindu *pandit*s at Balaji (a temple famous for spirit healing), and various tribal healers from India under the heading ‘shamans’, as opposed to his other subjects who are ‘mystics’ and ‘doctors’ of traditional systems of medicine in India. Nonetheless, Kakar rightly sees these differences within a framework of common goals.

While most of the previous discussion is focused on terminology, in my view, beliefs and practices are far more significant. As J. H. Hutton said in 1928 (as quoted by Andrea Acri),

Beliefs as to the soul in the \[Indonesian\] Celebes differ little, if at all, from those in Assam, and to read an account of a hill tribe in Borneo or the Philippines gives a Naga hills reader \[in India\] a vivid impression of reading of some Naga tribe with whose general culture he is perfectly familiar, but whose acquaintance he has never made… There seems to be a pretty clear cultural connection between the Naga hills and Melanesia.<sup>19</sup> Similarly, a Siberian shaman encountering a Nepalese *jhankri* would readily recognize her or him as a shaman from a foreign land. It is an unusual intellectual quirk of some academics to put undue emphasis on the significance of ideological and practical differences.

### 3.5 The origins of Tantric traditions

Scholarly theories about where the Indian Tantric traditions come from have developed rapidly in recent decades, and I will touch on some of the principal current theories, anti-theories, and points of debate here. But I would first emphasize that the current state of knowledge on Tantric literature is insubstantial in comparison to the evidence that is available in archives. It is no exaggeration, to echo Harunaga Isaacson (1998: 3), that several thousand Tantric works reach the present in manuscripts, and of those, the ones that have been published as editions number in the dozens. Those that have been translated in full are even fewer, and those that have been edited and translated well can be counted on one’s fingers. And this only addresses the written side of the Tantric traditions. The art-historical side is equally inadequate, and studies of living Tantric traditions are often wildly uninformed about the historical facts and nature of ‘Tantrism’, which often skews the ethnographer’s understanding of the scope and meaning of ‘Tantra’ in the present. All of this needs to be borne in mind when discussing such a broad subject as the origin of Tantric traditions as a whole. Anything anyone says about these traditions is inherently provisional. It would be a mistake, however, to think that all theories are therefore equal. A theory is only as good as its evidence and how adequately it explains the evidence compared to competing theories.

Many excellent scholars simply do not engage with the question of the origin of the Tantric traditions or have addressed it solely on the basis of a limited range of textual evidence. These approaches are understandable, given the 

*[p. 69]* ^pg82

super-abundance of Tantric texts, and the level of certainty that textual evidence can provide in the hands of an expert. Most of my closest colleagues and mentors in Tantric studies fit these categories. Alexis Sanderson, for example, has revolutionized our knowledge of the history of early Tantric traditions and the relationship of Tantric Śaivism with other religions and with the broader political milieu. His encyclopaedic knowledge of hundreds of early Tantric scriptures (most from unpublished manuscript sources), built up through over 40 years of close study, has yet to be adequately recognized for the light it sheds on early Tantra and early mediaeval India as a whole.<sup>20</sup> Yet he is only one person, and his publications say very little about particular areas of early Tantric scriptures with interesting similarities to shamanic traditions (e.g. the Bhūta and Gāruḍa Tantras). Sanderson’s scholarship has been more geared toward charting the landscape of existing evidence than questioning about ultimate origins of new ideas that cannot be traced in the existing textual corpus. And again, such an approach is reasonable given the current state of scholarly understanding. Nevertheless, when one starts looking at the details of the lesser-studied (yet totally prolific) underbelly of the Tantras—texts on magic, exorcism, snakebite, and a fascinating array of deities orthodox Brahmanical Hinduism would have considered beyond the pale—questions of the pre-Tantric genealogy of such elements beg our attention.

Christian Wedemeyer, in his award-winning 2013 book *Making Sense of Tantric Buddhism*, is dismissive of the very idea that subaltern or non-Aryan religious elements—which he baldly refers to as ‘the half-sense of primitive tribal rituals’—may have been a factor in the development of Tantric traditions (Wedemeyer 2013: 201). He is equally critical of the historical value of looking for origins in order to better understand cultural phenomena. One can hardly be expected to take Wedemeyer’s argument seriously, however, as it is heavily based on a cherry-picked selection of weak examples, taken out of context, that caricature serious scholarly theories.<sup>21</sup> This tendency borders on the bizarre in the section ‘But… Did They Really Do It?’ where Wedemeyer builds a case that virtually all modern ethnographies about transgressive Tantric practices are based on hearsay. I, for one, have spent time in the company of a Nepalese *sādhu* who uses a human cranium as his begging bowl, and a Keralan *sādhaka* who engages in Tantric sexual rituals. And I never thought that was very remarkable, given how prolifically these sorts of practices are described in classical and mediaeval sources, and documented in modern ethnographies and autobiographies.<sup>22</sup> But I digress from the focus on the origins of Tantric traditions, which regardless of Wedemeyer’s theoretical objections, I contend *does* have major significance for our understanding of later forms.

One might expect a book called *The Origins of Yoga and Tantra* to have something to add to this debate. However, the title of Geoffrey Samuel’s 2008 book is misleading, since it is rather an introduction to Indian religions in general, based on a survey of secondary sources—‘I’m not a Sanskritist’, Samuel apologizes in the preface. To his credit, Samuel does bring up some interesting broad methodological and theoretical considerations as an anthropologist looking over the shoulders of textual scholars.

*[p. 70]* ^pg83

In contrast to the aforementioned, and spurred by them in some respects, I closely reviewed three other recent publications that advance our understanding of Tantric origins considerably. The first is a 2017 article by Ronald Davidson, another of those whose work Wedemeyer critiqued (e.g. Wedemeyer 2013: 29–30). Davidson answered with a review of Wedemeyer’s book (Davidson 2015) and with the excellent article ‘Magicians, Sorcerers, and Witches: Considering Pretantric, Non-sectarian Sources of Tantric Practices’ (Davidson 2017), which garners a great deal of evidence for the influence of figures marginal to Brahmanical traditions as influencing the development of certain aspects of the Tantras, among other areas. Davidson (*ibid*.: 4) makes the critical point that in the late 19th century, when the first comprehensive census of India was taken, the literacy rate of adult Hindus was in the range of 5% (and keep in mind that literacy and intelligence are two entirely separate things that are frequently conflated in modern times). We have no way of knowing if this rate was consistent over the past 2,300+ years of writing in India, but there is little reason to suspect that it was not, once manuscript cultures became established. So we are working with a context where somewhere in the neighbourhood of 95% of the populace does not participate in the literary culture directly, and yet historians of religion in India rarely acknowledge this or make any attempt to discern the influence of anyone other than other literati.

Davidson takes us through sources such as the ancient Vedic corpus and the Pali canon, 7th century Gilgit manuscripts, Sanskrit story literature, and Tibetan and Chinese Buddhist works illustrating the presence and influence of a variety of magicians, witches, and so on throughout the pre-Tantric era, the Tantric era, and on down to the present. Many such categories of beings that we have good reason to believe were once human lineages became mythologized stock figures in Tantric and non-Tantric sources. In the wide array of sources that Davidson brings together, the practice of magic is often associated with low-caste figures, regarded as cultural outsiders by Brahmin authors in most historical literature. It seems like a no-brainer to suppose that some of these figures were influential enough to have contributed in some way to the formation of Tantric traditions—and in light of Davidson’s evidence, this supposition is confirmed.

Appearing the same year, Andrea Acri’s extensive article ‘Tantrism “Seen from the East”’ responds to Robert Dentan’s call for students of Hinduism, Buddhism, and Daoism to pay attention to the indigenous religions of peninsular Malaysia. Indologists have frequently pointed out the significant influence of Indian civilization on Southeast Asia, and often diplomatically claim that the influence went both ways—but seldom invest their time in exploring the ways Southeast Asian civilization and Austroasiatic language speakers native to India have influenced Indian civilization. Following Dentan, Acri explores the concept of Monsoon Asia (South and Southeast Asia, along with parts of southern China) as a zone that shared a common ancestral religious matrix.

One point of debate brought up by Acri, Samuel, and others is the question of whether Tantric traditions are orthogenetic (having a single source, almost 

*[p. 71]* ^pg84

always identified as the preceding Brahmanical tradition) or heterogenetic (having a diverse array of sources). If nothing else, the fact that the majority of the population did not participate in the culture of literacy necessitates being attentive to diverse influences. Frankly, the notion that 95% of the population were completely passive recipients of the religious ideas of the Brahmanical minority for thousands of years is absurdly pretentious, and the alternative that they had different religious cultures that had *no* influence on that same tiny minority is grossly unrealistic. Clearly, religious change was sometimes driven by charismatic non-literate actors in the deep past just as it has been in more recent times.<sup>23</sup>

The most tantalizing examples of probable Austroasiatic influence on Hinduism and Tantric traditions that Acri explores in some detail include the Semai storm god and ‘dark Lord’ Nkuu’ as chief of a host of dangerous demons and associate of a range of wrathful female deities, being linked to the Vedic Rudra, who developed into the Śiva-Bhairava complex in post-Vedic and Tantric traditions (Acri 2017: 97–100); the possible Austroasiatic origin of the word and concept of Sanskrit *piśāca* (flesh-eating demon), as well as the Sanskritic motif of a Paiśācī language as connected with  Austroasiatic communities (*ibid*.: 100); the derivation of Sanskrit *lakuṭa*/*lākula* (club, n. of a sect) and *lakulīśa* (‘Lord with a Club’, a central epithet of Śiva and the name for an incarnation of Śiva in Pāśupata sources) from Austroasiatic *ḷāṅgula* (‘penis, stick’); the probable Austroasiatic origin of the related word *liṅga* (phallus, among other meanings), so central since the beginning of Śaivism; the centrality of blood-offering in Austroasiatic-speaking tribal communities in India and their similar centrality in particular Tantric contexts; the importance of dragons (Skt. *nāga*) in Semai cosmology, their connection to the monsoon and Nkuu’, and the importance of *nāga*s in some Tantric contexts, as well as in popular religion in India to this day; and lastly, of great significance for this article, the possible connections between the roles and ideologies of Semai shamans and their ‘demonic wives’ with various similar figures in Indian religious history: the *vrātya*s from Vedic times, the Pāśupatas in classical India, and Kāpālikas and other sorts of ‘sinister yogis’ from early mediaeval India up to the present. I refer the reader to Acri’s article itself for all of the details of these numerous similarities. Acri is very cautious in evaluating the significance of the parallels he discusses but considers the similarities significant and worthy of further investigation.

One final source of note is Francesco Brighenti’s 2012 article, ‘Hindu Devotional Ordeals and Their Shamanic Parallels’. Like Acri’s, this is an extensive article that details rituals from a wide variety of sources both Sanskritic and from shamanic cultures in India and elsewhere. Brighenti begins with a discussion of the ancient Vedic rite of *yūpārohaṇa*, the ritual climbing of a pole, and discusses parallel rites practiced in Siberian, Indonesian, and American shamanism. He is cautious about pinning down the origin of this rite but speculates that it might have been incorporated into the Vedic tradition in Central Asia (before the spread of Indo-Aryan speakers into India), or have been borrowed into the 

*[p. 72]* ^pg85

Vedic tradition from a preexisting shamanic tradition in South Asia, among other scenarios. Brighenti next takes up the apparently related practice of swinging from hooks attached to a central pole (called *caṛak pūjā* in Bengal), detailing examples from various parts of India. This ritual is primarily practiced by *ādivāsi* (literally, ‘first inhabitants’, roughly synonymous with ‘tribal’) communities and low-caste groups (many of whom are likely to have originally been *ādivāsi*s), and in some areas by other Hindu castes, but never by Brahmins—it is highly unlikely that it was originally a Hindu practice that spread to the tribes. Brighenti emphasizes several times that the Vedic corpus includes no scriptural sanction for self-- torture and that no Hindu etiological myth exists to explain the origin of hook-swinging.

### 3.6 Shamans and Tāntrikas: Initial notes on shared practices

The *bhūtatāntrika* has many general similarities with tribal shamans. First and foremost, both are masters of spirits and experts in techniques of entering into trances, contacting powerful spirits, and healing the psycho-spiritually ill in their communities. On the other hand, when it comes to specifics, the Tantric material that survives bears little similarity to what we know of tribal shamans in South Asia: the initiation of a *bhūtatāntrika* practitioner is hardly described, whereas initiation is central to most shamanic traditions. No mention is made of undergoing any sort of physical ordeal, ascending a pole, or hanging from hooks. But one specific point of convergence is the centrality and meaning of iron in both contexts. Brighenti (2012: 118) says,

Suspension by means of iron hooks may be due to ‘a desire to insulate or isolate the hook-swinging devotee from impure or demoniacal influences.’ In the most archaic form of hook-swinging ceremonies, the body of the hanging penitent is touched only by iron. In India this metal is regarded as strongly anti-demoniacal, and several tribes of Middle India hold to the belief that iron tubes, clamps, cones, nails etc., manufactured by village smiths abiding by anti-pollution ritual rules, have the power to drive away evil spirits. In many shamanistic traditions across Eurasia, the connection between shamans and blacksmiths remains extremely close.

Austroasiatic tribes in India such as the Agaria and Asur are famous for their skill at smelting and refining iron, and it is likely that their ancestors are the ones referred to in Vedic mythology as dark-skinned warriors with iron fortresses—indeed, we have little record of iron smelting in northern India until centuries into the Vedic era, and it is regarded as the knowledge of outsider ‘*asura*s’ (*ibid*.: 162). Agaria mythology too refers to their primordial kings as living in iron fortresses (Elwin 1942: 86–88).

One of the major deities in the Bhūta Tantras is named *Lohaka*, Iron-Man. He is described as having a flat nose (*cipiṭanāsa*), a single horn, protruding fangs,

eight arms, and unlimited strength.<sup>24</sup> Lohaka is particularly invoked to attack demons who possess outcastes (*caṇḍāla*s). When invoked, he enters the body of the Tantric practitioner and allows him to execute various magical and healing actions. One of the principal deities of the Agaria tribe is similarly named *Lohāsur*, Iron-Spirit. In the early 6th century, Varāhamihira uses the same term used for Lohaka, *cipiṭanāsa*, to refer to the nose shape of a particular ethnic group (14.26), and incidentally, the same text contains our earliest surviving reference to the Bhūta Tantras. Another enigmatic connection of the *bhūtatāntrika* with iron is that he is expected to wear a bracelet on his left wrist made of *puṣpaloha* (literally ‘flower-iron’, probably to be identified as *wootz* steel) which is said to scare off possessing spirits.<sup>25</sup>

Another important deity in the Bhūta Tantras is Khaḍgarāvaṇa, a sword-- wielding god who was the subject of my master’s thesis at the University of California, Berkeley. In a long chapter on exorcism rituals involving Khaḍgarāvaṇa, one device repeatedly described is the creation of a manikin (*pratikṛti* or *putta* *lika*) representing the spirit out of flour and water, which the exorcist crushes, stabs, gouges, slices, and decapitates while pronouncing mantras urging Khaḍgarāvaṇa to do the same to the deviant spirit.<sup>26</sup> The shamanic tradition of the Sora people inhabiting parts of southern Odisha and northern Andhra Pradesh serves as another point of comparison. Sora shamans also wield a sword, and 

*[p. 74]* ^pg87

according to the classic study of Verrier Elwin (1955: 253), a Sora shaman may heal a possessed child by making a doll representing the child with flour and water, transferring the ghost to it, and cremating the doll in a mock funeral. One of the Sanskrit names for the Sora was Śavara, and it seems highly unlikely that a Tantric goddess like the Buddhist Parṇaśavarī was dreamed up in isolation from the female shamans of this tribe who dance in a trance and heal just as the goddess does.<sup>27</sup>

It is logical to work from the assumption that the original Tantric exorcists took cues from tribal shamans—or at least from lore about tribal shamans—and adapted some of that into the Brahmanical idiom of the Śaiva Tantras. Too little evidence survives of the Vedic era and classical Āyurvedic *bhūtavidyā* to say exactly which aspects of Tantric exorcism depend on the Brahmanical tradition and whether earlier Brahmanical exorcists were themselves drawing on tribal knowledge. On the other hand, historians tend to regard Indian civilization as arising from the mixture of the cultures of Indo-European, Dravidian, and Austroasiatic language families, and we surely should include the Tibeto-Burman family of languages in the Himalayan region as well. A great deal of scholarship focuses on the Indo-Aryan and Dravidian branches, but it is time to give further consideration to the currently smaller language and cultural units as well.

The Himalayan region is a particularly interesting place to study shamanism and Tantra since both have flourished there, side by side and with mutual influence, for over a thousand years. According to *Shamanism and Tantra in the Himalayas* by Müller-Ebeling *et al*. (2002: 31), shamans in Nepal regard the practices of *tāntrika*s and of Tibetan *bönpo* as offshoots of the primordial tradition of the *jhankri*s (shamans), whereas they regard mainstream Hinduism and Buddhism as external religions with significant influence.

### 3.7 Coming full-circle: śramaṇa s, shamans, and tāntrika s

To recapitulate my own hypotheses, based on the evidence currently available: shamans, like *bhūtatāntrika*s, are masters of trance and masters of working with spirits of nature (including deceased humans) to support their communities and heal the sick. I see the role of shamans as a religious legacy that originates in prehistory and spread over much of the world, but the word shaman itself is likely to have entered proto-Tungusic in northeastern Asia through the influence of Buddhism—specifically via the Buddhist *śramaṇa*s sent in the 4th century by Fu Jian, an ethnically Tibetan emperor who ruled the Former Qin dynasty. Whether these Buddhist *śramaṇa*s were themselves incorporating pre-Tantric spirit work into their teachings is an important question that is probably unanswerable, but it is conceivable that, like many lamas today, they were not only practicing ‘textbook’ Buddhism but were also influenced by pre-Buddhist shamanic traditions.

My research has also clarified that the similarities between shamans and *bhūtatāntrika*s seem to be mostly limited to generalities: theriocephalic (animal-- headed) deities and spirits in the pantheon, trance work, and exorcism. But I also uncovered some initial examples of specifics that point to possible direct 

*[p. 75]* ^pg88

influence of particular tribes: the parallel of the Bhūta Tantra deity Lohaka and the Agaria Lohāsur, along with the general concepts of iron as a spirit-repelling magical substance in both spheres, the Tantric and the tribal. Some ritual practices such as the use of a flour-manikin to represent a possessing spirit may also have a common origin.

An area that requires further research is the relationship between spirit work (exorcism, mediumship, etc.) in early Buddhist Tantra and that of the Śaiva Bhūta Tantras. The work of Davidson, Acri, Brighenti, and others has established beyond a reasonable doubt that non-Aryan communities, including tribal communities, were among those who contributed to the formation of Tantric traditions, including, I would add, the Śaiva Bhūta Tantras.

Another important area of inquiry remaining to be explored is the question of why we see this fusion of Brahmanical, Austroasiatic, and other influences in India during the second half of the first millennium. It may have simply involved a recognition of the skill and charisma of non-Aryan religious experts. It could have had to do with political interests in assimilating these diverse communities into the expanding sphere of Indian civilization. In my view, nostalgia must have played a role in the popularity of Tantric traditions with tribal themes. As Davidson (2017: 26) says,

I believe we see all of these activities in medieval Indian tantrism, with its cross-tradition borrowing, its emphasis on power, its desire for the foreign, tribal or extra-terrestrial aura and so on. The references to *mātaṅga* or *śabara* tribal peoples in the tantras are analogous to the modern appropriation of tribal charisma by non-tribal sorcerers, most evident in the designation ‘*baiga*’ in Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh.

Throughout time, as humankind has advanced in various ways, many people have regarded the dominant paradigms of their day as spiritual cul-de-sacs. In that situation it makes sense to look back to the age-old traditions maintained by people further from urban centres, and reimagine a healthier, more fulfilling, shamanic present.
