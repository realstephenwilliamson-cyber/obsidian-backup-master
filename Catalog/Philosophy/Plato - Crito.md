## About

A short dialogue by Plato. In prison, days before his execution, Socrates hears his friend Crito's plan for escape and refuses it, arguing that injustice must not be answered with injustice.

- **People:** Socrates and Crito of Alopece, a wealthy friend. In Socrates' reply the Laws of Athens are made to speak.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** 399 BC, in the prison, at daybreak. Crito brings word that the sacred ship from Delos has been seen off Sunium; its return ends the delay of the execution. Wikipedia places the scene about a month after the trial of the *Apology*.
- **Composition and authenticity:** no exact date. The IEP lists it among the early dialogues, written after Socrates' death and before Plato's first trip to Sicily in 387 BC; Copleston's *History of Philosophy* also calls it early. Wikipedia says it is widely held genuine, though Holger Thesleff doubted it (2009). Jowett thought it uncertain whether the visit and the offer ever happened.
- **Argument:** Crito offers bribes and flight to Thessaly. Socrates answers through the personified Laws, who argue that a state cannot exist without respect for its rules, so escape would wrong them. Wikipedia finds here an early statement of social contract theory; scholars disagree whether it demands obedience even to unjust laws (Richard Kraut argues it does not).
- **Place among the dialogues:** in Thrasyllus's arrangement, reported by Diogenes Laertius, it stands in the first tetralogy as *Crito, or On what is to be done*, with *Euthyphro*, *Apology* and *Phaedo*. Stephanus pages 43a–54e.
- **Why it matters:** it has stayed a reference point in discussion of the social contract and of obedience to law. Cicero read it as teaching gratitude to the state, and David Hume found in it the idea of a citizen's implicit promise of loyalty. The first printed Greek text appeared in Venice in 1513 (Aldus Manutius).
