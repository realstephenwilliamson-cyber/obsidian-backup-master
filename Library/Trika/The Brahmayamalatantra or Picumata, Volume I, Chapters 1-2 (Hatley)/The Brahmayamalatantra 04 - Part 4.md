Concerning the relationship between the extant recensions of the Bra Ya and the Svacchandatantra, the evidence is not especially strong. While the former lists the latter in its account of the canon, the evidence from such lists must be treated with caution, as the case of the Siddhayogesvarīmata and BraYā illustrates (discussed below). In the area of cosmology, the Bra Yā appears archaic in comparison to the Svacchandatantra. In this respect the BraYā appears comparatively archaic.?’4

Questions remain concerning the relation between the BraYa and the Siddhayogeśvarīmata, both being among the earliest extant goddess-oriented tantras. In contrast to the BraYā, the Siddhayogeśsvarīmata, which is fundamental to the Trika system, survives only in a short, likely secondary recension missing a significant amount of the material attributed to it by Kashmirian authors (TöRZsöÖK 1999: iv—-v). Possessing distinct pantheons, these Vidyāpītha texts nonetheless share much in character, both being siddhi-oriented scriptures rooted in archaic yogin? cults. One might suspect that the Bra Yá predates the Siddhayogeśsvarīmata given that the latter lists the former in its account of the Śaiva canon.?5? However, as Judit TÖRZSÖK (1999: ix) points out, the Bra Ya might also refer to the Siddhayogeśsvarīmata in the same context, for it mentions a Vidyāpītha text by the title Siddhā—one of several names by which Abhinavagupta cites the Siddhayogesvarīmata. This apparent circularity, which owes perhaps to ongoing revision or idealized text lists, suggests the need for weighing multiple types of evidence in determining relative chronology, evidence that appears lacking in this case. Irrespective of their chronological priority, the Bra Yā contains material concerning sakti triads that might potentially shed light on the cultic background to the Siddhayogesvarīmata’s triad (trika) of goddesses.+6°

1.9.5 The Brahmayāmala and non-tantric texts

Several works beyond the Saiva and Buddhist tantric corpora have relevance to the BraYa’s dating. These include texts which the Bra Yā seems likely to have known or drawn upon, and at least one text—the Devīpurāna—which clearly draws upon a chapter of the Bra Yā.

The Sivadharmasāstra

In chapter 4, I demonstrate that BraYa 83, which narrates the origins of the skull and skull-staff (kapālakhatvāńgotpatti), likely draws upon, perhaps indirectly, the Sivadharmasāstra’s ‘Origin of the Lingam (lingodbhava) myth. This is unsurprising since, as discussed in §4.2.1, the Śivadharmaśāstra’s version of this tale was highly influential. The Bra Y2’s likely knowledge of the Sivadharma-śsāstra has only modest implications for its dating. While the Sivadharmasāstra seems likely to be the oldest text of the Sivadharma corpus, and to predate most extant works of tantric Saiva literature, it is uncertain what this means in absolute terms: dates as early as the fourth or fifth century up into the seventh cannot yet be ruled out. It is also possible, if not likely, that the Śivadharma-sāstra’s influence upon the Bra Ya was indirect, though the intervening source or sources cannot now be identified. As such, the debt of the Bra Ya’s liùngodbhava episode to the Sivadharmasāstra only confirms that the Bra Yā postdates an important work of mainstream Śaivism in circulation by the seventh century.

On balance, I find this possibility much more compelling than possible references to the Devīmāhātmya or to an unnamed Śaiva scriptural text. As the Sattasaī was in circulation much prior the seventh century, this identification would have little impact on the BraYa’s dating.

1.11 Geographic and social horizons

A text as vast as the Bra Yz ought to offer clues into its region of origin, but this remains difficult to pin down with confidence. It can at least be asserted that the text is unlikely to herald from the far south or far north and northwest of the subcontinent.

In its first chapter, the Bra Yā provides an unusually detailed account of its revelatory “descent” (avatāra). The mandala of cremation grounds might have been inherited from older sources, at most shedding light upon the geographic horizons of the early tradition—though it could potentially reflect the geographic sphere of the Bra Yā in its earliest form. The more expansive geography envisioned in the BraYa’s revelation narrative might, on the other hand, reflect a broadening of the cults horizons by the period of the text's final redaction.

As for the specific place of composition, there seem to be multiple viable possibilities. Among the possible regions of origin, Orissa might seem a strong candidate: note, in particular, the prominence of Orissan sites in the sacred geography of the Bra Yā (table 1.16), and the text's references to Ekapādabhairava, an unusual one-legged form of the deity presiding over the Ekāmra pītha and common in Orissan-provenance sculpture, but uncommon elsewhere. Temples suggestive of tantric goddess cults are, furthermore, widely attested in the sculpture of this region, particularly from the ninth century.?%® A region, like Orissa, of “peripheral” status in the Brahmanical cultural world would certainly seem congruous with the sociolinguistic milieu of the text.

On the other hand, it is difficult to imagine obscure villages such as Brho-darī of Sindh and Kanavira, near Prayāga—neither of which seems traceable now—featuring prominently in the revelation narrative in the absence ofa genuine connection to the text. Between the two, Kanavira village has an especially strong claim, owing not only to its prominence in the revelation narrative, but also to the centrality of Prayāga in the Bra Ya’s cultic system. As observes Kiss (2015: 16),

“.. the fact that the centre of the initiation mandala of the Bra Ya is Prayāga may have some weight, and may lead us to the supposition that the birth-place of the text should not lie extremely far from Prayāga, modern Allahabad. In support of Prayāga being the place of origin of the text, one could also refer to Bra Yā 1.27-28, in which Sattikā, the Goddess herself, is said to be incarnated in Kanavīra, a village near Prayāga.”

Despite the narratives mythical and literary qualities, it remains a distinct possibility that one of its purposes was to elevate such individuals—redactors of the BraYā, or prior gurus of their lineage—to divine status within a idealized tantric metacommunity spanning a vast temporal, geographical, and cosmological expanse. Recently, Hans BAKKER (2014: 257—60) has advanced a different proposal for the provenance of the Bra Yā and other éarly Yāmalatantras. He suggests that the BraYā, at least in its early form, emerged in the Kotīvarṣṣa region of Gauda in the second half of the seventh century, more or less contemporaneous with the old Skandapurāna. This working hypothesis is partly based on Kotīvarsa's origin myth in Skandapurāna 171, which, as reviewed earlier, also accounts for the origin the Yamalatantras, headed by the Bra Ya. Another factor is the fact that the initiation mandala of Bra Yā 3 may demonstrate local knowledge of the Hetukeśvara temple of Kotīvarsa—although omitting mention of its tutelary godddess, Bahumāmsā. In BAKKER’s (2014: 259) assessment,

A date for the part of the Brahmayāmala under discussion \[Bra Ya 3\] that is slightly later than the composition and first redaction of the Skandapurāna could possibly explain this omission, although, admittedly, other reasons for the disappearance of the local tutelary goddess in the Picumata, such as the difference in context, cannot be excluded. The matter needs further research, but for the time being our working hypothesis is that at least this part of the Brahmayāmala came into being in the second half of the seventh century, when the Later Gupta dynasty was trying to restore authority in the region after the collapse of Harsavardhana’s empire, though the inception of the Yāmala tradition seems to have been contemporaneous with the composition of the Skandapurāna.

This “northeastern” hypothesis seems on the whole plausible. It should nevertheless be stressed that its grounds are not especially strong: Skandapurāna 171 certainly links the Yāmala-/Mātrtantras to Kotīvarsa, but the link may attest merely to association rather than origin. The BraYa’s knowledge of the pantheon of a major Saiva sacred site may also be accounted for readily on other grounds.
