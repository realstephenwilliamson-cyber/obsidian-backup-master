## About

A book by Max Horkheimer (1895–1973) and Theodor W. Adorno (1903–1969) of the Frankfurt School, written in wartime exile. It argues that Western reason, developed as an instrument for mastering nature, turns against itself and slides back into myth and new forms of domination.

- **Writing:** the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) says they wrote it from 1939 to 1944 in California. Their 1969 preface says it was written when the end of the Nazi terror was in sight.
- **1944:** first circulated as *Philosophische Fragmente* (Philosophical Fragments), issued in New York by the Institute for Social Research. The SEP describes this first version as a hectographed, limited-edition typescript.
- **1947:** a revised text, *Dialektik der Aufklärung*, published by Querido in Amsterdam. The SEP's Adorno entry says the revision toned down the Marxist economic vocabulary.
- **Later editions:** reissued in 1969 by S. Fischer, Frankfurt, with a new preface acknowledging Gretel Adorno's contribution. English translations: John Cumming (New York: Herder and Herder, 1972) and Edmund Jephcott (Stanford University Press, 2002), the latter from the text of Horkheimer's collected works, edited by Gunzelin Schmid Noerr.
- **Contents:** the opening essay on the concept of enlightenment; Excursus I on the *Odyssey*, reading Odysseus as the prototype of the bourgeois individual; Excursus II on Kant, Sade and *Juliette*; "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception"; and a chapter on antisemitism. The governing thesis: "Myth is already enlightenment, and enlightenment reverts to mythology."
- **Why it matters:** the SEP calls it the most famous work of the Frankfurt School's first generation, and Wikipedia a core text of critical theory that helped inspire the New Left. Adorno regarded his later major writings as excursuses to it. Habermas criticised it for a performative contradiction and a normative deficit.
