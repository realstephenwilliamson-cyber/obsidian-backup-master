### 7.1–25: Freedom from sin and death, enabled to live rightly

*[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-1|Text]], p. 298* · Paul begins with an analogy to the law or convention of marriage and how it no longer binds a spouse to that partner once the partner has died; likewise, the convention since Adam of being slaves of sin no longer binds the Gentiles since Christ has died. They are now bound to a new life in Christ. The language is very difficult to follow: the same terms are used with different referents and connotations, and the prevailing psychological interpretations are themselves suspect (see “The Enigmatic ‘I’,” p. 299). Paul’s point seems to be that these Gentiles who have turned to God in Christ are not bound to the limitations of their previous identity in Adam, in sin leading to death. They are not under Torah, because they are not Israelites, but they are nevertheless now free from the law of sin to live a Torah-based life, i.e., to live according to God’s standards. ^7-1

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-1|1]]:** The previous argument is continued. *The law*, or a convention; no article in Gk. “Nomos” (law) is not restricted to Jewish, Roman, or any other specific code in ch 7. ^7-1-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-2|2–4]]:** As the wife is no longer bound to her deceased husband, these Gentiles are no longer bound to Adam. ^7-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-2|2]]:** *Is bound by the law*, a convention \[i.e., of marriage\]; no article in Gk. ^7-2-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-3|3]]:** *She is not an adulteress*, i.e., sidestepper of that law or convention, since it no longer applies to her. ^7-3

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-4|4]]:** *You have died to the law*, i.e., not Torah law, but the law of sin bound to Adam (6.16–21). The convention or law of sin, detailed in 1.18–32, brought death to Adam’s descendants. ^7-4

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-5|5]]:** *Aroused by the law*, i.e., of sin, active for those bound to Adam; *members*, lit., “limbs,” see 6.13. ^7-5

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-6|6]]:** *Discharged from the law*, i.e., of sin as descendants of Adam; *so that we are slaves not under the old written code*, not slaves to an ancient inscription \[i.e., a cut in the body\]. Philo discusses conversion as the change from old customs (“fabulous inventions”) to becoming servants of God (e.g., *Spec. Laws* 1.51–53,308–314; *Virtues* 214). ^7-6

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-7|7]]:** *You shall not covet*, [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-14|Ex 20.17]]; [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^5-18|Deut 5.21]]. Coveting, the overwhelming desire for what is not one’s own, was the sin of Adam and Eve ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^3-1|Gen 3.1–24]]). On covetousness as “the source of all evils,” see Philo, *Spec. Laws* 4.79–99, and Epictetus, *Diatr.* 24.84–118. ^7-7

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-8|8]]:** *An opportunity*, or a pretext; *in me*, perhaps in Adam and thus his descendants (depending on how “me” is identified). *Apart from the law sin lies dead*, lit., “For without (a) law sin is dead” (there must be a norm in order to err according to it). *Commandment*, do not covet (see 7.7n.). ^7-8

> [!note] The Enigmatic “I” (ROM 7.7–25) *(p. 299)*
> Since Augustine’s description of his own conversion experience (*City of God*) drawing on this language, Rom 7 has been understood as Paul’s autobiographical struggle between the desires to do good and to do evil. The question most debated was whether the switch from “we” early in the chapter to “I” at v 7 represented Paul’s self-understanding before or after his turn to Christ. Although these views are still widely held, recent interpretations offer alternatives: Paul may be describing the plight of Adam (and Eve) in Gen 3, with which Paul’s language arguably overlaps; Paul may be using the diatribe convention (see “Diatribe,” p. 289) to describe the plight of a Gentile (such as Paul described in ch 1) when introduced to Torah, in contrast to the experience of a Jew who grew up with Torah. In addition to the uncertain identity of the “I,” what Paul means by *nomos* (usually translated “law;” see “Law,” p. 289) in this chapter, as well as how this passage informs his perspective on Torah, on human culpability, and on sin remain subjects of spirited debates.

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-9|9]]:** The prohibition causes one to recognize the wrongfulness of the prohibited act. ^7-9

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-10|10]]:** Lit., “and the commandment that is (to lead) into life (possibly [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^3-22|Gen 3.22–23]]; see also [[Torah 29 - Achrei Mot#^18-5|Lev 18.5]], [[Torah 51 - Nitzavim#^30-6|Deut 30.6]]), that is being discovered by me (to lead) into death.” ^7-10

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-11|11–12]]:** It is not the commandment that leads to death, but its abrogation by the person who receives the commandment. *And through it killed me*, “me” not in Gk. *Law . . . commandment is holy and just and good*, the problem is not the commandment itself. ^7-11

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-13|13–25]]:** Continuation of describing the power of sin at work in the body, even for the one who wills to do right. ^7-13

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-13|13]]:** This leads to the next logical question: Would Adam have been better off had he not been told to refrain from eating from the tree of knowledge? The answer is an unequivocal no. It revealed that sin brings about death, which would have gone otherwise unrealized, and thus it would not be clear that sin is to be avoided at all costs. *Through the commandment might become sinful beyond measure,* lit., “in order that through the commandment the sinfulness of the sin would cause itself to become preeminent,” i.e., easily identifiable as sin. ^7-13-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-14|14–23]]:** The rabbis (*Sifre Deut.* 32) identify these conflicting impulses as “yetzer ha-tov” (the good inclination) and “yetzer ha-ra” (the evil inclination). Similar struggles are discussed in 1QS 3.15–4.26; Epictetus, *Diatr*. 2.26.4. ^7-14

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-14|14]]:** *Spiritual*, having to do with spiritual matters, e.g., honesty, righteousness, etc. *Sold into slavery under sin*, lit., “sold to being enslaved under sin (error)”; Paul seems to indicate a kind of dual citizenship, being created to want what is right but following a transaction likened to selling a slave, finding oneself compelled to live in a different way than what one wishes for. Cf. [[Isaiah 50#^50-1|Isa 50.1]]; 11QPsa 19.9–10. ^7-14-2

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-17|17]]:** *No longer I. . . but sin that dwells within me*, indicating the awareness of fleshly passions arising without having sought to raise them and even hating the direction that they take one, which seems to be a result of the transaction that made one a slave, see vv 15–16. ^7-17

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-18|18]]:** *For I know that nothing good dwells within me*, lit., “for I understand not that which dwells in me”; the will to do the right thing, as commanded, is present, yet the ability to do so is inscrutably encumbered by the slavery to sin into which one has been sold. ^7-18

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-20|20]]:** This raises the question of culpability; the point may be that once one is sold into a cultural system and learns a way of life by habit, in this case the covetous behavior that is considered now sinful, that even when one wants to change, it seems insur-mountable to alter entirely one’s way of living in the new direction desired, because of the intensity of the passion for what one should not want to want. ^7-20

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-21|21–23]]:** *To be a law*, the “nomos” arising from various sources, all in the sense of “principle” or “norm,” rather than “Torah.” Paul draws on 1.18–2.29, and thereafter, that everyone knows what is right before God, and thus is responsible for intending and doing what is right. **21**: See [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^4-7|Gen 4.7]]. **22**: See Ps 19; 119. **23**: *Members*, or limbs, see 6.13. ^7-21

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-24|24]]:** The *I* seeks rescue not from Torah but from the *body of death*. ^7-24

**[[NRSVue Romans 07#^7-25|25]]:** *A slave to the law of God*, i.e., a member of God’s family and principles for living; *a slave to the law of sin*, i.e., a member of the human family descended from Adam and the constraining forces of sin and death. See 1QS 11.9–15; 1QH 4.30–38. ^7-25
