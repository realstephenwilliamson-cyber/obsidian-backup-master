**1.** [There are requirements regarding] the *s'chach* of a sukkah, and not all substances are acceptable [to be used for this purpose].[^1] For *s'chach*, we may use only a substance which grows from the ground,[^2] has been detached from the ground,[^3] is not subject to contracting ritual impurity,[^4] does not have an unpleasant odor,[^5] and does not have elements which fall off and wither constantly.[^6] ^5-1

**2.** When a person uses as *s'chach* a substance which does not grow from the ground, is still connected to the ground, or is subject to contracting ritual impurity, [the sukkah] is not acceptable.[^7]  
However, if he transgressed and used as *s'chach* a substance which has elements which fall off and wither,[^8] or which possesses an unpleasant odor,[^9] it is kosher.[^10] [Our Sages][^11] said only that one should not use these as *s'chach* lest one leave one's sukkah and depart. One must take care that the branches and leaves should not descend within ten handbreadths of the ground,[^12] so that one will not be uncomfortable[^13] when using the sukkah.[^14]  
If one used metals,[^15] bones, or hides[^16] as *s'chach*, it is unacceptable because these do not grow from the ground. If one suspended vines and the like over it until they made a sukkah, it is unacceptable, because they were not uprooted [from the ground].[^17]  
Should one use wooden utensils,[^18] mats that were made to lie on,[^19] and the like as *s'chach*, it is unacceptable, because they are subject to contracting ritual impurity. Similarly, using broken[^20] and worn out utensils as *s'chach* is unacceptable. Since these substances were subject to ritual impurity, [the latter law was instituted] lest one use broken pieces which have not yet attained a state of [unquestionable] purity. ^5-2

**3.** If one used foods[^21] as *s'chach*, it is unacceptable, because they are subject to contracting ritual impurity.[^22] [When one uses] branches from a fig tree which contain figs, runners from a grape vine which contain grapes, branches of a date palm which contain dates and the like,[^23] [the following rules apply:] We see - if the waste[^24] is more than the food;[^25] then we may use them as *s'chach*. If not, we may not use them as *s'chach*.[^26]  
If one uses as *s'chach* vegetables[^27] which, when they dry up, will wither, and none of their substance will remain,[^28] even though they are now fresh, their place is considered to be vacant, as though they did not exist.[^29] ^5-3

**4.** If one used as *s'chach* branches of flax which were not crushed and combed, they are kosher, because they are still considered to be wood.[^30] After the flax has been crushed and combed,[^31] it may not be used as *s'chach*,[^32] since its form has changed and it is as though it is no longer a product of the earth.[^33]  
One may use ropes made from palm bast or hemp and the like as *s'chach*, since their original form is unchanged and ropes are not considered to be utensils.[^34] ^5-4

**5.** [The following rules apply] when one uses arrows as *s'chach*.[^35] Those which are "male"[^36] are kosher;[^37] those which are "female"[^38] are not acceptable.[^39] Even though [ultimately,] they will be filled with iron,[^40] they have a receptacle. Hence, they are susceptible to contracting ritual impurity, as are all utensils with receptacles.[^41] ^5-5

**6.** A mat of reeds, of raw rubber, or of hemp[^42] - if it is small, we may assume that it was made to lie on. Therefore, it may not be used for *s'chach* unless it was [explicitly] made for this purpose.[^43]  
If it is large,[^44] we may assume that it was made for shade; therefore, it may be used for *s'chach* unless it was [explicitly] made to lie on.[^45]  
If it has a border,[^46] even a large mat may not be used as *s'chach*, because it is considered to be a receptacle.[^47] Even if the border were removed, it may not be used as *s'chach*, because it would be considered to be a broken utensil.[^48] ^5-6

**7.** Boards which are less than four handbreadths wide[^49] may be used for *s'chach* even though they have been planed.[^50] If they are more than four handbreadths wide, they should not be used as *s'chach*, even though they have not been planed. This is a decree [instituted] lest one sit under a roof and regard it as a sukkah.[^51]  
If one placed a board which was more than four handbreadths wide over [a sukkah, the sukkah] is kosher.[^52] However, one should not sleep under the board.[^53] A person who did sleep[^54] under the board has not fulfilled his obligation.  
There were boards that were four handbreadths wide, but less than four handbreadths thick. A person turned them on their side so that they would not be four handbreadths wide to use them as *s'chach*. This is not acceptable, because a board is unacceptable for use as *s'chach* whether one uses its width or thickness.[^55] ^5-7

**8.** A roof which is not covered by a ceiling[^56] - i.e., the plaster and the stones - but rather has only boards fixed in place, is not acceptable, since they were not placed there for the purpose of a sukkah, but to be part of the house.  
Therefore, if one[^57] lifted up the boards[^58] and removed the nails[^59] with the intent [that they serve] as a sukkah,[^60] it is kosher.[^61] [This applies provided] that each board is not four handbreadths wide.[^62]  
Similarly, it is kosher if one removed a board from between two others and replaced it with kosher *s'chach*, with the intention [that it serve] as a sukkah. ^5-8

**9.** A sukkah that was made for any purpose whatsoever - even if it was not made for the purpose of [fulfilling] the mitzvah - if it was made according to law,[^63] it is kosher.[^64] However, it must be made for the purpose of shade.[^65] Examples of this are sukkot made for gentiles, sukkot made for animals, and the like.[^66]  
In contrast, a sukkah that came about on its own accord[^67] is unacceptable, because it was not made for the purpose of shade. Similarly, when a person hollows out a place in a heap of produce and thus makes a sukkah, it is not considered to be a sukkah, [^68] because the produce was not piled there for this purpose.[^69] Accordingly, were one to create a space one handbreadth [high][^70] and seven [handbreadths][^71] in area for the purpose of a sukkah, and afterwards hollow it out till it reached ten [handbreadths],[^72] it is kosher, since its *s'chach* was placed for the purpose of shade.[^73] ^5-9

**10.** We may not use bundles of straw, bundles of wood or bundles of reeds as *s'chach*.[^74] This decree [was instituted][^75] lest one place those bundles on one's roof to dry out, and then change one's mind and sit under them with the intent [that they serve as] a sukkah. The person did not place the *s'chach* there originally for the purpose of shade. Thus, it resembles a sukkah that came about on its own accord.[^76]  
If one[^77] untied [the bundles], they are acceptable [for use as *s'chach*].[^78] A bundle is considered to be no fewer than twenty five units.[^79] ^5-10

**11.** Small bundles[^80] that were tied together [to be sold] by number[^81] may be used as *s'chach*.[^82]  
Similarly, if one cuts off the top of a date palm and the branches are bound to it, it may be used as *s'chach*, because elements that are bound naturally are not considered to be bundles.[^83] Furthermore, even if one tied the tops of the branches from one side, and they thus appear to be a single bundle with one of its two ends bound naturally and the other bound as a result of human activity, it may be used as *s'chach*. A single tree which is bound up is not considered to be a bundle, but rather a single piece of wood, since [the branches] are bound together naturally.[^84]  
Similarly, any knot which is not strong enough to hold when carried is not considered a knot [and the resulting bundle may be used as *s'chach*].[^85] ^5-11

**12.** A person who constructs his sukkah under a tree is considered as though he built it within his home.[^86]  
If one draped the leaves and branches of trees [over the sukkah], and then placed *s'chach* over them, and only afterwards detached them,[^87] [the following rules apply:]  
If the amount of [kosher] *s'chach* exceeded [the branches], it is kosher.[^88] If the amount of *s'chach* which originally was kosher did not exceed [the branches],[^89] one must move them after detaching them, so that they will have been put in place for the purpose of a *sukkah*.[^90] ^5-12

**13.** If one mixed a substance which may be used for *s'chach* with a substance that may not be used for *s'chach*[^91] and used[^92] the two as *s'chach*, even though the quantity of kosher *s'chach* exceeds that of the substance which was not acceptable as *s'chach*,[^93] [the mixture] is not acceptable.[^94]  
If one covered the sukkah with the two substances and kept them separate,[^95] [the following rules apply:] If there are more than three handbreadths of the substance which is not acceptable as *s'chach* in one place,[^96] whether[^97] in the middle of the sukkah or at its side, it is not acceptable.[^98] ^5-13

**14.** Where does the above apply? In a small sukkah.[^99] However, in a large sukkah,[^100] where there is a substance that is unacceptable as *s'chach* in the middle, it disqualifies the sukkah if there are four handbreadths of it.[^101] [If there is] less than that,[^102] the sukkah is kosher.[^103]  
Where the substance that is unacceptable as *s'chach* is at the side, it disqualifies the sukkah if there are four cubits of it.[^104] [If there is] less than that, the sukkah is kosher.[^105]  
For example,  
a) [the roof of] a house which was opened in the center and *s'chach* placed over the opening  
b)[^106] a courtyard surrounded by an *exedra*[^107] which was covered with *s'chach*  
c)[^108] a large sukkah over which was placed a substance that was not acceptable as *s'chach* near the sides of its walls.[^109]  
[In all these cases,] if there are four cubits [or more] from the edge of the kosher *s'chach* until the wall, it is not acceptable. If there is less than that amount, we view it as though the wall has been made crooked[^110] - i.e., the substance that is not acceptable as *s'chach* is considered part of the wall and it is kosher.[^111] This concept is a halachah received by Moses on Mount Sinai.[^112] ^5-14

**15.** What is a small sukkah?[^113] Any [sukkah] whose area is no more than seven handbreadths by seven handbreadths.[^114]  
[What is meant by] a large one? Any [sukkah] [whose area is large enough] that seven handbreadths by seven handbreadths of kosher *s'chach* will remain besides the *s'chach* which is not acceptable.[^115] ^5-15

**16.** If one used as *s'chach* substances that were acceptable as *s'chach* and substances that were not acceptable as *s'chach*, and placed them alongside each other, leaving no place with non-kosher *s'chach* more than three handbreadths in area,[^116] [the following rules apply:]  
If the total of the kosher *s'chach* exceeds that of the non-kosher *s'chach*, it is kosher.[^117] If there was an exactly equal amount of both substances, it is not acceptable[^118] even though there is not a single place which has three handbreadths [of non-kosher *s'chach*].[^119] [This decision is rendered] because non-kosher *s'chach* is considered to be open space. ^5-16

**17.** If one spread a cloth above [the *s'chach*][^120] or spread one below it to catch [the leaves] which fall,[^121] it is unacceptable.[^122] If one spread it [under the *s'chach*] as a decoration, it is kosher.[^123] Similarly, if one covered the sukkah with *s'chach* as required by law and adorned it[^124] with various types of fruit, delicacies, and articles[^125] which hang from either the walls or the *s'chach* as a decoration, it is kosher.[^126] ^5-17

**18.** Sukkah decorations do not reduce its height,[^127] but they do reduce its width.[^128]  
If the sukkah decorations are four handbreadths or more[^129] removed from the roof,[^130] it is unacceptable, because it is as though a person who sits there is not sitting under the *s'chach*, but rather under the decorations, which are foods and utensils that are not acceptable as *s'chach*.[^131] ^5-18

**19.** The [following rules apply when the] *s'chach* has open spaces through which the sky can be seen: If the area of the open spaces is equivalent to[^132] that of the space covered by *s'chach*, it is not acceptable, because[^133] the portion exposed to the sun will be greater than the shaded portion.[^134] Whenever the portion exposed to the sun is greater than the shaded portion, it is not considered as *s'chach*.[^135]  
If the *s'chach* exceeds the open space, it is kosher.[^136] ^5-19

**20.** When does the above apply? When there is no one open space[^137] of three handbreadths.[^138] However, if there is an open space of three handbreadths[^139] - whether in the center or at the side[^140] - it is unacceptable until one reduces [the space] to less than three.[^141]  
If one used substances that were not acceptable as *s'chach*[^142] - e.g., pillows and blankets - to reduce the space, it is kosher if the sukkah is large.[^143] If it is a small sukkah, it is not acceptable[^144] unless [the space] was reduced with a substance that is acceptable as *s'chach*.[^145]  
When the shaded portion of most of the sukkah exceeds the portion exposed to the sun, although in the lesser part of the sukkah the portion exposed to the sun exceeds the shaded portion,[^146] because[^147] as a whole the shaded portion exceeds the portion exposed to the sun, it is kosher.[^148] ^5-20

**21.** The proper way[^149] is that *s'chach* should be thin, so that the large stars can be seen through it.[^150] However, even though it is thick[^151] - like [the roof of] a house - and stars cannot be seen through it, it is kosher.[^152]  
If the *s'chach* is uneven[^153] - i.e., some of it high and some of it low[^154] - it is kosher, provided there is less than three handbreadths between the upper and lower [portions of the *s'chach*].[^155] If the upper portion [of the *s'chach*] is a handbreadth[^156] or more wide, even though it is more than three handbreadths above [the lower portion], we consider it to be descending and touching the edge of the lower portion.[^157] [This applies] provided it is aligned opposite the edge of the lower portion.[^158] ^5-21

**22.** When a person constructs one sukkah on top of another sukkah, the lower one is unacceptable.[^159] It is considered as though it had been constructed in a house.[^160] [However,] the upper one is kosher.[^161]  
When do we say that the lower one is unacceptable? When the inner space of the upper sukkah is ten handbreadths or more [high][^162] and the roof of the lower sukkah is strong enough to hold the pillows and covers of the upper sukkah,[^163] even if that is done with difficulty.[^164]  
However, if the inner space of the upper sukkah is less than ten handbreadths [high], or the roof of the lower sukkah is not strong enough to hold the pillows and covers of the upper sukkah, even with difficulty,[^165] even[^166] the lower sukkah is kosher.[^167] This applies provided the height of both together does not exceed twenty cubits,[^168] since [use of] the lower sukkah is permitted because of the *s'chach* of the upper one.[^169] ^5-22

**23.** A bed [with a canopy placed] inside a sukkah: If [the canopy] is more than ten handbreadths high,[^170] a person who sits under it does not fulfill his obligation,[^171] because it is considered to be a sukkah[^172] within a sukkah.[^173]  
Similarly, a canopy[^174] with a roof[^175] - even as small as a handbreadth: If it is ten handbreadths high, one may not sleep[^176] under it in a sukkah.[^177] By the same token, if one sets up four pillars and spreads a sheet over them, if they are ten [handbreadths high], they are considered to be a sukkah within a sukkah.[^178] ^5-23

**24.** In contrast, should one spread a sheet over two pillars[^179] or [use] a canopy that has a roof of less than a handbreadth[^180] - no matter how high they are[^181] - it is permitted to sleep under them within a sukkah.[^182] They are not considered to be a sukkah within a sukkah, because they do not have a roof.[^183] ^5-24

**25.** A borrowed sukkah is fit [to be used on the holiday].[^184] Similarly, a stolen sukkah is also fit [for use].[^185]  
What does the latter imply? If a person attacked a colleague, forced him to leave his sukkah, stole it, and dwelled in it, the attacker has fulfilled his obligation, because landed property cannot be stolen.[^186]  
[Similarly,] if he stole wood and made a sukkah from it, he has fulfilled his obligation,[^187] because the Sages ordained that the owner of the wood is entitled only to the monetary worth of the wood.[^188] Even if one stole boards and merely put them in place without attaching them or changing anything about them, he has fulfilled his obligation.[^189]  
If a person constructs his sukkah in the public domain,[^190] it is acceptable.[^191] ^5-25

[^1]: Some of the requirements mentioned by the Rambam were established by Torah law, while others are Rabbinic ordinances, as explained below.
[^2]: i.e., is an agricultural product and is neither found naturally - e.g., metals - nor produced from animals nor manufactured synthetically;
[^3]: and is not still connected to its source of nurture;
[^4]: This excludes articles used as food and those that have been fashioned into utensils. These three requirements are alluded to by [[Torah 47 - Re'eh#^16-13|Deuteronomy 16:13]]: "Make the Sukkot holiday for yourselves for seven days, when you gather in from your grain and grapes..."  
    [[Sukkah 12#12a|*Sukkah* 12a]] explains that the verse implies that the *s'chach*, the essential element of the sukkah, must resemble "the leftovers from the grain and grape harvest."  
    (The expression "alluded to" was used rather than "derived from" on the basis of the Rambam's commentary on the Mishnah, *Sukkah* 1:4. That expression implies that the concept cannot be derived from the verse per se. Rather, it was transmitted as a halachah l'Moshe miSinai, and the Biblical verse is merely an allusion.)
