### 15.1–15: Pilate condemns Jesus

*[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-1|Text]], p. 103* · ([[Matthew 27#^27-1|Mt 27.1–2]],[[Matthew 27#^27-11|11–26]]; [[Luke 23#^23-1|Lk 23.1–5]],[[Luke 23#^23-18|18–25]]; [[John 18#^18-28|Jn 18.28–40]]; [[John 19#^19-4|19.4–16]]). ^15-1

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-1|1]]:** *Handed him over to Pilate*, see [[Matthew 27#^27-1|Mt 27.1]]–2n, and [[John 18#^18-31|Jn 18.31]]. ^15-1-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-2|2]]:** *King of the Jews*, a claim that the Romans would understand as dangerous; they tolerated no kings except those, like the Herods, whom they had appointed. ^15-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-6|6–15]]:** *Barabbas*, Aramaic for “son of the father,” and thus the character is likely seen as a narrative double for Jesus, depicted by Mark as the son of God the Father. The name is not unknown, however (*m. Peʾah* 2.6; [[Berakhot 18#18b|*b. Ber.* 18b]]). There is no evidence that the Romans released prisoners, much less insurrectionists, at Passover. Further, were Barabbas to be released for the festival, the timing is off: the paschal lamb was eaten the night before. ^15-6

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-14|14–15]]:** Mark places the blame on the Jews, an increasing tendency in subsequent Gospels. After the dissemination of the Gospels, some Christians considered *Pilate* a convert to Christianity, and he is honored as a martyr in the Coptic Orthodox Church; his feast day is June 25. The transfer of guilt from the Romans—who crucified Jesus—to the Jews was then complete. *Flogging*, served to weaken the victim before crucifixion. ^15-14

> [!note] Jesus’ “Cry of Dereliction” *(p. 103)*
> Psalm 22 was a favorite of the early followers of Jesus for the interpretation of the Passion narrative (see “Scripture Fulfillments,” p. 99). No element is more dramatic than Jesus’ final cry, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” Mark reports the words in a combination of Aramaic and Hebrew and then provides a Greek translation. [[Luke 23#^23-46|Luke 23.46]] lacks this cry and presents instead a less passionate quotation of [[Psalms 031#^31-6|Ps 31.5]], while [[John 19#^19-30|John 19.30]] depicts Jesus stoically uttering a more philosophical phrase, “It is finished” or, “It is fulfilled.”
>
> Mark does not depict Jesus dying the noble death of Socrates (*Phaedo* 115b–118b) or the martyr Eleazar in the Maccabean Revolt ([[LXX 2 Maccabees 06#^6-30|2 Macc 6.30]]). Rather, the suffering that Jesus has predicted is fully depicted, as well as the irony that people mock him and do not understand. Jesus’ descending fate, communicated from the beginning of the Gospel and unrelieved even by a resurrection account (see Introduction and 16.1–8n.), is most powerfully depicted here. The other elements from Ps 22 noted in “Scripture Fulfillments,” p. 99, only serve to increase the theme of suffering through shaming.
>
> In Psalm 22, where the issue is likely a grave illness, the plaint is followed by God’s healing and a thanksgiving for it (vv 21b–31). Readers knowing the Psalm would anticipate this resolution, just as they know the resurrection account that Mark does not provide. Further, Mark depicts Jesus as crying out but not necessarily as despairing. In the Jewish lament tradition, bitter complaint can open the communication with God that gives rise to prayer and a plea for help (cf. [[Lamentations 3#^3-22|Lam 3.22–23]]). Thus, together with the tearing of the Temple curtain (15.38) and the utterance of the centurion (15.39), the cry may also signal an exchange between human and divine realms.

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-16|16–20]]:** Jesus is mocked by the Romans as a king, a parody of “Hail, Caesar!” The shaming and irony are emphasized more than physical pain. The *purple cloak* evokes royal robes, and the *crown* of *thorns* a gold circlet or perhaps a laurel wreath. ^15-16

### 15.20–47: The crucifixion and burial

*[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-20|Text]], p. 104* · ([[Matthew 27#^27-27|Mt 27.27–61]]; [[Luke 23#^23-18|Lk 23.18–56]]; [[John 19#^19-1|Jn 19.1–3]],[[John 19#^19-17|17–42]]). ^15-20

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-22|22]]:** *Golgotha*, Aramaic for *place of a skull*, may refer to the shape of the hill. ^15-22

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-23|23]]:** *Wine mixed with myrrh* (here and vv 24,34, see “Scripture Fulfillments,” p. 99). ^15-23

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-24|24]]:** The crucifixion is described in only one sentence. The extreme physical agony would have been known by all, but the Gospels emphasize the shame much more than the suffering. Rome reserved death by crucifixion for slaves and insurrectionists. *Divided his clothes*, the Psalms and Isaiah were used to provide some of the details of the crucifixion scene. ^15-24

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-33|33]]:** In apocalypses (e.g., *2 Bar.* 32), the conditions at the end-time replay those of creation; cf. [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-2|Gen 1.2–3]]. ^15-33

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-36|36]]:** *Sour wine*, a mild anesthetic. ^15-36

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-38|38]]:** *Was torn*, [[Mark 01#^1-10|Mk 1.10]]; [[Isaiah 64#^64-1|Isa 64.1]]. The tearing of the *curtain* could symbolize the presence of God’s power at the moment of Jesus’ death, the access to God, or a critique of the Temple and anticipation of its destruction. ^15-38

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-39|39]]:** At the Gospel’s climactic point, the *centurion* declares what the *scripture was fulfilled that says, “And he was counted among the lawless.”* f Or *blasphemed* g Or *the Christ* h Or *earth* i Other ancient authorities read *made me a reproach* followers of Jesus cannot (cf. [[John 11#^11-49|Jn 11.49–52]]). ^15-39

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-40|40–41]]:** The *women* who *provided for him* (see 1.30–31n.) see both the burial (v 47) and the empty tomb (16.1–8); for Mark they serve as witnesses that the empty tomb is the one in which Jesus’ corpse had been placed, though according to Josephus women were not allowed to serve as witnesses (*Ant.* 4.219). ^15-40

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-42|42]]:** *Day of Preparation . . . the day before the sabbath* ([[LXX Judith 08#^8-6|Jdt 8.6]]; [[LXX 2 Maccabees 08#^8-26|2 Macc 8.26]]), implies that the crucifixion was on a Friday. [[John 19#^19-14|John 19.14]] dates the crucifixion to the day of preparation for the Passover, not the first day of the Feast. ^15-42

**[[NRSVue Mark 15#^15-43|43]]:** Others expected the kingdom of God, so *Joseph* is not necessarily a follower of Jesus; cf. 12.34. By assuring that Jesus’ corpse is removed before nightfall, Joseph fulfills [[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^21-22|Deut 21.22–23]] (cf. Tob 1–2). ^15-43
