1.12 Provisional conclusions on the dating of the Brahmayāmala

Uncertainties surround both the absolute and relative chronologies of the BraYā, further complicated by the fact that the text appears to have multiple strata. Balancing a large number of factors, we may nonetheless narrow down the BraYā’s period of composition to a plausible range of dates, keeping in mind the provisional nature of this exercise. In the matter of dating I thus am inclined to set aside more extreme scenarios, such as the possibility that the late-sixth to early-seventh century redactors of the Skandapurāna knew of the BraYā in its current form, or that the Bra Ya’s original redactors knew the Devīmāhātmya (if indeed YoxocnHi is correct in dating the latter to the second half of the eighth century). How long it might have taken for the BraYa’s two or three strata to come together is a matter of speculation. It is possible, for instance, that the earliest stratum belongs to the final phase of the original Skandapurāna (last quarter of the seventh century?), while the last stratum may be closer in date to the Laghu-samvara (second half of the eighth century?). 2.1 Brahmayāmala: Union Tantra of Brahmā

Yamala has as its primary meaning “pair,” and in tantric literature, frequently has the specific sense of “coupled god and goddess,” especially in contrast to ekavīra or ekavīrā, a solitary deity (male or female, respectively). The Yāmala-tantras as a genre appear defined, in principle, by teaching the cult of a coupled supreme Godhead.” On this basis I favour the English rendering “Union Tantra” for the scriptures designated Yāmala or Yāmalatantra, as proposed by SanDERsoN (1988: 672). In the Brahmayāmala, the supreme, paired divinity comprises the deities Bhairava, more specifically Kapālīśabhairava, and the Great Goddess, whose primary names include Caṇndā Kāpālinī (“Fierce Skullbearer”), Aghorī (“Un-Dreadful;” also Aghoreśīt and Aghoreśvarī), and Bhairavi. As noted earlier, SANDERSON has shown that the gender polarity of this supreme divinity is imbalanced, for the mantric being of the supreme Goddess, the Nine-Syllable Vidyā, subsumes that of Bhairava and his female entourage."

It might appear incongruous for a Saiva tantra cast as a dialogue between Bhairava and the Goddess to bear the title Brahmayāmala, for the orthodox creator-deity Brahmā has remarkably little to do with the text. His role is confined to a narrative episode in Bra Ya 83, which contains a fascinating variant on the “Skull of Brahma” (brahmakapāla) myth, analysed in \$4.3. 2.2 Picumata: Doctrine of the Mingled Fluids

Alongside “Brahmayāmala,” “Picumata” figures as foremost among the titles and epithets appearing in the text’s chapter colophons. As mentioned, references to the title Picumata (sometimes Picutantra) within the body of the text cluster heavily in chapters 62—90. Indeed, the absence of the title Picumata from earlier chapters and from the Uttarottaratantra (chapters 91—104) is so striking that I am tempted to refer to the middle portion of Satka II, up to or possibly inclusive of the Uttaratantra, as the “Picumata stratum.”™™! A number of other tantras also bear the designation -mata, “Doctrine \[Tantra\],” such as the Siddhayogesvarīmata (a tantra of the Vidyāpītha) and Devyāmata (an early Pratisthātantra). The epithet Picumata actually occurs with twice the frequency of “Brahmayāmala” in chapter colophons, and the text is cited by this name and variations such as Pżcutantra in the Bhairavamangalā™ and Tantrāloka.\*? Throughout his oeuvre, SANDERSON highlights this epithets importance by referring to the scripture as the Picumata or Picumata/ Brahmayāmala.\*\* This choice has merit, for instance by clearly distinguishing the text from later tantras claiming the name Brahmayāmala. Vidhāna and its synonym vidhi connote in this context the processes of ritual. Procedures for mantra-incantation and consecration are, for instance, called japavidhāna and abhisekavidhi, the subjects of chapters 18 and 34, respectively. 2.4 Māülatantra: Root Scripture

In the revelation narrative of chapter one and at several other points in the text, the BraYā refers to itself as the mülatantra, the “Root Tantra” or “Root Scripture.”®? Yet although claiming the status of the foundational, originary scripture, it also describes itself as originating from a text of <sup>1</sup>25,000 verses called the Vimala or “Immaculate,” the primordial font of scripture. These claims are embedded within a theology of revelation that posits ‘scriptural wisdom (jṛāna, śsivajñāna; also śivaśāstra, etc.) as a transcendental essence which descends in streams or currents (srotas) from its primordial source at the apex of the hierarchy of reality levels or ontic principles (tattva), taking on concrete, linguistic form according to the capacities of its various redactors and audiences.®?

Perhaps the earliest surviving account of the ‘descent (avatāra) of revelation in aiva sources occurs in the Uttarasūtra of the Niśsvāsatattvasamhitā. In its simple description, scripture (the śāstra or sivatantra) emerges from the formless, supreme Ŝiva as subtle sound (nāda), which Sadāśiva sets down into language. This he transmits to the gods, whence it reaches the sages and mortals in various redactions (figure 2.1).%t A demonstrably later scripture, the Svac-chandatantra describes a more detailed genesis of scripture. Cf. Svacchandatantra 8.32b, referring to ãdhārabheda, “difference in the locus \[of revelation\].”
