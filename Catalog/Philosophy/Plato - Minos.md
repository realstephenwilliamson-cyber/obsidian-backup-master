## About

A short dialogue traditionally attributed to Plato, on what law (nomos) is. In the first half Socrates and an unnamed companion test definitions of law; the second half praises Minos, the legendary king of Crete, as the best of kings and lawgivers.

- **People and setting:** Socrates and an unnamed companion (hetairos), read by scholars as a student, a friend or an everyman. Wikipedia says in passing that the conversation takes place in Crete. No dramatic date is given.
- **Argument:** law is not simply what custom accepts, nor a city's resolutions, which can be wicked; Socrates proposes that law aims to be the discovery of what is. The companion objects that laws differ between cities, citing human sacrifice. Socrates answers that the one who knows makes true law, as Minos did, taught by Zeus, giving the Cretans laws that the Spartans took up.
- **Place in the corpus:** Aristophanes of Byzantium and Thrasyllus both grouped it with the *Laws*; Thrasyllus' last tetralogy runs *Minos*, *Laws*, *Epinomis*, *Letters*. Ancient commentators treated it as a preface to the *Laws*.
- **Authenticity:** disputed. Wikipedia says most modern scholars reject it (among them Jaeger, A. E. Taylor and Christopher Rowe); Grote, Morrow and Cobb defended it, and Leo Strauss wrote a commentary on it. August Boeckh (1806) assigned it to Simon the Shoemaker. The IEP lists it among works presumed authentic in later antiquity but doubted recently. John Dillon (BMCR, 2015) says only followers of Strauss now defend it; José Colen (2015) says it is gaining acceptance and that no ancient writer seems to have doubted it.
- **Date:** from its likeness to the *Hipparchus*, many scholars date it soon after the middle of the 4th century BC (Wikipedia).
- **Text:** Stephanus pages 313–321 (Perseus catalogue); Lamb's Loeb translation is in *Plato* vol. 8 (1927).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says it is often noted as introducing a theory of natural law, though the word for nature (phusis) never appears in it.
