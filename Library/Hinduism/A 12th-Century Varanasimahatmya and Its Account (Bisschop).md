*THE EARLY TANTRA SERIES*

*Tantric scriptures form the basis of almost all the various theistic schools of theology and ritual in post-Vedic India, as well as of a major strand of Buddhism (Vajrayāna). Among these schools, those centred on the Hindu deities Śiva and Viṣṇu spread well beyond the Indian subcontinent to Kambuja (Cambodia/Laos/Thailand), Champa (Vietnam) and Indonesia, while Buddhist tantrism quickly became pan-Asian.*

*With this mini-series, launched within the ‘Collection Indologie’, we are releasing some of the fruits of a Franco-German project funded from 2008 to 2011 by the Agence Nationale pour la Recherche and the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft. The series takes its name from the project’s title: ‘Early Tantra: Discovering the interrelationships and common ritual syntax of the Śaiva, Buddhist, Vaiṣṇava and Saura traditions’. Our aim was to study the interrelationships between the tantric traditions on the basis of fundamental source-material, which we would edit and publish for the first time. For this we made use of some of the exceptionally rich manuscript resources gathered in the twentieth century and studied by German and French research institutes at opposite poles of the subcontinent.*

*In recent centuries, the Tamil-speaking South is the only area where a vast corpus of Sanskrit texts of what was long the dominant school of tantric Śaivism continued being copied and so transmitted to the present day. So when, in 1956, Jean Filliozat secured a foothold in Pondicherry for French indological research, he created an ideal institutional base for the study of a forgotten chapter in the religious history of Asia. Gradually,* *the largest specialised manuscript collection of texts relating to the Śaiva Siddhānta was amassed, recognised in 2005 by UNESCO as a “Memory of the World” collection: The Śaiva Manuscripts of Pondicherry.*

*At the other end of the subcontinent, the cool climate of Nepal has preserved ancient manuscripts of texts of virtually every branch of Indian learning. Much of the early history of tantrism is thus preserved in the vast archive of Nepalese manuscripts microfilmed over more than three decades by the Nepal-German Manuscript Preservation Project (NGMPP),* *then partly catalogued by the Nepalese German Manuscript Cataloguing Project (NGMCP), run from Hamburg and from the NGMCP’s base in Kathmandu, the Nepal Research Centre (NRC). One Nepalese treasure,* *also included, in 2013, in UNESCO’s register, formed the corner-stone of our project: the ninth-century manuscript of the Niśvāsatattvasaṃhitā.*

*The Early Tantra Series is not a closed collection: as well as the editions and translations promised as part of the ‘Early Tantra’ project,* *studies of numerous related works were inspired or further advanced during the project’s workshops.*

##### Introduction

In the *Newsletter of the NGMCP*, Number 5, I gave a brief report on the existence of a 12th/13th-century Nepalese palm-leaf manuscript preserved in the collection of the Kaiser Library in Kathmandu (Bisschop 2007). The manuscript, microfilmed by the NGMPP on reel C 6/3, transmits a compendium of Vārāṇasīmāhātmyas culled from a variety of Purāṇas, some of which can be identified in published editions of the texts but many of which are not known otherwise, offering a unique glimpse into the sanctification of early medieval Vārāṇasī. As such it is a highly valuable work for scholars of Purāṇas and Vārāṇasī alike. A table of contents may serve as a brief introduction to the material preserved in the compendium:

When I started transcribing the first 13 chapters, an otherwise unknown Māhātmya attributed in the colophons to the *Bhairavaprādurbhāva* of the *Matsyapurāṇa*, I came across a chapter (*Vārāṇasīmāhātmya* 8) that proves to be of some relevance for tantric scholars, because it presents what appears to be a unique and fairly extensive account of a hypethral Yoginī temple in Vārāṇasī. The attribution of this Māhātmya to the *Bhairavaprādurbhāva* of the *Matsyapurāṇa* is almost certainly not authentic. As can be observed from the table of contents, the attributions of individual Māhātmyas to different Purāṇas are often spurious and the first 13 chapters, to which the account of the Yoginī temple belongs, form no exception. While there is no tradition recording the existence of a *Bhairavaprādurbhāva* of the *Matsyapurāṇa*, the case is different for the *Vāmanapurāṇa*. A look at the colophons of manuscripts in the apparatus of the Kashiraj Trust edition of the *Vāmanapurāṇa* reveals that a number of manuscripts of the *Vāmanapurāṇa* refer to various chapters starting from *Vāmanapurāṇa* 9 onwards with the colophon: *vāmanapurāṇe bhairavaprādurbhāve*.<sup>1</sup> The original author of the first 13 chapters of the compendium no doubt intended to attribute the Māhātmya to this section of the Purāṇa. There are a number of arguments for this. First of all and most obviously the Māhātmya consists of an instruction of Nārada by Pulastya. The same is the case in the *Vāmanapurāṇa*. As for the topic of the *Bhairavaprādurbhāva*, ‘Origin of Bhairava(s)’, this is narrated in *Vāmanapurāṇa* 44.30ff., in the context of the destruction of Andhaka by Śiva-Bhairava. After Śiva has been struck by Andhaka with his mace (*gadā*), seven Bhairavas arise from the blood flowing in different directions: Vidyārāja (east), Kālarāja (south), Kāmarāja (west), Somarāja (north), Svacchandarāja, Lalitarāja and Vighnarāja (*Vāmanapurāṇa* 44.32–38).<sup>2</sup>Together with Bhairava himself they make up the *aṣṭabhairava*. It is in the context of this narrative that the account of the Yoginī temple occurs, for after Gaṇeśa has been sent (*Vārāṇasīmāhātmya* 8.1–10), Śiva sends the Pañcamudrās, designated as Mātreśvarīs, and they too settle in Vārāṇasī (*Vārāṇasīmāhātmya* 8.11–14). The remainder of the chapter is devoted to a description of the site where they take up their residence. This place is clearly associated with the Yoginīs, more specifically the passage seems to describe a hypethral Yoginī temple compound. There are a number of features which point in this direction. First of all there are the Pañcamudrās themselves, their name indicating a connection with the *pañcamudrā*, the five insignia which mark the Kāpālikas. The names of *liṅga*s mentioned, such as Vīreśvara, Svargeśvara (installed by *svargasiddhi*), Mokṣeśvara (by *mokṣasiddhi*) and Pātāleśvara (by *pātālasiddhi*), hint at a Mantramārga/tantric background, but the site’s most prominent feature is a circle (*cakra*) of Clan-Yoginīs (*kulayoginī* ), referred to as ‘the supreme (*uttama*) Khecarīcakra’.
