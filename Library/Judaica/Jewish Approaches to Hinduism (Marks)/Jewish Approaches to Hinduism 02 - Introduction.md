## Introduction

Andalusia and Toledo, between 1130 and 1140: the most lauded Hebrew poet of his generation, Judah Ha-Levi, sees his Jewish world overturned and torn down by warring Muslim and Christian armies. At the same time, the pleasures and arts he had enjoyed as a courtier, and the philosophies of his time, now appear worthless to him. He turns to a Judaism of tradition for a fuller more compelling life, and realizes that he can live it truly only in Jerusalem. Writing about this Judaism in a book he calls, *A Defense and an Argument on behalf of the Abused Religion*, he remembers having read or heard that people living in far-away India claim to have buildings older than the Torah’s date of creation and a history older than Adam. Ha-Levi marshals everything damning that he knows about Indians – their idols, astral magic, trickery, argumentative nature – to attack their claims and save the truth of Scripture and the Jewish piety that he had discovered as his world fell apart.

Toledo, early 13th century. We don’t know much about the life of Jacob ben El\`azar. He lived in Toledo among Muslims, Christians, and Jews ruled by Christian kings. He had a cosmopolitan education, he read books written in Arabic, Hebrew, and Latin. His encounter with India came through a patron’s request to translate an Arabic version of a book that had reached Spain from distant India by way of Persia. Known as the *Panchatantra* in Sanskrit and *Kalila wa-Dimna* in Arabic, the book told a series of animal fables that illustrated principles of governing and strategy; it was counsel for ministers of kings. As Jacob translates it into Hebrew, he discovers to his surprise that it contains moral teachings of general applicability that are, in his words, “righteous,” “upright,” and “astute” – a “glorious selection of beautiful sayings,” which he recommends to his Jewish readers. This discovery puzzles him because every educated Jew and Muslim knew that Indians possessed neither divine revelation nor prophets to receive it. So from where did such “glorious” guidance derive? Jacob’s introduction to his translation tells us his solution. This book, he asserts, is the Indian *torah*, the instruction that Indian sages, using their own minds, decided to compose when they realized that their people “walked in darkness” through lack of prophecy.

1822, Zamosc, in Galicia. Samson Bloch sees himself stumbling in darkness too, a “wild hollow man” without a path to follow, going nowhere. 

*[p. 2]* ^pg15

This was the life he led while studying the traditional Jewish holy writings. He could find neither God nor God’s wonders in the world. But then he discovers what he calls “wisdom,” that is, secular knowledge, which opens a path into a world of light, and he can now find God in the wonders of nature. He also learns about foreign lands, and this knowledge enlightens him too. He decides to write a book of world geography that will lead his people, benighted as he was, into the wide world beyond narrow rabbinic knowledge. He calls his book, “Paths of the World.” One important land where he will lead his readers is India, a land upon which “Nature has emptied the cup of her beauty, splendor, and majesty.” He tells his readers stories about rivers, crocodiles, elephants, and snakes, but he is most interested in the people of India, their government, philosophy, and acts of worship. He shows that Indian philosophy and ethics are much like those of Judaism. Such human commonalities are one message of his book, but it also warns readers to learn from the world’s irrationality and ignorance, and Bloch finds examples of this in India too. Thus will knowledge of India enlighten Jews.

Bombay, November 13, 1859. A traveler from Jerusalem browned by Yemen’s piercing sun, wearing turban and robe, disembarks after a long sea voyage from Aden and stands on the shore listening to an incomprehensible language, until some wagon-drivers recognize he is Jewish and bring him to the local synagogue. Jacob Sapir Ha-Levi has come to meet his Indian “brothers,” the Jews of Bombay, Cochin, and Calcutta, but also to observe the peoples of the land, upon whom he gazes with great curiosity and initial admiration. In his travelogue he will extol the comfort of riding in palanquins, the skill of Indian carpenters, and the clever ways Indians cool their houses. He praises the beauty and peace of the Kerala backwaters and the variety of India’s fruits. But not their religion. He is curious, and he watches Hindus make offerings at street shrines and to images of gods in their homes, but he never talks with them or asks questions. To Jacob Sapir, Hindu worship is simply “idolatry,” the same idolatry portrayed in the Bible, eliciting revulsion. Believing that their religious rules prevent Indians from enjoying life, Sapir recommends that they free themselves by reading European books and converting to Christianity.

Jews have been writing about Hinduism since the 1st century CE. India and Hinduism, though never at the center of Jewish thought, claim a place in its history, in the picture Jews held of the wider world, of other religions and other human beings. When Jews lived in Muslim Spain of the Middle Ages, India and its beliefs were a fact in their world, pictured to them through Arabic travel reports and geographies, traditions of scientific knowledge, and the Arabic translation of the Indian classic, *Panchatantra*. Half this book examines what Spanish Jews did with that fact, how they fit it into their ideas of Judaism and their worldscape. Although these Jews lived in the same Islamicate setting, with the same sources of information, their images of Indians were surprisingly diverse – from Halevi’s quarrelsome 

*[p. 3]* ^pg16

idolaters to the esteemed scientists and philosophers of Abraham ibn Ezra, to Ben El\`azar’s laudable moralists, to the industrious citizens and ominous priests told by Benjamin of Tudela. In Christian Europe at this same time, knowledge of Indian beliefs reached Jews through stories of naked Indian philosophers yearning for the afterlife or debating morality with Alexander the Great. Immanuel Bonfils of Provence thoroughly re-imagined these Indian ascetics as rabbis who quoted Scripture and Talmud, recommended self-control, and espoused a Jewish concept of divine ineffability.

India’s religions then stayed on the margins of Jewish consciousness for several centuries, with the medieval images repeated or ignored, until the great European explorations beginning in the 16th century brought home new and dramatic pictures of India, and Menasseh ben Israel took up the question of India’s meaning for Judaism and for Jews suffering in Christian Europe. The Hindu belief in reincarnation, newly (re)discovered, struck him as particularly significant because it accorded with what had become a central doctrine in Jewish mysticism, and he had to ask why Jews and Hindus believed in the same profound truth. The next important change in Jewish ideas about Hindu religion emerged in the 19th century along two different paths. First, British and German orientalist research shaped Samson Bloch’s view of Hinduism, and the Enlightenment instilled a tolerance for foreign religions. Second, two Jewish travelers, David d’Beth Hillel and Jacob Sapir, visited India and became fascinated with the images and worship they observed at Hindu temples. But they interpreted Hindu worship, as did the Evangelical missionaries of the time, as simple biblical idolatry, reacting to it with revulsion over what they and the missionaries called “abomination.”

Thus, Jews have long been interested in Hinduism, and they had an interest, a stake, in its meaning for Jewish identity. Some authors saw their Judaism threatened by what they imagined as Hinduism’s ancient origins, magic, and astral worship. Some felt revulsion toward what they considered Hindu idolatry. Others were attracted to what they imagined as India’s moral teachings, science, civility, or mystical ideas. Bloch in the 19th century discerned essential parallels with Jewish theology and ethics. Many Jews wrote about Hindu rituals and holy places using the Hebrew terminology of Jewish ritual and synagogues, and about Hindu teachers using traditional vocabulary associated with rabbis. Jews had to decide whether Hinduism was or was not “*dat*,” a Hebrew word with a range of meanings centered around a religion of laws. And Jewish writers had to ask themselves how Judaism was superior and why? Reports about India led Jewish thinkers to recognize the complexity of Indian culture, and to take Hinduism seriously, as religious threat or confirmation or opportunity to learn.

This book will explore past expressions of the Jewish interest in Hinduism in order to learn what Hinduism has meant to Jews living mainly in the 12th through the 19th centuries. I am writing intellectual history, a history of Jewish ideas about Hinduism. This is the history of a topic, not the history of a particular idea, since Jews have formed various different ideas about Hinduism. 

*[p. 4]* ^pg17

Rather, this is intellectual history in the sense of trying to understand written ideas in their historical context. It assumes that we cannot adequately understand the meaning of written words without exploring that context. It seeks a depth and historical dimensionality of interpretation. In this case, I look at the cultural context, within and outside Jewish society, that provided images and ideas about India and its religions, and I examine the literary context, that is, an author’s writings which provide background to what he or she says about India. In the process and whenever possible, I apply a close literary reading of the specific words an author wrote about Indian religions.

As I approach each of the authors I study, I pose five related questions. The primary questions are, What is the author’s image of Indian religion? What are the sources of that image? And what ideas and judgments did the author form about Indian religion? After exploring these topics, I can then ask, How did the authors compare their Judaism with their image of Hinduism, and, if the material supports generalization, what were their underlying methods of comparing religions? This book is a history of answers to those questions.

It is a history of answers ranging over seven centuries, rather than a deep and broad analysis of answers from one defined historical period and society. Thus, the analysis of each author is limited and necessarily incomplete. I could have written much more about Judah Ha-Levi, Abraham ibn Ezra, Menasseh ben Israel, and Samson Bloch, and scholars with greater expertise than my own could have written more penetrating and broader analyses, and I have turned to their scholarship whenever possible. But the book’s purpose is to construct a history of ideas that changed over time with different writers in different settings.

In contrast, however, to several existing surveys of Jewish ideas about Hinduism, this book devotes an entire chapter to each author or text.<sup>1</sup> A useful intellectual history requires more than a few paragraphs devoted to each thinker. Of the Jewish authors who wrote about India, however, I chose to study only those who wrote about its religion. The authors I studied, moreover, should be considered case histories rather than an exhaustive analysis of everything written about Indian thought during the historical period that I explore.<sup>2</sup>

This history of Jewish ideas about Hinduism is not, then, a comparison of Judaism and Hinduism, or of specific Judaic and Hindu elements that might invite comparison. There are already several fine books and a journal that engage in such comparisons – notably, *Between Jerusalem and Benares:* *Comparative Studies in Judaism and Hinduism* (edited by Goodman, 1994), Barbara Holdrege’s *Veda and Torah: Transcending the Textuality of Scripture* (1995), Margaret Chatterjee’s *Studies in Modern Jewish and Hindu Thought* (1997), *Dharma and Halacha: Comparative Studies in Hindu-Jewish Philosophy and Religion* (edited by Theodor and Greenberg, 2020), and some of the articles in *The Journal of Indo-Judaic Studies*.

Nor is this book a work of theology, philosophy, or interreligious dialogue. That is, it does not aim to construct a Jewish theology of Hinduism, or to argue the truth or value of either of the two religious traditions, or 

*[p. 5]* ^pg18

to identify bridges of understanding. For this sort of project, I recommend the writings of the Orthodox thinker, Alon Goshen-Gottstein, particularly his insightful *Same God, Other god: Judaism, Hinduism, and the Problem of Idolatry* (2017). On the other hand, I hope my book, by illuminating how Jewish thinkers of the past conceptualized the theological relationship between Judaism (their own versions of it) and Hinduism (as they imagined it from imperfect sources and encounters), will provide perspective for Jews thinking about these questions today. Toward that end, this book’s conclusion will observe some general patterns in the history of Jewish ideas about Hinduism that I have analyzed, and will ask whether these patterns can be considered a “Jewish approach to Hinduism.”

I have been using two central terms that have problematic meanings. The first is “Hinduism,” and the problem applies as well to any of the so-called world religions, including “Judaism” and “Christianity.” How does one define these terms? Who speaks for Hindus, Jews, and Christians? The problem is the great variation, even contradictions, among the many forms of what might be called “Hindu” (and Judaic and Christian). In the case of “Hinduism,” a term that was coined only in the early 19th century, there is no one “ism,” no single system of thought, institution, ritual, mythology, community, region, or social organization that can define this term. As Robert Frykenberg has written, “The very notion of the existence of any single religious community by this name \[Hinduism\] has been falsely conceived.”<sup>3</sup> The word “Hindu” itself is a very old term (6th century BCE or older) which originally had a geographical reference: the people who live near the great river (Indus). But seeking some ideological and social unity among Hindus is the problem. Wendy Doniger has suggested a Venn diagram as a way to visualize Hindu unity – “a set of intersecting circles of concepts and beliefs, some of which are held by some Hindus, others by other Hindus.”<sup>4</sup> Julius J. Lipner proposed imagining Hinduism as a banyan tree with multiple roots, thus a polycentric collection of traditions, all of them authentic.<sup>5</sup> But the term remains controversial and questioned.

Except in this introduction and in the conclusion (where I intend the term provisionally), I have mostly avoided using the words “Hinduism” and “Hindus.” The medieval Jews I studied used the terms “India” and “Indians” in their geographic meaning. They wrote about *al-hind* in Arabic and *hodu* in Hebrew, as in *eretz hodu*, “the land of India,” and *anshei hodu*, “the people of India.” These Jewish writers did not know about or they ignored India’s Muslims, so they did not need a term to differentiate the two groups. Nor did they use a term like “Hinduism.” Following their usage, I will use the phrases “Indian religion” and “Indian thought.” The two Jewish travelers of the 19th century, however, did speak of “Hindus,” and I will follow their usage too.

The second term, “religion,” may be even more problematic. Scholars of the 19th and 20th centuries have been accused of defining “religion” too narrowly, from a Protestant preconception that emphasized inner experience, 

*[p. 6]* ^pg19

doctrine, or a sharp distinction between sacred and secular realms of life. But how to discover categories of religious life which apply to all the phenomena we want to call “religious” and which do not favor one religious tradition over others? Some critics have even questioned whether “religion” is an object that can be identified and studied at all, and whether studying “religion” inherently assumes a reality that cannot be demonstrated.

Nevertheless, I have already used the word “religion” (again intended provisionally) in this introduction and will use it again in studying how Jewish writers “compared religions.” When I do so, I will use this term in what Brent Negri calls “redescriptive” and “descriptive” ways, that is, etic and emic. By “redescriptive” he means an account of people’s thinking that employs classification systems foreign to those that the people themselves use.<sup>6</sup> Since the modern meaning of the term “religion” developed only in the 18th century, I will be interposing a foreign concept upon older writings. At the same time, in a more “descriptive” way, I will search for concepts used by Jewish thinkers which parallel or intersect the modern usage, so that my use of “religion” in relation to “comparative religion” will try to approximate how Jewish authors would have conceived of it, whether involving doctrine, ritual practices, revealed or human laws, institutions, or an idea of transcendence. (I acknowledge a tautological aspect to this enterprise.) Especially in discussing medieval thinkers, my use of “religion” will reflect their emphasis on beliefs. Many of them conceived of a human activity that combined beliefs with institutions and practices corresponding to those beliefs. Several of the following chapters will include brief studies of Arabic and Hebrew words used by the Jewish authors which broadly intersect with modern meanings of “religion.”

The chapters of this book are organized chronologically rather than by theme. Part 1 examines authors from the 12th through 14th centuries; most of them lived in Spain and were influenced by images of India circulating in the Islamic world, but others, living in Christian lands, drew their images of India from Greek reports and a Christianized Alexander Romance. Chapter 6, comprising Part 2 of this book, changes focus to look at Jewish interpretations of one scriptural verse, [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]], that became associated with Indian beliefs. The chapter centers on Menasseh ben Israel of 17th-century Amsterdam, the first Jew to draw this connection, but also jumps to current Orthodox thinkers who continue today to interpret this biblical verse as a reference to Hinduism and Buddhism. Part 3 of the book examines three 19th-century authors influenced by Orientalist and Evangelical views of Hinduism.

Certain themes or motifs recur in the history of Jewish images of Hinduism. One of the most important is the topic of revelation, which Indian religion supposedly lacked in contrast with Judaism. Most Jewish authors drew important theological implications from this difference. Other recurring motifs, taking various forms, were magic, idolatry, oppressive priests, Indian science and astrology, Indian morality and civic virtues, and the ancient origin of Indian beliefs and customs.

*[p. 7]* ^pg20

From the viewpoint of contemporary academic studies of religions, or of the goals of interreligious dialogue, all these Jewish authors, even the two travelers and the modern geographer, erred. They all failed to gather proper data, to listen to Indians, and to attain sufficiently objective standpoints and use neutral categories of comparison. Rather, what we find in their writings are only oblique reflections of Indian realities existing at their time, and judgments issuing from closed theological systems. However, this makes their views hardly less fascinating, and perhaps more so, as we proceed to investigate the assumptions, cultural influences, information about India, and reasoning of each author.
