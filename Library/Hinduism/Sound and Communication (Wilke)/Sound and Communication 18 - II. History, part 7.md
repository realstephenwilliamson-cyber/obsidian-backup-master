However, despite its fixation with commandments, one must not regard the Mīmāmsā in the same way as the Abrahamitic religions. For the Mīmāmsā, following the Veda is a simple commandment of common sense and the only rational way to lead one’s life. The Mīmāmsakas do not recognize any moral call or unshakable will of God, which has to be obeyed — as there is, for example, in the Ten Commandments of the Old Testament. And although they regard the Vedic commandments, i.e. all the minute ritual injunctions, as an undeniable categorical imperative — as dharma by definition! — the Mīmāmsakas’ position and practice are also a long way from ‘simply going through the motions’ in stubbornly performing one’s duty. On the contrary, they hold the view that every human being is striving first and foremost for happiness. They consider any other motivation for human actions (e.g. morality) to be of secondary importance. Having such a carefree attitude in relation to morality is no problem for them, for they believe that the answers to all soteriologically relevant questions — the right path to salvation, the right behavior — are also laid down in the original language and the eternal words of the Veda, as are all other words that we need to describe this world, such as “cow” or “water buffalo.”

On the one hand, it is true that the mantras, descriptions and offeratory formulas with the deity’s name serve as important motivators. This is exactly Kumārila’s position,!® who contends such assertions have the useful purpose of persuasion and render the injunctions attractive. On the other hand, his interpretation of such passages is strictly functional and often merely figurative. The gods are always related to a ritual act and have here (and only here) a function. Their image helps to explain the sacrifice, make those present aware of its greatness and laud its results. For instance a statement like “wind (एवय is the swiftest deity” is not seen as a statement about the god Vāyu’s nature, but as laid down to accomplish something regarding the injunction. The statement is highly meaningful, because its function is, according to Kumārila, to communicate that the sacrifice which is offered to a swift deity will always accomplish swift results. This is precisely what Patton terms “metonymy.” Kumārila speaks of a secondary or “implied meaning.” He rationalizes the ancient idea of mythical substances, i.e. the idea that the deities, such as the elemental god Vāyu, are not persons. For Kumārila the element wind becomes a deity in the sacrifice because of its quality of swiftness. In the statement “wind is the swiftest deity” something is communicated about the very nature of sacrifice itself, namely its efficiency and excellence. The description “Vāyu is the swiftest deity” is therefore clearly needed for the injunction and should be recognized as helping the accomplishment of the sacrificer’s purpose. 

According to Kumārila there is always mutual dependence of the Arthavāda and the excellence of the rite, and statements about deities are required to indicate the latter. The injunctions enjoined are always of primary importance and they are made equally attractive and excellent by the Arthavāda passages. !® This is indeed highly probable and in this respect there is a curious detail about Kumārila’s rationality that deserves special mention. We should remember that the times have changed since late Vedic times and Jaimini, not only in terms of an influx of rational theories, but also in terms of new religious movements. There are new options not only in choosing a profane or religious life style, but also multiple options within the religious field itself. In Kumārila’s time the great Lord Gods Śiva and Viṣṇu were already firmly established, and along with them new rites, new creeds, new revelations, new esoteric teachings, new soteriologies, and a personal religiosity which is no longer restricted to the Brahman “aristocrats.” The powerful universal gods and their revelations have become serious rivals to the Veda. In Southern India, temple culture assumes political dimensions, being sponsored by and in service of kings. There are now great Āgama temples rites for royal rulers and sensory modes of iconic worship that are more popular than the Vedic rituals. Religious imagination has shifted to theism. The cognitive, sensory and emotional appeal of the theistic movements was strong, even for the Veda Brahmins. It seems that Kumārila (or his family) could not resist either. ##### 4.4.2 Exegesis and Teaching Method of tat tvam asi

From the standpoint of the Mīmāmsā’s traditional understanding of language, the problem of the incommunicability of the Atman would be completely insoluble. The old authorities assumed that word and object are totally congruent. For every single individual word there exists precisely one individual class of objects, and vice versa. Under this premise, concepts such as the Ātman (as it is understood by Śaṁkara) would be mere chimeras — one would not be able to imagine the Atman any other way than as a physical or psychological self, and every sentence claiming that the Atman were neither the body nor the psyche would be nonsensical. Śaṁñkara’s whole philosophy would fall victim to Wittgenstein’s feared statement about the senselessness of all metaphysical statements — metaphysics would in fact be nothing other than a dent that Thought had acquired at the limits of language. If words could only be understood via their use within a community of speakers, the word “self” would always only mean my empirical self, because only the empirical self reacts with the world and with the participants in the language community. And in the same way, I would only be able to refer to their empirical selves. The problem of communicating metaphysical concepts only becomes soluble through the concept of Jaksana, which means that words can have a secondary signifying function or an implied sense which is intimately linked to the primary one, i.e. words and sentences can function as an “indexical sign” or “indication” and communicate their real sense or actual referents only when the context is carefully considered and the implied meaning is understood.

While the injunctions strengthen the idea of the soul indirectly, the idea of an eternal existence of the person comes to be strengthened by studying the Vedānta, i.e. the Upaniṣṣads. 

We already know that Śaṁñkara draws precisely the opposite conclusions regarding the nature of the self — refuting thereby not only the Buddhists, but most of all the Pūrva-Mīmāmsā. Śaṁkara’s self-luminous Ātman is completely stripped of doership and enjoyership. Whereas Kumārila conceives of a multitude of Ātmans, there can only be one universal Ātman for Śaṁkara, which is the same in everybody and pervades the entire cosmos as pure existence-consciousness-infinity/bliss. This unique non-dual “mystical” vision can be learned and communicated by investigating the /aksanā content of Upaniṣadic words and sentences.
