## 6 The Two Mysticisms Compared and Contrasted

Preceding chapters have considered the textual symbolisms and imageries employed by one notable beguine mystic, Mechthild of Magdeburg, and those which are implied or evident in the poems to a Hindu goddess, Lalitā Tripurasundarī. The present chapter will now attempt to trace possible correspondences and differences between the two traditions. Clearly there will be differences, but can any correspondences be discerned? In a brief section on metaphysical analysis, a paradigmatic reading will explore the phenomenographic ground between the two traditions. 

### Relationship of Body, Soul and God

In the pagan views of the body in classical antiquity the body was perceived as ‘potentially formless and eternal matter’, which was ‘held together, for a short lifetime, by the vivid soul of the well-born man’.[^1] At the end of bodily existence the soul would depart, ‘perhaps to the stars’.[^2] The Christian tradition in its view of the body basically follows Platonic or Aristotelian notions. 

In the Platonic view, the soul was gender-neutral, but the sexually differentiated body of man and woman represented a hindrance to the soul. In the Aristotelian view, soul was gender-determined and sex differentiation was a primary distinction among individuals, especially given the rational deficiency perceived in women.[^3]

The physical and sensual are not disparaged; indeed, they are positively exalted, not least by alchemical *tāntrikas*, who seek to preserve the body indefinitely in order to enjoy the pleasures that can be derived from it. 

For Mechthild the body is seen as the key area for the cause of sin. In likening a spiritual person to an animal that misguidedly drinks bitter seawater, and has to expel it to regain health, she says, ‘when we drink of the foul puddle of the world and make use of the baseness of our flesh according to the counsels of the evil spirit, we poison ourselves with our selves’.[^12] The ‘desires of our miserable flesh’ and the ‘lust and the weakness of the human senses’ can only be overcome by practising the seven virtues: to be just; to be merciful in distress; to be faithful in public; to be ready to help in secret; to be silent in distress; to be full of the truth; and to be the enemy of lies. Shame is to be found in the abrogation of one’s duty to uphold these virtues.[^13] The denigration by Mechthild of fleshly pursuits contrasts with the positive understanding of *tāntrikas*, certainly those of the left-hand variety, who roundly embrace sexuality, and who scorn the whole ethos of asceticism. Right-hand, or Samaya, *tāntrikas* accept sexuality but deprecate illicit or licentious behaviour; and the extension into celibacy becomes easy with the idea of purely internal worship.

### Love and Power

Love is a centrally important concept in both the beguine and tantric mystical traditions; however, there are distinctive approaches to this virtue. Anecdotally for beguines, love is an ‘emotive’ power, or a love of love,[^19] while for *tāntrikas* it is an ‘appreciative’ power, or a love of beauty. Putting it more mystically, beguines profess a ‘gold’ love while *tāntrikas* express a ‘diamond’ love. In the context of this thesis Mechthild and the author of *SL* respectively exemplify these types. This is highlighted in the differing understandings of the position of the soul *vis-à-vis* God or the Absolute. Mechthild is consumed by the love of God, melting in his fiery presence, and flowing in consonance with him; and since gold is chemically unreactive, she can legitimately claim to maintain her individual separateness from God. A Śākta Tantric, on the other hand, admires the beauty of the supreme Goddess in the same way that one would a sparkling diamond; and just as diamond slowly burns to carbon dioxide under high temperatures in the presence of oxygen, so the soul is 20

If we accept the two types of mysticism, beguine and tantric, as ‘sensational’, as bodily influenced, we can say that the ecstatic experience is intimately related to the endocrine functions of the body; and that being so, we might expect age to be a factor in influencing the experience. Maslow observed ‘that the aging body and nervous system is less capable of tolerating a really shaking peak-experience’.[^47] Mechthild’s ecstatic experiences tended to become less intense as time went on; and she concedes this when she says that the body, with its five senses, ‘does not have youth with which to sustain \[the\] fiery love of God’.[^48]

Since the intimate proximity of male and female was not available to virgin women, their salvation could only be found in spiritual propinquity with the male Christ. The strong implication is that the fire of love, *incendium amoris*, is as valid through imaginative focus, or image-readiness, as it is through actual sexual coupling. From an elementary perspective, sexual attraction is bound up with the processes of mental imaging: in the one course of events it will result in the physical act of sexual intercourse; in another course of events it will end with the imagination.

### Language and Mysticism

Mysticism serves to highlight in stark terms the flexible linguistic boundaries that govern the way humans construct their worldview. As mystics try to describe in words that which they feel or perceive, it only serves to demonstrate the ‘one dimensional’, or ‘flat’, character of language. There is a longstanding philosophical question in Eastern metaphysics as to whether we concretize reality in our minds by naming objects (a subjective idealist position). In other words, do we contrive to situate ourselves at the physical level through cognitive realization and articulation? Is reality configured by our perceptions? Granting this ‘perceptual fluidity’ it must logically in this philosophical outlook extend to gender categories. While it has been remarked that Tantrism has a polarized perspective, and views the female and male as quite distinctive entities, they are, none the less, essentially the same. This might be called an ‘identity-in-difference’ perspective.[^76] 

The relationship between language and mysticism is a complex and variable one. Outside the influence of Augustine in the fifth century, the understanding of mystical experience was that it related basically to the esoteric or hidden meaning of a text. The task of the reader or listener was to elucidate or intuit that mysterious meaning, and thereby realize God through a sympathetic appreciation. In that sense the text (or discourse), as mystical imprint, may be construed as mere (verbal) imagery; and such is evident in the prophetic visions of the Bible, but most especially in the Book of Revelations.[^77] The later monastic tradition, as promulgated by Gregory the Great and St Bernard, allowed visual imagery only the role of a didactic or mnemonic function; at best, it could be considered a stimulus to devotion, and as ‘an aid to an illiterate populace unable to penetrate the mysteries of the Word’.[^78] By the thirteenth century however images came to acquire a certain legitimacy, and even potency; and their use for devotional purposes was facilitated by monks and especially nuns. ### Specular Attitudes towards Women

In theory, tantric systems view women as being equal with men, since they reflect one aspect of the bifurcated Godhead; but, Śākta Tantric theology goes further, as it considers that women actually reflect the Godhead itself, with the result that the female is viewed in a superior light to the male; for she represents and embodies the Goddess. According to the *Tantramahāvijñāna*: ‘All women are your different forms’.[^102] To worship a woman is to worship the divine, and to attack or harm a woman is egregious. In practice, this positive view is hedged round with qualifications, and effectively women are seen as just a means to an end.
