At the same time it must be said that the concept of Nāda-Brahman is (now) one of the most opaque concepts found in Sanskrit Hinduism. Because the Nāda-Brahman has been adopted by various groups, its meaning has become extremely unclear and polyvalent over the course of time. Nāda itself means a soundscape and can best be rendered by the German word “Schall” which indicates sound vibrations. It encompasses many things, from audible vibrating sound to subauditory, infrasonic and supersonic sound, i.e. the whole spectrum of sound tapestries of the external world (physical sound and sound waves) and the internal world, the subjective effect of sound on the body and mind. It also includes aesthetic moods and sound-centered images. It is as far reaching as the inner sounds of the Yogis and the “sound of silence.” When Brahman took on the meaning of Nāda, it also absorbed all these meanings

Śārṁgadeva’ s Nāda-Brahman was associated directly with music and at the same time an extremely complex concept that absorbed all the Great Narratives of the past. The cosmological sound speculations of the Āgamas and Tantras and the wonderful sound world of the Yogis and Siddhas are only one part of Śārṁgadeva’s legacy, and Yogic inner sounds are not exactly the most important aspect in his Nāda-Brahman concept. But by declaring music to be a type of more pleasurable Yoga and fusing the Nāda concept with the Brahman concept, he referred to former Yoga traditions while producing a catchword enabling later religious practices and popularizations to develop. Thereby the term Nāda-Brahman coined by Śārṁgadeva later extended beyond the limitations of musical theory. It is even possible that it inspired new religious movements. Perhaps Sikhism (15th cent.) is the link between the modern meaning of the Nāda-Brahman and Śārṁgadeva’s idea. Even now practicing Sikhs attempt to merge with the “sound current” of the highest “word” or “name” through singing and prayer.

It was one of the topics that were so strongly suspected of being esoteric that “real” academics preferred to keep away from it to preserve their reputations. There is such a wide range of cultural connections, contingencies and differences which Nāda-Brahman enables us to approach - starting from ancient music of the spheres to which modern receptions often refer, right up to the expansion and dissolution of cultural boundaries in the modern period itself. We find Indian music particularly well-suited to understand cultural contingencies. In the classical age both Indian and Western musicologists occupied themselves with more or less the same dichotomies/trichotomies between the world, music and human beings. However, different models have had a paradigmatic role in providing explanations: here language, there numbers; here the human voice, there instruments; here aesthetic mood-related reception, there rule-based knowledge; here practice, there theory, here permutations, there proportions, etc. Apparently, however, precisely these discontinuities are the ones that turned Indian music in the 20th century into a major intercultural contact zone stimulating new musical and spiritual forms of expression in Western culture, from Olivier Messiaen’ s “Hindu rhythms” to Berendt’s New Age. The modern classical musician and the jazz historian used Indian conceptions of music in varying ways, but both were concerned with a spiritualization and resacralization of life. They were, in differing ways, making good a cultural deficit and seeking to overcome a modern sense of unease.

#### 6.2 The Nāda-Brahman as a Means of Healing for Western "Eye-People": the "World is Sound" in New Age

If one credits Berendt, Indian music had the effect of greatly heightening the consciousness when it became known in the West. He ascribes an inherent spiritual attitude to modal music-making and thus explains the success of Indian music in the 1960s — firstly in jazz and then in rock and pop, minimal music, meditation music and world music. He wanted not least to document this in his book The World is Sound: Nada Brahma. What the book certainly documents is foremost Berendt’s own “discomfort in culture” and his own interest in alternative spirituality. It is a very subjective and personal source. At the same time it points to larger processes of religious change and makes the reader aware that Indian spirituality was disseminated through many channels, one of them (and not an unimportant one) being music. Berendt’s book cannot be taken at face value, and yet it is a valuable document for the shifting values and world-views initiated in the counterculture of the 1960s and the 1970s. This generation rebelled against all traditional forms of life — starting with the suffocating “bourgeois” society, modern capitalism, politics, sexuality and family life, all the way to classical Western music and religion. The religious aspect increased in the 1970s in the heyday of Indian music and gurus coming to the West and a large number of Western youngsters “going native.” This “love story” with India was pretty short. It already ceased in the New Age of the 1980s, but the “New Consciousness” had already assimilated much of India and made it completely its own. This is precisely where Berendt’s cultural criticism and pronounced holistic creed becomes of interest. His work Nada Brahman is a good document of New Age spirituality and remained a “source book” of modern esoteric thought. It illustrates new habitus forms that are no longer purely Western or Eastern, but drew a great deal of inspiration from India. One of Berendt’s major theses is that Indian music created new religious interest, and this probably true at least in part. Precisely because Indian classical music is so vastly different from Western classical music, it provided Western music-making with new ideas, aesthetic styles and attitudes. Berendt spoke enthusiastically of a new hearing that he and others learnt from Indian music. 

Berendt wrote his book Nada Brahma especially for people in the West and associated with it a crisis diagnosis and a healing program critical of civilization. He urged Western culture to change because it was in the process of destroying itself with atom bombs, environmental pollution and AIDS. ##### 6.3.1 Music as a Yoga for Everybody and the Sound Mysticism of the Indian Musicologists

In the Saṁgīta-Ratnākara, Śārṁgadeva, the “inventor” of Nāda-Brahman concept, formulated the Nāda-Brahman as the answer to the old Tantric question of the means, suitable for leading at the same time to enjoyment of life and to ultimate liberation:

“The consciousness of all living beings, which turns itself by itself into the world (vivrta), is the ‘Sound-Brahman’ (nāāda-brahman). In this we invoke (Vupa-ās) bliss, the ‘One with a Second.’

The panentheistic view by the Śaivas automatically gives rise to the statement “the world is sound,” because if the great cosmic deities are sound and there is no world outside of God, all beings and things are already included.

##### 6.3.2 Developments in the Term "Brahman" and the Transformations of the Language Goodess

Where is the change, if one now no longer speaks of the “Word-Brahman” but of the “Sound-Brahman”? This question is interesting not least from the viewpoint of spiritual and philosophical history, because since the seminal Upaniṣad epoch Brahman itself has not been either especially sensory nor especially mythical. 

To answer the question, one has to consider that since its focus in the late Veda the Brahman has been something like a cultural program and an icon of Hindu spirituality. It is that which holds the world together at its very core, out of which the world arises and into which one also wishes to return. And even after the rise of the great gods, this thought persisted and was transferred to the images of gods that always included the super-personal aspect of Brahman. This focus on the Brahman as an absolute, immanent and transcendental reality had emerged very slowly. In the Veda it already underwent various stages of change: from linguistic expressions relating to the numinous, having affinity with it through poetic and cryptic diction and its intensive sound nature, and therefore being able to influence it, to the statement on the numinous that is understood to be the magical, symbolic dimension of worldly phenomena and the numerical and homologous way they are

*[p. 848]* ^pg872

networked. In a third stage, the Brahman then referred to this symbolic dimension itself. Finally, the Upaniṣads transformed the symbolic into the spiritual. The Brahman became the transcendental primordial foundation of existence and thus also of human beings. 

Bhartrhari’s interpretation (5th cent. BC) is difficult to understand without knowledge of these stages of metamorphosis. Although he follows on from the last change, the older stages remain operational in his thought. In particular it is the idea of Brahman as a magic symbolic network of things that continues to exert its effects even in the spiritualized concept of Brahman which stimulates Bhartrhari to conceptualize such a networked form for the semantic dimension of language. For him it is a obvious step to think in wholes and not in parts. This is why he thinks that the individual meanings of words appearing under his rational microscope divided up in a reductionist manner, come together in the sentence-whole and ultimately in a cosmic, comprehensive Brahman that 

he 

calls 

the 

“language principle” 

(ŝabda-tattva) or “Word-Brahman” (sabda-brahman). Bhartrhari uses these terms for a global form of meaning that can be perceived only by the spirit. The Nāda does not play any significant role for him. 

In the history of reception, however, this turned out differently. Rāmakaṇtha’s Nāda-Kārikā (1211 cent.) reified and theologized the “word principle” by replacing it with the Nāda concept, while presenting 

arguments 

based on Bhartrhari, i.e. 

linguistic arguments. Bhartrhari’s language principle was too abstract and internal for him. Meaning is something manifest, public and intersubjective, and must be manifest, public and intersubjective to be effective. For exactly this reason, according to Rāmakaṇtha the more specific Nāda concept that relates to audible acoustics has to be brought into play and given more emphasis. One should note that he uses only the Nāda concept, and does not speak of Nāda-Brahman, which would be expected by analogy to Bhartrhari’s Śabda-Brahman. Even if the argument somewhat resembles modern cultural studies, Rāmakaṇtha typically has no underlying rationalistic or materialistic intent. Instead, his Nāda is fed completely from Tantric-Āgamic ritual practice, in particular the Pauskara-Ā gama. This leads into cosmogonic language-ontology and ultimately into the mythical theological worlds of the Vedic goddess Vāgdevī (vāc), who represents an important link to the sound Brahman and explains its mythical substantiality in Śārṁgadeva’s sense. 

If one wishes to understand how the word becomes sound, the concept of Brahman alone is not sufficient. Instead, another Vedic linguistic concept also plays an important role, namely the Vāc, the goddess Language, also known as the goddess Voice. The Vāc, which denotes not only a goddess but also the human voice and language itself, is the

*[p. 849]* ^pg873

engine driving the transformation from word to sound. What differentiates the Brahman concept from the Vāc in Indian culture is its historical continuity. And one may add that although the Brahman constantly transformed itself, in its history there is ultimately no marked mythical break, but only a relocation into the transcendental (which can also be grasped where the Brahman is identified with the great universal personal deities). However, this complete shift into the transcendental does not apply to the concept of Vāc, as it has remained a typically “mythical substance” in Kurt Hübner’s sense. Vāc meant the voice and at the same time that which is spoken. Subject and object cannot be distinguished in the Vāc concept. This concrete, plastic iconicity was lost from the Brahman term in indigenous discourse since Upaniṣadic times, so much so that it needed the Orientalists to remind us of the powerful magic word the Brahman once signified. The Vāc, on the other hand, lost its defining power in the profane academic linguistic discourse and in everyday language, but led a secret life in esoteric and mystic metaphysical linguistic speculations and expressed itself publicly in new forms of the language goddess. 

One must remember that at Bhartrhari’s time the Vāc concept was totally replaced by sabda (the profane “word”), at least as far as the description of concrete language was concerned. There are the different languages (bhāsā) Samskrtā (the cultivated language), Prākrtā (the common language), Deśī (the diverse local languages), and there are the words (Sabda), and so a rationalistic linguistic philosopher and interpreter of the Veda such as Kumārila (7th cent.) can write a whole work on linguistic philosophy without the Vāc playing any role in it. The material of the words, the phonemes (varnas), is analyzed and the relationship between word (sabda) and meaning (artha) is debated, but the poor old Vāc, who has now been separated into word and meaning, is of no use to the linguists. 

Nevertheless, the mystic and universal mythical dimension of the Vāc is by no means lost. But its life is confined to remote esoteric magical corners. For example, the famous Rgvedic riddle verses on the cryptic, hidden language and the clear language of human beings (cat- व vāk verse) become invocation formulas in the late medieval Tantric Sarasvati-Upanisad, which we have already met in the Methodology section (1.10.3.1).
