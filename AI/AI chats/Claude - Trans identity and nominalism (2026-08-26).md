> [!quote] Me
> It occurs to me that the question of "is a trans woman a woman" is a question of nominalism.

There's something to that, and the sharpest version of the point is the voluntarist one: Ockham's nominalism travels with the idea that the order of kinds is *posited* rather than *found*. If "woman" is a name we assign, then extending it is an act of will — individual or collective — and the question becomes who holds the authority to name.

But I think nominalism underdetermines the dispute rather than diagnosing it. Two problems. First, both camps frequently argue like realists — just about different kinds. Gender-critical positions are realist about a biological kind; but appeals to gender identity as a stable developmental fact, or Bettcher's first-person authority, are also reporting something taken to be real. That's realism-about-X versus realism-about-Y, not realism versus its denial. Second, if you *do* go nominalist, "trans women are women" becomes neither true nor false simpliciter, and the argument immediately relocates to which convention we ought to adopt — which gets settled on grounds (harm, institutional function, coherence with adjacent practices) that aren't nominalist at all. The metaphysics gets you to the threshold and stops.

Haslanger is the case that really strains the framing. She's a realist about social kinds — gender as an actual position in a hierarchy — while holding that "what is a woman?" should be answered by asking which concept serves our legitimate purposes. Chosen target, real referent. That's conceptual engineering, and it isn't nominalism. My own read is that the deeper fault line is about reference-fixing: is "woman" a natural-kind term whose reference was ostensively locked to a biological kind à la Kripke–Putnam, or a term whose reference is determined by social role? That's a semantics question wearing metaphysical clothes.

Given where you work, the obvious move is that the Indian tradition ran this debate for a millennium already. Dignāga and Dharmakīrti's *apoha* is a fully worked-out nominalism — universals as exclusion, not as anything positive — against Nyāya realism about *jāti*. And Abhinavagupta's treatment of *vikalpa* sits somewhere unusual relative to both: conceptual construction as real activity rather than mere error, grounded in *svātantrya*. A category constituted by freedom rather than either discovered or arbitrary. That's a third position the Western debate never quite formulated.

> [!quote] Me
> I am thinking too simply maybe but if we take people's positions as not just bias but really grounded in where they stand on universals and particulars it could help the discourse.

It's not too simple — it's the right kind of move, and it has a name-shaped precedent in MacIntyre: disagreements look interminable precisely when the parties are drawing on fragments of incompatible frameworks without recognizing them as such. Making the framework explicit doesn't produce agreement, but it converts "you're arguing in bad faith" into "you're reasoning validly from premises I reject." That's a real upgrade, because the second is arguable and the first isn't.

The honest limit is that you can test whether the metaphysics is doing the work, and it often isn't. Check for domain-generality: is the person who's a realist about *woman* also a realist about *race*, *species*, *nation*, *disability*? Many aren't — they'll happily say race is socially constructed while insisting sex isn't. That asymmetry might be principled (there are real disanalogies between the cases), but it might also mean the metaphysical commitment was reverse-engineered from the conclusion. Same test cuts the other way: someone who is nominalist about gender but sharply realist about, say, class or oppression. Cross-domain consistency is the diagnostic that separates a genuine position on universals from a rationalization wearing one.

The other limit is that the practical questions don't wait for the metaphysics. Prisons, sports categories, clinical protocols — these get decided, and they turn on institutional purpose and empirical claims that survive whatever you conclude about universals. So the charitable framing improves the *discourse* without touching the *policy*, which is either a feature (it separates two things that shouldn't have been fused) or a reason people will resist it as an evasion. My guess is it works best as a private hermeneutic — a way of reading your opponents that keeps you honest — rather than as a public reframe you'd propose to both sides, since anyone can hear "your view is downstream of your nominalism" as a way of not addressing what they actually said.

> [!quote] Me
> Agreed. However, Larry May's work on genocide via normative accounts uses the nominalism debate in a a way that seems like it could "improve the discussion" at least from the theory perspective

Yes — and the reason May's case is stronger than the abstract version is that his nominalism debate is *forced* by a live legal problem rather than imported. The Convention protects national, ethnic, racial, and religious groups, and the Rwanda tribunals discovered they couldn't apply objective criteria: Hutu and Tutsi shared language, religion, and territory, with the categories substantially hardened by Belgian identity cards. So the jurisprudence drifted toward subjective criteria — self-identification and, more consequentially, perpetrator perception. Sartre's line about the anti-Semite making the Jew is the direct ancestor here, and May's broader work on groups (neither mere aggregates nor entities over and above their members, but individuals-in-relations) is built to sit exactly in that gap.

What makes it useful for your purpose is that May doesn't settle the metaphysics and then derive the norms. He asks what normative work the group-concept has to do — explaining why genocide is a distinct wrong rather than aggregated murder — and lets that constrain how much reality the group needs. The group can be thin metaphysically while the harm is thick, because membership is identity-constituting for the individual and because the perpetrator's act of sorting is itself part of the wrong. That's a way of taking nominalism seriously without the deflationary conclusion that nothing real is at stake, which is precisely the impasse the gender debate keeps hitting.

The transferable payoff, though, is a disanalogy rather than an analogy. Perpetrator perception is load-bearing in May's account *only because* the question being asked is about the perpetrator's wrong — the category gets fixed by the wrongdoer for the purpose of characterizing the offense, not for the purpose of characterizing the victim's identity. Which suggests that reference-fixing for a group term is indexed to the normative question you're asking, and that different questions can license different fixings without contradiction. Applied to your original case: the criterion appropriate for hate-crime statutes (how was this person perceived and targeted?) needn't be the criterion for clinical protocols, or for self-description, or for a sport's eligibility class. That deflates the assumption that "is a trans woman a woman" has one answer prior to specifying what it's being asked for — and it's not relativism, since each fixing stays disciplined by its purpose. It's also, I'd note, a harder sell than it looks, because both sides in that dispute have reasons to want the univocal answer.

Which loops back to your original nominalism point in a way that vindicates it more than the abstract version did. The universals debate turns out to matter not because settling it settles the gender question, but because it explains *why* the question keeps failing to settle: people are asking for a single reference-fixing to serve every purpose, and no group term has ever managed that — not Hutu, not Tutsi, not woman. > [!quote] Me
> It's actually a point Dan McKellan makes about the bible often, nothing has meaning on it's own (in this case, even the word woman) the meaning is provided by the consciousness preceiving the squiggles, soundwaves, Brail bumps, etc

Dan McClellan's version is doing something narrower than the general claim, and I think the narrow version is the stronger one. His target is the appeal to "what the text plainly says" — the move where a reading gets laundered as the text's own property so it can't be argued with. Pointing at the squiggles is a way of relocating the argument back onto the interpreter, who has reasons and can be asked for them. That's the same operation you've been running all thread: make the framework explicit so disagreement becomes arguable.

Where I'd resist is the slide from "meaning requires a perceiver" to "meaning is provided by the perceiver." The squiggles constrain, even if they don't determine. A Hebrew text won't yield just any reading, and it pushes back on some — which is why philological argument is possible at all, and why McClellan spends his time on grammar and lexicography rather than declaring the question open. And on the constructed side, the constraints aren't only in the marks: a lexeme has a use-history in a community, so meaning is neither in the ink nor in the individual head but in a practice that both are downstream of. Otherwise the supply sergeant's point collapses — he can't order what the requisition system doesn't recognize, and no amount of perceiving changes the stock number.

Your traditions have the sharper tools here. *Apoha* is exactly this: the word doesn't pick out a positive universal, it excludes — *go* means not-non-cow — so the category is constituted by the perceiver's discriminative activity while remaining answerable to causal regularities in what can be discriminated. Constructed *and* constrained, which is the position you need and the one the Western debate keeps missing by making it a choice. And on the other side, Mīmāṃsā's *apauruṣeyatva* is the maximal denial of your claim — meaning inherent in the sounds, no perceiver required, no author — precisely because they saw that if meaning depends on a perceiver, the injunction loses its binding force. Which is a live worry for you: if "woman" only means what the perceiver supplies, what stops the Iraqi householder's reading and the Army's from being equally final? Something has to constrain, and that's the work purpose-relativity was doing.

> [!quote] Me
> Shifting gears a bit but I have been thinking about the philosophical implications of the system I am developing in the commentary, inasmuch as they can be separated from the theology.  It strikes me as doing something like "hermeneutics as first philosophy" to riff on Levinas a bit

The riff on Levinas is doing more work than a riff usually does, because his phrase is a *displacement* claim — ethics doesn't sit on top of ontology, it precedes it and the ontological question arrives already answered by the encounter. If you want the parallel to hold, you need the same shape: not that hermeneutics is important, or unavoidable, or the method by which we access being, but that the interpretive situation is prior to the question of what there is. That's a stronger claim than most hermeneutic philosophy makes, and it's the one your material actually supports.

Two names are already standing where you're heading. Gadamer's *Sein, das verstanden werden kann, ist Sprache* is close, but Heideggerian: understanding as a mode of Dasein's being, which subordinates hermeneutics to a fundamental ontology rather than displacing it. Vattimo's "weak thought" pushes further — nihilism as the dissolution of being into interpretation, "there are no facts, only interpretations" taken as an ontological thesis — and he explicitly runs it through kenosis, which is uncomfortably adjacent to your Christology. Your system diverges from both at the point you've been defending all thread: your interpretations are correctable, and they're correctable by something. Vattimo can't say that. And the correction is not by better access to the source, which is where you leave Gadamer's fusion behind — it's by the arrival of the interpreter's position.

The genuinely non-derivative move, I think, is the one from the JSS Torch case, and it's ontological rather than epistemic. Prior fixings are already running on the field before any subject arrives to fix anything. That's not "meaning is perspectival" — it's that the interpretive situation has a structure independent of any particular interpreter, which gives you a real constraint without a fact of the matter, and it's what lets you avoid the relativist collapse Levinas's readers would immediately press you on.

But I'd flag the separability premise itself, since you offered it as provisional. *Prakāśa-vimarśa* is not a hermeneutic thesis with a theological application; the self-illumination of consciousness and its self-recognition are the same act, and if you strip the theology you lose the reason interpretation is prior rather than merely pervasive. The clean version might be that hermeneutics-as-first-philosophy is what your system looks like from outside, which is a diagnosis rather than a translation — and, pleasingly, your own thesis predicts exactly that.
