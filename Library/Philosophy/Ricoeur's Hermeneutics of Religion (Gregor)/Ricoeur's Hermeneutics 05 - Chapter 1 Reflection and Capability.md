## Chapter 1

## Reflection and Capability

*The Formation of Ricoeur’s Hermeneutics*

How does one begin to read Ricoeur? I don’t ask this question in the profound philosophical sense regarding the origins of thinking, or the problem of beginning—though we will get to that. Rather, what is the starting point for someone interested in Ricoeur’s work? This is a difficult question to answer, as there is no single book that is the obvious starting point—like Heidegger’s *Being and Time* or Gadamer’s *Truth and Method*—that sets out the themes and questions of his career. Ricoeur has written on a lot of big questions, from the nature of will and action to problems of language, mind, and memory, to questions of ethics, aesthetics, and politics. And of course, religion. The one book that is arguably Ricoeur’s magnum opus, *Oneself as Another*, is also quite difficult for the beginner because it is the culmination of several decades’ work, and because Ricoeur often writes as though the reader is already conversant with the issues and thinkers he discusses.

In short, Ricoeur’s authorship is vast and often difficult. There is, however, a unifying theme in his work. That theme is philosophical anthropology. What is the human being? What does it mean to be a self? How is self-understanding possible? What capacities are distinctive of the human being? These are Ricoeur’s programmatic questions, and this chapter will examine his approach to them.

The deepest root of Ricoeur’s thinking is the French tradition known as reflexive philosophy, which investigates the possibility of self-knowledge. Ricoeur remains loyal to the reflexive tradition by continuing this investigation, but we will also see how he modifies that tradition in significant ways. From Karl Barth and the theology of the Word, he learns that self-understanding does not begin with a self-positing act, but is instead initiated by an external, prior word of address. From the existential philosophy of Marcel and Jaspers, he learns that reflection is not the work of a pure, 

*[p. 12]* ^pg29

disengaged consciousness, but instead arises out of our more primordial participation in being. This also means that reflection is not primarily epistemological but ethical: The goal is not an intellectual intuition of the self, but to make contact with the *conatus*—the desire to be and the effort to exist that lies at the inner core of the self. The subject of reflection is therefore not the *I think*, but the *I can*—an insight that lays the basis for Ricoeur’s later work on human capability.

Ricoeur also takes up Husserl’s phenomenological method as an extension of reflexive philosophy, but he recognizes that no reflexive method (phenomenology included) can deliver on the promise of a pure, immediate intuition of the meaning of things. Reflection is inescapably hermeneutical, and so Ricoeur offers a distinctly hermeneutical approach to phenomenology. As he writes: “The ruling idea of this hermeneutical phenomenology is that if self-reflection is the goal, interpretation is the means” (EBI 43). This chapter will show how these insights form the basic commitments of Ricoeur’s work, so we can see how they inform his philosophical hermeneutics of religion.

### Reflexive Philosophy

Ricoeur received his earliest philosophical formation in the French tradition of reflexive philosophy. He was initiated into the reflexive tradition during his studies at the University of Rennes, where he wrote a thesis in 1933–34 entitled *Reflexive Method Applied to the Problem of God in Lachelier and Lagneau*. He wrote this thesis under the direction of Léon Brunschvicg, a prominent French reflexive philosopher, and the two philosophers named in the title were key figures in nineteenth\_century reflexive philosophy: Jules Lachelier and Jules Lagneau. Many of the thinkers associated with the reflexive tradition have passed into the history of philosophy without much notice, and are not well known in English-speaking scholarship. This tradition was, however, formative for Ricoeur and remained influential throughout all of his subsequent work, including his philosophy of religion. If we want to understand Ricoeur, then, we need to understand something about reflexive philosophy.

Reflexive philosophy is primarily concerned with the possibility of self-knowledge. Through the act of reflection, the thinking subject attempts to step back and reflect on what consciousness is doing when it knows, when it values, and when it wills. As Brunschvicg puts it, philosophy is “intellectual activity becoming aware of itself \[*pregnant conscience d’elle-même*\].”<sup>1</sup> Or as Ricoeur puts it: “Reflexion is that act of turning back upon itself by which a subject grasps, in a moment of intellectual clarity and moral responsibility, the unifying principle of the operations among which it is dispersed and forgets itself as subject” (TA 12). The reflexive act is one of gathering oneself up and gaining a clear—ideally, *transparent*—understanding of oneself as a knowing and acting subject. 

*[p. 13]* ^pg30

The reflexive method can be applied to a wide range of questions. Lachelier, for example, wrote his primary work on inductive judgment, arguing that its principles can be justified “by showing that \[they\] are essential to the ‘concrete and particular acts by which thought constitutes itself while seizing immediately upon reality.’”<sup>2</sup> Reflection seeks to identify the principles at work in our thinking and acting, and as such all of our human life is of philosophical interest. For Lachelier, “philosophy must comprehend everything” (SE 347–48). The crucial point is that in each case thinking turns back on itself in reflection.

Reflection begins from an initial situation of forgetfulness, which Ricoeur describes in an existentially charged passage: “I am lost, ‘led astray’ among objects and separated from the center of my existence.” Because of this dispersal, “I do not at first possess what I am.” I am scattered, absent from myself. The task of reflection is thus a process of re-appropriation: “I must recover something which has first been lost; I make ‘proper to me’ what has first been lost” (FP 45).<sup>3</sup> Reflection is a return to that which has been lost, a recovery of that which I most truly am.

The reflexive tradition has deep roots in the history of philosophy, drawing sustenance from thinkers such as Socrates, Descartes, Kant, Fichte, and Husserl. For all of these thinkers, philosophy is the search for “the *authentic* subjectivity, of the *authentic* act of consciousness” (HT 32). But while philosophical reflection originates with the ancient Greek ideal of *gnothi seauton*, it gains its distinctive reflexive form in modern philosophy. Its founding father is Descartes, whose appeal to the *cogito* sought to provide an indubitable starting point for his metaphysics, as well as the possibility of any other knowledge. *Cogito, ergo sum*: I think, therefore I am. With this act of judgment, Descartes posits botha being and an act: I *am* and I *think*.

Kant carried this tradition forward with his notion of the *I think*, which must be able “to accompany all my representations.”<sup>4</sup> Fichte radicalized the Kantian *I think* by defining it as self-positing. The self *posits itself*. The English word *posit* comes from the Latin term *ponere*, which means to *put* or to *place*,<sup>5</sup> as does Fichte’s German word *setzen*. To posit oneself means to set oneself forth, to take a position, to assert oneself: *I am.* Only in positing myself in this way does my self come into being. I have no being prior to this act.<sup>6</sup> Fichte’s idea of self-positing is more radical than Descartes’ because the self is not a substance but “a *doing*,”<sup>7</sup> an event in which I come into being for myself each instant in which I act.<sup>8</sup> As Fichte writes in his *Wissenschaftslehre*:

Our task is to *discover* the primordial, absolutely unconditioned first principle of all human knowledge. This can be neither provided nor determined, if it is to be an absolutely primary principle. It is to express that *Act* \[*Tathandlung*\] which does not and cannot appear among the empirical states of consciousness, but rather lies at the ground of all consciousness and alone makes it possible.<sup>9</sup>

*[p. 14]* ^pg31

Fichte argues that one has to reflect on this founding act as it initially appears, and then to abstract from everything foreign to it. The goal of this “abstracting reflection” \[*Reflexion*\] is to think this founding act as the basis of all consciousness. According to Ricoeur, this self-positing act is “the first truth” of reflection: *I am.* This “is a truth which posits itself” (FP 43). There is no way to verify it in the usual ways we verify facts, nor can it be deduced like a conclusion from a series of premises. The only evidence that can confirm this truth is the very act of self-positing, and each person must do this for herself.<sup>10</sup> 

According to Ricoeur, this self-positing is nothing other than reflection (FP 43). Fichte gives a programmatic statement for this radical reflection in his *Wissenschaftslehre*: “Attend to yourself: turn your attention away from everything that surrounds you and towards your interiority; this is the first demand that philosophy makes of its disciple. Our concern is not with anything that lies outside you, but only with yourself.”<sup>11</sup> This is precisely the picture of subjectivity that Ricoeur is going to challenge.

Fichte has something of a French counterpart in Maine de Biran,<sup>12</sup> who similarly argues that the “first simple judgment of personality” is this: *I am*.<sup>13</sup> Like Fichte, Biran argues that consciousness is not most basically a matter of “I think,” but of “I will”: *Volo, ergo sum* (I will, therefore I am).<sup>14</sup> Unlike Fichte, however, Biran insists on a duality of activity and passivity in the self. Where Fichte argues that “the intellect can be thought only as active,” so that the not-self is the active production of the self,<sup>15</sup> Biran takes exception and argues that “the real existence of the ego or I is to be found ‘in the apperception of the effort of which it feels itself subject or cause.’”<sup>16</sup> When I exert my will, expending effort, I apperceive myself. This is especially true of willed movement, in which my own activity encounters resistance.<sup>17</sup> Biran thus envisions, and seeks to enact, a philosophical reflection that will “penetrate the intimate relations” of the thinking subject with itself, “in the free exercise of its intellectual acts.”<sup>18</sup> We will see that Ricoeur sides with Biran in this debate; one of his guiding convictions is that willing always involves both activity and passivity, the voluntary and the involuntary, and likewise that human capability always involves both acting and suffering.

Ricoeur shares reflexive philosophy’s goal of self-knowledge, but he rejects the picture of the self as an absolute, self-positing I. Instead of summoning itself into being, the self is already *de*posited in being. As we will see, this is an insight that Ricoeur learned from the philosophy of existence, especially in the work of Gabriel Marcel and Karl Jaspers. Moreover, reflection is not an act of radical self-origination, since the self is summoned into being by the word of another, an event prior to the act of the self. This is a point he learned from Karl Barth, and it is to this lesson that we now turn.

*[p. 15]* ^pg32

### Karl Barth and the Theology of the Word

Karl Barth’s entry into the world of French philosophy was something like his view of revelation: the radical irruption of a foreign element. Barth gave his first lectures and meetings in Paris in April 1934, and during that time there was intense debate regarding the idea of Christian philosophy. Is such a thing possible? What might it look like?<sup>19</sup> During his time in Paris, Barth met with several of the main participants in the debate, including Gilson, Maritain, Marcel, and Brunschvicg.<sup>20</sup> For Ricoeur and these stars of French philosophy, Barth represented a striking rejection of Christian philosophy in the name of the freedom and contingency of divine revelation. Barth’s critique of philosophy is based on his conception of the Word of God as the radically transcendent and novel event of God’s self-revelation to human beings. Barth insists that divine revelation introduces a reality that is entirely unforeseen—a new reality that explodes human preconceptions about who God is, who we are as human beings, what the goal of history is, and how God should deal with us. Rather than submitting to prior philosophical conditions, which can only limit God’s freedom and cannot anticipate the highest of divine possibilities, Barth insists that the Word of God must be the starting point for any theology worthy of the name. Barth’s theology is consequently an attempt to free theology from its philosophical captivity so it can be itself—namely, a mode of thinking that follows after God’s self-disclosure, in contrast to the presumed autonomy of philosophical reflection. Barth thus argues that the Cartesian cogito is overturned by the primacy of God’s self-disclosure: “Descartes is wrong, then, when he says: Cogito, ergo sum. For by reason of this revelation, we are less certain of our own existence than of God’s existence for us.”<sup>21</sup> The living God has decided and acted prior to any human act. The act of faith is therefore not self-positing, but a correspondence to the divine act. We are posited by God. Therefore the Word of God, not the thinking subject, is the starting point of true self-understanding.<sup>22</sup>

Barth’s insistence on the primacy of the Word over against human consciousness became a central conviction in Ricoeur’s philosophical formation. On his account: “It was in fact Karl Barth who first taught me that the subject is not a centralizing master but rather a disciple or auditor of a language larger than itself.”<sup>23</sup> Ricoeur’s engagement with Barth also led to his reading other luminaries of twentieth-century German Protestant theology: Rudolf Bultmann, Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Gerhard Ebeling, Ernst Fuchs, Jürgen Moltmann, and Eberhard Jüngel. His interest in these thinkers arose from his larger interest in the philosophy of religious language (IA 25), and as we will see in chapter 5, these thinkers became important interlocutors in Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion.

*[p. 16]* ^pg33

Barth’s theology of the Word was not only influential for Ricoeur’s thinking on religious language. As he reveals in the passage above, Barth also helped open Ricoeur’s reflexive orientation to the recognition that the self posits neither itself nor the meanings it encounters in the world. This point has major philosophical implications for how we view human consciousness, action, language, relationships, and ethics. What is the origin of meaning? In modernity it is common to assume that meaning—the fact that things make *sense*—is an achievement of the self. I bestow meaning on things through my own acts of sense-giving. But Ricoeur, along with such thinkers as Heidegger, Wittgenstein, and Merleau-Ponty, challenges this view. When I get out of bed, leave my home, and make my way through the world, I do not encounter a chaotic barrage of *non-*sense. Instead, I encounter a world that is already constituted as meaningful. The meaning of the world is given prior to my own acts of consciousness, in and through my participation in the world. Thus when I seek to understand myself—my feelings, convictions, action, relations, and hopes—I can do so thanks to “a language larger than” (and prior to) myself. For Ricoeur, then, Barth’s insistence on the primacy of the Word becomes a basic hermeneutical principle. Just how Barthian Ricoeur is, however, is a question we will consider in chapter 5.

### The Philosophy of Existence: Marcel and Jaspers

Just as the self is not the origin of meaning or reflection, so the self does not establish its being through an act of self-positing. As Ricoeur writes in his 1965 essay “Existence and Hermeneutics,” the self finds itself “*already* posited in being” (CI 21). Ricoeur first encountered this idea in the existentially oriented philosophy of Gabriel Marcel and Karl Jaspers, both of whom he read for the first time during his stay in Paris in 1934. Marcel and Jaspers were significant for Ricoeur’s formation because they helped him ground reflection more firmly in concrete existence.

Ricoeur was introduced to Marcel by a friend, and he soon began to read Marcel’s work and attend the philosophical discussion groups Marcel hosted at his home. In 1947 Ricoeur published a study of Marcel in dialogue with Jaspers, entitled *Gabriel Marcel et Karl Jaspers. Philosophie du mystère et philosophie du paradoxe*. That same year he also published *Karl Jaspers and the Philosophy of Existence,* which he co-authored with Mikel Dufrenne. Ricoeur and Dufrenne had spent several years together during World War II, as prisoners of war in a German camp.<sup>24</sup> During this time Ricoeur studied Jaspers’ three-volume *Philosophy* extensively, and it would prove influential on Ricoeur’s first original work on the philosophy of the will.

*[p. 17]* ^pg34

For our purposes here, the question is how this philosophy of existence altered Ricoeur’s approach to reflexive philosophy and, in turn, his hermeneutics of religion. The influence is evident in the turn to more existential questions, as with Marcel’s investigations of being, embodiment, commitment, invocation, evil, the absurd, and hope,<sup>25</sup> and with Jaspers’ explorations of *Existenz*, transcendence, freedom, communication, limit situations, and truth. These themes are not usually within the scope of traditional reflexive philosophy—though in principle they are not outside that scope. In examining these existential themes, however, the philosopher discovers that reflection needs to be reconceived, since it is already situated in being.

Marcel describes reflection in a way that is highly reminiscent of both idealism and the reflexive tradition, using the term “recollection” to describe “the act whereby I collect myself as a unity.” Unlike German idealism, which revolves around a reflexive turn toward being *for oneself (Für-sich-sein*), or achieving a subject/object unity, in Marcel’s model of recollection the self discovers it does not belong to itself as its own lord and master. Nor is this notion of recollection a kind of Platonic *anamnesis* in which the soul recollects a truth that is already immanent in the soul. Instead, the self recollects its prior participation in being—not the eternal realm of the forms, but the concrete being of the world. This is why recollection is not a self-positing act: “\[T\]his gathering together, this concentration is also a relaxation, a letting go. It involves *abandoning to .  .  . , relaxing in the presence of .  .  . ,* without it being possible for me to specify in any way the substantive reality that these prepositions refer to. The path stops at the threshold . . .”<sup>26</sup> In this returning or recollection, the self does not achieve an intellectual intuition but instead encounters the ontological mystery.

For Marcel, the category of *mystery* is distinct from the category of *problem*. Whereas problems permit abstract, objective, functional solutions, mysteries like the unity of mind and body, the encounter with evil, and the experience of love<sup>27</sup> cannot be approached in this way. Reflection must encounter them in their concreteness; it requires participation in their being. Thus Marcel distinguishes between two types of reflection: “Primary reflection” aims at objectivity, but tends to object*ify*. Marcel argues that these deeper truths of being require *secondary* reflection, which strives to reconstruct “that concrete state of affairs which had previously been glimpsed in a fragmented or pulverized condition.” Marcel is not talking about subjectivism, since this reconstruction occurs “at the level of thought,” “in an intelligent and intelligible way.”<sup>28</sup> Secondary reflection is a way for thinking to attend to ontological mystery in all its fullness.

Ricoeur was drawn to the existential themes of Marcel’s thinking, and he embraced Marcel’s belief that reflection needs to be rooted in the fullness of being. The goal of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics is to recover contact with this 

*[p. 18]* ^pg35

ontological fullness, which gives itself in a surplus of meaning. At the same time, Ricoeur insists that this recovery must be rigorously reflective. Marcel’s model of secondary reflection strives for rigor (IA 7),<sup>29</sup> but Ricoeur found it necessary to intensify this rigor because he was not convinced Marcel gave primary reflection its due. Although primary reflection can be reductive and objectifying, it is also a necessary moment in the reflective process. This is why Ricoeur’s reflection ends up taking detours through disciplines like psychoanalysis, structuralism, biblical criticism, and the natural sciences. Although they are often reductive and objectifying, Ricoeur worries that Marcel too easily sidesteps these modes of primary reflection on his way to secondary reflection. Ricoeur argues instead for a longer detour through these disciplines in the interest of achieving a greater understanding at the end of the journey.

### Explanation and Understanding

This itinerary of detour and return became the basic movement of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics. It involves three essential moments: (1) The first moment is to attend to the meaning given in originary understanding—a first naïveté; (2) the second moment is one of alienation, in a detour through objective explanation; (3) the third moment is one of re-appropriation of meaning on the far side of alienation—a restored, *second naïveté*.

This itinerary also sets Ricoeur apart from some of the big names in philosophical hermeneutics, particularly the Romantic hermeneutics of Wilhelm Dilthey and Hans-Georg Gadamer. Although Gadamer is a major critic of Romantic hermeneutics, Ricoeur includes him in this lineage because he maintains its disjunction of explanation and understanding. This disjunction originated with Wilhelm Dilthey’s reflections on the differences between the disciplines in the university. Dilthey saw two basic modes of inquiry and knowing: explanation (*Erklärung*) and understanding (*Verstehen*). Explanation originated in the natural sciences (*Naturwissenschaften*) and was subsequently applied to the historical and human sciences. Dilthey argued that the human sciences (*Geisteswissenschaften*), on the other hand, operate on a distinctive epistemological basis that is irreducible to the objectifying inquiry of the natural sciences (TA 125–27). Romantic hermeneutics sees this distinction as a clear either/or. This opposition persists (albeit to a lesser degree) in the work of Gadamer, who seeks to defend the humanities and its model of understanding. According to Gadamer, the problem with scientism, objectivism, and positivism is that they proceed with the assumption that the human being—the subject of knowledge—has “the same mode of being” as the object of knowledge (TM 528). But if the natural sciences are useful 

*[p. 19]* ^pg36

for understanding the objectivity of nature, only the humanities can understand the truth of the human spirit and its mode of being—hence the title of Gadamer’s magnum opus, *Truth and Method*. Truth indicates the event of understanding, while method represents the model of critical reason and its objectifying efforts at explanation. Given this opposition, it has sometimes been suggested that the book might have been better titled *Truth or Method*.

Where Gadamer sees an opposition, Ricoeur sees an opportunity. On Ricoeur’s account, explanation does indeed objectify and reduce, but this mode of investigation can serve as a valuable detour in the overall movement toward understanding—what he calls the “hermeneutical arc” (TA 121, 124). Explanation is a detour that is only justified by returning us to higher levels of understanding. Hence Ricoeur’s motto: To explain more is to understand better.

Both Romanticism and existential philosophy are typically averse to those modes of inquiry that seek objectivity by stepping out of our originary participation in being. Ricoeur shares this concern, and sees the existential orientation of Marcel and Jaspers as important to keeping reflection on the right path. While the cultural phenomenon of existential*ism* tends at its worst toward a kind of dark, melodramatic subjectivism, for Ricoeur the philosophy of existence was concerned with a more concrete form of universality. The idea of universality is controversial insofar as it sometimes eclipses the historical, contingent, and particular, but Ricoeur does not want to give up all notions of universality—especially regarding the essential features of philosophical anthropology. If it is possible to seek a universal dimension of the human being, it is no longer through an abstract universality but through more careful attention to themes of embodiment and action, which Ricoeur explores at length in his subsequent work. While existential philosophy rejects a neutral, anonymous universality, it discovers “a new dimension of universality” in its attention to concrete existence: “Even when it speaks of the singularity of dying, of being free, of friendship, existential philosophy opens this singularity to the account of a common structure of being-human.”<sup>30</sup> In order to develop his account of this universality, though, Ricoeur sought a more rigorous philosophical method than is typically found in existential philosophy. For that he turned to phenomenology.

### Phenomenology

Ricoeur’s year in Paris, 1934–35, was intellectually eventful and set the course for much of his subsequent work. In addition to being the year of his first reading of Marcel and Jaspers, 1934 also marked his first encounter with phenomenology when his friend Maxime Chastaing introduced him to the 

*[p. 20]* ^pg37

English translation of Husserl’s *Ideas I.*<sup>31</sup> Ricoeur continued to study Husserl as well as Heidegger’s *Being and Time* through the late 1930s, learning German to study these untranslated texts, and in 1950 he published his French translation of Husserl’s *Ideas I*.

When Husserlian phenomenology began to make an impact in France, what drew the most attention was the principle of intentionality (IA 7)—that is, the principle that consciousness is always consciousness *of* something. The phenomenological definition of intentionality marks a radical break with Cartesianism insofar as it defines consciousness not by itself but by its intentional object. According to Ricoeur, phenomenology opened up a wider range of intentional states of awareness: perception, imagination, affectivity, apprehension of values, and even religious consciousness (IA 8).

Ricoeur found phenomenology promising because he saw its rigorous methodology as an extension of his training in the reflexive tradition. Several decades later he would write that phenomenology, at least in its Husserlian form, “remains a reflexive philosophy.” For Husserl it is a method of describing the essential structures of experience—perception, imagination, intellect, will, the intuition of value, and so forth. But it is also “a radical self-grounding in the most complex intellectual clarity.” This requires a bracketing—*epochē*—of the natural attitude, excluding questions of external existence and causal explanation in order to focus on “the privileged field of phenomenological experience, the domain of intuition par excellence.” In this regard Husserl returns to Descartes by arguing “that every apprehension of transcendence is open to doubt but that self-immanence is indubitable” (TA 13). 

The phenomenological reduction to the transcendental ego also recalls Fichte’s discovery of the self-positing *I*. Just as Fichte thinks it necessary for each individual to discover the purely active self for himself or herself, so Husserl invites each reader to discover the transcendental ego. Ricoeur recognizes this parallel, so it is not surprising that his description of the transcendental reduction sounds a lot like his description of the self-positing I of reflexive philosophy: “Initially I am lost and forgotten in the world, lost in things, lost in the ideas, lost in the plants and animals, lost in others, lost in mathematics.” This is the world of the natural attitude, in which I naively take things as they seem in my everyday interactions with them. The apparent presence of the natural attitude is a temptation, for “if I lose myself in the world, I am then ready to treat myself as a thing of the world.” I become a thing among things, “alienated” from myself, “external” from myself, “diverted” from myself. The phenomenological *epochē* suspends the experience of the world and self as given in the natural attitude in order to rediscover the ego as the transcendental source that constitutes the sense, or meaning, of all things. The *epochē* is a spiritual discipline, an *askēsis* that 

*[p. 21]* ^pg38

brings about a quasi-religious conversion (HAP 20). In a certain sense, it is not even a natural human capacity but rather a matter of “conquering oneself” in one’s natural humanity to give birth to the pure subject of phenomenological inquiry (HAP 26).

As with reflexive philosophy, Ricoeur was not convinced by Husserl’s more radical claims about the transcendental ego. In this, Ricoeur was in good company, following such thinkers as Heidegger, Marcel, Sartre, and Merleau-Ponty by giving phenomenology an existential turn. To some degree, Husserl himself made an existential turn. As his investigations expanded from problems of signification to the problem of perception, it became increasingly difficult to identify the ego as the active, constituting source of meaning. Husserl had to acknowledge more and more forms of passive synthesis, prior to the active work of constituting consciousness.<sup>32</sup> He also had to admit “the altogether existential notion of the horizon of subjective life,” that is, the Lifeworld \[*Lebenswelt*\], which is prior and “pregiven in the sense that every present activity surges into a world already there” (HAP 205). This is a theme that Heidegger develops more fully with his description of being-in-the-world. Existential phenomenology therefore requires its own kind of conversion, or, more precisely, a *re*conversion “from reflection to the pre-reflective” (HAP 209) to the more originary, fundamental modes of our being-in-the-world.

### I Can: Ricoeur’s Phenomenology of the Will

According to Ricoeur, the central themes, or “three melodic lines,” of existential phenomenology are embodiment, freedom, and otherness. The first two themes—the body and freedom—play a central role in Ricoeur’s first original work, an ambitious multi-volume project entitled *Philosophy of the Will*.

The first volume of Ricoeur’s trilogy, *Freedom and Nature: The Voluntary and the Involuntary*, offers a phenomenological analysis of the voluntary and involuntary aspects of willing. Ricoeur pursues this analysis as an eidetic phenomenology, which seeks to identify the essential structures of willing. This analysis leads through detailed descriptions of the reciprocal relation between the voluntary and involuntary aspects involved in making a decision, moving one’s own body, and consenting to necessity. 

In developing this ontology of the willing and acting subject, Ricoeur attempts to situate himself regarding his dual allegiance to Husserl and Marcel (H 2). He proceeds using the reflexive method of phenomenology inspired by Husserl along with the existential style inspired by Marcel.<sup>33</sup> Ricoeur dedicates the book to Marcel, noting that it was motivated by reflection on the work of Marcel. This is particularly true regarding the book’s orientation 

*[p. 22]* ^pg39

toward embodiment. For Marcel body, not language, is the primary focus of reflection. To quote Marcel’s famous phrase, “I am my body.” The phrase “my body” has a double valence: It is possible to view my body as an object, as my possession, as my own property, and yet my body is not only something I *have*; more fundamentally, my body is who I am. *I am my body*: This is the notion of the *corps propre*, the body as subject rather than object. Ricoeur credits Marcel with opening the way “to a philosophy of the body-subject” and thereby giving philosophy “the means for thinking embodiment.”<sup>34</sup>

On Ricoeur’s account, Marcel’s work on embodiment helped prepare the way for Merleau-Ponty’s *Phenomenology of Perception.* For Merleau-Ponty, intentionality is not most fundamentally an act of the intellect, but is realized in bodily movement. My most primordial engagement in and with the world is one of embodied action. This engagement is not mediated by inner mental representations, but in skilled bodily comportment to a world that is already given as meaningful. My movements are attuned to the meaningful possibilities in the world, which call to me and invite my participation.<sup>35</sup> For this reason, Merleau-Ponty concludes that consciousness “is in the first place not a matter of ‘I think that’ but of ‘I can.’”<sup>36</sup>

Ricoeur’s analysis in *Freedom and Nature* takes this theme of the embodied *I can* as a starting point. Both Merleau-Ponty and Ricoeur<sup>37</sup> begin with the *I can* as a deliberate departure from the Cartesian *cogito*, since the *I think* does not reveal the most basic features of the human being. More fundamental than the *I think* is the *I desire, I can, I intend* (FN 9). As we will see, this will become a central theme in Ricoeur’s critique of traditional reflexive philosophy: The *conatus* is more fundamental than the *cogito*. Also important is the way each of these capacities involves a reciprocal relation between the voluntary and the involuntary. When I choose, when I will, and when I act, I do so as body, and this involves both voluntary and involuntary aspects: My will both submits to and governs my body (FN 10). At times I experience myself as master over my own body, but at other times I experience myself as “given over” to it (FN 15). The subject is therefore both “master of itself” and “servant of that necessity” we experience in such involuntary aspects of our being as birth, the unconscious, character, and death (H 2–3).

With Ricoeur’s phenomenology of the body, the self-positing, self-reflecting I meets its own insurmountable limits. The *corps propre*—my *own* body, the body I *am*—“is the movement of being-in-the-world (*être au monde*), indivisibly voluntary and involuntary, as projected and as given” (HAP 209). By expanding the cogito to include the *corps propre*, Ricoeur exposes the impossibility of a purely active, self-positing I. With this expansion, the I is broken out of the “sterile cycle of the self’s constant return to itself,” and receives a more “nourishing and inspiring spontaneity” (FN 14). In the end it turns out that this sterile, self-enclosed I is not the self-positing origin of 

*[p. 23]* ^pg40

consciousness and freedom, but rather the condition of the will bound by fault, sin, or moral evil—the next stage in Ricoeur’s *Philosophy of the Will*, which we will discuss in chapter 2.

### Nabert and the Mediation of Self-Knowledge

In addition to this existential turn, Ricoeur’s approach to reflexive philosophy (and phenomenology) also takes a hermeneutical turn with the insight that the subject cannot discover the conditions of its own establishment directly, but can do so only indirectly in detours through the modes of discourse that figure the self. Ricoeur raises serious reservations about the reflexive ideal of self-transparency. Reflection cannot be “a return to the so-called evidence of immediate consciousness,” because the self is not given directly in “psychological evidence,” “intellectual intuition,” or “mystical vision.” “The first truth—*I am, I think*—remains as abstract and empty as it is invincible.” Whatever truth there is in this self-positing I, it “is a vain truth” (CI 17) that leads nowhere, least of all to genuine self-knowledge.<sup>38</sup> Reflection remains empty unless it reflects on the diverse “ideas, actions, works, institutions, and monuments that objectify” and thereby mediate our self-understanding (FP 43), for example, objects like art, music, writing, laws, religion, philosophy, institutions and everything else that is a manifestation of the human spirit. Reflection has to recapture the ego “in the mirror of its objects. Reflection is blind intuition if it is not mediated by what Dilthey called the expressions in which life objectifies itself” (CI 17).

What this means is that reflection needs to be understood as *interpretation*, not intuition. Ricoeur credits the French philosopher Jean Nabert (1881– 1960) with grasping this most clearly and thereby giving reflexive philosophy its most profound form. Nabert reoriented reflexive philosophy by showing that the only way to know the cogito is by deciphering the “documents of its life” (CI 18). This becomes a central insight in Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of the self. We understand the self indirectly, through the mediation of the texts that have formed the self and its distinctive ways of being-in-the-world. Our experiences, actions, affections, and convictions are understandable because they are mediated through exemplary symbols, stories, and songs, the metaphors, poems, and treatises that have told us who and how we are. The hermeneutics of the self therefore means an interpretation of the documents that have formed the self.

What exactly does reflection recover through these interpretive detours? Given Ricoeur’s focus on texts, we might suppose these expressions mediate a richer, fuller *idea* or *representation* of the self. While that much is true, Ricoeur warns against reducing reflection “to a single dimension”—namely, 

*[p. 24]* ^pg41

the epistemological approach in which reflection seeks to “ground the ‘objectivity’ of our representations.” For Descartes, Kant, and much of the reflexive tradition, epistemology is dominant. Ricoeur argues that while the epistemological concern with true representations is important, it belongs to the larger and more fundamental task of reflection: to recover the *act* of existing. *I am,* *I exist* is not originally a theoretical representation, the positing of an *idea* of my own existence. It is more fundamentally an affirmation of my desire to be and my effort to exist.

According to Nabert, reflexive philosophy seeks to grasp the reflection “which constitutes the subject itself, first of all, and then recaptures the laws and norms of spiritual activities in all domains immanent in these operations.” In other words, “to consider the human spirit in its acts and in what it produces in order to appropriate their meaning.”<sup>39</sup> There are two key elements here: the act and its product. Ricoeur articulates the two elements as follows: first, the act by which the self brings itself into being, and second, “the signs wherein consciousness represents to itself the meaning of its action.” From these two elements come two different ways of doing philosophy. Philosophy has often given priority to the latter, focusing on the “objectivity of idea, representation, understanding.” There is, however, an alternate tradition that subordinates representation to “the founding act of consciousness.” We find this in Spinoza’s account of the *conatus* or “effort to be,”<sup>40</sup> Leibniz’s account of appetite, Fichte’s act of self-positing, Schopenhauer’s account of will, Nietzsche’s account of the will to power, and Freud’s account of libido. For each of these thinkers, representation is secondary to the more primary work of effort and desire (CI 211–12). Thus while Nabert follows Fichte in arguing that we must begin with act rather than representation (CI 214), he goes further by arguing that the goal of reflection is not intellectual intuition of the self but recovery and re-appropriation of desire—the desire to exist and the effort to be. Nabert designates this re-appropriation of desire as *affirmation originaire*: primary, or originary, affirmation.<sup>41</sup>

Like Nabert, Ricoeur proceeds as a sympathetic critic of the reflexive tradition, seeking to reform it from within (CI 17). For Ricoeur, this reform takes place at several points.

First, Ricoeur follows Nabert (and Spinoza) in treating the desire to be and the effort to exist as more fundamental than ideas or representations.<sup>42</sup> This leads him to reconceive reflection as primarily an ethics rather than epistemology. Ricoeur does not mean ethics in the sense of determining principles of morality and duty but in Spinoza’s sense of a philosophical process leading from bondage to freedom of the will and happiness. The goal of reflection is to recover our existence as desire and effort. It is desire, or *eros*, because “it is never satisfied.” This desire is not, however, a simple lack or absence of being; it is effort, or *conatus*, because it has a “positive energy” 

*[p. 25]* ^pg42

and “dynamism” and is “the affirmative positing of a singular being” (FP 44). “Effort and desire are the two sides of this positing of the self in the first truth: *I am*” (FP 46). This point will be essential for Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, which studies the way religion liberates and regenerates this active core of the human being in order to bring about the rebirth of the capable self.

Second, reflection never achieves a “perfect self-identity” or “immediate self-possession.” As Ricoeur puts it: “We never produce the total act that we gather up and project in the ideal of an absolute choice,” which means “we must endlessly appropriate what we are through the mediation of the multiple expressions of our desire to be” (CI 222). Self-understanding is never possible directly or immediately, nor does it ever achieve true self-transparency. This is why Ricoeur reorients reflexive philosophy as a hermeneutics of the self,<sup>43</sup> which examines the products—that is, the language, cultural artifacts, and institutions—that make self-understanding possible. This is also how Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion will work. In order to understand the believing self, it is necessary to interpret the formative texts in which belief is articulated. In a 1971 encyclopedia entry on myth, Ricoeur puts it like this: “If we understand ethics in this way as the whole set of conditions for the realization of freedom, then all self-understanding is ethical and we can affirm that symbolic consciousness is the long detour by which the self discovers the conditions of its own establishment” (PA 174). 

Discovering “the conditions of its own establishment”—that is a great summary of Ricoeur’s model of reflexive philosophy, and of his hermeneutics as a whole. The self discovers that it is not absolute. It has not established itself through an act of self-positing, but finds itself “*already* posited in being” (CI 21). This means the self cannot discover the conditions of its own establishment directly, but can do so only indirectly in detours through the figures of the self. The self is posited in being addressed by the word of another. The hermeneutics of the self thus requires an essential moment of dispossession, letting go, abandonment (*dépouillement*) of the illusion of self-positing. Ricoeur sees this as the philosophical meaning of Jesus’ saying: “Who would save his life must lose it” (H 150).

This leads to the third point, which concerns the epistemic status of self-understanding. While epistemology is secondary to ethics, it is, nevertheless, a legitimate concern. The reflexive tradition originated with a claim to certainty. *Cogito, ergo sum* is Descartes’ indubitable truth, the foundation meant to withstand all of the doubts of the skeptics. But beyond skepticism lies the hermeneutics of *suspicion*, exemplified in thinkers like Nietzsche and Freud, who direct devastating assaults on the modern concept of the subject, arguing that claims to self-knowledge are more self-deception than self-certainty. The basis of this suspicion is the conviction that consciousness is not self-transparent and often conceals unconscious workings that it cannot (or will 

*[p. 26]* ^pg43

not) face directly. One’s beliefs signify something other than what one consciously believes, and so we need a hermeneutics of suspicion to distinguish the true sense from the merely apparent sense—the “sense under the sense” (PPR 215). Ricoeur insists that the hermeneutics of suspicion is a necessary challenge for reflexive philosophy and its ideal of self-knowledge, and likewise for the hermeneutics of religion. This is the topic of our next section.

### Suspicion: The Askēsis of Reflection

In the early stages of his hermeneutical turn, Ricoeur defines hermeneutics in terms of symbolic language. This hermeneutic of symbols is central to his project in his 1960 book, *The Symbolism of Evil*, which we will discuss in more detail in chapter 2. For Ricoeur, symbolic language involves multiple levels of meaning, with a level of direct, literal meaning pointing to an indirect, figurative level of meaning (CI 12–13). Interpretation is then the correlative “*work of thought*” in which we decipher this figurative meaning. 

Ricoeur’s 1965 book *Freud and Philosophy: An Essay on Interpretation* continues his hermeneutics of symbols, defining the symbol in terms of double-meaning—the split between a direct, literal meaning and an indirect, figurative meaning. For Freud, this is the difference between the manifest and latent content, and it entails a reductive hermeneutic that explains symbols and myths “as fruit of unconscious representations, as distorted expressions of the relation between libidinal impulses and the repressive structure of the super-ego . . .” (RM 318). This hermeneutic threatens to undermine reflexive philosophy by showing that self-knowledge is more a matter of self-deception than self-certainty. Suspicion even threatens more hermeneutical models of self-reflection by suggesting that the symbols and myths that have formed the self cannot be understood at face value.

This reductive hermeneutic also presents a major challenge for the hermeneutics of religion, showing how religious consciousness and its meanings are not what we think they are, but are instead masked expressions of the unconscious. Philip Rieff describes Freud’s method in these terms: “By acts of interpretation, the sacred becomes symptom. Sacred images are, then, the visible form not of grace but of sickness.”<sup>44</sup> Ricoeur wants to engage this challenge head-on, in order to see what hermeneutics can learn from Freud’s critique of religion.

Ricoeur soon recognized that Freud belonged to a cohort of similarly reductive interpreters. The first was Feuerbach, who reduced theology to anthropology by exposing the human tendency to project our own experience into ideas of God. Then came Marx, who exposed the false consciousness rooted in ideology, and later Nietzsche, who exposed the false consciousness 

*[p. 27]* ^pg44

rooted in the resentment of frustrated Will to Power. Ricoeur expanded this critical hermeneutics in the 1980s in his engagement with ideology critique, which began with Feuerbach and the young Marx in their account of ideology as “the general device by which the processes of real life are obscured,” or *inverted*. This idea develops through later thinkers like the Frankfurt school, Mannheim, Habermas, and Althusser, and its basic point is to expose the way ideology creates false consciousness. Ideology is the “distorting or dissimulating processes by which an individual or a group expresses its situation but without knowing or recognizing it.”<sup>45</sup>

Readers of Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud know how caustic their suspicious interpretations can be. Yet Ricoeur concluded that philosophy cannot sidestep the confrontation with suspicion, but must instead go through it. The hermeneutics of suspicion comprises a radical discipline of self-renunciation—an *askēsis—*that reflection must undergo if it is sincerely concerned with self-understanding.

This was not Ricoeur’s first reference to the concept of *askēsis*. He also saw Husserlian phenomenology in these terms. In his introduction to Husserl’s *Ideas I*, Ricoeur describes the phenomenological *epochē*—the bracketing of the natural attitude—as a spiritual discipline (*ascèse*) required in order to achieve the transcendental consciousness of phenomenological method (HAP 16). The phenomenological *epochē* is therefore an ascetic discipline for the philosopher. 

Psychoanalysis is likewise a work of *askēsis*, though it has the opposite effect to phenomenology because it displaces the origin of meaning from consciousness—transcendental or otherwise—toward the unconscious, “the transcendence of speech or the emergence of desire” (FP 422, 494). Psychoanalysis thereby presents a “decided antiphenomenology” that destroys the manifest sense of reality as given in and to consciousness. Psychoanalysis challenges reflexive philosophy from Descartes to Kant to Fichte to Husserl, but Ricoeur argues that philosophy must confront psychoanalysis as a kind of ascetic purification. The subject must abandon the illusion of being the origin of meaning, losing “the most archaic of all objects: the self.” Just as the Gospel says that one must lose one’s *psyche* to save it, so the reflexive subject must lose its dream of pure self-intuition to recover it symbolically. This is what psychoanalysis teaches reflection: “The self \[*le moi*\] must be lost in order to find the ‘I’ \[*le je*\]” (CI 20). For this reason, the ascetic discipline of psychoanalysis is beneficial, and even necessary, for philosophy because it prepares the way for more authentic self-understanding (CI 172).

This theme of ascetic renunciation returns throughout Ricoeur’s engagement with psychoanalysis. Ricoeur refers to an “ascesis of reflection,”<sup>46</sup> and a “discipline of reflection,” since “it brings about that dispossession of consciousness and governs the ascesis of that narcissism that wishes to be taken 

*[p. 28]* ^pg45

for the true Cogito” (FP 60). To be dispossessed of the origin of meaning in this way is a difficult process, just as it is painful to embrace the principle of Ananke, or Necessity, and also to renounce the nostalgia for a primordial father, who provides consolation and comfort amidst our fears, and an object for our desires (CI 458–59).

Displaced and dispossessed, consciousness now confronts itself as a task rather than a given (CI 172). Self-recovery is not possible by simply resolving to think more clearly and carefully, so that one at last constitutes oneself as a fully self-responsible thinker. That was Descartes’ strategy in the *Meditations*, which was an ascetic undertaking in its own right. But the anti-phenomenology of psychoanalysis, and the hermeneutics of suspicion more generally, undermine any attempt of consciousness to establish itself by appealing to itself. Nor can this lost meaning be recovered through an act of will, in a project of self-positing or self-creation. It must be given, and it must be received as a gift.

With this last suggestion Ricoeur looks beyond the ascetic constraints of Freud’s reductive hermeneutics and gestures toward a different hermeneutics—one that retrieves or recollects the symbolic meaning attested to by the symbols and myths that give meaning to the self and its existence. Ricoeur asks: “Does not this discipline of the real, this ascesis of the necessary lack the grace of imagination, the upsurge of the possible? And does not this grace of imagination have something to do with the Word as Revelation?” (FP 36). Ricoeur is not appealing to a return to the original naïveté of religious consciousness. The reductive hermeneutics of suspicion makes it impossible to return there, but it also clears the way for what Ricoeur famously calls a “second naïveté.” Beyond the iconoclastic destruction of its idols, perhaps religious symbols and narratives can speak anew (PPR 219). This is a wager rather than a posture of certainty, but Ricoeur argues that this wager is the condition for authentic, mature faith. The displacement of meaning means that the self no longer seeks to save itself, but can be open and receptive to transcendence, as mediated by the text. “The exegete is not his own master; to understand is to place himself under the object which is at stake in the text” (PPR 222). Or as Ricoeur will put it in a later essay: “I exchange the *me*, *master* of itself, for the *self*, *disciple* of the text” (TA 37). The *moi* becomes the *soi*.

### The Little Self that Could: Attestation

Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of the self is not a return to the cogito as the indubitable metaphysical foundation of knowledge. In *Oneself as Another*, Ricoeur describes his desire to chart a middle course between the exalted cogito of 

*[p. 29]* ^pg46

Descartes and the humiliated cogito of Nietzsche and Freud—a *wounded* cogito, which is characterized by fragility and uncertainty but also possesses sufficient confidence to assert itself in response to the masters of suspicion. He adopts the term *attestation* to describe the kind of self-assurance that is proper to the self. Attestation is not a guarantee but rather a mode of testimony, a kind of confidence or credence in which the self can assert: Here I am! (*me voici!*) (OA 21–23). It is an assertion of the self as a capable human being—an assertion not of certainty but of a living confidence on the level of faith, hope, and trust—that one’s capacities for speech, action, narration, and responsibility are not merely self-delusions.<sup>47</sup> Attestation = *I think I can*, *I hope I can*.

This vision is the animating principle of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion. His wager is that on the far side of the modern discourses of critique and suspicion, there is still meaning in religious texts, that these texts can still speak and reveal possibilities of liberation and transformation for the self and for the world. We will see in the chapters ahead that Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion is all about the ways in which religious discourse restores and reconstitutes the *I can*. First, however, we must confront the sobering reality of the I *cannot*—that is, the self bound by radical evil.
