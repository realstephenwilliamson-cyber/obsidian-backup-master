## About

A short book by the French philosopher Bernard Stiegler, written in response to the 2008 financial crisis. It calls for renewing Marx's critique of political economy for an age in which technologies take over human knowledge and memory.

- **French original:** *Pour une nouvelle critique de l'économie politique*. Wikipedia says this first part appeared in France as a short book in 2009; the CSCP's review page says it was published in French in 2010. The second part comes from Stiegler's larger *Ce qui fait que la vie vaut la peine d'être vécue* (2010).
- **English edition:** translated by Daniel Ross, Polity, 2010 (Wiley gives November 2010). Wiley lists the hardback at 100 pages; the CSCP page gives 154 pages (ISBN 978-0745648040).
- **Structure:** two parts, the title essay and "Pharmacology of Capital and Economy of Contribution".
- **Argument:** drawing on Plato's critique of memory placed outside the mind, Stiegler redefines the proletarian as a worker whose knowledge and memory pass into machines. Manual workers lose practical know-how and knowledge workers lose theoretical practice (Goldsmiths record). Capitalism captures desire and turns it toward short-term investment; Shaw's review says Stiegler traces the crisis largely to short-termism and carelessness. He contrasts life's work rooted in leisure (*otium*) with jobs (*negotium*) and calls for a new concept of economic value.
- **Context:** the CSCP review notes Stiegler's claim to commemorate the 150th anniversary of Marx's *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), though Shaw finds that the book does little more than make the claim.
- **Reception:** Devin Zane Shaw (*Symposium*, 2012) argues that Stiegler drops class analysis and offers institutional and moral remedies instead of political ones. Manuela Zechner and Bue Rübner Hansen fault its lack of feminist and postcolonial perspectives and its reliance on elites and policy.
- **Why it matters:** the Goldsmiths record presents it both as an introduction to Stiegler's thought and as a political call to action.
