## Czerniak Contingency PO KOREKCIE 2

*Stanisław Czerniak* 

 THE CATEGORY OF “CONTINGENCY” IN 

CONTEMPORARY ANTHROPOLOGICAL DISCOURSE. ODO MARQUARD, RICHARD RORTY, JÜRGEN HABERMAS 

*ABSTRACT* 

This comparative paper analyses in detail the contexts in which the “contingency” category was used by the philosophers mentioned in its title. While Odo Marquard and Richard Rorty situated contingency within the antifundamentalist discourse, especially in the sphere of philosophical anthropology, epistemology and ethics, Jürgen Habermas drew his conception of the contingency of human birth from the “human nature”—related discourse against modern-day genetic engineering. Marquard’s and Rorty’s theories differ in their philosophical assumptions (scepticism vs. neopragmatism). Among others, the author shows that none of the mentioned thinkers accepted the radically relativistic consequences of the debate around the “contingency” conception. In his analyses, he also makes frequent use of Marquard’s distinction between “arbitrarily accidental” and “fatefully accidental.” 

Keywords: contingency, coincidence, randomness, freedom, human nature, skepticism, arbitrarily accidental, fateful accidental, auto-creation. 

1\. INTRODUCTORY REMARKS 

In the post-modern current of contemporary philosophical literature (Odo Marquard, Richard Rorty), as well as in the works of philosophers who debate postmodernism (Jürgen Habermas), the concept of “contingency” often appears in a context in which its role is that of an anthropological category employed to describe that which is human. Its meaning fluctuates, however, depending on the type of philosophical discourse. This paper will attempt to sketch two such usages in two different discourses. The first we will call a discourse of the “deabsolutization” of human knowledge (or a discourse of “defundamentalization”), and the second a discourse of a philosophy of defence of the human individual’s autonomy in view of its endangerment by civilizational progress. 2\. THE CONCEPT OF CONTINGENCY IN THE ANTI-FUNDAMENTALIST 

DISCOURSE: ODO MARQUARD’S STAND 

For Marquard contingency as a characteristic of the human condition is related to human finitude, man’s inability to dispose of all the conditions of his existence, thoughts, and actions. As a subject of choices, he is not able to make (or not make) a causative decision, pertaining to his own birth. His imperfect intellect and short lifespan do not allow him to achieve absolute knowledge in a multitude of fields. Finally, his actions are not entirely controlled by his will, since they either bring unintended results which he cannot control, or they have been—at least partially—forced upon him by external conditions, or by what the subject encounters and not creates. Marquard confronts that which is contingent, relative and finite with that which is unquestionably certain, universal, and absolute, while his “defence of the accidental” confronts the “absolutizing man” project in the Cartesian, or Sartrean version (man is not granted absolute certainty of cognition, nor absolute freedom of self- or historical creation). As a result, contingency appears as that, which “I cannot change,” that which limits me as a thinking and acting subject, and teaches the humility of scepticism towards whatever happens to one. “We human beings—Marquard concludes—are always more our accidents than our choice.”[^4]

Does that mean that a thus-understood apology of the accidental boils down to the negation of man’s freedom? Marquard does not eliminate that notion from his philosophical vocabulary. First, next to “fatefully accidental” (*Schicksalszufällige*), or that which escapes human will, he distinguishes contingency that has its source within acts of will, “arbitrarily accidental” (*Be-liebigkeitszufällige*)[^5]—I did not have to do something, but I did it despite my ability to do otherwise.[^6] It should be noted, however, that the horizon of the possibility of human actions is determined—in Marquard’s view—by the horizon of that which steps beyond the field of manoeuvre of the acting subject, or by that which inadvertently conditions and defines its actions. Secondly, Marquard analyses different ways of neutralizing the effects of fatefully accidental to broaden the scope of the human experience of freedom. He looks at fatefully accidental as such, as well as its role as a factor limiting the arbitrary freedom of action understood as *Beliebigkeit.* 

In order to highlight that matter, it is worthwhile to begin with the fact that Marquard does not consider a potential, anthropological “heavy” weight, a causal interpretation on chance understood as “an event without a cause” (the “entirely causal” concept of accident in Krajewski’s terminology).[^7] He assumes that whatever happens to men, and becomes contingent in the order of their life plans, or the self-creation of their actions, always has its “own” reasons. 

The point is, those causal chains disrupt and distort cause-effect relationships initiated by our intended actions. Anthropologically, the concept of contingency does not collide with the deterministic model of the world, and Marquard does not engage in ontological-epistemological conflicts surrounding the uncertainty principle of Werner Heisenberg. From the anthropological point of view it does not matter whether the event that causes me to, for example, lose control over my own actions is—as such—an event devoid of cause (e.g., like the “free” movement of electrons in the nerve cells of a brain core), or if it is a link created by homogenous (in the sense of the class of a natural phenomenon) cause and effect conditioning. However, one of the conclusions Marquard reaches in the course of his analysis is that a phenomenon he calls “a surplus of determination” decides about the range of freedom of man as an acting subject. “Human beings are not free by virtue of the fact that they copy God (as quasi– omnipotent directors on the world stage, or by having unconditioned capability

Assuming that Marquard has two anthropological concepts of contingency that he employs in mind: A) the accidental understood as freedom of choice, and B) the fatefully accidental, we would be facing two potential outcomes related to the effect of the overabundance of determinants on the range of type A or type B contingencies. What would that mean specifically? 

Type A contingency refers to the number and degree of diversification of potential (achievable) goals of action. In this case a high degree of freedom would overlap with a most wide range of freedom of individual choice within a social sphere of action. As could be expected, Marquard refers here to the sociological circumstance that whenever the boundaries of the sphere of individual actions encounter rivalry between different centres of political power, or social authorities, individuals can act more freely (they choose from a bigger pool of options) than, for example, in the case of totalitarian systems where their actions fall under a unanimous, monopolized set of regulations. This interpretation reminds of Max Scheler’s diagnosis formulated in the 1920s in his *Problem of Sociology of Knowledge*:

“If a deepening divorce of church and state in the late middle ages, opposed to their so-called ‘organic’ bond in full bloom of that epoch, was, as noted by Comte, an increasingly certain warranty of the freedom of science due to scientists gaining an opportunity to win the authorities over for themselves—the limiting authoritarian binds had to weaken even more, since more new churches and sects emerged, mutually limiting and balancing each other out \[…\] True and absolute freedom of science had never, historically, stemmed from the autonomous strength of the scientific spirit itself, but only due to mutual competition of real, sociological factors in relation to sovereign philosophy. And how would the overabundance of determinants look in respect to type B contingency? Marquard seems to be taking into consideration that also irreversible “twists of fate,” through their sheer multiplicity and variety which neutralize their influence, can motivate one experiencing them to take a distanced stand towards fate in general, an attitude which deepens the subjective sense of freedom. It happens in cases where an individual can denounce random factors he or she was not aware of up until that point or, in other words, initiate experience of a pluralistic ambiguity of fate: 

“What man has to fear is not determination, but the undividedness of its power \[…\] It is general important \[…\] that the greatest possible number of determinants should be incorporated into our lives, which means incorporating, as well, the fatefully accidental pieces of reality that exercise their determining effect by being noticed. Thus it is advantageous for human beings when the boundaries of what they notice collapse. The most human of these ‘extreme reactions’ \[…\] are, as Helmuth Plessner and Joachim Ritter have shown, laughing and crying: that is the gentle ways in which we acknowledge previously unnoticed and repressed fateful accidents, which—balancing other determinants—codetermine human reality. By laughing and crying, we intimate our acceptance of what remained, officially, excluded from consideration, but is, unofficially, part of the story: namely, the accidents that (accidentally) thwart what is officially accepted. Through them, we laugh or cry ourselves free.”<sup>11</sup>

Laughing and crying become included into the sphere of human specificity as “borderline reactions” to random, accidental situations. Marquard however—as he concludes himself—does not follow Ritter’s-Plessner’s amalgamation, but rather both Ritter’s and Plessner’s interpretative paths separately when it comes to discovering the anthropological meaning of those reactions. While for Plessner laughing constitutes an expression of loss of balance experienced by the “eccentric I” against the (linguistic, social) surroundings as a chaotic “answer of the body” to palpable inability to continue routine behaviour,[^12] for Ritter[^13]—which Marquard highlights in the quoted passage—it appears as a particular tool for denouncing and the indirect taming of hidden, random life determinants. Denouncing in this instance is both an act of neutralization, and of relativizing importance. Such a function can be played by, for example, political jokes in totalitarian regimes: they allow to focus attention on that which is officially banned from public discourse due to internal censorship, or to openly name politico-public “non-reason” in order to more easily dealienate from it through a private rationality of denouncing laughter. But laughter not only reveals certain fateful determinants: a fate itself, the very principle of random contingency as such—so often forgotten by individuals whenever they are able to safely realize their life goals across longer periods of time—can become its point of reference, and of psychological crystallization. Such was the character of the sudden burst of laughter accompanying the dance of Zorba the Greek in the final scene of the movie: here laughter had a cathartic role, cleansing human self-knowledge from the mood of being enslaved by that which happens. Zorba’s laughter is not only an expression of approval for that type of contingency, an approval that emerges from an emotional-intellectual distance towards fate, but also a form of manifesting the “joy of life” stemming from the experience that one is continually a being that can have anything “happen” to it. 

In the description of contingency as a key *antropologicum* there appears a particular dialectic of physicality and spirituality. Through laughing and crying, reactions belonging not so much to a spiritual as a corporeal order, man neutralizes negative experiences of contingency in the spiritual dimension. Simultaneously, however, he is capable of neutralizing physical contingencies (including unexpected suffering) as a spiritual being, in other words, through spiritual techniques of enduring, sublimating or compensating suffering. Marquard recognizes both those types of reactions as manifestations of the taming of contingency, of situating it within the sphere of the subject’s approved own knowledge about the human condition. He assumes silently that “contingency” as a characteristic of the human condition constitutes a particular lack in the specific human make-up, which brings to mind Gehlen’s description of man as

Describing the potential human reactions to that challenge, Marquard takes two argumentative routes. The first relates to Herman Lübbe’s[^15] philosophy of religion, and is based on the assumption that there exist cultural institutions, such as religion, in reference to fatefully accidental, designed to “ease” (*Bewäl-tigung*) that very contingency. Marquard situates art[^16] next to religion in that same context, as through the phenomenon of form it supposedly neutralizes at least some of the experiences related to type A contingency. Religious experience makes it easier for believers in God to grant a deeper sense to that which is brought along by fate. The rules of art, on the other hand, allow the artist to reduce the anxiety he experiences in the face of a field of potential choices, which he has to make in his creative acts. Stylistic clarity and craftsmanship, once they are achieved, constitute a “selector” which increases the pace of the search for the “final” form of the work of art. Thanks to his own style, it is easier for an artist “finish” his work, and achieve a sense of artistic fulfilment of the “finis coronat opus” type. Form is a kind of a road sign helping imagination with creative work. Without it, it could lose focus, or get lost in fortuitousness of potential “final versions” of the work. Fulfilment of those cultural and social decontingential instruments is constituted, as we know, by individual mechanisms—laughing and crying in respect to fateful contingency. 

Another argumentative dimension in which Marquard takes up the question of “easing” contingency is his philosophical apology of the accidental in the sense of approval for established rules, practices, or traditions, which are always considered as starting points for human actions, and support of which constitutes the main postulate of Marquard’s social conservatism. Human beings at peace with the contingency of their own fate, subjects that are aware of their not being merely “a result of consciously made choices, but also of coincidences,” will be willing to keep the contingent status quo that they are at peace with. That preserving acceptance does not have to be preceded by any kind of former legitimization of tradition. It possess a “right” to last simply because it has been lasting, and because it continues to last. 3\. THE CONCEPT OF ANTI-FUNDAMENTALIST CONTINGENCY: 

RICHARD RORTY’S STAND 

Rorty differs from Marquard in the philosophical context (neo-pragmatism) in which he employs the concept of “contingency,” as well as his much stronger emphasis on its epistemological interpretation—which inevitably leads to more (and especially philosophical) differences. On the other hand, however, he and Marquard share many convictions which are rooted in the antifundamentalist paradigm. Rorty writes about the contingency of language, the contingency of

<sup>18</sup> Marquard, O. 1991, op. cit., 116.

the self, and the contingency of liberal society. What does that mean, collectively, in Rorty’s proposed interpretation? Languages (here Rorty interchangeably employs a “vocabulary” category), particular, individual selves or other socio-political orders emerge—like mutations in the Darwinian, biological sense—randomly, or as results of particular twists of causes which are impossible to predict with any degree of accuracy. What is more, there is no objective axiological hierarchy (within the range of cognitive, social, moral and other values) that could constitute criteria for their evaluation that step beyond history (or beyond the sphere of accidental constellations of historical factors). Hence, the vocabulary X, Newton’s theory, for example, is contingent in character, because it emerged accidentally in Newton’s mind (Rorty speaks here of a situation, in which “metaphorical use of gravitas was the result of cosmic rays scrambling the fine structure of some crucial neurons in his brain”[^19]), as well as because (Rorty assumes here the methodological view of Thomas Kuhn) it cannot be derived logically from previous theories, nor do there exist any valid theoretical criteria for recognizing its “superiority” against potential alternative theories. In other words, the accidentality of ideas is based on the fact that their appearance is independent of all laws (e.g., sudden epiphanies during creative processes), and on contingent circumstances which decide their historical fate. Newton’s theory proved useful in achieving certain particular goals, which in turn created the need for its further development, division into subdisciplines, and evolution over centuries. The ontic aspect of contingency (a contingent event possesses specific causes—Rorty, as well as Marquard, are not radical indeterminists—but their network is complex enough to escape any prognostics) goes hand in hand with the epistemological aspect: vocabularies do not serve cognizing minds to move closer to the objective truth, let alone the absolute truth (it is important to observe here that Marquard does not consider this distinction in the light of his concept), and there do not exist measurable criteria of choice between one and the other. The sole sense of their lasting in the sense of the actual cause of that lasting (Rorty supports the postulate of the so-called “naturalized epistemology”[^20]) rests on a random collection of historical events, against which certain of their functions manage to be successfully fulfilled. Hence, also for Rorty—and similarly Marquard—the philosophy of contingency is a philosophy postulating a divorce from the absolute in the form of absolute truth, absolute criteria of choice, moral norms imposed in an absolute fashion, etc. Where Marquard presents his opinion from the standpoint of a skeptic (man as a finite being is not able to possess knowledge that is of absolute validity), Rorty rejects those categories as belonging to an anachronistic (from a neopragmatic point of view) vocabulary. In his preferred interpretation Rorty underscores the “accidentality” of the concept of “contingency,” meaning that which is new and original. He highlights that contingencies constitute “successful” mutations, allowing their bearers to achieve astonishing successes. Meanwhile, Marquard—as we have witnessed—operated primarily with the notion of a contingent, fateful event, “the results of which we are unable to alter.” This kind of passive-fatalistic addition does not appear in Rorty’s optimistic concept. In the vocabulary of his philosophy, the categories “creative imagination” or “innovative metaphor” are in the foreground. Within the neopragmatic framework of Rorty’s thought, contingency acquires the meaning of something that—although often only in a distant perspective—breaks the established order. It serves the role of a ferment, an intellectual challenge and an ideological revolt. 

That difference between Rorty and Marquard in understanding “contingency” itself will also define their different interpretations of an attitude described by both as “acceptance of that, which is contingent.” For Marquard, the acceptance of contingency is a modus of “bearing fate,” which manifests itself, e.g., through laughing and crying, both of which grant us distance towards fate and allow to find different kinds of determining forces that define us, but at the same time counterweigh each other in their competing multitude, making that determination not complete (absolute), and the laughing subject thereby becomes one that liberates itself from fate’s influence (where two fight—and, according to Marquard, that numeral can refer to centres of power, institutions, social centres of influence, as well as worldviews—the third, the addressee and object of those influences, profits). Within Rorty’s vocabulary such a constellation of dependencies would disrupt its coherence, since the self is contingent in its context, and, emerging in the course of the usage of a particular vocabulary (“to create one’s own mind is to create one’s own language”)<sup>21</sup> constitutes its accidental creation. Also, the acceptance of contingency does not stem from acts of distancing (even if that distancing were to express acceptance of the situation to which the act of distancing is an “answer”), but is a source act of the self’s identity. The “I” does not hide—laughing and crying—behind a veil of that which happens to it, nor does it appear as an originally sovereign instance, secondarily employing competition between contingent influences. It is the “I”

This is why Rorty does not place so much weight on that which Marquard calls contingency of birth, and in a certain sense relativizes that random contingency as such: “The hope of Bloom\`s poet is that what the past tried to do her she will succeed in doing to the past: to make the past itself, including those very causal processes which blindly impressed her own behavings, bear her impress. Success in that enterprise—the enterprise of saying: “Thus I willed it” to the past—is success in what Bloom calls ‘giving birth to oneself’.”<sup>22</sup> Similar in tone are his references to Freudianism. On the one hand he highlights that the accidentality of individual idiosyncrasies (e.g. traumatic experiences from childhood) shapes the self, making it unique, but on the other hand, he reminds us that—in the light of the assumptions of Freudian psychoanalysis—those idiosyncrasies, belonging typically to the sphere of the unconscious, can be denounced cognitively, “to create present selves whom we can respect.”<sup>23</sup> Hence, the past is not only that with which a being conscious of its contingency can reconcile exclusively through distance, while the ability for the creative decontingency of that which is contingent is in fact more important than compensatory abilities. 

One can speak about a positive or negative understanding of freedom related to the acceptance of contingency. For Rorty, contingency is a source of positive freedom: we are free, because we imprint our own contingent mark on our own contingent fate. Marquard, on the other hand, presents negative freedom, which accompanies the intellectual taming of contingency or the act of “coping with it.” We do not depend entirely on ourselves, but that non-dependency is not complete, since it is contingent itself, and the negative freedom is focused on fulfilling (in the face of objective circumstances) the acceptable “limit” of one’s own needs within the framework of competing determinants that enslave us. That difference in interpretations is connected to, most likely, deeper philosophical differences between scepticism and pragmatism. Scepticism is a minimalist philosophy of self-restraint with regard to human expectations towards fate: against its rulings, our freedom can only happen to us, and is the result of disruptions in the field of determination. Pragmatism places axiological stress on the “creative” dimension of fate: every individual fate is a challenge for bearers of other fates, and simultaneously constitutes a self-referential task, an outline of auto-creation. Only by solving tasks (including the task of creating oneself)

The self, which does not constitute a challenge for itself, gradually annihilates itself and ceases—as Rorty often puts it—to exist, because its existence can be interpreted as reduced to creating its own vocabulary. Human existence—as a vessel for the human experience of freedom—is identical with the desire for creative acts. Marquard, on the other hand, does not define man as a *par excellence* creative being, but as a compensating being, and its freedom is not something existentially primal for the thinker, but rather something secondary—compensation for at least some ways in which individuals submit to their fate. Every enslavement and submission connected to, for example, an order of social enforcement, has its own “reverse side,” which reveals in Marquard’s reflections the phenomenon of compensation for that order, constituted by the chaos resulting from the overabundance of determinants. The experience of the freedom of individuals as users of contingent niches of sovereignty in the sphere of necessity and enforcement is allowed to constitute itself, *ex post*, by that chaos, which is a secondary, hidden property of order. Hence, Marquard considers contingency to be a special kind of space, intermediate between necessity and its compensations. Scepticism does not allow him to accept a more optimistic view: that the world is, by nature, creative and that freedom necessarily accompanies all creation. Obviously, Marquard also accepts the idea of the presence of creation—after all, type A contingency is of that particular character, and in every ascertainment of “I could have done something else” is embedded the sense of dignity of a sovereign subject of action. The contingency of freedom is limited, however, by random contingencies situated in the sphere of social, external determinations. As a result, it can be established only through the secondary freedom of a participant in a social game who exploits the “weaknesses” of the social system to his own advantage. 

One covert assumption of Marquard’s philosophy of contingency seems to be a specific adaptation of the principles of dialectics: contingency is and is not that which it is, and what it is undergoes compensation by that, which it is not. Type A contingency is a source of freedom, but simultaneously it is not, since it is ontically anchored in the field of influence of B type contingency. The latter defines our fate, but at the same time not completely, because as beings capable of laughing and crying, as well as capable of religious experiences, we can distance ourselves from fate. The Hegelian concept of the other of being included within being—is transposed by Marquard for use in the field of philosophy of action, according to which man at the same time controls and does not control his actions. Both having control and lacking it is contingent in this case, because any necessity is dialectically “mediated” by freedom, and freedom—by necessity. Rorty’s world is not ruled by dialectics of that kind, which introduces a kind of dualistic accent to reality. It is a monistic, self-creating universe, where creation

*[p. 42]* ^pg44

continuously happens to the world and to men inhabiting it. Freedom, on the other hand, manifests itself simply in consciousness, or self-knowledge about that situation. Contingency is the attribute of a world, while the freedom of human, contingent beings manifests itself in it directly, and not through a meandering path of dialectical mediations and compensations. 

Those general philosophical assumptions also influence the concept of the self-knowledge of freedom developed by both thinkers as the approving awareness of contingency. For Rorty, this self-knowledge has a monistic character as well: it constitutes the self-confirmation of the contingent creativeness of the world. For Marquard, on the other hand, self-knowledge of freedom constitutes itself in a strictly ontic crevice between that which is, and simultaneously is not, a necessity, hence does not possess the quality of a simple statement, but rather the dialectic character of a negation of a negation. Fateful contingency excludes our absolute sovereignty of action, but “contradiction” to that type of conditioning takes on the form of the distanced stance of one who is laughing.[^24] As a consequence, Marquard ascribes much greater importance to the category of “referring to one’s own contingency,” which he situates within the notional field particular to the entire school of Joachim Ritter,[^25] as well as the philosophy of compensation, which is absent in neo-pragmatism. Rorty, on the other hand, develops under that terminological label a concept of acceptance by the subject of contingency of its own stand (in the sense of presented views), which can be reduced to the realization that the contingency of an opinion itself does not present any methodological obstacle in the assertive statement of those views. Rorty leans towards identifying freedom with the approved presence of contingency. Free is who uses contingent tools to solve contingent problems. Marquard avoids equating the two; it is not contingency alone that constitutes the final source of freedom, but the way in which one approaches it, the compensations it offers, as well as the ability to exploit the interplay of the blind, contingent forces in which we are involved. Seen from this perspective, contingency in its broad, anthropological interpretation is an expression of finitude, as well as the weakness of man, which pushes him towards compensating that weakness, a historical cunningness which uses an abundance of contingent determinants, towards aesthetic activities, and religious stands that make the existence of contingencies bearable, or “*lebbar”*—as Marquard would put it. The relativistic discretion concept (it is irrelevant what I choose, because every choice holds the same value) has sense exclusively on the grounds of absolutism, which has at its disposal criteria allowing the justification of that discretion as equality. Rorty himself does not take the accusation of radical relativism against his philosophy of contingency seriously, refuses to tackle any adversary who uses that argument, and silently embraces some of the absolutist assumptions he disavows. He argues that it would mean acquiring the language of the enemy and falling into methodological naïvety, which one should avoid. 

Marquard’s anti-relativistic arguments, on the other hand, are commonsensical in character and refer back to his professed primacy of “givenness.” From the cognitive, or moral, point of view we cannot “not care,” because existing, established, practices are a distinguished context, hence they suggest to the individual not all the identically valuable preference systems that are possible, but precisely one—the contingent one. 4\. THE CONCEPT OF CONTINGENCY IN THE FUNDAMENTALIST 

DISCOURSE: JÜRGEN HABERMAS’S PERSPECTIVE 

However one were to define the range of similarities between the assumptions and main theses of Rorty’s and Marquard’s philosophies of contingency, there is a fundamental characteristic that connects both of them—they constitute an element of the anti-fundamentalist discourse within contemporary philosophy. The discourse is, as we have suggested before, at once anthropological, epistemological and ethical in nature, and could be expressed by the formula that absolute truth does not exist, or is inaccessible to us, as are absolute (absolutely certain and obvious) justifications of actions, and absolutely (universally) important moral norms (Marquard’s established practices do not hold such a status either). What exists are merely contingent truths, stands, and norms. If that indeed is the fact, then contingent, finite beings—such as human subjects—should approve of that situation and conclude that it constitutes their anthropological uniqueness and dignity. How that uniqueness and dignity will be interpreted in detail is secondary and, as we hoped to show, both philosophers differ quite radically on the subject. Yet, there is a particular starting point of reflection that unites them: anti-absolutism, anti-universalism, and praise of contingency as the consequence of “divorce from the philosophy of absolute principles.” 

We are facing a radically different argumentative strategy in a philosophical discourse that treats “contingency” as a phenomenon which constitutes a particular foundation and a source of warranty for human freedom. In his *Die Zukunft der menschlichen Natur* Habermas refers to the anthropological views of Hannah Arendt in that respect, and highlights the fact[^27] that human birth (understood

It gives it the sense of being “irreducible” to a result of all the social communication-related events of one’s own life. 

Where does this feeling of sovereignty come from? Habermas—again following Arendt<sup>28</sup>—will highlight the aspect of what is “new” and “unique” in every newborn—a moment that is supposed to constitute a necessary condition of the freedom of the future subject of actions. Habermas and Arendt root the fact that my actions (and their products) observed in my adult life can break away from any and all stereotypes in the *novum* of nature that is every newborn individual. All possible outbursts of human originality in the cultural dimension base on that “original novelty”. One cannot foresee that which I will create as a scholar and a poet. Similarly, nobody could predict precisely the shape of my physicality before I was born. Habermas and Arendt seem to assume that there are certain causal dependencies between those different spheres of contingency’s appearance, or that reality possesses a quality that allows for new phenomena within it only when it appears in its different ontic segments simultaneously. 

As it is easy to observe, birth here is not, as in the case of Marquard, analysed as that which man “cannot free himself completely from” in his finite life (if I was born as a male child, I could change my sex as an adult, but my life is too short for me to fully relieve myself from the burden of sex as a contingent stigma of birth), or as something that constitutes boundaries to his freedom, but as a source of human autonomy and sense of freedom. If, at the moment of my birth, I were not a purely natural creation, I would not possess the awareness of being rooted in something other than a network of social dependencies. In other words, as at the moment of birth also my physicality was at least partially a social fact, I would be deprived of a convincing argument in a debate with anyone who, following Marx, would wish to reduce me to an “entirety of social

relationships.” That which is social would constitute me, or co-constitute me in all the dimensions of my existence. But such a reality would limit my freedom substantially, because it would make me the result of somebody else’s intentions. 

What seems to be particularly interesting in Habermas’s approach is the absolutist thread noticeable in his line of thinking. According to him, birth is an absolute source of human freedom in the sense that human contingency should not be destroyed by anyone, pre- or post-natum, without the anticipatory inclusion of the obvious, future goods of an individual. Habermas allows for prenatal medicine focused on the removal of the genetic defects of the foetus.<sup>29</sup> This entails a certain limitation of the contingency of birth itself, but medical intervention stops before it reaches a certain “natural norm” in the form of biological health. When removing foetal defects in keeping with natural health standards, a doctor will take consideration of this natural dimension (in this context Habermas employs a “negative eugenics” category). However, someone wishing to burden medicine with the task of championing “positive” eugenics (in Habermas’s terminology), i.e. one that would correspond with certain social, contingent impressions about human “perfection,” will have that very nature in disregard. Thus-motivated medical intervention into the natural human condition would be on par with other products of human ingenuity, such as cars or computers—it would assume the status of an object with an expected “utility value.” Someone who already at birth appears—through his fundamental physicality—as a bearer of such value in the light of social expectations extending beyond the order of nature, cannot be free. Rorty emphasizes that freedom is based on the acceptance of contingent tools, which we discover and with which we create ourselves. But is an individual, who already at the moment of his birth appears as a tool for realizing the needs of others, at all capable of creating himself? The question is not about the banal circumstance in which my birth is related to, for example, certain parental hopes which I can as a free, contingent being fail to fulfil, but about the de-contingentization of my birth and myself (my physicality), being designed in a specific way, and as a newly born being not happening to nature as its creation in accord with natural standards, but rather being “created” by both alien demand and science’s competencies. An individual “designed” by genetic engineering is not a fully contingent being at the moment of birth, but constitutes the embodiment, or the materialization, of an eugenic project. Hence, it in effect questions the absolute character of the natural sources of the humanness which transpires through the phenomenon of contingent birth. 

Habermas inscribes his defence of the “contingent character of human birth” as an inalienable source of human freedom (in an understanding of the term limited to his proposed critique of “positive” eugenics) into a philosophical program in which the category of “human nature” occupies a privileged posi-

“A liberal society is one whose ideas can be fulfilled by persuasion rather than by force \[…\] by free and open encounters of present linguistic and other practices with suggestions for new practices. But this is to say that an ideal liberal society is one which has no purpose except freedom, no goal except willingness to see how such encounters go and to abide by the outcome.”[^32] 

Habermas is far more sceptical in this respect (although his scepticism, which leads to absolutist conclusions, is closer to Descartes, than Marquard), and, somewhat between the lines, poses the question: what would happen to my freedom if, as a result of social consensus (when all participants of a democratic discourse, without surrendering to any obligation, will agree to recognize genetic engineering understood as “positive eugenics” as worthy of social approval), I would become deprived of the status of a contingent being at the moment of my birth? Rorty, by accepting the idea of the possible multiplicity of “human kinds,” and playing with the Nietzschean category of the “new man,” does not seem troubled by such a perspective. Positive eugenics appears to him simply as a new metaphor that could potentially lead to the emergence of *hominum novus* (Rorty stated: “Changing languages and other social practices may produce human beings of a sort that never before existed,” hence human beings whose languages have changed profoundly, “would thereby become a new kind of human beings.”[^33]). In Dewey’s footsteps, Rorty recognizes “imagination as the chief instrument of the good.”[^36] Habermas wishes to authenticate good through referring to the “meter” of “universal validity.” It is a deeper difference than Rorty is ready to admit in his polemic with Habermas: “Our differences concern only the self-image which a democratic society should have, the rhetoric which it should use to express its hopes.”[^37] Habermas’s emphasis on the absolute character of the contingency of birth is for him not simply rhetorical in character, a mere persuasive figure with which he wishes to manifest his loyalty towards a certain existing ethos, which in turn his philosophy wishes to articulate. It is not rhetoric employed to persuasively present a certain common standpoint, but a philosophical declaration that, for Habermas, is something more than just a quasi-poetic metaphoric attempt, a contingent creation of the imagination. The importance of this issue is attested to by Habermas’s radicalization, to a certain degree, or, in this context, de-formalization of the understanding of a key philosophical reflection of the “communicative reason” category. He looked in his writings for communicative rationality in the very discourse, a discourse that was free from distortions, and for formal *a priori* conditions of validity which its different types are obliged to meet (e.g. scientific or ethical discourse). In *Die Zukunft ...* he imposes additional “material” conditions that anchor communicative rationality in, as he calls it, “natural, normative foundation of life” or “the ethical self-understanding of the species, shared by all moral persons”[^38] on those procedural, formal requirements. The rationality of a discourse does not appear here as its purely immanent feature. Also a discourse that meets certain immanent, logical requirements, and idealizing conditions of communication free from distortions, has to respect the “normative foundation of life.” It is not enough to “have open encounters”—as formulated by Rorty—and respect “their outcome,” but one also has to respect *a priori* boundaries that should not be crossed in their result. A specified definition of the apriority of those boundaries is a matter of philosophy. As a result, Habermas, as opposed to Rorty, situates philosophy above democracy[^39] in the hierarchy of importance, and that is—along with the simultaneous assumption that philosophy is neither a pure rhetoric nor a kind of poetry—the fundamental difference between his and Rorty’s stands on contingency. According to Habermas, the very actions of imagination do not seem to hold enough warranty to secure the universal, ethical self-knowledge of a species from undergoing consensual-discursive-democratic destruction.

In conclusion: that, which is important for the anthropological usage of the category of “contingency” is not its definition-based meaning (as we have witnessed, both Rorty and Habermas define the contingency of birth as a biological *novum*), but its placement within a certain discursive whole. Habermas and Rorty operate a similar concept of contingency within the framework of different discourses. The first is a post-modern discourse on the identity of liberalism. The second seeks the universal legitimizations of all social practices, including the practices of liberal society, however it does not describe those legitimizations as “metaphysical” (Habermas considers himself to be a post-metaphysical thinker<sup>41</sup>), nor does it seek them outside social reality (the concept of the “natural foundations of life,” or Kantian ethics, are examples of principles abstracted from the self-reflections of social beings).
