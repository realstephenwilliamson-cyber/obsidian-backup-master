## 5. A Summary of the Saiva Theory of Recognition

ZOG

The Supreme Lord, who has the nature of awareness, makes His own Self into an object of cognition, even though it is not an object of cognition, because the Cognizer is unitary. ... As He recognitively apprehends \[parămrśati\] His Self, so, because everything is contained within Him, He appears as blue, etc.

—IPV 1.5.15, 1:267-68

How do the Śaivas answer the challenge of skepticism? Before the Navya-Nyāya,' the Hindu realists remained locked in the problematic defined by the Buddhists—that is, they attempted to justify the interpretation of an uninterpreted datum. Their final recourse often was just to deny the value of the skeptics’ questioning the validity of constructions we all accept in daily life—in effect stipulating that there cannot be such doubts.

Chapter 3 has shown, however, that the Pratyabhijñā theory of recognition is articulated within an ambitious correlational agenda—of radical encompassment. The Pratyabhijñā thinkers attempt with their theory of recognition both to make their tantric tradition philosophically intelligible to others and, ultimately, to convey to them the soteriological realization. They follow the approach of Śiva described in Abhinava’s verse quoted in the epigraph of the fourth chapter. That is, they turn everything back into Him. As is evinced in the epigraph of the present chapter, the central myth of Śiva emanating the universe through Śakti is explained as a process of self-recognition. Such recognition, thereby given a primordial status, is recovered through transcendental inquiry as constituting the very facts of epistemology and

*[p. 86]* ^pg106

ontology that the Buddhists say preclude it.? The primordial status of this recognition in turn makes the philosophical inquiry that realizes it into tantric ritual.

The Pratyabhijñā thinkers’ theory of recognition is a brilliant synthesis of the understandings developed in the debates between the Hindu realists and the Buddhists, their own traditional symbolic and ritual resources, and the linguistic idealism of Bhartrhari. As is the case with many aspects of their thought, this theory is so complex and multivalent that it resists a linear presentation. Nevertheless, I must endeavor to impose an appropriate order in presenting and interpreting some of their statements.

I will first comment on the vocabulary in which the Śaiva theory of recognition is expressed. Then I will give some basic explanation of two related complexes of ideas contributing to the Śaiva theory not yet treated: The most important of these is Bhartrhari’s interpretation of Indian traditions of sacred language, the Śaivas’ use of which I am comparing with the Christian philosophical theology of logos. The other of these complexes is tantric understandings of memory as the repository of sacred language in the form of mantras. Next I will take up in a broad structural manner the ways in which the model of the Lord’s self-recognition is related to ordinary experience, from the two directions of emanation and return? Thus I will first discuss how the Śaivas also appropriate Bhartrhari’s scheme of levels of the concretization of speech in explaining the emanation of the Lord’s self-recognition. I will then examine how the theory of recognition elucidates features of the system’s simultaneously philosophical and soteriological modus operandi of return presented in chapter 3—for example, transcendental philosophical investigation, the revealing of Sakti, the inference for the sake of others, and the structuring of the system in terms of the gnoseologically internalized ākta means-type. Finally I will explain the strategies the Śaivas use to respond to the Buddhist reductio ad absurdum arguments against recognition, and the relation of these strategies to their own positive theory of recognition. This summary of central features of the Śaiva theory of recognition will enable us to proceed in the next two chapters to considering the ways in which it is implemented in epistemology and ontology. It is in considering such implementations that the

question of justification becomes important.

### Basic Vocabulary of the Saiva Theory of Recognition

First, comments on vocabulary. The Śaiva theory of recognition is elaborated with three sets of terms. The first is the word pratyabhijñā

itself along with cognates such as abhijñā, which are usually unproblematically translated as just “recognition.” The second set comprises various derivatives from the root mrś, such as vimarśa, parāmarśa, pratyavamarśa, āmarśa, and so on. Alper has designated this class the “mrś terms.” There has been much discussion about the interna? relations of this set.t Whether or not there are some differences in connotation between these terms themselves, previous scholars have not appreciated their significance in the context of the debates about imagination (vikalpa) and recognition treated in the previous chapter. The words derived from mrś convey notions of linguistic interpretation, judgment, reflection, apprehension, and so forth, all of which have a

recognitive structure.

The third set of terms is derived from attaching various initial prefixes to the second prefix sam and the root dhà, for example, anusamdhāna, pratisaṇmdhāna, and abhisamdhi. Again these develop the significance of recognition through notions of synthesis or association, particularly the synthesis between different moments of experience (as analyzed by the Buddhists)—under the rubric of language.’ All these terms have extensive backgrounds in linguistic and epistemo-

logical speculations before the Pratyabhijñā school.

In the Pratyabhijñă texts, these terms are variously defined by one another, used interchangeably, and placed in close functional relationships. They are also employed disjunctively. The task here of summarizing the complex Pratyabhijñā theory of recognition before considering its implementations is already difficult; it is not possible to engage in a tortuous philological examination of usages in the abstract. The following presentation will be made on the basis of the synonymies and homologies between the classes of terms.” I will make my translations according to the contexts of use, leaving the important Sanskrit terms in brackets. I will often direct the attention of the reader to the overarching problematic in my glosses of the mrś and anusamdhāna terms, for example, as, “recognitive ascertainment.” On a few occasions it seems best to translate these terms simply as “recognition.”

The Linguistic Monism of Bhartrhari

We may now take up two important sources of ideas incorporated into the Śaiva theory of recognition that have not yet been focused upon. The first of these is the linguistic monism of Bhartphari. Bhartrhari’s system represents a philosophical development of ancient Indian understandings of sacred language—important from the Veda through the Tantras.’ It is articulated in the context of grammatical

*[p. 88]* ^pg108

and linguistic speculations of the Pāṇinian school. Bhartrhari metaphysically interpreted the sacred language of revelation as the monistic Word Absolute (śabdabrahman). Another term for the highest level of language in Bhartrhari’s thought, which the Śaivas often use, is

Supreme Speech (parāvāk).?

For Bhartrhari, the Word Absolute is much more subtle than what is ordinarily understood as language—words in sentences referring to separate objects. It is the essential source from which the semantic dichotomy is idealistically emanated. In the first verse of the Vākyapadtya Bhartrhari extols this principle as the Ultimate Reality generating the very sounds of speech along with the entire world of objects:

Brahman, the Word-Principle, which is without beginning or end, is the cause of phonemes, and manifests itself as objects. From it there is the creation of the universe.”

On the basis of his exalted understanding of language Bhartrhari maintains that, through its proper use and the understanding of it in the discipline of grammar, one can achieve liberation.”

This monism posits an intrinsically linguistic character of experience, on the basis of which one is able to make conventional references of words to objects. As Bhartrhari explains, the linguistic plenum thus grounds knowledge and communication:

lf there were to end the eternal condition of cognition having the nature of speech, awareness could not be aware.” For it is this \[condition that cognition has the nature of speech\] which makes possible recognitive judgment \[pratyavamarśinī\]. This \[condition\] secures \[upabandhinī\] all the branches of learning, the crafts and the arts. Due to it, everything created is classified.”

The ordinary interpretation of experience gains access to the higher level of language through flashes of intuition (pratibhā), and communication is successful through its manifestation (sphoṭa). It should be clear that Bhartrhari’s basic position is diametrically opposed to that of the Buddhists.”

Tantric scriptures and other writings predating the Pratyabhijñā system already reflect an appropriation of the system of Bhartrhari. The originator of monistic Śaiva philosophical apologetics, Somānanda, on the other hand, devotes substantial space in the Sivadrṣṭi to defending Śaivism against Bhartrhari’s school. He is not concerned with repudiating it, but only limiting its scope as a description of the ultimate nature of things and a soteriological path. Somānanda “encompasses”

*[p. 89]* ^pg109

or “imperializes” Bhartyhari by identifying Supreme Speech with Śiva’s

creative Śakti.'5

Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta elaborate greatly on the positive aspect of the interpretation of Bhartrhari in Somānanda as well as the earlier tantric traditions. They identify Supreme Speech with the Lord’s self-recognition (ahampratyavamarśa).'\* As observed in the epigraph at the beginning of this chapter, and as will be further analyzed below, the Pratyabhijñā thinkers explain their cosmogonic myth of Siva emanating the universe through Sakti as this process of His self-recognition.

As Śiva's cosmogony itself is a process of recognition, the Śaivas maintain that a kind of recognition is integral to the nature of all experience. Abhinava explains that “having the nature of synthesis \[anusamdhāna\] is invariably concomitant with consciousness.” He describes recognition (pratyabhijñā) as “the root of any of the means of cognition \[pramāna\], i.e., perception, inference and scripture,” or simply says that it “is any of the means.”” This is expressed in the later text following the Pratyabhijñā school, the commentary Mānasollāsa on the Dakṣināmaūrtistotra: “Recognition \[pratyabhijñā\] is the means of establishing \[sādhana\] all the means of cognition \[pramānṇa\]."”

The Christian philosophical theology of logos also was developed through the identification of earlier Greek and Hebraic understandings of the metaphysical grounds of the intelligibility of the world as integral features of God’s nature.” It is of great interest that the logos has frequently been identified, from the Church Fathers to the nineteenth-twentieth-century absolute idealists, as a modality of God's self-knowledge or self-recognition. God's understanding of the rationality by which He creates the world—whether ex nihilo, through emanation, or through a combination of such notions” —must primordially be an understanding of something within Himself. St. Anselm

of Canterbury puts it concisely:

Hence, that Spirit, supreme as he is eternal, is thus eternally mindful of himself, and conceives of himself after the likeness of a rational mind; nay, not after the likeness of anything; but in the first place that Spirit, and the rational mind after its likeness. But, if he conceives of himself eternally, he expresses himself eternally. If he expresses himself eternally, his Word is eternally with him.?

God creates the world from this very self-expression:

Hence, when the supreme Spirit expresses himself, he expresses all created beings. For, both before they were created, and now that they have been created, and after they are decayed or changed

*[p. 90]* ^pg110

in any way, they are ever in him not what they are in themselves, but what this Spirit himself is.”

Such an understanding of the Word is elaborated in Thomas Aquinas’s classic discussions of God's knowledge within himself of all things other than himself, and this knowledge as the cause of those things.” The Russian “Sophiologist” Sergei Bulgakov describes Wisdom in the same way:

The first and most fundamental question which we must ask is this: is the content of the life of the world, or rather are the divine principles on which it is based, something new for God himself, which was unknown to him prior to creation and which was lacking in him, apart from his relationship to creation? It is enough to raise this question to be able to see the obvious answer. For as soon as we admit that the principle of creation is something new to God himself, we must recognize a certain incompleteness in God without creation. This inevitably forces us to the further conclusion, that the creation of the world, is in some sense, a sort of self-revelation for God himself.”

Thomas Aquinas clearly explains how our knowledge, or recognition, is ultimately grounded in this divine knowledge:

Natural things are midway between the knowledge of God and our knowledge: for we receive knowledge from natural things, of which God is the cause by His knowledge. Hence, as the natural objects of knowledge are prior to our knowledge, and are its measure, so, the knowledge of God is prior to natural things, and is the measure of them; as, for instance, a house is midway between the knowledge of the builder who made it, and the knowledge of the one who gathers his knowledge of the house from the house already built.”

Truth is found in the intellect according as it apprehends a thing as it is; and in things according as they have being conformable to an intellect. This is to the greatest degree found in God. For His being is not only conformed to His intellect, but it is the very act of His intellect; and His act of understanding is the measure and cause of every other being and of every other intellect, and He Himself is His own existence and act of understanding. Whence it follows not only that truth is in Him, but that He is truth itself, and the sovereign and first truth.”

### Traditional Religious Understandings of Memory

Before analyzing in more detail the Śaiva understanding of how divine self-recognition is related to ordinary experience through emanation and philosophical inquiry, one other area of constitutive influence in their theory must briefly be considered. Another pan-Indian and especially tantric theme closely related to that of sacred speech is the religious valuation of memory.? Most obviously, memory operates in the perpetuation of sacred language along with other culture. Abhinava refers to this operation in stating that the succession of memory (smaraṇaparamparā) regarding Ultimate Reality (brahman) known from the scriptures and other sources is a spiritual means.”

More profoundly, memory is the means of access to the essential level of language. The adept gains such access through the memory of the exemplars of this level of language in the form of mantras. This gives him salvific-”cosmogonic” as well as lower, derivative powers.

Abhinavagupta explains:

The followers of scripture maintain that memory, which is enlivened by mantra, etc., is like a wish-fulfilling gem which is capable of bestowing all powers \[siddhi\]. Thus \[it has been said\]: “Memory itself, taking on the nature of contemplation \[dhyāna\], etc., is the wish-fulfilling gem which manifests your Lordship.”

When conceived as the repository of sacred language, memory is accorded a metaphysically primordial nature. In the course of a discussion of mantra in the Tantrāloka, Abhinava quotes the authority of scripture in positing memory as the very origin and synthesizer of all

experienced diversity:

In the Trisirobhairavatantra, before the extraction of the mantras, it is explained: “Memory is the remembering which is there from the beginning in all things, and actually has the nature of mantra. It effects the apprehension of the essential nature of the object of cognition.” Memory generates the essential nature of, and colors all things in multiple forms which abide everywhere. It is the attainment of one’s own nature and ultimately has the nature of consciousness. Ít resides within the manifestation \[of everything\]. Therefore one should know that it is called Supreme Being \[sattā

parā\].®

The same notion is expressed by Abhinava in a benedictory verse to a chapter on memory in the IPV, using the Upaniṣṣadic metaphor of the cosmic thread:

*[p. 92]* ^pg112

We praise Siva, who strings together in series on the string of memory, the heap of jewels that are the objects of words \[padārtha\], which are heaped up in the jewel room which is His heart.”

These exalted valuations of memory do not usurp but support the transcendental nature of recognition. The Buddhists attempted to refute recognition by denying the groundedness of the application of the mnemonic resources of interpretation to present experience. The conception of memory as primordial in effect says that all experience is the reexperience of what is remembered—this is recognition.” This way in which a formulation of recognition as transcendental may be related to an understanding of memory as transcendental is important to keep in mind. It enables us to understand how a transcendental recognition would not depend upon the contingencies of creation and

activation ordinarily experienced in memory.

The Christian philosophical theology of logos and other formulations of the transcendental nature of recognition, synthesis, disclosure, and so on, are spared this problem in various ways—deliberately or just by nature. Plato's understanding of the soul's anamnesis of Ideas known before birth is well known.” Much of Christian theology, deeply influenced by Neoplatonism, used this notion% to describe the soul’s “recollection” in itself of the Ideas in God's logos/mind as well as God Himself. In Christian mystical prayer there is also commonly a process of recollection of the soul, and of God in the depth of the

soul.”

lt must be emphasized that the higher form of memory discussed here is something much deeper than the ordinary contingent memories of life. We shall see that in expounding their theory of ordinary memory in response to the Buddhists, the Saivas actually reduce it to a deeper sort of recognition.”

The Structure of Self-Recognitive Emanation

Next on our agenda is considering, in a broad structural manner, how the Pratyabhijñā thinkers relate their conception of the Lord’s self-recognition to ordinary experience—by explaining the processes of emanation and return. I say that we are considering this area of their thought in a broad structural manner because we are interpreting its presumption “intratraditionally” as the interpretation of myth and philosophical/ritual practice. The final aspect of the Pratyabhijñā correlation, the ostensibly universal explanation of ordinary experience,

*[p. 93]* ^pg113

is made in the epistemological and ontological arguments to be taken up in the next two chapters.

We first turn to the Śaivas’ interpretation of the myth of emanation as a movement from the Lord’s self-recognition into recognitions transcendentally constituting ordinary experience. In explaining emanation in terms of self-recognition, the Śaivas incorporate Bhartrhari’s own account of the fragmentation of the unitary Word Absolute. Bhartrhari described this process in a series of either three or four stages, depending upon whether one understands him like the Śaivas to have actually posited Supreme Speech (parāvāk) as the highest stage identical to the Word Absolute.

The Śaivas identify three of Bhartrhari’s stages (they skip a lower one) with the main cosmic levels of the Trika. We considered the Trika levels in the third chapter, as locating the spheres of operation of Abhinavagupta’s spiritual means-types (upāya). To remind the reader, I list some of the homologous items corresponding to each of the stages in the descending order of emanation:

1\. Śiva. The Śakti as well as the cosmic level designated Supreme (para). The Śakti of Will (icchā). The condition of nondiversity (abheda). The means of Śambhu, that is, iva

(śambhava upāya).

2\. Śakti. The Śakti as well as the cosmic level designated Supreme-Nonsupreme or Intermediate (parāparā). The Śakti of Cognition (jñāna). The condition of simultaneous diversity and nondiversity (bhedābheda). The means of Śakti (śākta upāya).

3\. The individual person (nara/aṇnu). The Śakti as well as the cosmic level designated Nonsupreme or Lower (aparā). The Śakti of Action (kriyā). The condition of diversity (bheda). The

“individual means” (nava upāya).

Because of its intrinsic difficulties, and since our task is already complicated enough, I will not present Bhartrhari’s scheme of speech stages independently. We will consider it only as it is appropriated by the Śaivas.” In their synthesis, the Śaivas describe emanation as a move from a unitary state of self-recognition to the concrete structure of reference involved in the applications of interpretation in multiplistic experience. For the sake of simplicity, this process will be described in terms suggesting present perceptual experience. However the same process occurs in all types of outer and inner experience and reflection.“

*[p. 94]* ^pg114

I will now present a long passage in which Abhinavagupta describes the process of emanation. This will be followed by some general remarks about each of the descending stages. I will further discuss various points made in this passage in relating the paradigm of recognition to the Pratyabhijñā modus operandi of return. For greater clarity, I will divide the continuous discussion according to the Trika levels:

Recognition \[pratyavamarsa\] has the nature of verbalization \[śabdana\] as an inner talking \[antarabhilāpa\]. That verbalization does not at all depend upon the establishment of reference by convention \[samketa\]. it has the nature of unbroken wonder, and is like an inward nodding of the head. it is the life of language sounds of the realm of Māyā, such as \[the phoneme\] “a,” etc., which have their reference fixed through convention. lt is called Supreme because it is the inner foundation of recognitive judgements \[pratyavamarśa\] such as “This is blue” and “l am Caitra,” and because it is perfect. It is called Speech because it speaks the universe by means of recognitive judgement \[pratyavamarsa\]. For this reason, “as its own essence,” i.e., in the form of Consciousness, and as resting \[viśrānti\] in itself—it is the “arisen,” i.e., always unset, eternal “I.” Therefore it is said to be the chief “freedom of action” of the Supreme Self, and “Lordship,” i.e., the nature of one who lords, and non-dependence on others.”

Then there is the Supreme-Nonsupreme \[parāpara\] in the

condition of Seeing \[paśyantī\], at the level of Śrī Sadāśiva. Here there arises the recognitive judgement \[pratyavamarśa\] having the form “this,” which \[because it begins to fragment the Self) involves ignorance. Nevertheless there is still the \[proper understanding that the judgement “this”\] rests \[viśrānti\] on the condition of “I.”

There is the Lower state when there is rest on the condition

of “this.” This state belongs to Viṣṣṇu, Viriñca, Indra, etc., who rule over the womb of Māyā.®

In the first section Abhinavagupta identifies Supreme Speech, which is the transcendent ground of concrete reference, with the Lord’s self-recognition at the Trika level of Supreme. This takes the form of a completely unfragmented “1.” The next section concerns the second level of speech according to the Śaiva exegesis of Bhartrhari, called “Seeing” (paśyantī). Abhinava identifies this level with the Supreme-Nonsupreme level of the Trika triad. Here there is the awareness of the object as dependent upon the subject. It has the expression “I am this.”“

*[p. 95]* ^pg115

In the third section, Abhinava identifies Bhartrhari’s lowest level, called “Gross” (vaikharī), with the lowest member of the Trika. This level comprises ordinary interpreted experience, from the recognitions of external objects (such as “This is that pot”) to those constituting the components of the physical and psychic person. At this level self-recognition is fragmented in various ways. In order to emphasize that the interpreted object is apparently separated from the subject, this level of experience is typified with the expression “this.” The Śaivas also follow Bhartrhari in characterizing this level by the apparent separation of language from its objects. From this point of view, it may be more fully expressed with the phrase used to typify the recognitive structure of linguistic ascription: “This is that.” It is also characteristic of this level of experience that recognized objects are distinguished from each other.“

The Philosophical and Soteriological Return

We may now turn to relating what has been said so far to the frameworks of the Pratyabhijñā system's modus operandi of return. This will greatly deepen our understanding of the system as both soteriology and philosophy. We may begin with the central positive formulation of the modus operandi as the transcendental epistemological and ontological—and śākta upāya—revealing of Śakti. The Śakti that the subject is discovered to possess is the reason in the inference that establishes his self-recognition as the Lord. Now, in the passage above equating Supreme Speech with the Lord's self-recognition at the Supreme level, these principles are also equated with freedom of action and Lordship. The latter are other terms for Śakti.

Thus Śakti, the mythological description of Śiva’s sexually united consort, power, and mode of emanation, is self-recognition.” Utpaladeva

explains this clearly:

The ascertainment \[adhyavasā\] “This is a jar” is the Supreme Lord’s Sakti, who transcends name and form; it appears as the Self and not as “this.”

Abhinava elaborates:

The ascertainment \[adhyavasā\] judges \[parāmrśantīḷ?” word and object, characterized \[in the verse\] by name and form, as one, in the manner of “This is that.” \[That ascertainment\] is the Supreme Lord's Sakti, who has the nature of recognitive judgement \[vimarśa\].

*[p. 96]* ^pg116

lt appears only “as the Self,” that is, non-separately from “I.” However, it never appears as “this,” that is, as separate \[from the

Self}.®

Some repetition may help in developing the next point. The transcendental inquiries to be taken up in the following chapters are concerned to show the nature of experience as Sakti. They will take the form of showing that the recognition of interpretation is fundamental to ordinary experience—and that such recognitions are forms of the Lord’s self-recognition. Śakti, again, is the reason in the inference that structures the Pratyabhijñā system. We are lead to the startling realization that self-recognition, the thesis-goal of the Saivas’ inferential-ritual methodology is identical with the reason that justifies it. That is, one is inferentially led to the recognition that one is the Lord, because everything is one’s self-recognition. Utpala explicitly places the interpretation of the two chief Śaktis of Cognition and Action in terms of recognition, in the position of inferential reason:

He \[the subject\] is the Great Lord since it is necessarily the case that he is recognitively judging \[vimarśattvena niyatena\]), and since that very recognitive judgement \[vimarsśa\] is the pure Cognition and Action of God \[deva\]."

The inferential-ritual process may be further elucidated in terms of the other characteristic theme of the Pratyabhijñā system along with the sākta upāya in general—the purification of conceptualization (vikalpasamskāra). The lower and intermediate stages of the emanation of self-recognition further define the reverse course that this purification has to take. I will comment on some other ideas found in the passages recently considered. First, it is notable that in the passage on emanation Abhinavagupta explains the awareness at the middle level as belonging to Sadāśiva. Sadāśiva in a more elaborated account of cosmic levels is supposed to possess Pure Wisdom (śuddhavidyā)}—the operative insight in the śðkta upāya and the Pratyabhijñā system.” Abhinava describes the structure of awareness at this level in the manner I explained Pure Wisdom in chapter 3—as the emanation-knowing awareness of the object resting on the subject, that is, “I am

At the lower level recognition takes the form of gross conceptual constructions. Here there is a loss of the emanation-knowing structure of Pure Wisdom in experience; objects are differentiated from the subject, from language, from each other, and occur in differentiated

*[p. 97]* ^pg117

circumstances. Again, experience at this level is typified “This” /="This is that.” For example, “This is that pot.” Because such constructions are unifications of what appears as differentiated, Abhinavagupta describes them as instances of reflected recognition (chāyāmayī pratyabhijñā).® He also describes them, in the manner of what must be cognitively refined in the śākta upāya, as impure (aśuddha).™“

Now I direct the reader to the two passages above where Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta state that the recognitions of ordinary experience are Śakti. Both passages also assert that the recognition that is Śakti appears as the Self rather than a separate “this.” These passages are expressing the insight of the middle level of Pure Wisdom about the reality informing the lower level of fragmented experience. That is, they are endeavoring to bring about the purification of conceptualization. This endeavor is thematized by Abhinavagupta. In the same section where he talks about the impurity of ordinary conceptually constructed experience, he states:

Thus the \[lower\] condition of conceptual construction \[vikalpa\] should be understood in the synthesis \[anusamdhāna\] of the pot, etc., \[expressed\] “This is that pot.” However, in these levels of synthesis \[anusamdhāna\], the Wisdom Power \[vidyāśakti, a.k.a., śuddhavidyā, Pure Wisdom\] flashes strongly, like lightning. Thus the gurus admit them to be first means to the Supreme State.”

Using the body as an example of a differentiated object, Abhinava states:

If, cognizing the all-around perfection \[sarvatah pūrnatvam\], i.e., the lack of separation, of the body, etc., one could perform the synthesis \[anusamdhāna\] “l am this,” he would be at the level! of Sadāśiva.%

Abhinava later makes the rhetorically marvelous statement:

The means \[upāya\] to be depended upon to know the nonduality which is Ultimate Reality is nothing but cognizing the nondiversity in the diversity of the manifestation of a pot.”

We may now understand the operation of the Pratyabhijñā system—an inference working through revealing Śakti in transcendental inquiry, and a purification of conceptualization—as a rediscovery and reintegration of the self-recognition fragmented in ordinary linguistic ascription. Because the Pratyabhijñā theory of recognition so fully

*[p. 98]* ^pg118

encodes the Śaiva cosmogonic myth, the inquiries that disclose it become a tantric ritual that bestows salvation."

These considerations lead us further back to the Śaivas’ overarching theological negations of methodology that we considered in chapter 3. I there explained the negations on the bases of the conceptions of grace and self-luminosity. I also expressed the view that Abhinava articulates the same insight in his conception of the anupāya or “nonmeans” of spiritual realization in his Tantrāloka and Tantrasāra. In his discussion of the nonmeans, Abhinava propounds a conception more fundamental than but homologous to that of the identity of inferential reason and conclusion in the Pratyabhijñā. This is the denial of the ultimate validity of any relation between a distinct spiritual means

(upāya) and goal (upeya):

The relation of means \[upāya\] and goal \[upeya\] is an illusion of grossness of cognition. Ìt is the Action Sakti which is the cause of both bondage and liberation.”

What use is there with reasonings regarding the self-luminous principle of consciousness \[samvittattva\]? ... All means \[upāya\], external and internal, depend upon it. How could they be means \[upāya\] regarding it? . . . \[Objects of different kinds of experience, such as\] blue, yellow and pleasure are only awareness \[prakāśa\], that is, Siva. Since there is \[really only\] this supreme nonduality which has the nature of awareness \[prakāśa\], what relation of means \[{upāya\] and goal \[upeya\] could there be which is other than it? For that \[relation of means and goal\] is only awareness \[prakāsa\].®

It is again the Lord's omnipotence and self-luminous unity that preclude all relationships of distinct means and the goal. This general conception of practical theology is exemplified in the relation of steps in the Pratyabhijñā inference. In negative modus-operandi language, the Pratyabhijñā system only removes the obstacles that obscure the ever-existent fact that everything is one’s self-recognition as Śiva.

This practical theological understanding may be yet further situated intellectually by some abstract considerations about the relation of Śiva’s emanation to the return to Him. The rediscovery of self-recognition through the Pratyabhijñā system is cast by the thinkers in a manner that may be described as teleological. I recall the section treated in chapter 3 where Abhinava describes the scope of the Pratyabhijñā system as the comprehension (avāpti) of all the features of internal and external experience.® Abhinava here describes this

*[p. 99]* ^pg119

comprehension as a “growth of recognitive judgment” (vimarsarūdhi).® He then cites a verse from Utpaladeva’s Ajadapramātrsiddhi that makes the point clearly. This is perhaps the most frequently cited verse throughout Abhinava’s commentaries. Utpaladeva says:

The accomplishment of the purpose \[krtārthată\] of the separated recognitive judgement \[vimarśa\] “this“”—is the recognitive judgement \[vimarśa} of rest \[viśrānti\] in its own essential nature \[expressed\] “I am He.”®

The early-twentieth-century Sanskrit commentator Harabhatta Shastri elaborates:

The insentient object of recognitive judgement \[vimrśya\] is separated \[from the Self, as is expressed\] “this.” The accomplishment of its purpose \[krtārthatā\], is characterized by rest \[viśrānti\] in its essential nature, which is consciousness. Because of the identity \[of that separated judgment “this”\] with the illumination of consciousness \[caitanyaprakāśa\], \[the accomplishment of its purpose\] has the nature of the self-recognition \[ahampratyavamarsātmā\] of the living being \[expressed\] “I who appear as the marvelous diversity of various things am He.” On this basis, the insentient thing, by rest \[viśrānti\] in I-hood \[ahambhāva\], by unification with the subject \[pramātr\] who has the essential nature of the recognizer \[vimrśtr}—attains noninsentience \[ajadatva\]. Noninsentients really have the unitary essential nature of the Supreme Subject \[parapramātr\]. Thus only the nondual, noninsentient Subject appears as perfectly complete \[pāripūrnyena\] everywhere. This is the

purport.“

Shastri’s passage is notable for elucidating that Utpaladeva’s expression of the soteriological recognition (“I am He”) comprehends the structure we know as the application (upanaya) step of the Pratyabhijñā inference (“I myself who appear as the marvelous diversity of various things am He”). The philosophical arguments, as has been explained, are the means for the fruition /return of phenomena in the Lord’s self-

recognition.

Now the progress of phenomena toward self-recognition is nothing but a clarification of their ultimate nature as self-recognition. Cosmogony and teleology are the same. Abhinavagupta clearly reflects upon such a conception. He compares the recognition constituting ordinary experience to a point of rest in a paradoxical journey between the identical

origin and goal of Śiva’s self-recognition:

*[p. 100]* ^pg120

That which is called recognitive judgement \[parāmarśa\] is the absolutely final and true \[pāryantikam eva pāramārthikam) place of rest \[viśrāntisthānam\]; and it only has the form “l.” In traveling to a village, the intermediate point of rest \[madhyaviśrāntipadam\] at the root of a tree, is explained to be created as expectant of that \[final point of rest\]. ... Thus also blue, etc., existing in the intermediate recognitive judgement \[parāmarśa\] as “This is blue,” are established to consist of the Self. For they rest upon the root rec-

ognitive judgement \[parāmarśa\] “1.”\*

This idea of ordinary experience as a midpoint between an identical origin and goal should not be confused with the midpoint of the Trika cosmic triad, the location of Pure Wisdom. As has been explained at length, the Pure Wisdom that animates the Pratyabhijñā system is located at a stage of the homologous emanation and return. It is located at the midpoint between origin/goal and ordinary experience—as it were, a midpoint to the midpoint.

The Śaivas’ identification of the origin of the cosmos, the spiritual means, and the soteriological goal attained by such means, should be seen as exemplifying in an intensified form a generic accordance between people's basic interpretations of the world and their behavior. All presuppositions about truth, fact and value can be understood as describing noncontingent “given” conditions about the world, whether or not they are traced all the way back to the cosmogony. Human activities, including of course the goals of such activities, get their very sense from these conditions.

I am not attempting here to address the question of which comes first, interpretations or actions—myths or rituals—but only affirming how the former identifies the world within which the latter operate. This is the basic insight articulated by Clifford Geertz in terms of worldview and ethos.” This is also the notion emphasized—perhaps too strongly for a universal prima facie description, but it applies here—by Mircea Eliade’s characterizations of ritual, that is, the telos of ritual, as the reenactment of the cosmogony.® Inasmuch as what I have called assumptions about noncontingent states of affairs or foundations are unavoidable—even a cultural relativist engages in intellectual practices the nature.and purpose of which are defined in terms of the nonrelativist world of values (usually democratic), plurality and nonepistemic causes he or she takes to be the case.

This basic accordance between belief and practice may be elaborated or intensified in a variety of ways. The very process of systematic theological or philosophical reflection will make it more explicit.

*[p. 101]* ^pg121

Justificatory foundations and justified practices and goals are tied together by discourse itself. The accordance will be even more pronounced in transcendental or metaphysical arguments that attempt to elucidate what necessarily underlies, or in a certain way is recapitulated, in all experience, beliefs, and actions.

The more a system of beliefs reduces the number of foundations, and thus strengthens their explanatory power, there will also be a strengthening of these foundations’ accordance with human practices. The Saiva conception of deity, uniting a fully monistic ontology with theistic omnipotence, is unusually encompassing.’ Classic monotheism, especially in its metaphysical formulations, often approaches a monistic world-explanation.” Jesus is both cosmogonic Alpha and the

eschatalogical Omega of all human activity.

The philosophical theology of logos exhibits this heightened accordance with the variety of “epistemic practices.” All human acts of knowing are understood to come, in Platonic terms, from anamnesis/
participation in the light of the Ideas contained in the divine mind; or in Aristotelian terms, from a movement from potentiality toward the actuality of the divine intellect.” In salvation, the human recapitulation of the divine act is most complete. As Augustine puts it, the human image of the trinity in Memory, Intelligence, and Will comes closest in likeness to the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost when redirected toward God through grace.” The Śaivas do not merely “simulate and participate.” For them, spiritual practice along with all other human experience and behavior are disclosed as nothing but the divine act of

cosmogonic self-recognition.”

These general and specific observations about the accordance between belief and practice are all relevant to the hermeneutic circularity of the practice of philosophical inquiry. As I emphasized in chapter 1, however much a philosopher's views may change, he or she must always begin and end in a set of assumptions that are his or her own. And, despite great areas of consensus or overlap between different schools of thought, the methodology of specific instances of philosophical practice will be inseparable from these positions.

This basic accordance/circularity will be even more pronounced in cases of the conservative intellectual practice of apologetics for one or another tradition. Even granting the soundness of the positions defended, the fairness and cogency of the reasoning, and the new discoveries to which it leads, the philosopher knows in advance the conclusions he or she is attempting to reach. Unless the apologetics is abandoned, the philosopher will strive to maintain a basic continuity

*[p. 102]* ^pg122

between the original positions and the new discoveries adduced. The Śaiva effort to make a spiritual practice out of transcendental argument, homologizing the relation of inferential reason and conclusion to the unifications inherent in their tantric myth and ritual, represents a particularly radical effort to transfigure the whole enterprise of

philosophical discourse.

From the point of view of our own critical reflection, the identity of reason and conclusion found in the Pratyabhijñā inference may seem already to admit a vitiating circularity. It would thus be a blatant example of the “closure” of metaphysics. However, the circularity is not vitiating. The soteriology is not presumed from the extratraditional perspective, but is supposed to be demonstrated through publicly assessable arguments. Indeed, these arguments, attempting to find mediation for the Śaiva positions against the radically divergent views of Buddhist skepticism, required an enormous amount of hard “methodologically detached” and creative thinking. In effect the transcendental inquiries that Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta developed provide “reasons for the reason” that is Śakti/self-recognition.” Such a cognitive advance is what distinguishes hermeneutic from vitiating

circularity.”

It is only within the intratraditional perspective that the Pratya-bhijñā does not do anything. The new Śaiva philosophy, with all its technical procedure of justification, is a path of return in a circular

journey that never really departs.

Reconciling Unity and Multiplicity

One other issue must be considered. There were two phases of the Buddhists’ attacks against the Śaivas. The first was the denial of recognition as the establishing means (sādhaka pramāṇa) for the Śaiva soteriology. We have so far in this chapter considered how the Śaivas attempt to subvert this criticism with their theory of recognition. Second, the Buddhists attempted to adduce repudiating means (bādhaka pramānṇa) against recognition. These consisted of reductio ad absurdum arguments, alleging the impossibility of unifying a multiplicity.

The thinkers deal with this phase of the Buddhist arguments in a manner that is more occasional and less systematic than the other. Many of their more substantial comments regarding it are made in the section on action, which, as we shall see, deals with the ontological issues of the nature and structure of what exists. It should be obvious that the logical possibility of the coexistence of unity and multiplicity

*[p. 103]* ^pg123

is important to a consideration of the structure of things. At the beginning of the section on action, Abhinava discusses the challenge of the Buddhists’ reductios. Reflecting summarily about all the philosophical arguments with the Buddhists, he states: “In all of the prima facie view, this is the crux: How can a multiplicity be unitary?’76

I will present some of the Śaivas’ approaches to dealing with this problem in my own order. The fundamental strategy by which one avoids contradiction is distinguishing the applications of contradictory attributes. While it is impossible that something is botha and nota in the same aspect, it is possible that it is both in different aspects. The Śaivas do resort to this strategy of claiming that there is a differentiation of the spheres (viṣayabheda) of unity and multiplicity.” In the second chapter on action, Abhinavagupta situates an assertion of Utpaladeva to this effect by formulating the following objection of an

opponent:

There may be this doubt: Unity and multiplicity are mutually contradictory. How can they exist in a single thing? Thus there is a way of destroying the soundness \[of a unity of multiplicity\]

with a repudiating means of cognition \[bādhaka pramāna\].”

Utpaladeva’s verse answers:

The internal reality \[tattvam\] in that is unitary. That very \[internal reality\], upon obtaining the status of the objects of the senses, reaches the condition of the multiplicity of places, times and essential natures.”

In the respect in which everything is internal and ultimately identical with Śiva, it is one; in the respect that it is emanated, it is many.® The Śaivas attempt to extend this understanding of a differentia-

tion of spheres to cover cases such as universals, substances, wholes, and so on, which apparently have external unity as well as multiplicity. For example, one recognizes a group of external particulars with the single word “cows,” or recognizes the body of Caitra as unitary amidst changes of place, time, and form.® Such categories are the objects of the mental constructions (kalpanā) fragmented from the Lord’s self-recognition in the manner explained in the body of this chapter. For the Saivas these syntheses of external multiplicity, as engaged in by the subject, ultimately derive their unity from their internal source.®?

What seems to me to complicate this explanation is that the Śaivas do not want to say that the ostensibly external unities-of-multiplicity

*[p. 104]* ^pg124

are illusory. The Śaiva tantrism is concerned not with a denial but with a transfiguration of experience as Śakti.® For the Śaivas, whatever is experienced is real; the only exceptions to this reality are things that appear, and are then sublated by later experience, such as perceptual illusions. Abhinava explains the case of the recognition of action in a unitary and multiple substratum:

The body of Caitra appears as multiple, that is, as differentiated with respect to various places, times and forms. However, it does not at all lose its unity, as is expressed “This indeed is he.” That very \[body of Caitra\] is an object“ having a nature which is both single and multiple. It is the same with action. Because there is a manifestation of it, it is real. Although \[illusions such as there being) two moons, etc., also appear, they are unreal. For at a later time their soundness, that is, the continuation of the operation of cognitive means \[pramāna\]) regarding them, is destroyed \[unmūülana\]. This \[later realization that there was an illusion could be expressed\] in a form such as “There are not two moons.” However, the recognitive judgement \[vimarśa\]) having the form “Caitra goes” persists and is not experienced to be sublated \[unmūlyamāna\] by

anything.”

I understand the difficulty as follows: It may be granted that experienced unity and multiplicity have different spheres. However, how can it be claimed that everything is one at the same time that multiplicity is real?\* Would not the problem of unity and multiplicity persist on a cosmic scale? If external unity-and-multiplicity is in some sense real, would not the logical problem remain there? On the other hand, perhaps there is a “synergistic” relation between the understanding of unity as the essence of multiplicity and the differentiation of spheres—together these ideas may account for what needs to be accounted for. Perhaps the differentiation of spheres avoids the contradiction as a provisional notion, which is comprehended in the understanding of everything as unitary. It is hard to come to a clear decision about these matters—perhaps because of the difficulty of conceiving of any sort of absolute unity!

Sometimes Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta make assertions to the effect that it is the special nature of the Lord that enables Him to unite multiplicity. Thus they claim that only pure Consciousness (svaccha/nirmala cit), as they understand it, could unite the diversity of action.” In the following passage, Abhinavagupta suggests that the Lord's power avoids the difficulty:

*[p. 105]* ^pg125

Recognitive judgement \[parāmarśa} is will \[icchā}\], which has the nature of the desire to act. Everything which is to be created exists as undifferentiated within that \[will\]. . . . This universe is nothing but \[the Lord's) own Self, is real \[satyarūpa\], ultimately has the nature of awareness \[prakāśa\], and is nothing but the unity of unbroken awareness \[prakāśa}\]. The Great Lord manifests it in a condition of differentiation; \[and this condition of differentiation\] itself is ultimately nothing but awareness \[prakāśa}. Thus His Lordship is explained to be agential freedom \[svātantrya\]. \[This agential freedom\] has the character of the accomplishment of that which is very difficult.®

This solution really is a deus ex machina! It would be circular to argue that the special nature of the Lord allows Him to resolve unity and multiplicity—unless there were other reasons to believe that there is such a Lord.

Another frequent strategy by which the Śaivas deal with the alleged contradictoriness of unity and diversity is analogy. Various instances of the apparent coexistence of unity and multiplicity in ordinary experience are adduced to support the possibility on a more cosmic scale. In the first passage quoted above where Abhinava epitomizes all the reductio arguments, he continues:

To this it has been replied: He, who has the essential nature of consciousness is like a mirror. \[In this way} there is the possibility of a diversity of appearances without contradicting His unity. What is the contradiction? Therefore, the Essential Nature of things,” though unitary through the force of recognition \[pratyabhijñā\], accommodates within Himself contrary divisions of His essential nature.”

Besides reflection, other analogies used to lessen the paradox of the one and the many are a painting on a wall; the unitary vista from the top of a mountain; the movements of a dancer; and the relationships between waves, rivers, and the ocean.”

Like the assertions of the Lord’s special nature, these uses of analogy are not sufficient to answer the Buddhists’ logical objections. The Buddhists have challenged the possibility of a unity of multiplicity not only at the cosmic level, but as comprising all the features of ordinary experience. It begs the question to have recourse to ordinary experience when that is included in what is in dispute. However, granting a realist view, the analogies do support the Saivas’ theology.

*[p. 106]* ^pg126

The possibility of a monistic Lord emanating the universe would be strengthened by instances of unity-and-diversity familiar to everyone.

The fact that the Śaivas do not focus on the Buddhist reductios in the same consistent, systematic manner as they focus on the recognitive interpretation of experience per se suggests that they had difficulty with them. I have expressed the opinion that their recourse to a differentiation of the spheres of unity and multiplicity is not definitely unsatisfactory, although it is opaque. The arguments considered do not appear able to remove all of the logical doubts suggested by the Buddhists.

However, I believe that all three classes of argument have a secondary status in relation to the chief Śaiva theory of the transcendental recognition of the Supreme Lord. If it is compelling to believe that all experience is constituted from a primordial and personal unity as understood by the Śaivas—then their suggestions would be helpful in working out some of the likely consequences. The weight the Śaivas give to working out their theory of recognition in relation to considering the reductios suggests that they may have understood the situation in the same way.” Perhaps difficult logical puzzles or paradoxes are inevitable in any grand account of the world, and other factors can dictate which ones it is best to live with.

As I have already stated, I believe that the success of the Śaiva demonstration of a universal rational foundation for their soteriology depends upon the validity of their implementations of their paradigm of recognition in the areas of epistemology and ontology, to be considered in the next two chapters. The next chapter will examine the Śaivas’ treatments of three important epistemological topics—cognition, memory, and conceptual exclusion. The success of the Pratyabhijñā system in the arena of epistemology depends upon the reduction of these experiential processes and their ostensible objects to modalities of the Lord’s self-recognition.
