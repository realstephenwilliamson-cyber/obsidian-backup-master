The way things were passed on is very complex. The most varied players were involved and India was transmitted not only via the scholarly discourse (“Orientalists” and Indology), but also via popular religious meaning-worlds, as is demonstrated by the Nāda-Brahman. Not only the Hindu and Yoga master Sivandanda was involved, but also the Indian Sufi Inayat Khan, the Radha Soami masters influenced by Sikhism, the Theosophists interested in the occult, the German jazz historian and Indian music lover Berendt, a generation of jazz musicians, and today also individual self-presentations on the Internet. What remains the common denominator, despite all the differences, is the interest in a universal myth that embeds the individual in a more comprehensive whole and allows it to participate in this whole. The Indian trigger for this world-view construction and the forms of habitus associated with it is obvious. According to one of the main theses of this book the unique feature of Sanskrit Hinduism was that over the centuries, despite literacy, India held fast to orality and a marked culture of performance. Out of these special framing conditions of a “sonic culture” there developed a habitus of “third space” that generated its very own forms of everyday religious practice, symbol production and intellectuals’ cultures and fostered the formation of a world-view that did not play myth and logos off against each other. It was the formation of a world-view that brought about ever new variations of “universal myths” tying a strongly participatory world-orientation into a rational discourse. In the modern period this world-view formation was apparently especially appealing to Western people. The increasing familiarity with India in the nineteenth century was, according to one thesis in the Nāda chapter, the initial trigger for modern spirituality in Western societies. 

The impact of British rule in India, and the transformation processes that occurred during the colonial and post-colonial period, have been well researched in the past two decades. The more recent research correctly emphasized the two-sided, strongly interactional processes and construction conditions in the colonial period, which generated new formations, in particular a spiritualization and universalization of Hinduism and the interface between religion and nation. However, to date not much consideration has been given to just how strongly this period also affected modern Western spirituality. To put it in clear terms, during the modern period Indian religiosity has changed less than European and American religiosity. The beginnings of a new globalized spirituality in Western countries go back to the second half of the nineteenth and the early twentieth centuries. In the 1960s and 1970s they were significantly intensified both quantitatively and qualitatively, being enhanced by many personal encounters with India and Indian

In the course of the successful spread of New Age since the 1980s, stimuli from India have already been so habitualized, adapted, transformed, made one’s own and mixed with other cultures that their origin can no longer be recognized as Indian. What has become clear in academic study since the 1990s, is that New Age-like spirituality was not a passing phase of youth culture, and continued to exert its influence even after the name had gone out of fashion again. At the beginning of the twenty-first century it must be admitted (nolens volens for some) that this is a phenomenon to be taken seriously. It has established itself almost unnoticed alongside and even within the traditional religions and has become normal. 

In modern Western spirituality the process of rationalization and disenchantment, which Max Weber noted in Western culture, has clearly been broken, reversed and actually inverted. An extreme change in world-view and habitus has taken place. Becoming familiar with Indian religiosity was, we opine, actually one of the reasons behind this. The qualitative and quantitative extension of the religious field by getting to know Asian religions (and precisely Sanskrit Hinduism and its modernized forms) contributed significantly to this change in habitus. Our basic argument was simple: India was so interesting because it showed that things could be done differently than at home. Getting to know this culture triggered, as it were, light-bulb effects: “Oh I see, it can be done that way as well.” This connects the very different players Berendt, Kurt and Messiaen (discussed in the Nāda chapter) all of whom, through cultural contact, and specifically by learning about classical Indian modal music, gained a new insight into music and music-making, assimilated the new influences creatively, drawing their own consequences. In terms of religiosity, this means that familiarization with the Asian religious cultures, especially Hindu religion(s) (with which the greatest amount of contact took place in the nineteenth century and the 1960s and 1970s) enlarged not only the spectrum of religious choices and options, but also changed and expanded one’s view of what could be regarded as religion at all. One of the most important stimuli and consequences was that the span of transcendency shifted and one recognized that the boundaries between the sacred and the profane were not god-given and fixed.

The theses should be seen rather as hypotheses that would need more scrutiny and sound foundation in further research. 2\) Getting to know India expanded the range of religious choices and the view of what was one’s own. One of the most important effects was to give one’s “own other” a new valency. Not least, India’s impact made it possible for things that had so far not been dominant and part of the subculture to come to the surface and gain a huge amount of ground. This means that the Indian inspiration was not only used to make what was “other” into one’s own, but also very often served to rediscover the buried, alternative, deviant and non-dominant “own” aspects and revaluate them. For example, in Antiquity and since the Renaissance European esotericism was indeed a more or less powerful, underground movement, but not the dominant culture, right up to the modern age. Ideas of pantheism had been around since Neoplatonism, but they did not have the same culture-shaping power as in India. In Christian societies there had always been subject-centered religion and alternative religious popular cultures that did not correspond with the official religion (one only has to consider the “free spirits” (“Brüder und Schwestern im freien Geiste”) and beghards in the Middle Ages), but the strong church structures prevented them from flourishing. Religious mixtures were already woven into folk Catholicism, but often they were theologically not quite acceptable and much in the folk religion had an air of “superstition” about it. The discovery of India enlarged the discursive framework for perceiving and evaluating one’s “own other.” India provided theologies for aspects that had thus far been scorned as inferior and deviant, with the result that — at least for a substantial segment of Western population — the subculture lost its stigma, became socially acceptable and could move from the periphery to the center. The highly developed culture of India offered schemata for giving greater value to mysticism (which had always been a very subjective form of religion), revaluating so-called superstition, allowing magic to appear unproblematic, shifting the scope of transcendency, and redrawing the boundaries between soul and body, sacred and profane. In short, it gave way to establishing alternative models to the Christian dogmatic and ecclesiastical demarcations.

5\) New cultural processes of providing meaning and new sign productions (“neue kulturelle Sinngebungsprozesse und Zeichenproduk-tionen”) always run along the axis of selection and combination. 6\. Sensual Substance: Nāda-Brahman 

20\. Kriti to the dancing Śiva (Natarāja) of Cidambaram Composition by Muttusvami Diksitar Singer: Nandakumar, Mysore (Recording A. Wilke, copyright Nandakumar) 

Typical Carnatic (Southern Indian) classical music (see chapter 6.3.4, Devotional 

Nāda-Yoga in South Indian Classical Music), in which the line Cidambareśvaram cintayāāmi, “I commemorate the Lord of Cidambaram” is repeated in an artistic style of singing. This is an example of a kriti, the most highly developed form of devotional composition. One feature typical of a Kriti is that text and music are composed together. Unlike poems such as the Saundarya-Lahari, kritis are complex compositions in which the sound of the language is part of the composition. As in the hymn to Mahiṣṣṣāsuramardinī, vowel and consonant sounds, alliterations, etc. are not understood as independent text ornamentation that is later set to music, but are themselves musical content. Text and melody must blend into one other completely. Ideally the impression should arise that these particular words could only be sung to this particular melody. Most Kriti composers wrote in various languages; this composition is in Sanskrit. It is typical of classical Indian music that a Rāga is developed quite slowly — in Southern India this is done “in terraces” like the construction of a temple. This occurs in the करव (“starting to sing”), in which the singer or instrumentalist elaborates the melodic material of the Rāga from the fundamental sound, but does not give it rhythm. Instead of the Tāmpūra discussed in chapter 6.3.9 (The Tāmpūra as Total Representation of the Nāda-Brahman), the singer has two fundamental tones on his “Śruti box,” which replace the Tāmpūra.
