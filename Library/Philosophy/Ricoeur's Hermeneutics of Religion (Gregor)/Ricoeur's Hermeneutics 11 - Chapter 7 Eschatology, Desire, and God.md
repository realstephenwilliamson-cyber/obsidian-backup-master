## Chapter 7

## Eschatology, Desire, and God

REVELATION AS NONVIOLENT APPEAL

In the last chapter we saw that Ricoeur tries to develop, at the levels of both ethics and religion, a model of the call and response that maintains the integrity of both the self and the other. The self is summoned by a call that is genuinely other than itself, and yet it also has certain capacities to hear and respond to this call. Ricoeur is looking for a model of revelation—in both an ethical and religious sense—that is transcendent and disruptive, but which does not violate the integrity of the capable human being.

This is a long-standing concern in Ricoeur’s work. We saw the continuity from Ricoeur’s early account of transcendence and immanence to his later account of the call and response. One waystation on this journey is Ricoeur’s essay from the mid-1970s on the “Hermeneutics of the Idea of Revelation, where he advances a model of revelation as a “non-violent appeal.” Revelation is a call, a summons from that which is other, but this appeal is not a violation of the self. It is not an authoritarian, heteronomous, rationally inscrutable event—what Bonhoeffer refers to as a “positivism of revelation”<sup>1</sup>—that obliterates the capacities of reason and demands a *sacrificium intellectus* (H 132). Nor is revelation a heteronomous demand on the will. Prior to commanding the will, revelation appeals to the *imagination* as it opens new existential possibilities (HIR 37), and while revelation does call for obedience, it only does so within the context of the awakening of *desire*.

All of this might lead us to suspect that Ricoeur is trying to protect human autonomy against the heteronomy of revelation. This suspicion might even seem well-founded, since Ricoeur models so much of his hermeneutics of religion after Spinoza, Kant, and Hegel. These are three of the most influential modern philosophers of religion, and their interpretations of religion are 

*[p. 146]* ^pg163

definitively modern insofar as they are crafted to maintain human autonomy against the claims of religion.<sup>2</sup> Some of their influence does come through in Ricoeur, who similarly wants to give a critical appropriation of religious traditions and texts. This is evident in Ricoeur’s reading of Spinoza, Kant, and Hegel, but also Marx, Nietzsche and Freud, as well as Barth, Bultmann, and Bonhoeffer. Ricoeur’s interpretation of religion makes major concessions to the modern critical mindset and its “available believable”—as is evident in his demythologizing interpretation of such Christian beliefs as the resurrection, judgment, and justification.<sup>3</sup> But Ricoeur also challenges the strictly modern, secularizing forms of autonomy, arguing that revelation undermines the idea of a self-positing, self-mediating cogito. The text has priority over the activity of the thinking subject, which presumes to be “its own master and transparent to itself” (H 112). The self is not the origin of its own meaning. Instead, self-understanding is mediated by the meaning revealed in the (biblical) text.

The autonomous self undergoes a hermeneutical conversion to become disciple of the text. If revelation does not violate the self, it does undermine the pretense of the self to be radically self-positing and autonomous. The faithful self is dependent on revelation for its self-understanding, but this is a “dependence without heteronomy” (H 152). What Ricoeur proposes, then, is to overcome the opposition between heteronomous revelation and autonomous reason, and to develop a model that preserves the integrity of both revelation and reason in a “living dialectic.” Between heteronomy and autonomy Ricoeur catches sight of a third possibility, which he calls *theonomy*. Unlike heteronomy, theonomy is not the opposite of autonomy but is rather an external source that makes freedom and even autonomy possible. Theonomy takes autonomy to its limits, revealing an autonomy that surrenders the illusion of self-sufficiency (Ta/oA 290). In sum, revelation establishes *a dependence without heteronomy,* but at the same time, *an autonomy without self-sufficiency*. Theonomy preserves the transcendence of revelation without obliterating the self, and it preserves human capacities of reason, conscience, imagination, and desire without falsely exalting the self.

### Theonomy and Desire: The Case of Abraham

By making autonomy relative to the revelatory word, Ricoeur breaks with Kant, who maintains that religious symbols must not conflict with moral autonomy. In Kant’s view, the religious symbol exists for the sake of moral regeneration, and if a religious claim or command conflicts with the moral law, it is clearly illegitimate. Consider, for instance, Kant’s interpretation of the *Akedah*—that is, Abraham’s binding of Isaac. God performs a divine 

*[p. 147]* ^pg164

speech act, commanding Abraham to sacrifice Isaac. Surely this is a good candidate for violent revelation, if ever there was one. God commands the unthinkable: the violent sacrifice of Isaac. Kant concludes that in such a case, one could safely conclude that the command is not of divine origin. Kant’s pure moral religion offers a clear negative criterion: “If something is represented as commanded by God in a direct manifestation of him yet is directly in conflict with morality, it cannot be a divine miracle despite every appearance of being one (e.g., if a father were ordered to kill his son who, so far as he knows, is totally innocent)” (RBMR 124). Moreover, such claims are tainted with the possibility of error, and the small chance that there is no error is outweighed by the certainty that it would be wrong to violate this human duty (RMBM 204).<sup>4</sup>

In the case of Abraham, Kant’s rationalist hermeneutics of religion articulates something that seems like common sense: Don’t kill your children. And yet this biblical narrative also seems to contain a level of meaning that cannot or should not simply be dismissed by offended reason.<sup>5</sup> But how does one think this scandalous narrative? In chapter 4 we saw that Ricoeur goes beyond Kant’s pure philosophy of religion with his category of testimony (*témoignage*), a category that is important for Ricoeur’s reading of the Akedah. A singular event like Abraham’s obedience cannot be justified according to general laws, or as an example of a general rule, or as a symbol, which is still close to ideality. Instead, this singular event must be understood in terms of testimony, which is attuned to the contingent and particular (CI 343, RSV 32–33).

In the case of Abraham and Isaac, the testimony is located within the larger context of God’s covenant with Abraham. As Ricoeur observes, Abraham obeyed twice: Prior to obeying the call to sacrifice Isaac, he obeyed the call to depart from his homeland. Ricoeur describes this call as a divine event—the pure event of the kerygma. It is entirely unexpected. It calls Abraham into an obligation to obey. And yet obligation and obedience are not the primary concerns of this call. Instead, the call is primarily related to Abraham’s *desire*. Abraham’s obedience was not merely a “sacralization of moral obligation.” His obedience was not an act of the naked will submitting to a heteronomous command, because it awoke in him the desire for “a better country,” as the Epistle to the Hebrews puts it (CI 343). The call was first addressed not to Abraham’s will but to his desire, which was grounded in God’s covenant promise to make him into a great nation, to bless him, and through him to bless the whole world ([[Torah 03 - Lech Lecha#^12-2|Gen. 12:2–3]]). Likewise, God commanded Abraham to sacrifice Isaac, but this command arises within the context of God’s earlier covenant to make Abraham’s descendants as numerous as the stars, and to give them the Promised Land (Gen. 15 and 17). This point is vital to Ricoeur’s model of revelation as nonviolent appeal. 

*[p. 148]* ^pg165

The revelation—God calling Abraham out of his homeland—is not a blunt heteronomous demand for obedience. The command calls for obedience out of the awakening of desire. Moreover, this desire is given form through the imagination, which is charged with new existential possibilities by revelation (HIR 37). The call of revelation does require obedience, but only within the context of a quickened imagination and awakened desire.

Having said all that, the binding of Isaac still looks like a case of the purest heteronomy, with God commanding unthinkable violence just to show he can. The meaning of the narrative, however, is not simply God the bully showing Abraham who is boss. Echoing Kierkegaard, Ricoeur reads Abraham’s “absurd obedience” (CI 343) as the suspension rather than simple abolition of the ethical. The binding of Isaac is the very “model of the destabilizing exception to the routine of duty.” The universal prohibition against murder is relativized by the absolute relation to God. As Kierkegaard shows, the point of the narrative is not that God can do whatever he wants because he is God. Rather, the point is that the immanent orders of social ethics—particularly Hegelian *Sittlichkeit*—are not the highest claim on a self. In short, that the ethical is not God.<sup>6</sup>

In teleologically suspending the ethical, the exception of Abraham reveals something positive—namely, the “supraethical dimension of love.” Abraham is justified by his love of God, and his exceptional case “can be held as revelatory of the *fundamentally excessive* character of love as over against justice” (Ta/oA 290; TB 124–25). The logic of superabundance governs this paradoxical narrative. By drawing attention to the particular, to the exception, this narrative aids in the education of justice by “the economy of the gift” and hospitality toward those often overlooked by a community’s abstract principles of justice: “the widow, the orphan, and the stranger at the gates” (TB 127, 129). Rather than advocating a violent nominalism in which a divine command legitimates murder, the Abraham narrative reminds justice of “the singularity and nonsubstitutability of persons” (TB 131).

Here Ricoeur might take another point from Kierkegaard regarding the supraethical dimension of love. What is essential is not merely Abraham’s love for God, but also his love for Isaac. As Johannes de Silentio argues in *Fear and Trembling*, Abraham is not to cease loving Isaac, but to love him all the more. “The absolute duty can lead one to do what ethics would forbid, but it can never lead the knight of faith to stop loving.” From an ethical perspective Abraham’s act can only appear as hatred, but if he does in fact hate Isaac, Abraham can be sure that God does not require this sacrifice. “He must love Isaac with his whole soul. Since God claims Isaac, he must, if possible, love him even more, and only then can he *sacrifice* him, for it is indeed this love for Isaac that makes his act a sacrifice by its paradoxical contrast to his love for God.”<sup>7</sup> Abraham’s love—for God and for Isaac—is the 

*[p. 149]* ^pg166

hermeneutical horizon in which God gives this ethically unintelligible command. On Ricoeur’s reading, this horizon means the command is theonomous rather than bluntly heteronomous.

Ricoeur also makes this point in *Thinking Biblically*, where he argues that Abraham’s response is not an “overly narrow” heteronomy, but rather a “loving obedience” (TB 117, 119). Abraham exhibits a “free consent to a properly unequal relation between a superior and an inferior” (TB 115). Once again Ricoeur characterizes this response in terms of theonomy, which is a “summons to a loving obedience.” This is not the pure Enlightenment ideal of autonomy, since the self is dependent on a prior, “antecedent” command that comes from God. But neither is this pure heteronomy, since it “proceeds from the love that is God,” who engenders rather than prevents autonomy. This autonomy is not self-governing, but is instead “understood as the summons to responsibility” (TB 133). To recall Ricoeur’s earlier phrase, it is a dependence without heteronomy and an autonomy without self-sufficiency.

The love in question is not a vague sentiment. It is articulated in God’s covenant with Abraham—a covenant between “a relational God” and “an equally relational human being.” This love is the context within which God issues this command. This covenant is marked by a fundamental inequality, as it is a relation between God and human, but there is nevertheless a certain reciprocity insofar as it involves “an exchange of *promises*.” For this reason, “the Other is not the Utterly Other, for he accompanies human beings’ history and establishes for them their position of standing ‘before God’” (Ta/oA 287). Ricoeur thus finds an I-Thou relation within the text, and a similar I-Thou relation in the giving of the Decalogue at Mt. Sinai. The law has a thoroughly relational character, grounded in the covenant. Likewise, for Abraham God is not the Wholly Other in the sense of an Unknown God who barges in to demand the unthinkable; this is the God who has made himself known to Abraham in the covenant. The covenant takes place in a shared history, and this is the context for God’s command to Isaac. Abraham’s obedience was absurd, unintelligible from the perspective of the universal, but within the context of the covenant and Abraham’s history with God it was justified—by faith. 

The Akedah is a limit case, testing the limits of Ricoeur’s interpretation of revelation as a nonviolent appeal, since it involves the prospect of a very literal violence against Isaac. Much more could and has been said about the Akedah, but our present concern is with the very nature of the command itself. In addition to the violence commanded by God, it might also seem that God utterly violates Abraham’s capacity for any sort of moral discernment or understanding. As Kierkegaard argues, Abraham becomes unintelligible to everyone but God. Would this sort of ethical isolation render Abraham a more coherent, capable self, or would it abolish his capacity for discernment? Would he ever be able to trust God again? Ricoeur’s point is that the meaning 

*[p. 150]* ^pg167

of the command is inextricable from the covenantal relation, one that engages Abraham’s imagination, desire, and reason.

By highlighting this covenantal horizon, Ricoeur provides a valuable supplement to Kierkegaard’s reading of the Akedah, which emphasizes the radical discontinuity of revelation. What Ricoeur’s reading seems to lack, however, is the sense of fear and trembling Kierkegaard draws out. This is not only a question for this particular reading of the text, but also for Ricoeur’s definition of revelation as a nonviolent appeal. The appeal seems to flow so smoothly from call to response, transcendence to immanence. Where Kierkegaard’s phenomenology of faith turns up the volume on the sense of scandal and offense, Ricoeur tends to turn that volume down. Ricoeur’s faithful self is just so *receptive*, the relation of call and response so irenic, it would be helpful to hear more about the hostility of autonomous reason toward that which threatens it.

The Akedah also tests the limits of Ricoeur’s interpretation of religion as restoring human capability. Is the capable human being the measure or ultimate goal of revelation? With Abraham the central theme seems to be covenant more than capability. God called Abraham and established a covenant with him for the sake of reconciling the world to himself—not simply to restore capability. If the narrative does suggest a concern with capability, it lies in God’s *Bildung*, or formation, of Abraham—not into a morally autonomous Kantian or a reasonable member of society, but into a deeper capacity to trust, and to love, beyond the limits of mere duty.

### Eschatological Desire and Desire for the Eschaton

We have seen that desire has a central role in ethics, and as we just saw with Abraham, desire also plays a central role in the life of religious faith. Desire is a prominent theme in Continental philosophy of religion, but this is not a new insight. In patristic and medieval Christian thought, desire plays a vital role in our orientation to God. Lactantius, for instance, argued that the Stoic ideal of *apatheia* would deprive us of all the affections that move the soul toward God. Likewise Augustine, for whom spiritual maturity is all about the right ordering of affections; desire and its frustrations are all about leading us to the goal of finding our enjoyment in God. Gregory of Nyssa describes the way God draws us to himself through an infinite desire that is never satiated, but instead always deepens as God reveals more and more of himself. Likewise Maximum the Confessor, who argues that desire “brings about an insatiable spiritual movement that drives us toward divine things . . .”<sup>8</sup> It is also worth noting that many of these thinkers do not oppose *eros* and *agapē*, 

*[p. 151]* ^pg168

but instead see them as interwoven and at times interchangeable<sup>9</sup>—a point Ricoeur makes in *Thinking Biblically* (TB 125).

Desire is also central to Aquinas’ account of the theological virtues. As the cardinal virtues enable the (imperfect) happiness that is proportionate to our nature, so the theological virtues direct us toward perfect happiness, which consists in the Beatific Vision of the divine essence. The theological virtues “direct us aright to God”; they are “infused in us by God alone”; and they are only known via divine revelation. And whereas the cardinal virtues can be acquired by our natural capacities through habituation, the theological virtues are only acquired by the power of divine grace. In Aquinas’ words, they are “entirely from without” \[*ST* I-II, 62, 3\]. This is a case of grace perfecting nature. As Aristotle observes in the opening of the *Metaphysics*, all human beings by nature desire to know, and according to Aquinas this natural desire finds its perfect fulfillment in the Beatific Vision. The contemplative vision of the divine essence is eminently desirable, since it is the perfect fulfillment of our nature.

This teleological account of the desire for God fits well within the anthropology and ethics of medieval Christianity, but one does not find this kind of teleological argument very often in Continental philosophy. One is more likely to hear something about man as a “useless passion” (Sartre). One thinker who does, however, argue in terms of a teleological orientation toward the divine is Kierkegaard. For Kierkegaard, the “absolute telos” of the human being is eternal happiness—that is, immortality, eternal life.<sup>10</sup> Moreover, desire plays an important role here. In relation to the absolute telos, passion should be raised to its highest point.<sup>11</sup> This passion, or desire, orients the self toward the highest realization of its humanity: eternal happiness.

This aspect of Kierkegaard’s thinking raises considerable suspicions. Isn’t this talk about desiring one’s own eternal happiness just a spiritualized narcissism? This is the complaint of Levinas, who objects that Kierkegaard’s concern with salvation is egoistic.<sup>12</sup> As one commentator put it, for Levinas the problem is that Kierkegaard wanted to go to heaven.<sup>13</sup> For Levinas, both true ethics and true religion entail renouncing my egoistic care for my own being, both in this life and in the life to come. True religion is a religion of responsibility for the other rather than a religion based on the desire to be, a religion without the *conatus*. 

This is not to say that there is no place for desire in Levinasian ethics. In *Totality and Infinity* he describes a distinctive mode of desire he calls *metaphysical*. In contrast to the ontological desire of the *conatus essendi*, striving to persist in its own being, metaphysical desire is a desire for the “*absolutely other*,” for “a land not of our birth, for a land foreign to every nature, which has not been our fatherland and to which we shall never betake ourselves.”<sup>14</sup> This is the desire of Abraham rather than Odysseus, who strives to close the 

*[p. 152]* ^pg169

circle by returning home. Like Abraham and Moses, the ethically responsible self never enters the Promised Land, but is instead reoriented toward the future, an “eschatology without hope” for itself.<sup>15</sup> 

Where Ricoeur describes Abraham’s desire as a chasm to be filled,<sup>16</sup> Levinas describes this desire as arising from abundance rather than lack. This strikes me as a better way to characterize the desire that lies at the origins of faith, hope, and love. Several philosophers and theologians have argued that the desire that orients us toward God is an *eschatological* desire—eschatological in the sense that this desire arises from the desire of the other. My desire of God is a response to being desired by God.<sup>17</sup> Eschatological desire arises from plenitude, not emptiness. It is not a lack, a need to be satisfied, an appetite to be satiated and then forgotten. Once a hunger is satiated it is forgotten, but eschatological desire does not abate. To quote Levinas again, “The Desired does not fulfill it, but deepens it.”<sup>18</sup>

Ricoeur describes something like eschatological desire in his commentary on the *Song of Songs*, where he describes “the play of desire itself, or rather of two desires intermingled.”<sup>19</sup> Similarly, he writes of the double meaning in the phrase “love of God,” which can be read as the love of God “addressed to” us, and our own love of God, which arises in loving obedience and response to God’s love. Ricoeur therefore proposes to write the phrase as “love (of) God” (TB 119).

There is a good image of this eschatological desire in the gospel of John, in Jesus’ conversation with the Samaritan woman at the well.<sup>20</sup> Jesus tells the woman that all who drink from that well will thirst again, but those who drink from the living water Jesus gives will never thirst again. The water at the well is a prime example of desire as lack, since it provides a temporary satisfaction to a biological need. Yet Jesus speaks of living water that will put an end to thirst. At first glance, this too sounds like desire as need or lack: Once the living water has satiated that spiritual need, the thirst or desire will cease. But notice what Jesus says: “The water that I will give will become in them a spring of water gushing up to eternal life” ([[John 04#^4-10|John 4:10]], [[John 04#^4-13|13–14]]). A spiritual thirst—need—is fulfilled, but this does not mean that desire ceases. A spring of water gushes up, endlessly—giving more and more water. An endless flow of undesired water is not a good thing. It is flooding, drowning. Yet Jesus invites the woman to a spring of water that gushes up to life. The need is extinguished, but the desire only deepens.

This account of desire also seems to me a good way to think about the conatus and its role in the development of the theological virtues of faith, hope, and love. These are not formed by habituation, but are infused through an act of divine grace—a grace that also initiates a new desire for being. Just as desire lies at the root of the ethical aim of living well with and for others, 

*[p. 153]* ^pg170

so desire lies at the root of the religious aim of glorifying God and enjoying him forever.<sup>21</sup>

It is therefore surprising that it is precisely at this point that Ricoeur turns deontological, abandoning his appeal to the conatus when talking about the desire for life beyond death. Instead of writing about our desire to be and effort to exist, he stresses the need to accept one’s own mortality as a condition of finite existence. There is an “infantile component of the desire for immortality,” which we must renounce to accept our own death (FS 261). Ricoeur then characterizes eternal life in very this-worldly terms. In one interview from 1969,<sup>22</sup> Ricoeur describes his conception of eternal life in terms of the fullness of the present moment—akin to the Kierkegaardian instant in which eternity touches time, as well as Bultmann’s idea of existential decision or Tillich’s idea of the “eternal now.”<sup>23</sup> 

One can appreciate Ricoeur’s resistance to an otherworldly understanding of eternal life, which can turn into a nihilistic denial of life in this world. This otherworldliness, along with the sentimental pictures of heaven that appear in popular spirituality, cries out for the hermeneutics of suspicion—but not only suspicion. We also need a hermeneutics of retrieval that returns to the texts and shows that this otherworldliness is also unbiblical. For instance, if we observe Jesus’ statements in the gospel of John, eternal life is connected to resurrection and the coming kingdom of God, not a nihilistic or gnostic escape from the body and the world. As Ricoeur observes, this neglect of the kingdom of God is one of the shortcomings of existential interpretations that focus on the interiority of the individual and ignore the full scope of eschatological hope, which is communal, political, and even cosmic. Ricoeur makes this point in his essay “Freedom in the Light of Hope,” observing that for Christianity, this eschatological hope is grounded in the Resurrection—both the empty tomb of Christ and the promise of the future resurrection of the body and the “recapitulation of all things” (Irenaeus) (CI 409). Ricoeur lays out these basic Christian beliefs in order to offer a philosophical approximation of the Christian idea of hope. Contra Hegel, the Resurrection does not set up a realized eschatology, but instead is given in the mode of hope, which “effects an irruption into a closed order” (CI 411). There is an “excess of meaning,” which gives rise to thinking but not a closed system of thought (CI 412). This appeal to limits is, the reader will remember, the basic point of Ricoeur’s post-Hegelian Kantianism. Ricoeur’s philosophical approximation of resurrection hope is deeply Kantian: It concerns the regeneration of the will bound by radical evil—a liberation that is not in our power, but is given through the power of religious symbols. There is also hope, an expectation, in the postulate of immortality and in the “practical possibility of the highest good,” in which “the just life and the happy life” coincide (CI 420–21).

*[p. 154]* ^pg171

For what, then, can we hope? That is one of Kant’s big four questions, and Kant allows the postulate of immortality. Yet, on this question Ricoeur becomes even more critical than Kant and moves closer to Levinas. In Ricoeur’s later reflections, collected in *Critique and Conviction* and *Living up to Death*, Ricoeur describes his search for a joyful, even “cheerful” process of “living to the end.” The joy in this process can only come on the far side of a process of renunciation, which he characterizes with Freud’s idea of mourning and Meister Eckhart’s idea of detachment. 

If “learning finally how to live” is to learn to die, to take into account accepting absolute mortality without salvation, resurrection, or redemption, I share all the negative here. I too, do not expect resurrection for myself . . . I yield my spirit to God *for the others.* (LD 85)

For Ricoeur, this renunciation means renouncing the *conatus essendi*, and likewise the concern with one’s own personal identity and personal resurrection (CC 156). This is what we see in Jesus, whose resurrection Ricoeur demythologizes so that the manner of Jesus’ self-giving death gives rise to resurrection in the spirit of the church community. Shades of Hegel and Bultmann. As a consequence, eschatological hope is now an ethical transfer to the others who will live after me (LD 42). “Let God, at my death, do with me as he wills. I demand nothing, I demand no ‘after.’ I cast upon others, my survivors, the task of taking up again my desire to be, my effort to exist, in the time of the living” (CC 158). And just as they may remember me, so Ricoeur hints at the possibility of being remembered by God (CC 161): 

Nothing is owed to me. I expect nothing for myself; I ask for nothing. I have renounced—I try to renounce!—claiming, demanding. I say: God, you will do as you will with me. Maybe nothing at all. I accept no longer being. (LD 44)

Ricoeur writes that he is struggling with himself, trying to learn how to live by learning how to die. This no longer seems like a mere philosophical approximation of hope, which would give a rational counterpart of the Christian symbol of resurrection. Ricoeur is re-interpreting the Christian kerygma here, so the question of what that kerygma really means is no longer an academic question that must respect the boundaries between philosophy and theology. Ricoeur is making a grand wager. But is this wager one of hope, or is it a wager of despair? Here I mean despair in the Kierkegaardian sense of a refusal of divine promise or hope: If God makes the promise of resurrection and the recapitulation of all things, then it seems presumptuous to declare that detachment is a better attitude than hope and trust.<sup>24</sup>

### The Religious Aim: Capability and Union with God

It is a strange irony that Ricoeur affirms the conatus against ethical formalism, but refuses it at this final stage of the hermeneutics of religion. Recall the passage we cited above: “The exclusion of desire from the sphere of ethics has had disastrous consequences,” insofar as it excludes the material concern with happiness and freedom (CI 453). One wonders if the consequences are equally disastrous for the hermeneutics of religion. Does the exclusion of the material concern for eternal happiness end up misconstruing what we might call the religious aim?

But wait—what is the religious aim, anyway? Ricoeur argues that the aim of religion is the regeneration of human capability. But is that the best hermeneutical framework for understanding religion?

Ricoeur adopts this framework from Kant, but he also expands the Kantian hermeneutic. Religion not only regenerates the will, liberating bound freedom, but also registers across all the fundamental capacities of the self—speech, action, narration, and imputation. As we have seen, this is why Kantian morality is too narrow a hermeneutic for understanding religion. It simply is not grounded deeply enough in concrete, particular religions. Instead, it is an interpretation of Christianity according to the *a priori* concepts of moral reason, leading to a purportedly pure moral religion that can be normative for interpreting and evaluating the phenomena of concrete religious faith. That is why Kant finds it so easy to conclude that religion is all about recognizing our moral duty as divinely commanded and becoming morally pleasing to God.

As I was writing this, a question occurred to me: What would it look like if Kant were to write a catechism for his pure moral religion? One question it might include is this: *What is the final end of all creation?* Kant’s answer to this question sounds good enough: The final end of all creation is, in his words, “the glory of God.” Kant sees that phrase as the “best expression” of the final end—*but* only if it is understood correctly: “For, nothing glorifies God more than what is most estimable in the world, respect for his command, observance of the holy duty that his law lays upon us, when there is added to this his magnificent plan of crowning such a beautiful order with corresponding happiness.”<sup>25</sup> This happiness, however, is only truly fitting when we are morally worthy of it. The *summum bonum* for which we hope is therefore to experience happiness proportionate to one’s moral worth. We can hope for divine blessedness as the final end of all things, in which our good ends ultimately succeed and coincide with happiness, but that hope has to remain hope rather than an object of theoretical knowledge. For Kant there is a real danger of religion becoming idolatrous with regard to beatitude or divine blessedness;<sup>26</sup> this blessedness is the appropriate moral disposition in relation to God, 

*[p. 156]* ^pg173

since it consists of fear and love of God. It is not, however, the final end of moral striving, but rather “a means of strengthening what in itself makes for a better human being \[i.e.\] virtuous disposition” (RBMR 201). Kantian religion is unable—or perhaps unwilling—to recognize the possibility that the final end and the highest good consists in just this—loving, and being loved by, God. Not loving as a strictly affective experience, but a cognitive one as well: knowing, and being known by, God. And not knowing as a merely intellectual relation, but a relation of personal communion. Yet, for Kant, all of this is secondary to the happiness that comes from having done one’s duty.

My question, then, is this: Despite his break with the narrow moralism of Kantian religion, is Ricoeur still limited by Kant’s orientation to the practical? Is the regeneration of human capability really the best hermeneutical lens for interpreting religion? To be sure, Ricoeur understands capability in a broader ethical sense, more akin to Spinoza than Kant, but what is missing from this hermeneutics is the desire of the self to know, and be united with, God. I am not speaking here of theoretical cognition, but of an existential, relational knowing of God—interpersonal communion. Robert Louis Wilken has a fitting phrase for this in the subtitle of his book *The Spirit of Early Christian Thought*: “seeking the face of God.”

For Christianity, the point of eternal life is not that the ego gets to go on living forever and ever—as though the everlasting persistence of being is itself the greatest good. In that regard Ricoeur is right to talk about the need to give up the conatus. Rather, the point of eternal life is that it consists in the relation to God—in a response to the desire of the Other, knowing and being known by God. From the Gospel of John: “And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent” ([[John 17#^17-3|John 17:3]]). As Augustine writes in the opening lines of the *Confessions*, our desire as human beings is to praise God. And God, who has created us for himself, stirs us to take pleasure in this praise. As Ricoeur himself puts it, the covenant is a relation between a relational human being and an equally relational God. Yet for some reason Ricoeur fails to see this as the focal point of eschatological hope.

I raise these questions not only on behalf of some personalist piety, but also for hermeneutical reasons. The desire to know, love, and be united with the divine is a vital feature of religious life in many diverse forms, both Eastern and Western. In Christianity it is described in terms of union, participation, adoption, *theosis,* and deification. As [[2 Peter 01#^1-4|2 Peter 1:4]] puts it, we become partakers of the divine nature. Ricoeur gets closer to this in *The Symbolism of Evil*, where he insists on holding together the juridical acquittal with the grafting into the true vine, and “the mystical immanence of life by the Spirit” (SE 278). But this seems to disappear from his later hermeneutics of religion with its focus on human capability. There is, however, room to develop this theme of 

*[p. 157]* ^pg174

capability in precisely this direction, to identify the capacity for union with God as one of the features of the self: the *homo capax* as *capax Dei*.

The question of a human capacity for the divine is, of course, a debated issue in the Christian tradition. Is there such thing as a natural capacity, a point of contact in the human being as such? Ricoeur’s own Reformed tradition has been critical of this idea, appealing to Calvin’s principle *finitum non capax infiniti*: the finite is not capable of the infinite. This stands in opposition to the Lutheran view that the finite is capable of the infinite—*finitum capax infiniti.* Karl Barth objected that the Lutheran view prepares the way for the Romantics and Idealists, who exploited this principle such that divine transcendence ends up collapsing into immanence. But it need not be a choice between a natural capacity or incapacity. This is why Dietrich Bonhoeffer argues that the finite is capable of the infinite—not in itself, but by the infinite.<sup>27</sup> Union with God is not simply an activation of our intrinsic capacities, but requires the gracious intervention of the divine.

Andrew Louth writes of something similar in the Orthodox tradition regarding the way theosis, or deification, reconstitutes our capacities. Theosis is a deification in which our humanity is not left behind but transformed, with its capacities reconstituted. As Louth writes, theosis is a “reconstitution of our humanity, a reshaping, a straightening out of all the distortions and corruptions that we have brought upon our humanity by misusing—abusing—our human capacities . . .”<sup>28</sup>

This is definitely more of a theological extension of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of the capable self, and it is not clear whether this is a direction he would want to go. Ricoeur shows little interest in anything like mystical experience.<sup>29</sup> His approach to religion is always about textual, linguistic, and symbolic mediation, not the immediacy of experience. I am sympathetic to Ricoeur’s hesitation here, and yet we might still wonder whether his philosophical hermeneutics of Christian texts ought to attend to the theme of union with God, given its prominence in the biblical text.

We might also wonder if Ricoeur fails to break sufficiently with Kant’s moral interpretation of religion. Ricoeur appeals to desire—the conatus—against the narrow constraints of Kantian moral religion, but he could go further and consider one of the most pervasive desires of the confessing soul: communion with God. This is not an appeal to religious psychology, but a hermeneutical question: Which has more textual warrant as a framework for understanding biblical religion—the regeneration of capability, or communion with God? The category of capability seems much more like an external philosophical category imported into the text than a focal concern of the texts themselves.

There is, however, something like communion or participation in the divine nature in Ricoeur’s reading of Spinoza. For Spinoza, the *conatus* is the power 

*[p. 158]* ^pg175

by which all things persevere in their being; it is the power, the life, the actual essence of things. The power or life of finite things is a mode, an expression, of the infinite power, life, essence that is God. The goal of Spinoza’s *Ethics*, and likewise religion, is to overcome the bondage and increase the power to act through the improvement of the understanding, moving from the passivity of inadequate ideas to the activity of adequate ideas. Hence Ricoeur’s gloss on Spinoza: “We are powerful when we understand adequately our, as it were, horizontal and external dependence with respect to all things, and our vertical and immanent dependence with respect to the primordial power that Spinoza continues to name ‘God’” (OA 316–17).

Is this a model of participation in God? Perhaps. But how far does Ricoeur follow Spinoza down this path? And what becomes of the idea of God as (a) transcendent and (b) personal if one follows Spinoza? 

Spinoza’s concept of God is often taken to be pantheistic, if not atheistic, but Ricoeur sets aside those controversies. He takes Spinoza at his word when he denies his atheism (OA 315n.21), but there is still the question of pantheism. Ricoeur notes our “immanent dependence” with regard to God, but notice that he also describes a “vertical” dependence. Does this preserve—or perhaps “smuggle in”<sup>30</sup>—some degree of transcendence in this idea of vertical dependence? Moreover, what should we make of Ricoeur’s later claim that he is “not sure about the absolute irreconcilability between the God of the Bible and the God of Being (understood with Jean Nabert as ‘primary affirmation’ or with Spinoza as ‘*substantia actuosa*’).” Does Ricoeur ultimately move away from his earlier understanding of God as transcendent person? That is the topic of our next chapter.
