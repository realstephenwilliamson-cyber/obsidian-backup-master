## About

A short dialogue of Plato in which Socrates recites a funeral oration for Athens' war dead, claiming he learned it from Aspasia, Pericles' companion. Most of the work is the speech itself, which, Wikipedia says, refers to the funeral oration Thucydides gives to Pericles while parodying the genre.

- **People and setting:** Socrates and Menexenus, son of Demophon (who also appears in the *Lysis* and *Phaedo*). They meet in Athens as Menexenus comes from the Council Chamber, where a speaker is being chosen for the public burial of the fallen.
- **Dramatic date and anachronism:** none of the sources opened gives a dramatic date. The speech mentions the Peace of Antalcidas of 386 BC (245e), about a dozen years after Socrates' death, which David Sansone calls by far the most blatant anachronism in Plato. Wikipedia adds that some events mentioned postdate Aspasia's death.
- **Composition:** Sansone (Cambridge, 2020) dates it to the mid to late 380s. The IEP puts it in an early-transitional group, c. 387–380 BC; Britannica counts it among the early works.
- **Authenticity:** Wikipedia says it has come under some suspicion, perhaps because the dialogue serves mainly as exposition for the oration; Aristotle's repeated references to it support Plato's authorship. Sansone treats it as genuine until proven otherwise, though a reviewer notes that puzzles remain.
- **The speech:** praises Athens and the autochthony of its people, recounts Marathon and Salamis and the sparing of the Spartan captives from Sphacteria, and ends with the dead charging their sons to live bravely and surpass their fathers. Britannica says it is often read as a satire on the patriotic distortions of history in such speeches; Sansone reads it as specious rhetoric that distorts and falsifies.
- **Place among the dialogues:** the seventh tetralogy, with the *Hippias Major*, *Hippias Minor* and *Ion*; Wikipedia calls it unique in being mainly a frame for an oration. Stephanus pages 234a–249e.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it one of the few surviving sources for Athenian funeral oratory.
