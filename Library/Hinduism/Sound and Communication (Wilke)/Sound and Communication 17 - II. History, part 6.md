###### 4.1.1.1 Language Ornamentation

An old Indian saying states that however beautiful a beautiful woman is, she is never truly beautiful without her jewelry. Beauty has to be cared for; it must be cultivated. Beauty and decoration in this sense are not only a visual category, but also a spiritual and metonymic one. Adornment is a metonym or indexical sign that does not so much denote extravagance or excess, but instead embodies a social representation of culturedness, propriety, appropriateness and completeness.” Beauty has to do with cognition and sensory awareness: one can see whether someone cares for this woman and perceives her as beautiful. If an Indian woman puts on her jewelry, she also signals the fact that she finds herself not only beautiful and attractive, but also socially acceptable and properly dressed — without it she would feel undressed, disorderly and “naked.” If she does not put it on, she suggests the opposite: it would be inauspicious, indecorous, and disrespectful, or signal widowhood, menstruation or that her husband is away on a journey. The comparison of a “(woman’s) face,” a “lotus” and the “moon” is nothing other than a popular stereotyped statement that must be present in all ornate poetry, i.e. classical kāvya literature. Daṇdin’s examples are primarily model statements. They could have any stereotype as content. For example with a little imagination, if it is not a woman who is the object of praise, but a prince, one could replace “face” with “fame” and “lotus” with “sun,” and go through all the variations of comparison using these words instead. In the final analysis the semantic contents are interchangeable. To a great extent Daṇdin’s poetry is a huge set of building blocks and rhetoric devices for constructing ever new patterns of artistic patchwork and overcoding sense with alluring tropes of sensuality. Its beauty lies in the permutations.

The ornamentalists’? masterpiece, however, is the 51658 (“fused verse”), which can be viewed as the exact opposite of a sound figure. While in the sound figure the redundancy of the phonetic material was taken to a maximum, here redundancy is reduced to a minimum. One and the same verse in the Śleṣa style has two quite different meanings. Depending on how one interprets it semantically and analyzes it grammatically, it speaks of two quite different things, or makes very different statements about certain things. 

For instance, Daṇdin’s first example of this stylistic type makes use of the double meanings of the words rakta (“red or “in love”) and mandala (“circle/disc” or “\[governmental\] district/kingdom”). The interplay with some other words (also with double meanings) allows his following verse to be translated in two different ways: 

asāv udayam ārūdhah kāntimān rakta-mandalah /
rājā harati lokasya hrdayam mrdubhih karaih IP? 

1\) “He is lovely to behold in his success, beloved in his kingdom. The king steals the hearts of the people with tender hands \[= with charitable works\].” 

2\) “She is lovely to behold as she rises, with the red disc — the radiant \[sun\] steals the hearts of the people with tender rays.” 

The Śleṣa technique was actually perfected by Dandin’s successors in such a way that poems could be written that, for example, told the stories of the two epics Mahābhārata and Rāmāyana at the same time. However, the development of this technique continued in a rather different direction. It was not Daṇdin’s formalistic acrobatics that were continued, but instead the subtle nuances of sense that can occur in one and the same verse moved into the foreground. This was in fact a natural development. A careful look at Śleṣa poems reveals that these parallel but different readings are not true parallels. For example, the use of the word kara (“hand”) for a) work and b) ray \[of sunlight\] is metonymic in the first case and metaphorical in the second. That the word rājan means “king” and “sun” at the same time rests on the fact that both are derived from the same root Vrāj (“to beam, to radiate, to emit rays”). So the “fused” poetic style called Śleṣa is actually something other than what the combination-mad ornamentalists thought: it reveals deep linguistic structures by plumbing and researching etymological and semantic fields.

Here we are on the threshold of the poetical concept of dhvanií (“individual, eloquent sound that carries expression,” “suggestion”), which became the epitome of a powerful rival model to pure ornamentation, and inspired for the first time a general theory of signification and poetic expression. 

This significant, historically greatly effective shift within Indian poetics was brought about in the late ninth century by Ānandavardhana, who synthesized Bharata’s rasa aesthetics and the ornamentalists’ alaṁkāra aesthetics into a coherent theory. Ānandavardhana attaches prime importance to the meta-linguistic dhvani, and uses this term for both the suggested psychological, cognitive and emotional content of speech figures and the suggestive power of poetic language that is able to incite moods and affections. Erwin Gerow suspects two major social reasons for Ānandavardhana’s interiorized aesthetic theory and re-evaluation of poetics: on the one hand the decline of sophisticated public dramas and societies of wealthy literati (producing and enjoying highly artificial art-forms) due to reduced sponsorship and less political and cultural stability in post-Gupta times, and on the other, growing devotionalism 

with 

its employment of song, music, and “desophisticated” poetry. Within this general climate Anandavardhana’ s theory of poetry as expressor — irrelevant of genres and with a strong focus on non-literal intention and rasa — was a lasting success. ##### 4.3.1 Philosophical Fundamentals and Pragmatic Rationality

In contrast to the positions of the (Buddhist) Yogācāra and the later (Advaita-)Vedānta, according to the Pūrva-Mīmāmsākas the world perceived by the senses is real. Kumārila strongly objects to the Buddhist doctrine that only cognitions are real, which he considers completely untenable.® Cognitions devoid of real objects, the “void”? or “emptiness” of the external world, the illusory and momentary character of all things and the lack of a permanent substratum - all these Mahāyāna Buddhist doctrines are utterly unfounded according to Kumārila, because there is no mode of proof or valid means of knowledge 14277744.) to ascertain them.” The most basic and primary means of knowledge is sensory perception. The Buddhist claims turn facts upside down - at least according to Kumārila, who argues that sensory perception precedes ideas. There is a sensory basis for all ideas, i.e. ideas form only because of sensory data prior to them. However, complete knowledge of reality merely through the senses is impossible, which in turn is taken as a reason for rejecting the empiricism of the materialistic Cārvākas, for example. 

The Mīmāmsakas propound a realistic theory of knowledge, i.e. a “real” physical world (recognizable in its being that way), and a positivistic heuristics based on the power of language to communicate meaning and on the truth of the Veda. 4.3.2 

##### 4.3.2 On the Theory of the Eternity of Linguistic Statements

The general Mīmāmsā theory concerning the eternal existence of linguistic statements and the eternal relationship between word and meaning — which is at the heart of the Mīmāmsā philosophy - is divided into three steps: proof of the existence of eternal phonemes (varna), proof of the existence of eternal words (sabda), and proof of the eternal existence of the Vedic statements. The latter presupposes the (albeit not very convincing) “proof” of the Vedic text given above: “Since no authors are documented, the Veda is authorless. Since there is no author, the Veda word is cannot be doubted and eternal” or in short, “Because there is no initiator, the Veda has no beginning.” In contrast to this, however, the other proofs are more than mere sophisms and are to be taken seriously. Tellingly, they are much more elaborate and anticipate points of view that are still discussed in linguistic philosophy today.

##### 4.3.3 Areligious Religion

With the help of the three proofs of the eternal existence of phonemes, words and Vedic statements, the eternal existence of the Veda as we know it today has been determined - at least in the opinion of the Mīmāmsakas — and from now on should no longer be doubted. Deities only continue to exist as their names. The Pūrva-Mīmāmsā regards Vedic mantras, invocations and descriptions of the gods (devatāā) as 

arthavāda (“laudations”, lit. “explanatory passages,” “supporting statements”). Texts on gods are laudatory, highly figurative speech and always functional. They serve the sacrifice by eulogizing and supporting it. Śabarasvāmin defines devatā as a relative term with precise practical application. The choice of the deity’s name in the offeratory formula is not random, but dependent on the ritual context, and expressive of the desired goal.\* Kumārila and later commentators suggest rather blunt functional definitions: “That which is enjoined for the sake of something is its devatā.”” The gods are counted among the “instruments” of ritual action like rice, firewood and chanting, which help to bring about the result.® The gods do not bestow the fruits as a theist would hold, but rather the act of offering itself generates something entirely new.
