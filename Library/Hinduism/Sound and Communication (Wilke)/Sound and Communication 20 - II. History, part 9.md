The Yogaśikha-Upanisad, one of the Yoga Upaniṣads (which are commonly assumed to be late), associates the four stages of speech with the Yogic energy centers (cakras), the contemplation of sound (ववत) and the rise of the cosmic energy (kundalini) in the body.” Grammar and general linguistics are no longer a path to liberation, and the element of sound - physical sound, resonance, “unstruck” interior sound and the esoteric physiology of sound — comes to the forefront. It seems grammar and linguistics were not enough to satisfy the expectations of many recipients; they wanted to find a spiritual, religious sense in the Vēkyapadiya verse 1.159 and could not believe that grammar was enough to fulfill soteriological claims.

Bhartrhari apparently adopted the Yogic-Tantric terms Vaikharī, Madhyamā and Paśyantī because they provided a powerful phenomenological model that serves multiple explanatory functions. The three levels of speech illustrate experientially the continuity of consciousness and language where others see a profound gap. They help to explain the sphota theory and simultaneously allow a profound insight into the nature of human thinking and consciousness, the nature of reality and therefore also into the nature of Brahman. And not least they help to clarify the relationship - and possible coincidence — of grammar study and liberation. Let us look at each of these closely related issues separately. 

Within Bhartrhari’s system, the terms Vaikharī (uttered and heard speech), Madhyamā (mental speech, i.e. thought) and Paśyantī (“visionary” speech, i.e. intuitive insight of meaning) communicate in a phenomenologically comprehensible manner that there is no dividing line between language and consciousness. According to Bhartrhari, cognition and consciousness must also be called “word,” “speech” or “language,” and the Vrtti on Vēkyapadiya 1.1 explains why: “Because, in our cognitions we identify objects with their words (Sabdopagrahi) and our cognitions are intertwined with the words (Sabdopagrāhya), they are essentially of the nature of the word \[language\] ... The manifestation of the word which is in everybody as one with the spirit is for the purpose of communicating what is within.”™® Speech and cognition are intrinsically interwoven and the latter is also a form of speech. Structurally, the three terms represent different (both gross and subtle) states of speech and degrees of linguistic comprehension which merge in the unified image. The special focus on the “Seeing” dimension reflects Bhartrhari’s interest in a general theory of language. All the way through his Vēkyapadiya, he stresses these two aspects of a word: the physical, perceptible 

aspect (correct speech forms) and the imperceptible, inaudible meaning aspect (sphota). The quotation of the mysterious Rgveda verse (see above) and its juxtaposition with the endeavor of the grammarians captures his linguistic program well. The determination of correct linguistic forms (grammar) and the analysis of language as such (general linguistics) have an almost religious character for Bhartrhari, since also the “fourth” part ~ human language - contains elements of ineffability: the meaning aspect and the “visionary” way in which it is grasped. In Vākyapadīiya 1.126 ff. he says that the mighty creator god Prajāpati is present to a greater extent in perfected, correct speech (sādhví vāc) than in less perfect speech forms, because incorrect or wrong words are less suitable for communicating the intended meaning.™ And Vāēkyapadīya 2.143-1.145 states that the understanding of meaning occurs in a sudden internal flash (pratibhā) and cannot be explained in any manner. Exploring the nature of language, one learns about the complex structures of the mind: the successive thought processes and the holistic nature of comprehension. Grammar/linguistics help us to grasp the nature of thinking and consciousness and therefore also have privileged access to dimensions that exceed the material and audible word. Cognition rests on pre-reflexive intuitions, and understanding only takes place when we grasp the indivisible meaning-whole “before” and “beyond” the signs. Even though Bhartrhari’s major distinction is the two-fold word (the audible one and the mental one), his language-style changes when speaking about the Paśyantī dimension, into a hymnic, highly metaphorical language, and one of the common terms used in this context is “light.”

#### 5.3 Mantras as Divine Self-Revelation

The deities of the Tantra are no longer mythological figures like those found in the Purāṇas, and they hardly exist in Tantric literary spaces either. Instead they appear as specific mantras. As already discussed, the term mantra therefore refers not to an important verse dedicated to the deity, like the Vedic mantras. Rather it is usually a cryptic combination of letters, and the Tantrists go so far as to say that this special combination of sounds does not just denote or invoke the deity, but actually is the deity. But a Tantric mantra is not only identical with the deity — it also harbors power. 

Just like the Veda, the Tantric mantras are regarded as sounds that have always existed. The texts always tell the story of the mantra as a myth of annunciation and not as a myth of creation. In the case of the Āgama Tantras it is normal for the deity itself to announce its mantra and this revelation generally takes the form of a dialog. In the Śaiva Tantras the goddess (Pārvatī) asks the questions and her husband Śiva provides the answers, while the reverse situation occurs in the Śākta Tantras. A very extensive dialog is found in the Netra-Tantra, an Āgama work composed around 700-850 AD“ and exclusively dedicated to the “Mrtyuñjaya mantra,” the mantra of the “Conqueror of Death” (Mrtyuñjaya), a form of Śiva that brings healing and liberation. The immortality mantra OM JŪM SAH is the answer to the goddess’s request to the god at the beginning of the work to reveal the central mantra for finally overcoming the sufferings of the world: 

“Although your supreme power (vīrya), O god ... \[will always remain\] unrecognizable and immeasurable in its nature, it has certainly brought blessing to all \[beings\] from out of your mouth ... O you God of Gods, who blesses the whole world, all ... animals, people and gods suffer under the threat of disease and the threat of poison and spirits. Hundreds of deaths come upon them, they suffer attacks of fever, diseases of the lungs and consumption ... how could they be happy? Their life-span is ... limited, and their lives are threatened by the powerful, who have grown great through your glory and your strength ... O Lord of the world, have mercy upon them ... and tell us of a remedy to free them from all these \[ills\].”®
