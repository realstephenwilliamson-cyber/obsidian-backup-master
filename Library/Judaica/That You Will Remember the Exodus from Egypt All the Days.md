הלילה , it בחצי

למען תזכר את יום צאתך מארץ מצרים כל ימי חייך

(Very loosely inspired by my reading of Zohar Parshas Bo 2:39b - 2:40b, p. 194 - 200 in Pritzker Ed.

The relevant Talmud is Brachos 12b - 13a)

I made it my goal this year to find an interpretation for what the Haggadah is bringing when it quotes Elazar Ben Azariah saying in the Mishnah that Ben Zoma's statement helped him understand why we mention Yetzias Mitzrayim at night.

I always found the question strange. As the Haggadah tells us directly, ויהי

happened in the middle of the night. It seems that Ben Azariah should have asked why we mention it in the day instead!

Like Ben Azariah, gray is just starting to show up in my beard, so maybe I can chap a little bit now.

"That you will remember the Exodus from Egypt all the days of your lives."

"All," says Ben Zoma, means night, while Chachamim say it means Yemos Hamoshiach.

We see in a B'raisa that they go on to dispute whether we will remember the Exodus at all after the final redemption, with Ben Zoma saying no and Chachamim saying yes.

We know from the holy Zohar that "all" can mean Shekhinah, so the machloches must hang on the question of whether our passuk is referencing the Shekhinah in exile or the Shekhinah as united with HKB"H. Ben Zoma's paradigm here is of Galus and the paradigm of Chachamim here is the Geulah.

Based on this, let's see what Ben Azariah learned from Ben Zoma and then we'll consider what Chachamim are bringing here.

Ben Azariah starts by taking it as a given that we should mention the Exodus in the day. This is like saying that when times are good, when we are free from oppression and may openly pursue Torah, the type of life we can call the "daytime;" that is the natural time to remember that we were slaves and we were liberated. We must not take our security and freedom for granted, rather we must always remember that we owe it to Hashem. Had he not taken us out, we would still be there so of course, we should speak of the Exodus in the daytime.

The original question then becomes about why one should remember liberation when one is not free. These Tana'im lived under brutal oppression by the Romans and had first-hand experience of bitterness and darkness. I would not blame Ben Azariah for saying that he didn't see the relevance of mentioning the Exodus in a time lacking in freedom, just like one couldn't blame a

*[p. 2]* ^pg2

death camp inmate for questioning what Passover can mean to someone who is submerged in the depths of unholiness.

Ben Zoma's answer says that even in the worst places, the Divine Presence is with us. Even under the Romans, the same G'd who brought us out of Egypt is with us. The night is when we cannot sense justice or see the transcendent beyond because we are in the dark. However, since night is "all" and "all" is G'd, we know that the final redemption is already guaranteed. As it says in Tehillim, "darkness and light are the same to You."

This is what Ben Azariah learned from Ben Zoma: that even in a Galus paradigm, we should orient ourselves towards the awareness that Geulah is integral to the special relationship Hashem established with us and therefore our deliverance is inevitable.

So far so good.

Now let's turn to the dispute between Ben Zoma and the Sages.

Where Ben Zoma holds that it is natural to remember the Exodus in good times and an act of holy defiance to remember it in bad times, the Sages assert the exact opposite.

What's a given for the Sages is that we should mention the Exodus in Olam Hazeh. In other words, what is natural is to say that while we are in a world of apparent brokenness, we should draw our continuing strength from the knowledge that G'd himself established us as a people and set us on this historical course. What Chachamim learn out from the word "all" is that even after Tikkun Olam is effected, we will continue to understand what we really are: that we were taken out of servitude to the Sitra Achra to be the ones who facilitated His dwelling in the lower world.

Ben Zoma pushes back and brings a pasuk to argue that in Yemos Hamoshiach, we will not recall the Exodus. What he is saying here is that this life, this Jewish suffering we experience in history is painful enough. Real Geulah, he is arguing, will completely negate the forces of evil to the point that even the thought of deliverance from them will be not just irrelevant, but downright inappropriate. It's like he's saying that holding onto the painful memory of all that has gone wrong since we left Egypt would be like bringing the klipos themselves with us into Paradise.

Chachamim counter that Ben Zoma's pasuk really means that the memory of the final Geulah will be more prominent than the memory of the Exodus, not that the Exodus will not be remembered at all.

A parable is brought to explain. The situation is compared to one who encountered a wolf, was saved, and then related the story to others. He then encountered a lion, was saved, and likewise, recounted the story. Finally, he encountered a snake, forgot the other incidents, and only told the story of the snake.

*[p. 3]* ^pg3

What's going on here? On the surface it looks almost like this parable supports Ben Zoma instead of Chachamim! A closer examination yields another reading. Let the wolf represent Y'tzias Mitzraim, the lion can represent all the tzuris of Jewish history, and the snake represents the final war before the complete redemption.

The problems and deliverances of Olam Hazeh, the lion, call to mind the problems and deliverances surrounding the Exodus. This is because they are of the same kind. When over the course of our individual lives and collective history, we experience deliverance from evil, it's like we are leaving a personal or generational Mitzraim. That is to say, we have the experience of being released from the clutches of the Sitra Achra into the freedom to live authentically as ourselves.

It is a mitzvah for us to remember the Exodus now because it is the premise and prototype of our relationship with G'd. Israel is the nation that is brought out of Egypt to become Hashem's treasured people and Hashem is the G'd who descends into Egypt to pull His people out. This is the mode of all the subsequent redemptions throughout history from Esther to the events of 1948.

The final redemption will be of an entirely different character. The transition from Olam Hazeh to Yemos Hamoshiach will not remind us at all of the deliverances in history or Y'tzias Mitzraim. This is why the person in the parable forgot the wolf and the lion.

Chachamim insist that it will still be a mitzvah to remember the Exodus in Yemos Hamoshiach, but in that time, the paradigm will have shifted into a different mode altogether. We can consider the experiential difference between remembering that which comes to mind easily and remembering that which is forgotten. Even more poignantly perhaps, we can consider the difference between the fresh memory of trauma and successfully recalling a repressed memory when one has grown enough to handle the recollection.

What Ben Zoma calls night corresponds to what the Sages call Olam Hazeh, and indeed, the miracle of Passover happened in the depths of night. Midnight is the time of obscurity and uncertainty. It is the intermediary phase, the liminal time. Yesterday is finished and tomorrow only exists in potentia. The Malach Hamaves passed over our homes only after we rid ourselves of chametz, after we were no longer under the dominion of an alien king, but before we were brought out into the service of Hashem. Our chains were broken, but we had not yet left the jail cell.

It was during that intermediate phase that we partnered with G'd in the war against the Sitra Achra. We consumed the Pesach, the slain earthly representation of Egypt's heavenly prince, while G'd destroyed the dominion of that prince above.

The entire post Exodus phase of history has been like that night. We no longer serve alien gods like our fathers did, we are partnering with Hashem through mitzvos, but we still contend with evil.

*[p. 4]* ^pg4

In the present time, the mitzvah of remembering the Exodus is fulfilled almost automatically by anyone living a Jewish life. In Yemos Hamoshiach, it will require intention.

If Ben Zoma is saying that after the complete Geulah, we should leave painful memories and the klipos associated with them behind, Chachamim are saying that we should and must bring all those memories with us! It will be essential to understand the whole narrative of Yisroel and how every part led to the messianic resolution. The suffering endured in Galus is ultimately meaningless when viewed only in the context of Galus. It is when we veiw it in the context of Geulah that we can learn anything from abject horror. Vital to that task is understanding the essential part Pharoah, Haman, and Hitler had in our story and how their influence shaped us.

When our eyes are completely opened to how the rashayim and the klipos associated with them participated in the Jewish story, we can understand how even the Sitra Achra had a part to play in the process of universal redemption. Nowadays, it is a dubious, even cruel game to exalt the role of our oppressors and killers, precisely because this world is broken and desperately in need of fixing. In the messianic era, when the universal Tikkun is complete, everything, even evil itself, will be returned to its holy source. The alienated part of the whole that underlies the idea of otherness and sits at the root of unholiness will not just be reincorporated but its own essential spark of holiness will be revealed.
