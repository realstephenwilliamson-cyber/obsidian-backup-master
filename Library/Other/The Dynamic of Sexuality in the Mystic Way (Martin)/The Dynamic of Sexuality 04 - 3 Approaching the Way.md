## 3 Approaching the Way

The intention of this thesis as case study is to compare and contrast the modalities of particular mysticisms within the Christian affective and Hindu Tantric traditions; specifically, that sort evidenced by the Beguines, and that sort articulated by exponents of Śrīvidyā Śākta Tantrism. In both sub-traditions mentalized imagery can be used to depict the relationship of the self (soul) to (divine) reality. The ideological, doctrinal and soteriological[^1] goals within the two traditions are quite different. Although this is granted, an attempt will none the less be made to harmonize elements of their metaphysical viewpoints. How this is done will require adapting a particular research methodology, which in this case is that of phenomenography. I hope to show that this recently developed research approach has a valid application to the understanding of mystical experiences. 

### Introductory Remarks

Although this study will necessarily focus on textual renditions of mystical phenomena it is not for all that a mere paper analysis. Staal contends that studying mysticism can most fruitfully be undertaken only by those who have some personal mystical experience; at least, that is, in regards to the subjective aspects of mytsicism, if not necessarily to the behaviouristic or physiological aspects.[^2] For Coward and Penelhum *a priori* scholarly treatments of mysticism can lend a quite different perspective to that which practising mystics might bring to such studies.[^3] And King argues that while a reductionist study of mysticism based upon doctrinal aspects may not find cross-cultural similarities, the personal experiences of practising mystics may discern correspondences.[^4] This has special relevance with regard to Tantric systems, where a guru is considered a prerequisite for (proper) practice. The general caution is given that, ‘The fool who, overpowered by greed, acts after having looked up \[the matter\] in a written book without having obtained it from the guru’s mouth, he also will certainly be destroyed’.[^5] This is not to suggest that any scholar who ventures to perform a mere textual exegesis will be destroyed, but it is important to consider the historical and anthropological studies surrounding the praxis.[^6] At any rate, because the secret and oral meanings of texts are closely guarded, access that scholars have to tradition is limited to those living exponents who are ‘willing to discuss openly Tantric concepts and practices’.[^7] The present writer has a background of twenty-four years of practice in a derivative form of Tantric mysticism and this informs his outlook to a considerable extent; associated with this is the cultural context of a Catholic Christian upbringing. So the following analysis is filtered through a peculiar personal experience.

Of the various approaches to studying mysticism – dogmatic; philological and historical; phenomenological; physiological and psychological[^8] – it is the third, the phenomenological, which I shall be concerned with here, or at any rate a variation of that. As a first point, the term phenomenology can be understood as referring to ‘the immediate objects of experience’, rather than the study of experiencing individuals.[^9] The foundational object of experience in this case study is the Other, conceptualized as the transcendent God in Christian mysticism, and as the polarized opposite being (intrinsically identified with the self) in Hindu Tantrism. It might be said that the mystical experience is for Christians an outcome of prayer and meditation, and for Tantrics a catalysis of ritual visualization and yogic techniques. Phenomenonally, the experience relates to quite different things; and although this abstract Other is instantiated in different ways by affective mystics and *tāntrikas*, I shall contend that the underlying basis for spiritual perception is adducibly similar. In fine, the psycho-mental state that characterizes mystical experience is effected by the activation of *kuṇḍalinī*: that essential kinetic energy of the godhead, which is enclosed in the human body, and which explodes into consciousness during mystical experience. 

### Phenomenology and Phenomenography

The term ‘phenomenography’ has been used mainly in the field of education. It arose out of the work of researchers in the Department of Education and Educational Research in Gothenburg, Sweden, in the early 1970s, as they sought to understand the learning capacities of students.[^10] As one of the pioneers, Ference Marton, defines it: 

Phenomenography is a research method adapted for mapping the qualitatively different ways in which people experience, conceptualize, perceive, and understand various aspects of, and phenomena in, the world around them. [^11] 

A clear example comes from a study of thirteen to sixteen year old students from Swedish compulsory schools who were asked to give a physical explanation of sight, or the fact that we can see an object in front of us. Five qualitatively different ways of accounting for sight were gleaned from the subjects: 

1\. The link between eyes and object is ‘taken for granted’; it is not problematized: ‘you simply see’; 

2\. There is a picture going from the object to the eyes. When it reaches the eyes, we see; 3. There are beams coming from the eyes. When they hit the object we see; 4. There are beams going back and forth between the eyes and the object. The eyes send out beams which hit the object, return and tell the eyes about it; 

5\. The object reflects light, and when it hits the eyes we can see the object.

While 5 is the modern scientific understanding, conception 2 resembles the ancient Greek atomists’ concept of *eidola*, while 3 is close to Euclid’s idea of the ‘beam of light’.[^12] 

These early studies at Gothenburg led to important insights into student learning: 

First, the question of what is learned in any learning task is not unproblematic – different students can understand one and the same text, or indeed one and the same concept, in a number of different ways. They do not, however, each understand it in their own unique way; rather, a set of qualitatively different ways of understanding can be arrived at which has an internal logic with respect to the intended meaning of the text. This has become a corner-stone of phenomenographic research. Second, there are qualitatively different ways in which students go about, or approach the tasks of learning, namely a *deep approach* which is distinguished by a search for meaning in the text and a *surface approach* which focuses instead on the words comprising the text. The third insight was into the relation between approaches and outcomes: students who adopted a deep approach achieved overwhelmingly superior understanding of the message of the text and also retained information better than their surface studying colleagues.[^13] 

Phenomenographical research is generally conducted through interviews, and as such it is a ‘second-order reflection over empirical depictions of the nature of an experience’.[^14] The transcripts are analysed and the various ways of experiencing are reduced to categories of description, which are assigned, or ‘discovered’, during the analysis; they are not determined in advance, as is traditionally done in content analysis.[^15] The unit of phenomenographical research, or *way of experiencing something*, is to be understood as an internal relationship between the experiencer and the experienced. Regarding the person and the world, the impact of manifest reality as it is experienced represents a unique interaction, since the person’s ‘biography’ is unique. So, it is not possible to separate the description of the world from the describer. Phenomenography sees the individual as having only a partial or fragmented view of the world, and moreover each individual brings a unique view to the experience of phenomena.[^16] 

According to Svensson, phenomenography is an empirical research tradition that lacks an articulated metaphysical foundation.[^17] It is acceptable to conduct phenomenographical research within the framework of the main ontological categories of materialism or idealism; the individual researcher simply chooses whichever mode. The relationship between knowledge and the conception of the world is an essential one: knowledge is created through thinking about external reality, and is not merely empirical or rational. There is no absolute invariable truth; instead, knowledge about reality varies depending upon the thinking of the individual. In accord with Marton and Booth, Svensson opines that ‘reality presents itself in human thinking as different related entities having the character of forming units or wholes. What we refer to as knowledge is based on this differentiation of wholes’.[^18] The

epistemological assumptions behind the phenomenographic approach include, *inter alia*, the importance and ‘fruitfulness’ of descriptions; and the characteristic of this research is the distillation of ‘categories of description’.[^19] 

For Marton, phenomenography utilizes a non-dualistic ontology, in that object and subject are not separate, and the subject’s experience of the object represents a relation between the two. Basically, there is not a split between a real, objective world and a subjective world of mental representations. There is only a ‘really existing world, which is experienced and understood in different ways by human beings’.[^20] Again, the apprehension of reality is a fact of *relationship* between object and subject, which can be seen either in relation to the ways others might experience the same thing, or to the same indvidual’s way of experiencing other things. Marton argues that although phenomenography does not differentiate the object of experience from the experiencer it does not thereby ‘imply that the object is identical with the way in which it is experienced’. Rather, the object is a ‘complex of the different ways in which it can be experienced’; and since ‘\[t\]hese different ways are logically related to each other, it is in this sense \[that\] they are experiences of the same object’. This ‘logically structured complex of the different ways of experiencing an object’, Marton calls the ‘outcome space’; and, being ‘the thing as it appears to us’, is just the phenomenon itself, in contrast to the Kantian ‘noumenon’, or ‘the thing as such’. He concludes that ‘\[a\]n experience of an object is . . . not a subjective shadow of the real object, but a part of the whole which is subjective and objective at the same time’.[^21] 

The relationship of phenomenology and phenomenography is a contested one. While there are similarities between these two in terms of their shared object of research, that is, human experience and awareness, they only partly share a common ground of theories and methods.[^22] Marton cites three areas of disagreement. Firstly, Husserlian phenomenology advocates that the researcher ‘bracket’ (hold in check) her or his own preconceived notions in studying the experience of others. Secondly, while phenomenology seeks that essence of experiences which is the point of commonality amongst many, phenomenography seeks to understand the variations in experience of the many. Thirdly, phenomenology emphasizes the distinction between immediate experience and conceptual thought, and the latter must be ‘bracketed’ while searching for the former; phenomenography on the other hand seeks ‘to describe relations between the individual and various aspects of the world around them, regardless of whether those relationships are manifested in the forms of immediate experience, conceptual thought, or physical behavior’.[^23]

The term phenomenography has been used by Nelson Pike in his analysis of states of ‘infused contemplation’, or mystical union as understood by Christian mystics.[^24] He builds on the work of Augustin Poulain, who discourses on the method of scientifically understanding ‘descriptive mysticism’. From the observation of facts, Poulain considered the task of the theologian to be that of seeking clarification and organization; that is, in effect, to compile ‘classifications’. The ‘facts’ to be taken account of here are the ‘mystical states’ or ‘graces’ which the great contemplatives have found in themselves; in other words, these are ‘phenomenological facts’. In the absence of the ability to engage with mystical experiences, either through direct connection, or through interaction with the experiencer, it is necessary to follow written reports. Pike uses the following analogy: 

If we think of descriptive mystical theology as a ‘science’ dealing with ‘observed facts’, we must think of it on the model of, for example, history – that is, as an enterprise in which the facts of primary interest are of the observable sort (they were, or at least could have been, directly observed) but in which the investigator (the ‘scientist’) does not (usually) have *direct* access to them. Paradigmatically, the investigator is not the one who makes the key observations. *His* sources are, as Poulain says, ‘documents’ in which the key observations are *reported* by those who have observed the facts of primary interest.[^25] 

Simply, then, phenomeno*graphy* might be said to be ‘the study of *phenomena* by way of *reports* thereof’.[^26] We have it seems two apparently independent understandings of the term phenomenography. I shall call the first, pedagogical phenomenography, and the second, literary phenomenography. Both approaches can find applications in this thesis. 

If it is said that mystical experiences encompass qualitatively different ways in which God/Brahman is perceived and described, and that manifest reality represents this existent as phenomenally expressed, then it may be amenable to (pedagogical) phemomenographical research. If, furthermore, the writings of mystics are considered as vehicles for teaching and instructing, a *deep approach* will prove more enlightening than a *surface approach*. This thesis must be considered a limited phenomenographic analysis of texts and traditions, and not a full study, since no interviews were conducted.[^27] I use a ‘literary phenomenographical’ method inasmuch as I shall be studying the phenomena of mysticism through the reports of Mechthild’s experiences in her book, and Śaṅkara’s eulogizing report on the beauty of the Goddess.[^28] However, it is also a pedagogical phenomenographical study, since my analysis will involve reading Mechthild’s text in the light of a tantric conceptualization and ‘discovering’ categories of description, which are the images, and then contextualizing her perspective as being a way of realizing the phenomena of the *kuṇḍalinī* experience.[^29] In
