## Authorship and Date

First John is the traditional title of one of three documents collectively called the “Johannine Letters.” None of the three either names the author or claims to have been written by the author of the Gospel of John; the title instead reflects the understanding that there is a relationship among the three documents, and as well as between them and the Gospel of John. The text shares much of the vocabulary, and a number of themes, with John’s Gospel (e.g., light and darkness \[[[1 John 01#^1-5|1 Jn 1.5–7]]; [[1 John 02#^2-9|2.9–11]]; see [[John 08#^8-12|Jn 8.12]]; [[John 12#^12-46|12.46]]\]; abiding in Christ \[[[1 John 02#^2-27|1 Jn 2.27–28]]; see [[John 13#^13-34|Jn 13.34–35]], also [[John 15#^15-5|Jn 15.5]]\]; new commandment \[[[1 John 02#^2-7|1 Jn 2.7–8]]; [[John 13#^13-34|Jn 13.34–35]]\]; eternal life \[[[1 John 01#^1-2|1 Jn 1.2]]; [[1 John 02#^2-25|2.25]]; [[1 John 03#^3-15|3.15]]; etc., and [[John 03#^3-16|Jn 3.16]]\]). However, given the significant differences in style and content between the letter and the Gospel, most modern scholars do not believe that 1 John was written by the author of the Fourth Gospel. Rather, they propose a “Johannine school” or “Johannine community” sharing a common vocabulary (frequent usage of words such as “truth,” “light,” “darkness,” “lies,”) and theological outlook (a dualistic worldview expressing tension between the Johannine community and other early followers of Jesus). The book of Revelation, whose author is identified as “John” ([[Revelation 01#^1-1|Rev 1.1]],[[Revelation 01#^1-4|4]],[[Revelation 01#^1-9|9]] and 22.8), also was linked to the apostle John and was considered part of the Johannine corpus as early as the second century, though not without dissenters (e.g., Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* 7.25.1–2). Modern scholars typically do not count the book of Revelation among the Johannine documents.

## Canonical Status

The earliest attestation of 1 John is in the letter of the Christian author Polycarp, *Phil*. 7.1, dated ca. 117–120. While some argue that 1 John predates the Gospel of John (ca. 90–100 CE), most scholars place its composition to sometime after the writing of the Gospel, therefore putting its date of composition at approximately 100–110. Likewise, the location of the community to which 1 John is written cannot be firmly ascertained. Western Asia Minor (modern Turkey), in Ephesus, is the location most often named by tradition. Recently, however, Syria has gained supporters as a possibility.

## Historical Context and Literary History

In contrast to 2 and 3 John, 1 John lacks the elements typically found in a letter (e.g., salutation, identification of recipients and senders, greetings to local persons); hence it is more accurately described as a persuasive essay or exhortation. Its author wished to encourage and instruct fellow believers following a recent acri-monious schism, which cannot be outlined with certainty (see 2.19).

Most likely, tension between two distinct views regarding the nature of Jesus prompted this schism. The author asserts that Jesus “has come in the flesh” (4.2). Others—perhaps those who left the community—deny this claim. They may represent a theology known as “Docetism,” from the Gk *dokeō*, which means “to seem”; according to docetic belief, Jesus only *seemed* to be human but was fully, and only, divine. The author associates a non-corporeal view of Jesus with the “spirit of the antichrist” (4.3), a term referring not to a supernatural agent, but to false teachers. The author appears concerned that other community members may find a strictly divine portrayal of Jesus appealing—hence the repeated encouragement that they are the children of God (3.2; 4.4,6; 5.19), with the implication that the others are not.

## Interpretation

In contrast to the Gospel of John, 1 John displays no polemical attitude toward Jews or toward the Torah’s commandments. Although the author states: “I am writing you no new commandment, but an old commandment that you have had from the beginning” (2.7), the following verse asserts: “Yet I am writing you a new commandment that is true in him and in you.” The “new commandment” likely refers to [[John 13#^13-34|Jn 13.34]], where Jesus states, “I give you a new commandment, that you love one another. Just as I have loved you, you also should love one another.” These texts do not offer a new set of precepts that would replace the Torah. Indeed, the idea of loving one’s neighbors (e.g., [[Torah 30 - Kedoshim#^19-18|Lev 19.18]]) is one of the central tenets of Judaism. The well-known Rabbi Akiva, who lived approximately one hundred years after Jesus, is said to have declared, “Love your neighbor as yourself—this is a major principle of the Torah” (e.g., *y. Ned*. 9.4). Other contemporaneous Jewish documents likewise reflect the importance of loving one’s neighbor in connection with loving God (e.g., *T. Iss*. 5.2). If there is anything “new” about the commandment to love one another in the Johannine texts, it might be the last part of the statement from the Gospel: “Just as I have loved you.” (See “The Concept of Neighbor,” p. 645.)

*[p. 519]* ^pg550

First John’s use of images of light and darkness (1.5–7; 2.9–11), which reflect the Gospel of John’s influence ([[John 08#^8-12|Jn 8.12]]; [[John 12#^12-46|12.46]]), also appear in Second Temple Jewish writings found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Several scrolls, for example, denounce competing groups by labeling them “sons of darkness” who are led by an “Angel of Darkness,” while their own supporters are identified as “sons of light” who are followers of the “Prince of Light” (IQS 3.10–21; 1QM). This association does not show, however, that the original readers of 1 John were Jewish; their pagan background is indicated by the exhortation to avoid idol worship (5.21) in the document’s abrupt final statement. The fact that citations from the Tanakh are not used to substantiate the author’s points likely indicates that this Gentile audience was unfamiliar with Jewish Scripture.
