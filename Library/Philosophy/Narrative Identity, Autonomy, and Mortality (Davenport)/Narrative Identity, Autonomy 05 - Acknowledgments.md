## Acknowledgments

The main ideas for this project were prompted by an APA presentation given by John Lippitt in Boston that led to his book review of *Kierkegaard After MacIntyre*, and to his challenging essay in *Inquiry*, which broadened the discussion to narrative theories of identity, in general. My coauthor Anthony Rudd began to develop responses to Lippitt’s objections and to Galen Strawson well before I did, and this book is directly indebted to his extensive work on these topics. In particular, I had the opportunity to comment on an APA presentation that Anthony gave in San Francisco in 2007, and I began to develop the “narrative realist” idea in that exchange. I am also much indebted to his 2005 paper in *Inquiry*. Anthony, John, Ed Mooney, and I also shared an extended email exchange with about “narratoskepticism,” which helped me a great deal; Anthony and I have continued discussing these issues through summer 2011. I also learned from extensive comments from George Connell, who kindly read two chapters of this work in draft format, and from comments by Jason Mahn and others when I presented the “Purity of Heart” material at an APA meeting in Chicago in spring 2009.

More recently, I have benefitted from Patrick Stokes’s challenging questions about the relation of whole lives to present subjects. Patrick, along with Adam Buben, coedited the volume on *Kierkegaard and Death* that encouraged me to write the text now developed into Chapters 4 and 5, though my essay in their collection could only summarize my main analysis and present a tentative answer to the “problem of mortality.” I thank Indiana University Press for permission to reprint some of that content (now in Chapter 5). Patrick also encouraged me to engage with Galen Strawson’s work, which I have attempted in Chapter 1—and I would like to thank Strawson for comments on a draft of this section, which saved me from some errors. Let me also thank Princeton University Press for permission to quote extensively from several volumes in the Hong translations of Kierkegaard’s works.

Early versions of material now in Chapters 2 and 3 were also read by referees for *Inquiry* whose extensive feedback was very helpful and in favor of publication, although we eventually decided that these topics could be 

*[p. xiv]* ^pg15

better treated in a short book. Drafts of Chapters 2 and 3 also circulated among interested parties at the *International Kierkegaard Conference* at St. Olaf College in June 2010, leaving me in the enviable position of trying to respond to some criticisms that are appearing in print before this book itself. Since then, multiple referees for Routledge also kindly pressed me to clarify the main themes of this work, to improve the discussion of Ricoeur and to address embodiment, and to make the account of “narravive” more rigorous—all of which I have tried to do. Like me, some of them wished for a better term for this concept, but we have not been able to find one. However, my first editor at Routledge, Erica Wetter, encouraged me to press on.

I had extensive debts at Fordham. Initial drafts of what are now chapters 2–4 were completed on a faculty fellowship in spring 2009. Although my colleagues have a healthy skepticism of narrative theories, my former chair John Drummond kindly responded to a version of my narrative analysis of autonomy presented in September 2010, and I’m indebted to him for drawing my attention to Dan Zahavi’s work as well. My colleague Merold Westphal has also been a faithful respondent who has challenged me to be careful about the kinds of “autonomy” I attribute to selves on Kierkegaard’s view. The same can be said for Andrew Henscheid, who completed a Ph.D. thesis arguing that Kierkegaard’s conception of agapic love requires a conception of selves somewhere in between traditional versions of autonomy and heteronomy. Another Fordham graduate student, Peter Seipel, made helpful corrections to most of the manuscript (Chapters 2–4), and several students in a graduate seminar on “Autonomy, Care, and Self” offered interesting objections to my brand of narrative realism. Chris Kim, a bright undergraduate, has also raised questions deriving from Judith Butler’s work (which I hope to address in future work).

A project of this sort is bound to seem incomplete, because its central issues connect (like many spokes from a single hub) to whole literatures—e.g., on personal identity, time, meaning, action, emotion, love, free will, friendship, virtue, and hermeneutics—that I have barely been able to gesture toward. Some relevant work, such as Kerby’s book, I found too late. Moreover, as always, new work relevant to my topics has appeared since I began writing for this project. Given my hope to keep this book short and accessible, I have not been able to do justice to all papers in Atkins and Mackenzie’s collection, *Practical Identity and Narrative Agency*, though I have benefitted from several of them. Likewise, I have not commented on all the relevant papers in Dan Hutto’s collection, *Narrative and Understanding Persons*, and have only touched on a small sample of the relevant work in literary theory and psychology. Even within the limits of philosophy, there is so much interest in narrative identity theory now that I fear some glaring omissions are inevitable in a short book trying to bridge multiple areas. That said, it goes without saying that work in this area owes a large debt to the pioneering eff orts of Alasdair MacIntyre. At Notre Dame, I also

Finally, I thank my family, and especially my wife Robin and my children, for bearing with me as this work was completed during some diffi cult months in 2010 and 2011 when circumstances defeated multiple eff orts to improve our living situation. This work, finalized in less than ideal conditions, could not have been finished without Robin’s invaluable help and constant encouragement.
