ABSTRACT The text we publish here 

is the first chapter of Bernard Stiegler’s 

*La Télécratie contre la Démocratie*. The 

broad argument of Stiegler’s book (and of 

this introductory chapter) is that what he 

terms “telecracy” is ruining democracy by 

short-circuiting the normal mechanisms 

of politics and destroying the foundations 

of citizenship, as understood since the 

Greeks. As a result, television and the 

wider “televisual” program industries have 

become the central political issue within 

our societies.

KEYWORDS: democracy, telecracy, citizenship, media, sociation, association, dissociation

1 ON THE RIGHTS AND DUTIES OF THE CITIZEN

\> These are very difficult and often distressing times. The moment has come for society itself to mobilize, to assume its responsibilities, to “take itself in hand,” as they say. To stop offloading its problems on to those 

*[p. 3]* ^pg3

it increasingly accuses of incompetence as it in fact abandons its own competences to them. By “competences” here, I mean that set of rights and duties which makes up what we call citizenship.

The competences of the citizens are those of society in general. What then, you may ask, are the “competences” of a society? I am speaking, precisely, of the competences by which this society forms itself into a body, into a *social* body; of the competences that make it possible to produce what, at the risk of employing a neologism, I shall term *sociation*.<sup>2</sup>

Sociation is the competence of society and not just of its re presentatives. However, for a society to function, and particularly to function as a democratic society, it has to be able to delegate its will to those representatives, and to delegate it as a diversity of wishes (desires), which, though diverse, become social insofar as they produce *nonherdlike collectivity* – wishes which these representatives embody, then, and express in principle through appropriate political and social organizations. Hence, these representatives are mandated.

The delegation of competence is a major social competence of the democratic societies. But that competence can be exerted only insofar as it does not constitute a loss of competences on the part of the citizens, but rather the extension of their competences; and it can be exerted only through the pooling of those competences, that is to say, through sharing and debating them in the time-delayed mode of the political and social organizations.

Now, this time-delayed mode is what has been destroyed by the “real time” of live communications and by the “just-in-time” adjust ment of politics to public opinion, which has, as a consequence, become an *audience*. It is precisely this which characterizes telecracy. Telecracy is what eliminates political and social *organizations* – under a welter of “talk shows” and other studio-based programs, which are already, in themselves, political tele-reality<sup>3</sup> – and, with them, public opinion.

Political representatives may, then, short-circuit their constituents and, at the same time, short-circuit democracy – with fi rst demagogy, then political populism being substituted for the latter. But, more gen erally, it is the political and social representatives, from the elected re pres entatives of “the people” to the leaders in charge of the “govern ance” of companies (“concerned” or “motivated” more, perhaps, by the system of stock options than by the future of what is no longer their company, but a concession granted to them by the transient shareholders they represent), who no longer in fact represent society in its capacity to form itself into a body – that body we term social insofar as the social bond that is *philia* sustains it as desire for a common future.

2 THE SOCIAL INCOMPETENCE OF PROVIDENTIAL 

FIGURES AND THE LOSS OF PARTICIPATION

Being short-circuited as representatives of social organizations by real time and mass-media live coverage (being compelled, for example, to 

*[p. 4]* ^pg4

adapt their pronouncements to the fitful rhythms of television programs and to produce slogans rather than ideas),<sup>4</sup> the political repres entat ives tend in their turn to short-circuit the political organizations by which they are mandated. These social representatives, who no longer represent society, thereby become incompetent. Insofar as their competence is something social, it is the fact of their being representatives that constitutes it, not their mere individual competences, whether psychical (intellectual and affective), technical or consisting in any other form of “expertise.”

In this context, it is clearly tempting for politicians no longer representative of organizations to turn themselves into the representatives of society’s drives – society then projecting all its phantasms into these forms of post-political representation, embodied in a man or woman tend ing to pose as a providential figure, that is to say, as a human being whose personal destiny coincides with the destiny of her country.<sup>5</sup> *Sociation* in a democratic society is what guarantees peace, but it is also what demands the participation of everyone (of all the citizens) in political life (through the selection of representatives accountable to the organizations that mandate them). *Sociation* is the only real answer to insecurity, because insecurity is what is necessarily engendered by the loss of participation.

Loss of participation is what results from the political short-circuits occasioned by the domination of political life by the audiovisual media. That loss of participation de-legitimates political organizations, while at the same time creating a sort of political suffering. This is why political representatives are tempted to detach themselves from their organizations, short-circuit them in their turn and speak directly to that suffering, using the same techniques as those employed by the media to short-circuit political and social organizations: participation through representation is what is aped, simulated, caricatured, and destroyed by contemporary political marketing techniques – the product of telecracy and inspired by it in the form of tele-reality, “talk shows,” and other forms of pseudo-participative “interactivity” – by laying hold, as they are now doing, of blogs, Internet sites, and convergence technologies,<sup>6</sup> thus extending their range of media tools.

Political marketing, by which political misery is spread, seizes these technologies to turn them into simulacra that are the very negation of that social participation without which there could be no sociation – in other words, no social peace. And yet, alongside the association *Ars Industrialis*, which has made this an article of faith,<sup>7</sup> I believe that these digital technologies, which are manipulated today by the populists of telecratic power – those gravediggers of a democratic future – have enormous potential to counteract precisely that telecracy and effect the reconstitution of a democratic industrial society.

More precisely, I believe:

E that these technologies have a potential for entirely novel forms of associative organization;

*[p. 5]* ^pg5

E that this potential must be at the heart of a radically new political and industrial project; and

E that *it is this very potential that is masked by their exploitation by the techniques of political marketing*, preventing *the emergence of a new industrial model* that is best exemplified in the possibilities latent within this technology and which we can sense is made both possible and necessary by technological development.

This is true insofar as it is the political uses made of information and communications technologies by electoral marketing that systematically bring logics of dissociation into play, creating dissociated milieus. This is a point I began to develop in my book *Réenchanter le monde. La valeur esprit contre le populisme industriel*.

3 THOSE WHO LISTEN AND THOSE WHO SPEAK: 

SOCIATION, ASSOCIATION, DISSOCIATION

Dissociated milieus are symbolic milieus that have been industrially disorganized, that is to say, desocialized and desymbolized, and hence organizations that tend to become asocial, that is to say organizations without *philia*, without those *affective bonds* that attach those who make up the human group to one another. Aristotle teaches that these bonds are the condition for any form of political life and that they are the only ones of value (i.e. the only ones that hold good) against the vagaries of destiny.

Because these bonds are systematically destroyed by tele cracy today, the police is one of the growth industries of the future, making France potentially unlivable, like São Paolo or downtown Los Angeles: since the political bond that is *philia* no longer exists, peace has to be imposed by threat and repression – and by regression. This amounts to transforming political peace into veiled urban warfare, if not indeed civil war, this latent social violence, often provoked by vexatious action on the part of the police, sporadically actualizing itself, as already happens (including in France) in the form of riots or irrational, desperate acts of transgression or as expressed in the organization of armed gangs in the face of social disorganization – as was recently the case in Sao Paolo.

This tendentially asocial organization destroys what constitutes the very condition of political life: namely, associated milieus. Symbolic as sociated milieus, which provide the conditions for sociation, are what are destroyed by the application of the rules of the industrial division of labor to symbolic life in general: as a consequence of this industrialization of the symbolic, the members of society are separated into producers and consumers of symbols.

This telecratic organization is clearly not created intentionally to destroy the symbolic – anymore than the internal combustion engine was created to destroy the atmosphere. But, in the current state of affairs, this is the effect it actually produces (just as the internal combustion engine is tending to suffocate car drivers, not to mention the fact that these cars are, in the urban setting, becoming slower 

*[p. 6]* ^pg6

than two-wheeled vehicles, that they are paralyzing each other and will destroy the car industry if it is not transformed *quite soon* into a new industrial model).

Moreover, but more seriously, marketing, which subserves industrial economies of scale – or, in other words, the massifi cation of behavior – comes into a *de facto* contradiction with symbolic life, which is genu inely symbolic only insofar as it produces singularities through the series of exchanges in which it consists.

Language is the prime example of how a symbolic milieu is, by its structure, an associated milieu, such that it enables singularities to be constituted and expressed: in interlocution, which is the *life* of language, addressees – that is to say, listeners – cannot understand – and thus cannot have the speech they hear “addressed” to them – unless they are able in their turn to speak and become the addresser or speaker; to become the persons who speak, and speak as *no one else* could in their place.

In other words, it is possible to understand a language only inasmuch as it is possible to speak it and speak it in a singular fashion, that is to say, to employ Saussure’s terms, diachronically, and language is, in this respect, consubstantially dialogical: speech is a symbolic exchange and this exchange forms a circuit in which those who receive a symbolic address in the form of words, pass on what they have received in the form of other words to other addressees. And, in so doing, they participate equally in the trans-formation of language itself: by speaking, they produce a process of individuation. In this respect, for speakers, language represents a potential for individuation and forms what Simondon calls a “pre-individual fund.” At the same time, for linguists for example, it itself constitutes an individual, and itself, as a collective individual, is individuated: it is a concretization of the *we* formed by its speakers, who are themselves psychical individuals, *I-*s.

In language there are no addressees, that is to say, listeners, who are not *also* addressers, that is to say, speakers: only on that condition does a speaking *I* constitutes itself as *I*, that is to say, as *subject*.<sup>8</sup> The life of language is interlocution. This interlocution is what the audiovisual mass media short-circuit and destroy (thereby short-circuiting the processes of transindividuation,<sup>9</sup> which is particularly clearly observable in contemporary language), but it is also what the use of the media prescribed by the tricks of the political marketing trade simulates and fakes up through the implementation of “interactive” techniques, which are precisely the opposite of such an association productive of sociation or, in other words, productive of a future – and also, productive of desire and of singularities: of a multiplicity of desires capable of producing oneness as unity of a future.

The communications policy of Ségolène Royale is particularly inter esting in this connection. This politician’s intuition tells her that the political body suffers from a lack of participation: this is what I have termed the suffering of desire, and it is something to which she addresses herself through the medium of her blog, *Désirs d’avenir*. 

*[p. 7]* ^pg7

This latter is, however, merely a *simulacrum* of sociation: it consists in imposing on the associated symbolic milieu that is the Internet<sup>10</sup> modes of operation which are inspired by the communication techniques recently implemented by the program industries, such as that of “Star Academy,” of which Patrick Le Lay is so proud.<sup>11</sup> The “Star Academy” model, which I studied in *De la misère symbolique 2: La catastrophe du sensible*, is what makes it possible to produce *pseudo*-participation and *pseudo*-association – in other words, illusions of participation and sociation – while short-circuiting those agencies of sociation (that is to say, of transindividuation) that are the social and political organizations. In short, it is something which, in the long run, can produce only more frustration and disillusionment.

Instead of manipulating the psychological frustrations caused by symbolic impoverishment, transformed thereby into political impoverishment, Ségolène Royale would be better advised to develop a genuine political program for socializing the digital technologies, supporting systematically, through a reinvented public policy, the constitution of new forms of associated milieus; this would also be an industrial project, that is to say, a new model of political economy – the only credible option for reconstituting a desire for a future.

4 THE TIME OF THE DEMOCRATS, AS AGAINST 

GENERALIZED IRRESPONSIBILITY

Today, service companies, marketing in general and political market ing in particular, tend to impose hegemonically dissociative modes of use of the symbolic techniques and technologies through which a society always produces sociation.<sup>12</sup>

In all societies, the symbolic is constituted by exchanges which it is possible, by means of techniques, to oversee, thus establishing what is called social control. These techniques are both rites (in magical societies) or forms of worship (in religious societies) and mnemotechniques, such as writing in the Great Empires and the ancient city-states, or mnemotechnologies in industrial and hyperindustrial societies. These techniques and technologies are, then, what make possible both sociation (and what it assumes, namely the association – that is to say, participation – which the associated milieus make possible) and dissociation.

Greek society, as I have attempted to show in *Réenchanter le monde*, formed itself through a participatory socialization of alphabetic script as a technique of memory and symbolic exchange: it is through the practice of this technique that the citizen constitutes himself, and it is as it modifies the relation to language – which thus becomes *logos* – that he constitutes himself as citizen, that is to say, as subject of rights. The Sophists had already tried – like today’s industrial populism, but on a much smaller scale – to impose a dissociative use of script – and this is why the philosophers criticized and combated them, Socrates being foremost among the critics.

*[p. 8]* ^pg8

Plato shows that when the knowledge-medium that is language is transformed by the technique of writing, it tends to become exclusively an instrument of power, and threatens the life of the polity. At the same time, it is precisely the techniques of writing that make it possible to constitute a citizenship. They do so, however, through the medium of schools – that is to say, on condition that the polity agrees to make the necessary effort and investment for the technique that creates *dis*sociation to become the organization of a new system of *as*sociation – and, beyond the schools, through the Academy and the Lyceum, which, as philosophical schools founded by Plato and Aristotle and as prototypes of the universities, are in the highest degree sites of political individuation and citizenship.

Today, the primary and secondary schools and the universities are in ruins because we have failed properly to pose the question of the associated milieus and the risks of dissociation induced by the changes in the techniques and technologies of memorization, information, communication, and relationship<sup>13</sup> – and one of the main issues in the 2007 Presidential Elections was to draw the consequences from this.<sup>14</sup> Our age is confronted with the same danger sophistry represented for the Greek city-state (which knew civil war), but this danger is raised in our day to the global level. It is also faced with the same imperative of inventing a new politics of association through the reasoned socialization of the techniques and technologies of relationship – but these again are the stakes in a global struggle of an industrial nature.

Insofar as they essentially set in motion telecommunications systems (tele-graphy, tele-phony, radio/tele-diffusion, tele-vision, networks of networks, etc.), today’s technologies are, as organs of power – as tele*-* *cracy* – what threatens demo-*cracy* and undermines it from within. But, insofar as they constitute technologies for relating between the two terms linked by a *telos,* that is to say, a *finality* (a motive, a desire), these tele-technologies are also the only possible way to invent new forms of social bond and civil peace.

The time has come for democrats to show the reality of their attach ment to democracy. Society must take itself in hand and, to this end, become *associated* or, in other words, organize itself. And it must organize itself as a movement: it must set about confronting what it is threatened with – namely, an unprecedented political and social collapse.

We all feel the imminence of this risk, and we are all responsible for avoiding it. It is not enough to condemn some particular politician, male or female, for having brought us to this point, for having got us into such a mess. We must, above all, *take action*. Not destructive, furious, suicidal, or vengeful – that is to say, instinctual – action, but action that is thought-out, collectively conceived, debated, and deliberated, to overcome the very great difficulties of this age.

Taking ourselves in hand means not allowing ourselves to be lulled by the easing of existence that characterizes the societies in which we live – societies which are, for that reason, decreasingly social, if the 

*[p. 9]* ^pg9

social does, indeed, mean the fact of being associated. The relieving of the burden of existence is what leads to the renunciation of life, insofar as it is, precisely, an existence and not mere survival. The easing of existence is the product of the fact that the service economy, which is the generalized organization of dissociation,<sup>15</sup> has slowly but surely accustomed us to no longer assuming responsibility for our ways of life, having abandoned these to marketing “concepts” – including those which seek today to substitute for parents and free them from the ever greater difficulties of bringing up their children – difficulties caused by precisely that marketing, either through the morning television targeted specially at children, video games, or so many other “facilities,”<sup>16</sup> including ritalin.<sup>17</sup>

In this context, political misery consists in substituting post-political marketing concepts, hammered home in populist – in other words, irresponsible – slogans, for the political life that is *sociation* by association.

The relieving of one’s burden and of the burden of one’s existence is what hyperindustrial society has put at the heart of its economic system, and it is what produces a generalized irresponsibility, of which politicians of both sexes are merely reflections, at the same time as they are the functional translation by denaturation of a democracy that has turned into telecracy.

The title of the French Employers’ Organization’s 2005 Summer Conference suggested that the world had to be re-enchanted, and I decided to take this movement of French business at its word and propose that it reflect upon the (re)-constitution of a movement of the physical and moral persons of France in general – persons we call citizens and their social organizations. In *Réenchanter le monde: La valeur esprit contre le populisme industriel*, I offer *conceptual* analyses which seem to me capable of renewing the political, and also economic, debate – including as a business project.

Here, I propose to *translate* these questions to the level of political *action*: my project is to contribute to forming initiatives which open up alternative possibilities and, in so doing, re-launch a social criticism worthy of the name. That is to say, a criticism capable of having effects, or of influencing those who can have effect and thereby helping them to rebuild responsibility, beginning with their own.

Helping them here means assisting them, through the organization of an awareness of public opinion, to become aware themselves of a danger and find the courage to fight against telecracy and for democracy. No one has the right to dispute that those who are able, and wish, to act – the political and social representatives (however “short-circuited” they may be, and however tempted they may in turn be to short-circuit the long circuit of democracy) – are, despite their unconscious and sometimes irresponsible temptations, sincere democrats.
