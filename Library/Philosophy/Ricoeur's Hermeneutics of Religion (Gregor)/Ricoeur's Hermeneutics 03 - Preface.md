## Preface

Hermeneutics begins in the middle of things, in the concrete particularity of one’s situation. It does not assume the view from nowhere, but recognizes that one must always begin where one is and seek greater understanding from that starting point. Paul Ricoeur’s students report that he often began his seminars with a hermeneutical question: “From where do you speak?”<sup>1</sup> In the spirit of this question, then, it makes sense to open with a few words on my own starting points in reading Ricoeur.

I first read Ricoeur as an undergraduate, in the summer between my sophomore and junior years. I was a philosophy major with a strong interest in philosophy of religion, existentialism, and a growing fascination with phenomenology and hermeneutics. During a visit to a used book store in Vancouver, I came across a used, slightly beat-up copy of *The Philosophy of Paul Ricoeur*—the collection edited by Charles Reagan and David Stewart. I had heard of Ricoeur, was intrigued by what little I knew, and thought this anthology might give me a good introduction to his thinking.

Over the course of that summer, I worked through several of the essays in the book. Some of the essays on the will and language were challenging for a novice like me, but I could see there were important issues at stake—even if I couldn’t tell exactly what they were. But what really caught my attention were the essays in the last section, on religion—especially “The Critique of Religion” and “The Language of Faith.” Ricoeur’s essays on religion were something new, quite unlike the sort of Anglo-American philosophy of religion I was used to reading. Ricoeur was asking different questions, with different interlocutors and reference points. How is faith possible in our late-modern world? How can ancient religious texts continue to address us here and now? Like Kierkegaard and Pascal, whom I loved, these essays were 

*[p. xiv]* ^pg15

existentially interested, but instead of personal pathos they had a more cautious, scholarly tone and a noticeable concern with method.

I kept reading Ricoeur into the fall semester, and had a chance to put my research to work in a course on Postmodern Philosophy. Ricoeur wasn’t on the syllabus, but in most courses I used my term papers as an occasion to dig into whatever reading I was already doing on my own. I wanted to write something about religion in postmodernity, having been perplexed and vexed by our class readings of Mark C. Taylor. Taylor seemed to be doing his best to fit analytic philosophy’s caricature of Continental thought: self-indulgent free-play with an infinitely malleable text. In Ricoeur I found another, more compelling model for reading biblical texts—one that seemed to take the text seriously, but was also attentive to the complexities of meaning and the fragility of testimony.

As a Christian reader of the biblical texts, I had an existential commitment to these texts as Scripture, as the Word of God. I was also becoming aware of the complexities of our hermeneutical situation, recognizing that interpretation is always a finite, fallible, and fallen affair. And as a fledgling Christian philosopher, I was starting to see that Christianity actually gives us good reason to see hermeneutics as a blessing rather than a tragic necessity. The Christian understanding of creation shows that interpretation is part of our creaturely being, and the Incarnation—the Word made flesh—shows a God who is happy to enter into the hermeneutical situation, rather than withholding the real Truth in an eternal presence outside of time. This was an insight I was gaining through reading people like Merold Westphal and Jamie Smith, and likewise my mentors Bob Doede and Jens Zimmermann.

Two years later I started graduate school at Boston College, where I had the opportunity to study under some of Ricoeur’s students, most notably Richard Kearney and Richard Cobb-Stevens, and to engage in conversation with my fellow grad students reading Ricoeur, like James Taylor and Sebastian Purcell. This was a time of real deepening in my understanding of Ricoeur, who ended up playing a significant role in my dissertation: “*Anthropologia Crucis*: A Philosophical Anthropology of the Cross.”<sup>2</sup>

Over the past two decades Ricoeur has become, alongside Kierkegaard and Bonhoeffer, one of my go-to thinkers. My reading of Ricoeur is motivated by my own hermeneutical starting points. Like Ricoeur, I am a Protestant Christian in the Reformed tradition (his background Huguenot, my own affiliation, Presbyterian). But also like Ricoeur, my thinking is more influenced by Lutheran than Reformed theologians—in particular, the theology of the Word that comes out of Luther’s understanding of the *verbum efficax*, the efficacious word that creates and brings radically new ontological possibilities into being. This line of thinking led from Luther through Kierkegaard and Barth, as well as Heidegger and Bultmann, into subsequent theologians like Dietrich 

*[p. xv]* ^pg16

Bonhoeffer, Gerhard Ebeling, Ernst Fuchs, and Eberhard Jüngel—and more recently, Robert Jenson and Oswald Bayer. Thus I am very interested in the way Ricoeur follows in this tradition, as well as the ways he departs from it. Ricoeur offers a philosophical approximation of this theology of the Word, expanding it into a larger account of the way that religious discourse—and discourse in general—works to disclose new possibilities for being and acting in the world. For Ricoeur, the religious word addresses the human being—in imagination and desire—to regenerate the will that is bound by radical evil. The hermeneutics of religion is therefore concerned with the rebirth of the capable self. 

This book is a critical examination of this last claim. What exactly does it mean to say that religion regenerates human capability? And is this the best way for philosophy to understand religion? Given this aim, I will focus on the development of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, looking at his key philosophical and theological influences. I make no attempt to document his influence on subsequent thinkers or movements, like David Tracy and the “Chicago school” of theological method, Richard Kearney’s “anatheism,” or liberation theology—all of which bear the influence of Ricoeur’s work. My aim here is to understand and assess Ricoeur’s own project.

My reading of Ricoeur is, in Ricoeurian fashion, both sympathetic and critical. I am highly sympathetic to Ricoeur’s model of philosophical hermeneutics, and his account of imagination—particularly regarding its role in discourse—is unmatched. I also find his hermeneutical approach to the philosophy of religion a very helpful methodology, especially compared to the way most philosophy of religion operates. The hermeneutical detour through primary textual sources is an excellent model for understanding religion on its own terms, rather than starting with a priori commitments to abstract philosophical categories. 

That said, my enthusiasm for Ricoeur has never been without reservations. How exactly does Ricoeur understand the revelatory aspect of the Bible? Is it the Word of God because it is existentially significant, or is it existentially significant because it is the Word of God? What should we make of Ricoeur’s revisionary interpretations of such themes as the cross and resurrection, sin and forgiveness? And central to our concerns in this book, is the primary aim of religion really the regeneration of human *capability*? Given Ricoeur’s insistence that philosophy of religion should start from primary texts, it is a bit puzzling that he adopts this Kantian category as the hermeneutical key to understanding religion. It seems to me this category misconstrues our understanding of Christian faith, not to mention other religious traditions. If the theme of capability is present in the biblical texts, it is subordinate to a more fundamental theme: the reconciliation, union, and communion of humanity with God. 

*[p. xvi]* ^pg17

My critique aims to be immanent, drawing on Ricoeur’s own writings and methodological commitments, as well as the texts and thinkers he works with. And while I am critical, this is the fruit of my own “loving struggle” with Ricoeur’s work. My goal is to find ways to improve Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, an approach I find eminently insightful in pursuing the philosophical understanding of faith.
