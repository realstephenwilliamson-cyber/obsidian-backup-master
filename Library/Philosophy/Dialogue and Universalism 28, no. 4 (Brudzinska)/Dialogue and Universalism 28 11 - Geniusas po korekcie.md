## Geniusas po korekcie

*Saulius Geniušas* 

MUSICAL WORKS AS IDEAL OBJECTS: 

PHENOMENOLOGY OF MUSIC AND ITS IMPLICATIONS 

FOR PHILOSOPHICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

*ABSTRACT* 

In light of recent studies in the phenomenology of music, the essay engages anew in the classical phenomenological controversy over the ideal status of musical works. I argue that musical works are *bound idealities*. I maintain that the listener’s capacity to *apperceive* physical sounds as musical melodies, which can be *repeatedly* and *intersubjectively* experienced, accounts for the ideality of musical works. Conceived of as bound idealities, musical works 1) are bound to the acts that sustain them; 2) do not have retroactive validity; 3) are inseparable from their reproductions; 4) are modified by the performances. I conclude with some reflections on the importance of bound idealities for the phenomenologically-oriented philosophical anthropology. 

Keywords: phenomenology of music, Edmund Husserl, Roman Ingarden, purely intentional object, bound ideality, philosophical anthropology. 

INTRODUCTION 

As I keep writing this paper, the Kreutzer Sonata is playing in the background. At the moment, I am listening to it being performed by Itzhak Perlman and Vladimir Ashkenazy. A little while ago I heard it being performed by Jascha Heifetz and Benno Moiseiwitsch. I also recall numerous other performances of this work, although currently, the names of the performers escape my memory. There are other performances of this piece that I either have not heard, or even have not heard of. *How can one and the same musical work be heard at a number of different times, and despite such a plurality of occurrences, be recognized as one and the same?* 

The classical phenomenological answer to this question runs as follows: musical works are *ideal* objects, and it is their ideal nature that allows for them to 

*[p. 232]* ^pg234

be heard at different times and places. This answer suggests that, on the one hand, musical works must be embodied in the here and now if they are to exist. On the other hand, they must transcend the here and now if they are to be recognized as musical works. The ideal nature of musical works implies that musical works are supra-temporal and supra-individual. Yet does such a qualification suffice to clarify the ideal nature of musical works? In what follows, I want to return to these classical determinations and to present some controversies in phenomenology over the ideal status of musical works. It is probably true that, as Anthony Pryer has recently put it, this classical problem has never been fully resolved (See Pryer, 2013, 200). It nonetheless seems to me that classical phenomenology provides us with rich resources and intriguing answers to this question. 

The view that musical works are ideal objects was central to phenomenology ever since its emergence. In 1908, Husserl’s student and follower in Göttingen, Waldemar Conrad (1878–1915), published “Der ästhetische Gegenstand. Eine phänomenologische Studie.”[^1] This work is of importance first and foremost because it offered the earliest phenomenological investigation of music in print.[^2] The analysis Conrad offered was mainly based on those resources that he found in Husserl’s *Logical Investigations* (the model of *apprehension/content of apprehension*, which Husserl introduced in the *Logical Investigations*, was central to Conrad’s analysis), so much so that due to its proximity to Husserl’s own investigations, Conrad’s contribution remains to this day underappreciated, since it is thought to be nothing more than a mechanical application of Husserl’s method in aesthetics (see Angelucci, 2009, 53). 

The *Auseinandersetzung* between really existent concrete things and ideal objects was central to Conrad’s analysis.[^3] Conrad argued that the aesthetic object is “an ideal one, for which it is essential to be realizable” (Conrad, 1909, 453). Conrad further conceptualized its realization in terms of its concretization in space and time, and he maintained that even though such a concretization provides the aesthetic object with “sensuous vividness,” it does not make it a real object in the actual world. 

It thereby becomes clear that although Conrad attributed ideality to works of art, he did not aim thereby to relegate works of art to the domain of eternal truths that are reachable only through pure thinking. Rather, according to Contrad, aesthetic objects, such as works of music, are ideal objects, although of

Another of Husserl’s students in Göttingen, Roman Ingarden, refused in his conversations with Conrad to accept the view that musical works are ideal objects (see Ingarden, 1989, 10–11; see also Stratilkova, 2010, 81) and who articulated the central reasons why a mere characterization of musical works as ideal objects is unsatisfactory. Let me note in passing that in 1915, Conrad’s posthumous essay on the aesthetic attitude was published (see Conrad, 1915). This essay suggests a radical change in his thought (see Angelucci, 2009, 55). I can only conjecture that Ingarden’s critique of Conrad’s earlier view provided a strong motivation to modify his position. In what follows, I will present this critique as well as offer a response to it. In this regard, I will build on the basis of Edmund Husserl’s conception of musical works as *bound idealities*. Husserl himself has provided us only with a blueprint of such a conception, and to the best of my knowledge, to this day we lack a more precise determination of musical works as bound idealities. This is the gap that my following reflections will strive to fill. 

MUSICAL WORKS AS IDEAL OBJECTS 

Musical works must be ideal objects because they can appear in many spatiotemporal positions without losing their numerical identity—such is the most common reason, which compels one to assert that musical works are ideal objects. In contrast to real objects, musical works are neither temporally nor spatially individualized. 

Following Husserl (Husserl, 1973/1999, §65), one can draw a distinction between *embodiment* and *individualization* and say that musical works must be *embodied* in a particular time and space, they *must* make a spatiotemporal appearance if they are to exist at all. Nonetheless, their spatiotemporal embodiment is essentially different from that of real objects in that *ideal objects are not individualized by their embodiment*, i.e., they can be embodied at a number of different times, in a number of different places. This realization enables one to draw a twofold distinction between real and ideal objects. First, in contrast to real objects, ideal objects can obtain a number of different embodiments, even simultaneously. Thus, even though I cannot hold two separate conversations simultaneously, one and the same work of music can be simultaneously heard in a number of different places. Secondly, time does not affect ideal objects the way it affects real ones. If I am to see an old friend after many years of her being gone, time will leave its scars on her face, her voice, and on her general demeanor. By contrast, it is only in the imprecise and metaphorical sense that a musical work could be said to grow old. It is the real changes in the real 

*[p. 234]* ^pg236

audience and the real performers that can lead to a musical work going out of fashion, or just being forgotten. The characteristics of the musical work itself (e.g., the aging timbre of the sound of the violin) cannot bring about such changes. 

AGAINST THE IDEALITY OF MUSICAL WORKS: 

INGARDEN’S CHALLENGE 

The claim that musical works are ideal objects finds a serious phenomenological objection in Ingarden’s works. As Ingarden argues in the *Work of Music and the Problem of its Identity* as well as in the *Ontology of the Work of Art*, musical works *cannot* be regarded as ideal objects. Consider the already mentioned claim: an ideal object cannot be *temporally individualized*. If one is to take this claim seriously, one has to assert that an ideal object cannot begin its existence in real time. To be sure, the question regarding the beginning of the existence of the musical work is no less troubling than the politicized issue regarding the beginning of human existence. Does a musical work begin to exist once it is conceived in the mind of the composer, or once it is transcribed into paper, or is its beginning marked by the *Fassung letzter Hand*? Fortunately, in the present context, we can stay clear from this conflict of interpretations, for at least one thing remains uncontroversial: it is clear that musical works do begin to exist. Even though it remains unclear at what particular time one is to celebrate the birth of Mahler’s Symphony no. 5, it is nonetheless clear that it did not yet exist in the 19th century. But if musical works begin to exist in a real time, should one not say that they are temporally individualized? It seems that with performative works in general, and musical works in particular, we have stumbled against objects that break apart the established dichotomy between reality and ideality if only because they do not appear to fit in either category. Such, indeed, is the view recently defended by Anthony Pryer (Pryer, 2013). Needless to say, such an observation does not settle the matter, but only sharpens the question: how exactly are we to conceive of the ontology of musical works? Pryer’s own suggestion runs as follows: we should “free performance from the ‘tyranny’ of preexistent works,” i.e., “dissolve the supposed hierarchy between work and performance” (Pryer, 2013, 200), which he takes to mean that “there are no musical works as such, there are only musical performances” (Pryer, 2013, 212). This suggestion does not appear to provide us with a novel solution to the problem concerning the interrelation between works and their performances. It only splits the ontology of music into two ontologies, viz., the ontology of the individual performable and the ontology of the musical works (what Pryer calls “the general type ‘musicness’” (Pryer, 2013, 213). The question continues to linger: how are we to understand the ontology of the musical works? 

*[p. 235]* ^pg237

Let us not overlook an important distinction between musical works, on the one hand, and such ideal objects as mathematical theorems, on the other. While the latter are *discovered*, musical works are *created*. And thus, although it makes no sense to speak of the existence of musical works before they have been created, it makes good sense to claim that a scientific law was already valid before it was discovered. It thus seems that in contrast to scientific laws, musical works cannot be qualified as ideal. And thus, Ingarden insists that a musical work 

“may not be held to be an ‘ideal’ object. For it is not admissible to affirm of one and the same object that it is ideal and that it originates in a definite span of time from real causes. Yet there can be no question that each musical work is brought into being in a definite time span by the creative activity of the composer” (Ingarden, 1989, 10–11). 

Musical works appear to be neither real nor ideal. How, then, can their nature may be determined more precisely? Ingarden’s solution to this dilemma lies in the suggestion that musical works are *purely intentional objects*. In this regard, musical works are just like all other sorts of works of art. However, unlike many other works of art, musical works have a special structure, which concerns their unfolding from the beginning to the end. This is what musical works share with literary works. With attention to the processual nature of musical works, Ingarden contends that musical works are neither temporal, nor atemporal, but rather *supra-temporal*; he further argues that their supra-temporality relies upon their quasi-temporal structure (cf. Mitscherling, 1997, 69). 

Musical works cannot be qualified as temporally determined objects. There are at least three reasons for this. First, since such categories as *here* and *now* do not apply to them, musical works cannot be qualified as objects enduring in time. Secondly, in contrast to their performances, musical works do not begin and do not end at a particular time. This means that musical works are not temporal processes. Thirdly, musical works are not temporal events either, since events, according to Ingarden, do not extend beyond the span of a concrete now; by contrast, musical works appear to be enduring objects. Thus, musical works are neither objects enduring in time, nor events, nor processes, and we can take this to mean that musical works are not temporally determined objects. 

Nonetheless, we can say, as Jeff Mitscherling has done earlier, that even though musical works are not processes; they form “‘a Gestalt of a higher order,” and this they do in virtue of their constituting a *continuum*” (Mitscherling, 1997, 173). While a process is necessarily incomplete, a continuum is characterized by completeness. According to Ingarden, the completeness in question (that is, the completeness of the continuum of all the phases), is what points to the supra-temporality of the musical work. We can thus say that a musical work is supra-individual, that it is supra-temporal and that it has a peculiar temporal 

*[p. 236]* ^pg238

structure; and we can say this precisely because a musical work is a supra-temporal continuum. 

We can now understand that, at each and every instance of time, while we are listening to musical works, we hear not only tones, but also musical works themselves; we hear *both* precisely because tones fill out a particular phase of the continuum, thereby realizing the musical work. All these phases, taken in their completeness, make up the essential structure of the musical work. The tones we hear are temporal objects; yet all of them either are, or at least can be, determined in advance, and this predetermination is what allows us to claim that musical works are supra-temporal and supra-individual. The recognition of the supra-individual and supra-temporal nature of musical works is what enables Ingarden to account for the *identity* of musical works. 

Yet does Ingarden provide sufficient reason to give up the contention that musical works are ideal objects? It does not seem to be the case. Arguably, Ingarden’s critique of the claim that musical works are ideal objects is based on too narrow a comprehension of ideal objects. More precisely, what Ingarden’s critique does not take into account is the distinction between *free and bound idealities*.[^4] As we will soon see, it is not possible to defend the view that musical works are ideal objects without drawing a distinction between different types of idealities. And even though the distinction between free and bound idealities has already made its appearance in the context of phenomenological reflections on music, its genuine relevance remains concealed. 

MUSICAL WORKS AS BOUND IDEALITIES 

So as to make sense of this distinction, let us turn to two illustrations of free and bound idealities that Husserl provides in the *Experience and Judgment* and in “The Origins of Geometry.” In both works, logico-mathematical systems, such as, among others, the Pythagorean theorem, exemplify free idealities. By contrast, in “The Origin of Geometry,” the German word “Löwe” illustrates bound idealities. The word “Löwe” is ideal in that it cannot be spatiotemporally individualized: it can be uttered a number of times in a number of places without losing its identical sense. However, the ideality of this particular word is not free in that it is bound to a particular linguistic community. Outside the German-speaking world, this word loses its sense. Thus, this ideality is not free in

that its validity and its universality are spatio-temporally circumscribed. Similarly, in the *Experience and Judgment*, Husserl turns to a particular *civil constitution* as an example of bound idealities. On the one hand, any constitution is an ideal object insofar as its meaning can be reactivated by a number of citizens and due to these reactivations, the constitution retains its identical sense. Yet on the other hand, this ideality is not free in that its validity is not universal. Much like the German word “Löwe,” a particular civil constitution is not valid once and for all; it remains spatiotemporally bound to a particular territory, a particular time, and a particular community. 

Husserl invites us to conceive of musical works as bound, and not free, idealities. However, a detailed account of what such a characterization entails is not to be found in his writings. This is unfortunate, since the mere claim that musical works are bound idealities lends itself to a variety of interpretations. Consider, for instance, Bruce Benson’s *The Improvisation of Musical Dialogue*—a phenomenological study of musical works that interprets the distinction between free and bound idealities in the following way: 

“What he \[Husserl\] calls *free idealities* (for example, geometric theorems) have little connection to any particular historical or cultural context. One does not, for instance, need to know much about the early Greeks to be able to understand the Pythagorean theorem; one only needs to understand basic geometry. *Bound idealities*, on the other hand, are those having a particular place in cultural history, such as novels or musical works” (Benson, 2008, 7–8). 

According to Benson, to claim that musical works are bound idealities is to maintain that they are bound to a particular place in cultural history. However, I would suggest that an account that ties bound idealities to a particular socio-cultural milieu lacks justification, if only because it overlooks the distinction between *embodiment* and *individualization*—a distinction that plays a crucial role in Husserl’s account of idealities. Clearly, the Well-Tempered Clavier can be performed in a number of different cultural frameworks. At least as far as I can see, there are no principal reasons that would restrict a musical work from making a possible appearance outside the boundaries of a particular cultural history. 

What sense is one to make of musical works as bound idealities? This qualification suggests that musical works are not available “once and for all” and “for everyone” because in contrast to free idealities, they are created, but not discovered. Thus, the Kreutzer Sonata (Husserl’s favorite illustration of musical themes) is unavailable to the pre-nineteenth-century world. In contrast to free idealities, it cannot be claimed that musical works exist retroactively. Such is the first restriction that affects the embodiment of musical works. 

There are other restrictions. After being discovered, a free ideality, such as a particular heliocentric model of the universe, can be forgotten. Yet such forgetfulness

*[p. 238]* ^pg240

does not signal that the model itself loses its validity. The Earth continues to move around the Sun irrespective of what we know or do not know. By contrast, once a bound ideality, such as a musical work, is forgotten, we lose grounds to speak of its existence. If our knowledge of what the choruses of Sophocles sounded like is reducible to few theoretical abstractions, one loses the grounds to speak of their existence. One can thus say that bound idealities, such as musical works, must be sustained by concrete acts if they are to exist; and the absence of these acts signals their non-existence. More precisely, musical works, as bound idealities, are contingent upon the creative acts of the composer, reactivations of the performer, and the receptive acts of the listener. 

Such a conception of musical works as bound idealities answers Ingarden’s objection that I have spelled out above. Not all idealities are free, and thus, the mere fact that musical works have a precise beginning in objective time is not sufficient to assure us that they cannot be ideal objects. The distinction between free and bound idealities safeguards the ideal nature of musical works. 

AGAINST THE IDEALITY OF MUSICAL WORKS: 

THE HERMENEUTICAL CRITIQUE 

However, the conception of musical works as bound idealities gives rise to new problems and so as to spell them out, I would like to turn back Benson’s critique of the Husserlian approach to musical works. According to Benson, a conception of musical works as bound idealities suggests that while the task of the composer is that of defining the boundaries of the work, the task of the performer is that of reproducing the work without modifying it (hence the need to be familiar with the original context). Yet such a model, Benson goes on to say, illegitimately restricts the activities of the performer and therefore distorts the actual musical practice. Husserl notwithstanding, a performance is an interpretive and not merely a reproductive activity, and interpretation is inseparable from modifications. These modifications need not necessarily constitute deviations from the work itself; they could also constitute the work’s enrichment. But if so, then the conception of a musical work as a bound ideality suppresses the existence of the musical works; such a model illegitimately reduces the work to a fixed abstraction. 

Clearly, the legitimacy of this critique hinges upon the assertion that to regard a musical work as a bound ideality one should restrict the role of the performance to merely reproductive activity. There are good reasons to be cautious about this implication. One should not overlook that in the context of his brief analysis of bound idealities in the *Experience and Judgment*, Husserl draws a distinction between *reactivation in the proper sense* and the *merely figurative reactivation*: 

*[p. 239]* ^pg241

“We must distinguish the possibility of reactivation *in the proper sense,* by the citizen, who in his civic will bears within himself the national will and who is a functionary of this will, from the possibility of *figurative* reactivation by the outsider, for example by one who understand the constitution in a merely ‘historical’ fashion” (Husserl, 1999, 321; 1973, 266–267) 

Arguably, to conceive of a musical performance as a merely reproductive activity would amount to a merely figurative, merely historical, reactivation. Just as in the case of civic constitution, so in the case of performing musical works as well, reactivation in the proper sense is meant to realize the meaning of the work within the changing conditions, to fulfill this meaning within a new set of circumstances. But if so, then here we come across yet another important distinction between free and bound idealities. On the one hand, the meaning of free idealities, such as the Pythagorean theorem, is not affected by the different reactivating acts. On the other hand, bound idealities, such as musical works, are themselves affected by various performances in such a way that the sense of the musical works obtains new characteristics on the basis of these performances. 

Yet can such a view be supported on the basis of Husserl’s phenomenology? Does not Husserl claim, with regard to literary works, that “the one identical signification of the many exemplars of *Faust* is the ideally one *Faust*, or the significance of its many reproductions is this one unique Madonna” (Husserl, 1999, 323; 1973, 268)? By implication, should one not also say that the numerous performances notwithstanding, the musical work retains its one unique identity? With these questions in mind, I would like to turn back, at least briefly, to the question regarding the relation between musical works and their performances. 

MUSICAL WORKS AND THEIR PERFORMANCES 

Let us consider one of Husserl’s brief manuscripts from 1905, intended as a clarification of some of the themes dealt within the lecture course *Phantasy,* *Image Consciousness, and Memory*. In this text, published as “Appendix IX” to Hua XXIII, Husserl argues that actual performances of musical works are reproductions of the original. Yet what exactly is the original? “The original is the sonata just as Beethoven meant it” (Husserl, 1980, 158; 2005, 189). Is one then to say, as Benson does, that in Husserl’s view, while the composer’s task is that of producing the musical work, the task of the performer is that of faithfully reproducing it? Not necessarily, for Husserl writes: “everyone has his ideal Beethoven. Every artist interprets him differently” ((Husserl, 1980, 158; 2005, 189). In short, “the original” here need not be considered psychologistically, as a private possession of a private individual. Rather, the conception of “how Beethoven conceived the Sonata” is nothing other than the conception of how it ‘should’ be played” (Husserl, 1980, 158; Husserl, 2005, 189). 

*[p. 240]* ^pg242

The conception of the original is by no means an exclusive prerogative of the performers. Rather, each and every act of listening to a musical work already entails an implicit conception of the original. Without such a conception, no matter how implicit and inarticulate it might be, one could never proclaim that the artist has either succeeded, or failed, to bring out what is entailed in the work itself. Yet as we know, fulfillment and disappointment of such a nature all-too-often are inseparable from listening to a musical work. 

I would suggest that the distinction between the original and its reproduction is an essential and irreducible dimension of *aesthetic listening*. *Aesthetic listening is essentially two-sided*, directed both at the original work (which is ideal) and at its real reproduction. The sounds that I grasp aesthetically, I apperceive as intentions directed at the original, as attempts to express the original. I anticipate the performance to be faithful to the original, i.e., I expect the piece to be performed the way it should be performed. Moreover, since the ideal work can be performed more or less adequately, *aesthetic fulfillment* and *aesthetic disappointment* go hand-in-hand with aesthetic listening. 

Needless to say, there are other types of reproductive consciousness besides the performance of musical works. Portraits, pictures, caricatures, theater performances—they are all cases of reproductive consciousness. Yet musical performances stand out from the other types of reproductions in that hearing them performed provides us with the only available access to the works themselves. I can very well be familiar with the person represented in the portrait, picture, or a caricature; I can also familiarize myself with the play by reading it rather than seeing it on stage. Yet when it comes to musical works, we can *only* access them through listening to their real performances. We can, of course, “read” the notes in silence, yet arguably, this act of reading is a peculiar kind of internal listening. Only insofar as I am capable of internalizing the experience of listening, can reading the notes constitute a meaningful experience. Thus curiously, it is exactly what I never *really* hear—the ideal work itself—that plays the normative role in my act of aesthetic listening and thus stamps each and every performance as a more or less adequate presentation of the work itself. 

I would like to return to Husserl’s curious claim that “everyone has his ideal Beethoven. Every artist interprets him differently” (Husserl, 1980, 158; 2005, 189). I would suggest that this claim entail a curious interrelation between the musical work, conceived as a bound ideality, and its actual performances. On the one hand, as I have already argued above, to hear a performance is to be aware that what one hears is a real reproduction of an ideal work. Yet on the other hand, to claim that “everyone has his ideal Beethoven” is to suggest that our very conception of ideal works itself derives from their real reproductions. Not only for the listener, but also for the composer, the real sounds, whether actually heard or imagined, provide the phenomenal basis for the constitution of ideal works. 

We thus find ourselves in a circle: our conception of the ideal work is founded upon either actual or imagined performances; our evaluation of actual performances

*[p. 241]* ^pg243

is based upon our conception of the ideal work. On the one hand, we evaluate reproductions by comparing them with the original work. On the other hand, the very sense of the original is built upon reproductions. Such a productive circularity enriches our understanding of musical works as bound idealities. Musical works are bound to concrete performances, and in a twofold way: On the one hand, they are built upon concrete performances; on the other hand, they determine performances as reproductions of the ideal work. 

CONCLUDING REMARKS 

What, then, does it mean to conceive of musical works as bound idealities? On the basis of the foregoing analysis, I would like to single out the following characteristics: 1) To claim that musical works are bound idealities is to argue that they do not have retroactive validity. 2) It also means to suggest that musical works are bound to the concrete acts that sustain their existence, i.e., the creative act of the composer, reactivating acts of the performers, listening acts of the audience. In the absence of reactivating acts, the existence of musical works is inconceivable. 3) To qualify musical works as bound idealities is to suggest that the actual performances of musical works modify the meaning of these works. It is therefore false to assume that while the task of the composer is that of “producing” the musical work, the task of the performer is only that of reproducing it. 4) Finally, musical works are bound idealities in that they are inseparable from their reproductions. Nowhere can we encounter these works in their purity. We can only hear *real* sounds, yet insofar as these sounds are apperceived as musical, they are heard as reproductions of musical works. 

Musical works are not the only kind of works that we are to identify as bound idealities. Not only other works of art, but also other kind of human accomplishments merit the same qualification. One might consider this circumstance a limitation of the foregoing analysis, if only because it does not provide an answer to the question concerning the specific nature of musical works. I have to concede that the analysis of musical works as bound idealities remains incomplete. 

What philosophical sense can be assigned to the debate between Husserl and Ingarden on the presumably ideal status of musical works? Is there anything philosophically substantial in this debate? It is by far not clear how the qualification of musical works as purely intentional objects or as bound idealities differ from each other. Both the qualifications invite us to avoid psychologistic reductions of musical works to mental experience; so also, both the qualifications suggest that the musical work is not reducible to the score. Moreover, both the qualifications suggest that the musical work is neither a real object, nor a free ideality. According to both Husserl and Ingarden, the musical work lies 

*[p. 242]* ^pg244

somewhere in between reality and free ideality; it is just that Husserl and Ingarden qualify this ideality in different terms. Are we to conclude that the controversy over the ideal status of musical works is merely philological, that it lacks any substantial basis and that therefore it does not present us with any meaningful alternatives? 

I do not think so. This controversy invites us to reflect on the meaning, types and boundaries of ideality. Should one agree that musical works are bound idealities, one would have to significantly broaden one’s understanding of the typology of ideal objects. One could not help but would have to conclude that not all idealities are free idealities, i.e., that free idealities constitute just one type of idealities alongside bound idealities. The qualification of musical works as free idealities points to a peculiar *independence* of musical works from those acts that sustain them. The peculiar phantasies that I intend in dreams and daydreaming can also be qualified as purely intentional objects, yet these objects differ essentially from musical works, for they are fundamentally singular, unrepeatable, and unshareable. To be sure, should we cut the intentional bond that ties musical works to the purely intentional acts that sustain them, we would in the same gesture bring about the destruction of musical works. Nonetheless, precisely because musical works are *works* of a peculiar kind, their existence entails a specific kind of independence, no matter how fragile it might be, from those acts that support their existence. The existence of musical works necessarily relies upon a configuration of a large plurality of intentional acts, which appear to be of three different kinds: 1) the acts that bring about the existence of musical works; 2) those that underlie their performance, and 3) those that trigger their aesthetic appreciation. 

Let me stress here that such a broadening of the field of ideality, which emerges from the phenomenological consideration of the ideal status of musical works, carries important implications for the further unfolding of phenomenologically-oriented philosophical anthropology. Admittedly, none of the authors addressed in this paper have thematised musical works within such a framework. So also, other phenomenologists who claimed to have been developeded philosophical anthropology (I especially have in mind Max Scheler and Helmuth Plessner) did not focus either on musical works, or on other types of bound idealities. We should nonetheless ask: *what exactly are musical works,* *conceived as bound idealities, bound to?* To this question, we have to offer the following answer: they can only be bound to human existence itself. Besides human beings, there are no other beings capable of creating, performing, and appreciating musical works. These specifically human acts—creative, performative, and evaluative—indicate a specifically *human* dimension of life; they point to the human being’s capacity to raise human existence beyond the level of mere mundaneity. Through these acts, the human being does not reach the sphere of ideality whose existence precedes and whose purity transcends the boundaries of mundane life. Rather, bound ideality manifests itself as a highly 

*[p. 243]* ^pg245

paradoxical sphere: both immanent and transcendent, both mundane and ideal. It manifests itself as a specific component of a specifically *human* world. Bound ideality is a specific configuration of sense, a specifically human creation, which lifts human life, conceived as natural life, beyond the boundaries of its merely natural existence. *What kind of being is the human being if she/he is to be capable of constituting such a dimension of existence?* A detailed answer to this question cannot be offered in the framework of this paper. I have to limit myself to a rather general observation: the bond that ties human beings to each other at the level of bound ideality is by no means rare. Quite on the contrary: it points to a specifically human form of intersubjective life. It is *this* very bond that makes that form of intersubjectivity, which is constitutive of specifically *human* existence, possible. It underlies the possibility of specifically human identity—personal, social and historical.
