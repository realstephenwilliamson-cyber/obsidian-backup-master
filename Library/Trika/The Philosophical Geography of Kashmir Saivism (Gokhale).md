The Philosophical Geography of Kashmir Śaivism 

Pradeep P Gokhale Central University of Tibetan Studies, Sarnath I have coined the title of the paper after Gilbert Ryle’s expression ‘Logical Geography of Concepts’, which he introduces in his magnum opus “The Concept of Mind”. Determining the logical geography of a concept, for Ryle, means to find out how the propositions in which the concept occurs are logically related with other propositions; with which other propositions they are consistent, and with which they are not. Similarly, in this paper I will try to locate Kashmir Śaivism on the map of Indian Philosophy. I will try to see how it is conceptually related with some other philosophical systems of India. To what extent it goes together with others and to what extent it does not. 

The map of Indian philosophy is a complex of diverse systems. The diverse interconnections among them may be best described in the way Īśvarakṛṣṇa in *Sāṅkhyakārikā guṇa*s: 

describes the interrelation among the three *anyonyābhibhavāśraya-janana-mithuna-vṛttayas tu guṇāḥ*. 

According to this description, the strands of *Prakṛti* tend to dominate each other, draw on each other, become sources of each other and join each other to form couples. *Darśana*s of Indian philosophy relate with each other in a similar way. Hence, we have orthodox versus heterodox systems, theistic versus atheistic systems, dualistic versus non-dualistic systems, realistic versus idealistic systems which try to dominate each other. Sāṅkhya and Yoga, and similarly Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika are mutually supporting systems, and they also join each other in forming couples. Similarly, Pūrvamīmāṁsā and Uttaramīmāṁsā support each other in some respects and oppose each other in other. On this complex background, it may be asked: “Where can Kashmir Śaivism be located? In what way is it related to the other systems? To what extent is it compatible and to what extent incompatible with other systems?” These are some of the questions that I will try to address in this paper. 

*[p. 2]* ^pg2

By the term “Kashmir Śaivism”, I mean the philosophical system which emerges from the Śaiva āgamas and tantras and assumes a systematic form through the works of Vasugupta, Somānanda, Utpaladeva and Abhinavaupta. Śaivism as a religion or a religious sect is a complex phenomenon. But here I am not concerned with that aspect. Even as a philosophy, I am not concerned with all the doctrines and concepts involved in it. I restrict myself to those aspects which can relate Kashmir Śaivism with other philosophical systems. 

Kashmir Śaivism: Vedic or Non-Vedic? 

 An important question, in this respect, would be as to how we locate Kashmir Śaivism between the two rival camps: Orthodox and Heterodox. Kashmir Śaivism cannot be counted among the orthodox systems because its authoritative text is not the Vedas but texts like the Tantras and the Ᾱgamas which have their own independent tradition. 

Some scholars have pointed out the affinity and interaction between the Ᾱgamic and the Vedic tradition at ritualistic as well as metaphysical levels. G. V. Tagare, following K. C. Pande, points out how Śaṅkara, the Vedānta philosopher followed the Tantric practice of *Śrīcakra* worship and how his visit to Kashmir must have influenced the Tantric form of Śiva worship in Kashmir. He also points out how, many ideas in Śaṅkara’s *Dakṣiṇāmūrti-stotra* resonate with Utpaladeva’s *Īśvara-pratyabhijñā-kārikā*.[^1] 

But in this process of correlating the two systems one is likely to ignore the important difference between the Vedic tradition and the Ᾱgamic one, which is essentially social. The Vedic tradition is rooted in and supported by the views of the upper caste men, whereas the Tāntric/Ᾱgamic tradition is rooted in and supported by common people’s religious practices. As it is said in *Tantrālokaviveka*, systems such as the Vedic system emphasize differences (*bheda*) whereas systems such as Śaivism emphasize non-difference (*abheda*).[^2] The term *bheda* here refers not only to difference but also to a discriminatory and hierarchical social approach. Kashmir Śaivism: A Para-Vedic System? 

Of course, accommodating the rival tradition, while maintaining one’s own identity, creates a tension. Kashmir Śaivas try to resolve the tension by proposing a hierarchy of *pramāṇa*s in which Ᾱgamas have the highest status whereas the Vedas have a lower status. Abhinavagupta, following *Mālinīvijayatantra*, points out that though both the Vedas and the Śaiva Ᾱgamas are authored by the lord Śiva, the latter are superior to the former because the Ᾱgamas do not make the rules of purity and impurity (*śuddhi-aśuddhi*) applicable at the stage of embodied liberation (*jīvanmukti*), whereas the Vedas do prescribe such rules applicable even at that stage.[^7] In *Tantrālokaviveka,* Jayaratha brings out the distinction between the two traditions in terms of exclusiveness and inclusiveness (*saṅkoca* and *asaṅkoca*). The Śaiva Ᾱgamas are thought to be superior because they are inclusive in their approach.[^8] In *Tantrāloka* Abhinava says that in religious conduct one should be a Kaula and Śaiva, in worldly conduct, however, one can be a Vaidika. This suggests that in worldly practices Abhinavagupta was ready to compromise with the discriminatory culture prescribed by the Vedas and the Smṛtis, but in religious/spiritual matters he was insisting on the non-discriminatory path prescribed by the Ᾱgamas and the Tantras.[^9] 

Hence Kashmir Śaivism cannot be called Vedic because the main text of the school is other than Vedas. But it cannot be called non-Vedic or anti-Vedic, because it regards the Vedas too as a divine creation. The Metaphysical Map of India 

However, it may be argued that the question, whether a system is Vedic or non-Vedic or Para-Vedic, has social significance, but the core philosophical and metaphysical issues cut across these distinctions. So now let us consider the question as to where Kashmir Śaivism can be located on the metaphysical map of India. Among the metaphysical systems of India, Lokāyata marks an extreme, which accepts neither soul nor the cycle of rebirth and redeath. Next to Lokāyata is Buddhism, which accepts the cycle of rebirth and redeath, but not the soul. All other systems including Kashmir Śaivism accept both the soul and its transmigration in some way. So now the question is where we can locate Kashmir Śaivism among them. 

Three Major Paradigms 

The metaphysical scene in India can be studied in the light of three paradigms: the Sāṅkhya paradigm, the Vaiśeṣika paradigm and the Theistic paradigm. The first two paradigms indicate two ways of explaining the empirical world while the theistic paradigm represents the tendency to bring the whole world under a single governing principle. 

The Vaiśeṣika and Sāṅkhya paradigms represent two different types of metaphysical thinking. I want to suggest that almost every metaphysical system of India chooses one of them and develops its ontology in its light. Hence it is possible to regroup the soul-affirming systems into two camps: the Vaiśeṣika camp and the Sāṅkhya camp. 

Vaiśeṣika and Sāṅkhya: Difference between the two paradigms 

The Vaiśeṣika and Sāṅkhya paradigms radically differ from each other along the following axes: 

*[p. 6]* ^pg6

(1) It is well-known that the theories of causation they accept are contrary to each other. According to the Vaiśeṣika theory an effect is not pre-existent in the cause but is a new phenomenon. According to Sāṅkhya on the other hand any product is potentially or non-manifestly present in its cause. It is never entirely new, but only a new form assumed by the same old material. 

(2) These two theories of causation are not just two different descriptions of the same phenomena. They have implications for the creative processes that take place in the world. According to Vaiśeṣikas the material substances (earth, water, fire and air) originally exist in an atomic form. Subsequently they combine and these combinations acquire newer qualities. According to Sāṅkhya the process starts with nature consisting of subtle psychic elements, namely pleasure, pain and delusion, coexisting in a state of equilibrium and when the equilibrium is disturbed, the combination of the psychic elements manifests itself in ever grosser forms such that the five material substances appear on the scene as part of the final manifestations. An important point here is that for Sāṅkhya the process of creation is only a process of manifestation. 

(3) Accordingly, for Sāṅkhya, the causal process is a process of manifestation or unfolding, which passes from subtle to gross, from psychic to material, from non-manifest to manifest. Hence the subtle objects of the senses, namely odour, taste, colour, touch and sound, join together to produce the gross material elements, namely earth, water, fire, air and ether. According to Vaiśeṣika, it is the other way round. Atoms of the material elements of earth, water, fire and air and also the eternal substance called ether (*ākāśa*) are ‘the given’ of the world. Their sensible qualities are the emergent phenomena. 

*[p. 7]* ^pg7

(4) Both the systems accept the duality of soul and body. (By ‘soul’ I mean ‘*ātman*’ according to Vaiśeṣika and ‘*puruṣa*’ according to Sāṅkhya.) But they conceptualize the soul in different ways. The *ātman* of Vaiśeṣika does not have consciousness as its essence, but all the psychic qualities such as cognition, pleasure, pain, desire and aversion arise only in *ātman* as the latter’s contingent qualities. The *puruṣa* of Sāṅkhya on the other hand has (pure) consciousness as its essence, but the psychic phenomena such as cognition, pleasure, pain, desire and aversion do not belong to *puruṣa*, but to *prakṛti*, or its evolute, *buddhi*. 

With regard to all other soul-affirming systems of Indian philosophy it can be said that each system is influenced by one of the two paradigms due to which the metaphysics of soul-affirming systems gets dichotomized.

After *Māyā* and its accessories we have all the twenty-five *tattva*s of Sāṅkhya which are the constituents of the empirical world. 

Transformation of Sāṅkhya *tattva*s: 

*[p. 11]* ^pg11

 Though Kashmir Śaivism accepts all the Sāṅkhya *tattva*s - it subordinates them to the non-dualistic nature of ultimate reality. Hence it transforms them. Let us consider the major transformations it brings about: 

Two types of causation: Sāṅkhya regards *Prakṛti* (*Mūlaprakṛti*) as the (1) 

cause of the whole universe. From *Prakṛti* arises *Buddhi*, from *Buddhi* arises *Ahaṁkāra* and so on. But now according to Kashmir Śaivism, *Paramaśiva* with His creative power is the root cause of the universe. Abhinavagupta tries to accommodate both these causal processes in a hierarchical order. He says that there is twofold causality: Ultimate and Created. (*Pāramārthika* and *Sṛṣṭa*) The ultimate causality is the divine causality and the other causality is that which the Lord creates through the power called *Niyati*.[^19] Two types of ignorance: According to Sāṅkhya, *vivekajñāna,* that is, (2) 

the knowledge that discriminates between *prakṛti* and *puruṣa* causes liberation whereas non-discrimination, that is, *aviveka* or *ajñāna*, causes bondage. (*kevalī* and *mukta*). This whole picture changes when the Sāṅkhya *tattva*s become subservient to Śaiva metaphysics. Śaiva systems and Vedānta systems: Parallel Diversity 

As a monistic or non-dualistic system Kashmir Śaivism can be compared with other theistic/monistic metaphysical systems. 

The first point which can be noted in this context is that just as under Vedānta we have different forms of theistic metaphysics - Absolute non-dualism, Dualism and intermediate systems which assume different forms - Qualified Non-dualism (Viśiṣṭādvaita), Vedānta of difference and non-difference (Bhedābhedavāda) and so on; in a parallel way, in Āgamic-tantric Śaivism also we notice these three varieties. We have non-dualism which Kashmir Śaivism advocates. We have dualism advocated by what is called Siddhānta Śavism. And we also have an intermediate Dvaitādvaita school of Śaivism represented by Lakutīśa-pāśupata-darśana and Śaktiviśiṣṭādvaita found in Śrīkaṇṭha’s *Brahmamīmāṁsābhāṣya*.[^22]

The second important point in this context is that although there is a broad similarity between the two spectra of Vedānta and Śaiva metaphysical systems, when you go deeper, many points of difference may come to surface. This is very true of Kevalādvaita Vedānta and Kashmir Śaivism. For both, the ultimate reality is pure consciousness - *citi* or *caitanya*. Both of them accept the role of *māyā* in the concealment of the identity between the individual self and the ultimate reality. Both explain the relation between the ultimate reality and the world in terms of *ābhāsavāda*[^23] and *pratibimbavāda*. But apart from these similarities there are important differences between the two metaphysical systems and perhaps they are more important than the similarities. Some of the major differences are as follows: 

(1) In Kevalādvaita, the identity between Brahman and *Jīvātman* and also that between Brahman and the world; is reductive or eliminative. “I am Brahman” means “I do not exist, only Brahman does.” “Everything is Brahman” means “Only Brahman exists, nothing else really exists.” In Kashmir Śaivism the identity is an inclusive identity. That is why ‘I am Śiva’ is understood as Self-recognition, that is, *Pratyabijñā*, where I and Śiva, both are real and yet identical. Similarly, creation of the world is not a false superimposition on the Reality, but it is a free play (*līlā,* *krīḍā*) of Śiva. 

(2) The Brahman of Kevalādvaita is isolated (*kevala*) and absolutely non-changing (*kūṭasthanitya*). *Śiva* is always accompanied by *Śakti* which represents the power of *Śiva* to create everything, know everything and so on; which is expressed through its active and dynamic aspect, namely vibration (*spanda)*. 

[^23]: Even if the terminology is the same, meaning differs. In Kevalādvaita ābhāsa refers to false appearance, whereas in Śaivism it refers to manifestation.(See, Tagare (2002), p. 37). Similar is the case with pratibimbavāda.

*[p. 14]* ^pg14

(3) For Kevalādvaita, regarding oneself and the world as real is a part of the basic misconception or *mithyājñāna* which causes bondage. Hence complete detachment from worldly and otherworldly pleasures (*ihāmutraphalabhogavirāga*) is a necessary prerequisite of the knowledge of Brahman. For Kashmir Śaivism, oneself, the world and all forms of life are manifestations of *Paramaśiva*. So, these manifestations are enjoyable as manifestations of *Śiva*. Hence one should not reject them or detach from them. This leads to a welcoming approach to life in its various forms. 

Kashmir Śaivism and Vyākaraṇa-darśana 

Apart from Kevalādvaita, there are two Indian idealistic systems, with which Kashmir Śaivism has a special conceptual relation. One is the Śabādvaita system of Vyākaraṇa as developed by Bhartṛhari and the other is Yogācāra Buddhism. Abhinavagupta criticizes both, but incorporates some of their insights in his formulation of Śaivism. 

The impact of Vyākaraṇa-darśana on Kashmir Śaivism is visible at least at three levels.

Kashmir Śaivism and Yogācāra Buddhism 

Like the Vyākaraṇa school, Buddhism too has a complex relation with Kashmir Śaivism. Buddhism is known for its atheistic position, denial of the self and emphasis on impermanence. Śaivism is opposed to Buddhism in all these respects. But there are two points on which the two schools meet. One meeting point is Idealism. According to Yogācāra Buddhism, nothing exists beyond consciousness. The same is the position of Kashmir Śaivsim. The second meeting point is epistemological. Abhinavagupta follows the Buddhist epistemology of 

*[p. 18]* ^pg18

Dignāga and Dharmakīrti at least in two respects. Firstly, he regards nonconstructive perception - *nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa* as the highest form of knowledge. Secondly the self-illuminating nature of consciousness is a common feature between Buddhist and Śaiva epistemology. 

However, one difference emerges at this level, particularly with respect to *savikalpaka* and *nirvikalpaka* cognition. Buddhist epistemologists generally have a negative approach to *vikalpa*s. *Vikalpa*s are false constructions and hence they distort the true nature of reality. Abhinavagupta, on the other hand, develops a positive approach towards *vikalpa*s. In *Tantrasāra* he explains *sattarka* (right thinking)[^32] as the first limb of Yoga, which consists of developing proper *vikalpa*s. From this state of *vikalpa*s one progresses towards the *nirvikalpaka* state not by abandoning all *vikalpa*s, but by processing or modifying them. This method of processing or modifying *vikalpa*s is called *vikalpa-saṁskāra*[^33]. Hence the ultimate goal of *Avikalpa* reality is to be achieved not by abandoning *vikalpa*s, but by processing and modifying them. 

Before I complete this journey, I would like to mention two filiations of Kashmir Śaivism: one with the Gītā and the other with the philosophy of the Marathi saint Jñānadeva.

Kashmir Śaivism and Jñānadeva 

 I will conclude by pointing out that Kashmir Śaivism is specifically relevant to the philosophy of the saints of Maharashtra. The works of Jñānadeva, particularly his Cāngadevapāsaṣṭī and Anubhavāmṛta, testify to the fact that he was under the influence of Kashmir Śaivism.[^41] His doctrine of the world being a manifestation of *vastu*[^42], which is popularly called Cidvilāsavāda, distinguishes it from Kevalādvaita which regards the world as a manifestation of *Māyā*. His notion of *śiva-śakti-samāveśa* refers to a special type of Advaitic relation between *Śiva* and *Śakti* which is called *sāmarasya*.[^43] In *Anubhavāmṛta* Jñānadeva presents a refutation of *ajñāna* as well as that of *jñāna*. While refuting *jñāna* he refers to Vasugupta’s statement from *Śiva-sūtra*, namely “knowledge is bondage” (“*jñānaṁ bandhaḥ*”)[^44]. These and other evidences clearly indicate the influence of Kashmir Śaivism on Jñānadeva. In fact, Jñānadeva synthesizes Śaivism with Vedāntism and the Bhakti tradition.[^45] In this synthesis Śaivism seems to help Jñānadeva in distancing himself from Brahmanism.
