4.2 The Brahmayāmala’s adaptation of the Origin of the Lingam (Zngodbhava) myth

4.2.1 The Sivadharmaśāstra and the Origin of the Lingam Chapter (adhyāya) three of the Sivadharmaśāstra preserves a comparatively early version of the ‘Origin of the Lingam (\#ñgodbhava) tale. I would suggest that the redactors of the Bra Yā have in part modelled their narrative upon this, directly or indirectly. While it was not their only source of inspiration, the influence appears substantial. This debt is not unique, for numerous other tellings on this theme seem likewise indebted to the Sivadharmasāstra.

4-2.2 The luminous lingam and divine altercations

The Origin of the Lingam (\#āgodbhava) episode of the BraYa forms only one layer of the chapter's narrative. Taken as a whole, the chapter stands apart from the Sivadharmasśāstra and major purānas, for tales of the Origin of the Lingam and the Skull of Brahmā, treated in Bra Ya 83.144c—63, are not elsewhere brought together so explicitly. Nonetheless, themes from these myths do blend together in some of their purāṇic tellings, as I will show, and it is possible that the Bra Yā knew sources which did combine them. In this section I examine /ingodbhava narratives from a wider range of literature, elucidating the development of the narrative, its intersections with the other myths, and its adaptation in the Bra Yā.

As outlined in \$1.6, the BraYāSāra knew and drew upon the larger recension of the BraYā, of which it claims to be the “essence” (sāra). Concerning the issue at hand, two scenarios seem most plausible: first, that the Bra YaSāra drew upon the BraYā, and that the Devīpurānṇa subsequently drew upon the BraYāSāra, with no direct recourse to the BraYā. A second possibility is that the Devīpurāna drew directly from the BraYā, while the Bra YāSāra drew upon both the BraYā and the Devīpurāna. In this second scenario, the redactors of the BraYāSāra would have calqued chapter 68 upon chapter 83 of the Bra Ya, chosing, however to substitute some of their source-text with parallel material from the Devīpurāna 119—possibly because this was well-known and appealing. I see no compelling reason to entertain additional, more complex scenarios. There can in any case be little doubt that the Devipurāna has extracted the tale of Khatvāsura from one of the two recensions of the BraYā and inserted this, somewhat awkwardly, into its tale-cycle concerning the anti-god. After demonstrating this, I will return to the question of which of the two redactional scenarios is more likely.
