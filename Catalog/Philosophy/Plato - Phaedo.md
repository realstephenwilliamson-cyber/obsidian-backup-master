## About

A dialogue by Plato on the immortality of the soul, set on the day of Socrates' death. Phaedo of Elis tells Echecrates how Socrates spent his last hours in argument with his friends, and how he died.

- **People:** in the frame, Phaedo and Echecrates, a Pythagorean of Phlius, where the story is told; in the narrated scene, Socrates, the Thebans Simmias and Cebes (pupils of the Pythagorean Philolaus), Crito, and briefly Xanthippe, among others. Plato was absent: "Plato, if I am not mistaken, was ill."
- **Dramatic date:** 399 BC, in the Athenian prison. The execution had been put off while the sacred ship was away at Delos.
- **Composition:** no exact date. The IEP places it in the middle group, c. 380–360 BC, with the *Symposium* and *Republic* II–X, but notes that stylometry tends to count it among the early dialogues. Copleston puts it between Plato's first and second Sicilian journeys; Jowett called it intermediate. These sources treat it as genuine.
- **Arguments:** the cyclical argument (the living come from the dead, as opposites from opposites); recollection (learning is recalling what the soul knew before birth); affinity (the soul is akin to the invisible and divine); and a final proof from the Forms: soul brings life and cannot admit death. Between them come Simmias's objection (the soul as a lyre's harmony) and Cebes's (a weaver who outlasts many cloaks). A myth of the afterlife and the account of the execution close the work.
- **Place among the dialogues:** in Thrasyllus's first tetralogy as *Phaedo, or On the Soul*, after *Euthyphro*, *Apology* and *Crito* (Diogenes Laertius); the last of the dialogues on Socrates' final days. Stephanus pages 57a–118a.
- **Why it matters:** it was much read in antiquity: the main surviving commentaries are by Olympiodorus and Damascius, and Gregory of Nyssa modelled his dialogue on the soul and resurrection on it. Wikipedia traces to it Western pairings such as soul and body, reason and emotion.
