## PART III: New paths to India: 19th century

*[p. 170]* ^pg183

### 7. Samson Bloch: Indian philosophy and priestly system

The 18th century closed with the publication of two Jewish books that summarized some of the new secular knowledge of the time in the fields of physics, chemistry, mathematics, technological inventions, natural history, and foreign countries, but said little or nothing about India. The chapters on geography in Baruch Lindau’s *Reshit Limmudim* (1789), a Hebrew textbook on general secular knowledge, were limited to Europe and Turkey. India did appear in half a page of Phinehas Elijah Hurwitz’s widely read *Sefer Ha-Brit* (1797) as brief notes about “the great city” of Calicut under English rule, the large number of Jews in Malabar, and the Indian belief in human transmigration into animals. This belief takes the form of a garbled version of Menasseh ben Israel’s picture of vegan Indians who held the horns of dying oxen in order to be reborn as an ox.<sup>1</sup> In 1822, however, Samson Bloch published the first volume of his new book on world geography, titled *Paths of the World*, devoting this volume entirely to the continent of Asia. His chapter on India, influenced by Enlightenment values, gave more attention to the sub-continent than had any previous Jewish text, and was the first to turn openly to German literature on India to provide a new and fuller picture of its people and natural history. India thereby became part of the science of geography that European Jews were beginning to study in the 19th century.

Bloch’s intellectual journey

*Shevilei \`Olam* (Paths of the World) is a world geography written in a florid biblical Hebrew and published in several volumes between 1822 and 1856 in Galicia. It attracted a large Jewish readership, especially in Eastern Europe, and even into the 20th century, and was republished several times and translated into Yiddish.<sup>2</sup> The author, Samson Bloch (1785–1840), was a major figure among the 19th-century Galician exponents of the Jewish intellectual and social movement called *Haskalah*, the so-called Jewish Enlightenment.

Like many other members of this movement (who called themselves *maskilim*), Bloch received a rich rabbinic education but eventually rejected 

*[p. 172]* ^pg185

it for its ignorant narrowness. In the introduction to *Shevilei \`Olam*, he imagines himself wandering blindly in traditional rabbinic paths, or really without a path, and then setting out on new paths to the outside world, a new intellectual and moral journey. Bloch tells his readers that his dear father had educated him “in the paths of knowledge of the holy writings,” in “the truths of Scripture and the sea of Talmud,” yet he had not truly known God at that time. When he called out to God, he was merely parroting empty words, for he was “a wild hollow man” who could not recognize God’s paths or “observe the wonder of His wonders.” He read the Bible’s images of nature literally.

I imagined that from the wall of the city of my birth and outward, the sky was like a tent to dwell in like a spread curtain of alabaster and marble \[from [[Isaiah 40#^40-22|Isa 40.22]]\], and I thought the stars in their pathways were burnished bronze nails firmly fixed \[[[Ecclesiastes 12#^12-1|Eccl 12.1]]\] in their splendor and brilliance sparkling over the heights of the skies.

Bloch pictures this time in his life as groping in a dark wilderness with blind eyes, like the other Jews that he knew. His soul had sunk into “the depths of foolishness” (*petayut*), until one day at his uncle’s house, he discovered “wisdom and knowledge,” by which he meant Haskalah writings on the secular sciences. “With joy and exultation, her \[wisdom’s\] beauty and power flowed over me,” and Bloch’s world changed “from darkness to light and from shadows to order” (echoing [[Job 10#^10-22|Job. 10:22]]). His goal then became that of “saving my nation,” which without such knowledge, “wandered without a path; the path for my people is knowledge.” He would make known to them this “path of knowledge and moral education (*musar*).” His book would contain “knowledge for understanding all the lands of the world.” Bloch claims, however, that his book is only following in the footsteps of the great scholars Maimonides and Elijah, the Gaon of Vilna (1720–1797). Bloch quotes Elijah’s disciple Baruch ben Jacob (Shklov), who proclaimed in the Gaon’s name that without knowledge of the sciences there cannot be wisdom of Torah, “for the Torah and the sciences are connected together.”<sup>3</sup> As for Maimonides, *maskilim* had rediscovered his *Guide for the Perplexed*, which had been neglected in European Jewish scholarship for several centuries. The Haskalah embraced the book as a model of strict rationality and freedom from superstition.<sup>4</sup>

Bloch’s introduction to *Paths of the World*, and particularly his goal of saving his people through “the path of knowledge,” reflect many of the goals of the Haskalah, which had been developing since the time of Moses Mendelssohn and Israel Samoscz in the middle of the 18th century, first mainly in Central Europe but gradually also in the Jewish communities of Eastern Europe, and in many styles and versions. Shmuel Feiner summed up “the common lines” of the Haskalah movement as “a sense of disquietude with cultural backwardness in the era of significant development in 

*[p. 173]* ^pg186

Christian Europe; a tremendous passion for secular knowledge; criticism of the limited range of the \[Jewish\] religious library; … high regard for the Hebrew language.” From the beginning of the Haskalah, its proponents had been reshaping “the Jewish library” by writing “books in Hebrew on language, astronomy, mathematics, medicine, and philosophy.”<sup>5</sup>

In a recent article on scientific writings composed by *maskilim* in the first half of the 19th century, Tal Kogman situates Bloch’s book in the framework of several other books aimed at introducing European Jews to the latest knowledge about physics, chemistry, mathematics, technological inventions, plants, wildlife, and foreign countries.<sup>6</sup> These works built upon earlier scientific textbooks of the Haskalah, foremost among them Baruch Lindau’s *Reshit Limmudim* (First Studies) of 1789, which introduced readers to a wide range of secular knowledge, especially “natural history.”<sup>7</sup> Bloch intended his book as an expansion of Lindau’s section on geography, but he made use of many additional sources, not only Jewish ones but mainly works in German, and he often names his sources explicitly to show that he gathered his information from reliable scholars of the world. One of them, for example, Eberhard August Wilhelm von Zimmermann (1743–1815), a geographer, zoologist, and traveler, provided Bloch with the latest botanical discoveries; Bloch’s description of elephants in India comes from von Zimmerman.

Bloch explains that his book begins with Asia because “most of the events of the holy writings occurred there.” But since this first volume includes lands farther east than Arabia and Persia, where ancient Jewish events occurred, one might wonder what he expects his readers to learn from his descriptions of India, Tibet, China, and Japan. Part of the answer appears in Bloch’s general claim, in his introduction, that learning about nature would enable Jews to understand the wonders of God’s creation. Bloch’s descriptions of foreign flora, fauna, and landscapes would serve this function. But what did he want his readers to learn from his accounts of the government, languages, crafts, and beliefs of these faraway lands? He often pointed to foreign parallels with Jewish history or society, as when he compared Persian with Jewish customs or showed that Jews, Indians, and the Chinese teach the same ethical principles. His readers could recognize shared values in foreign forms. But as we will see in the case of India, Bloch also identified errors and deficiencies in foreign societies, with the purpose of teaching his readers lessons in correct thinking and *musar* (morality) – “the path of understanding and morality,” as he wrote in his introduction.

In the history of Jewish study of foreign lands, Bloch was an intellectual pioneer. *Shevilei \`Olam* was the first Jewish geography book to cover Asia and Africa extensively. The volume on Asia contains chapters on Arabia, Persia, India, China, Tibet, Japan, and briefly Burma and Siam. He organized each chapter logically under the headings of geographical topics. In the case of India, these were the Land, including climate and rivers; Growth, which included a long discussion of animals; Inhabitants 

*[p. 174]* ^pg187

and their ways of life; Food; Languages and Knowledge; and Government. Although this organization appears systematic, Bloch actually mixed a variety of subtopics and stories within the general categories and gave more space to exotic subjects that fascinated him, such as crocodiles, elephants, and snakes. A comparison of the space he gave to natural history (land, rivers, climate, flora, and fauna) with the space, double in amount, devoted to Indian society, culture, and government shows his fascination with foreign ways of life.

He begins his chapter on India with extravagant praise for the land, weaving together phrases of high biblical poetry that originally referred to the Land of Israel.

In the excellence of the produce of the sun and fruit of the moon it \[India\] is an ornament to all the lands; for it is a pleasant land, good and broad, a land planted and settled from ancient of ancient days.<sup>8</sup> A land with Nature’s eyes upon it, founded from the beginning to make it a garden of Edens \[sic\] in the world, and a crown of beauty and a joy of the whole earth; for only upon it did she \[Nature\] empty the cup of her beauty, splendor, and majesty, only into its bosom did she sprinkle the plenteous rains yielding wealth and the blessing of the heavens in abundance.<sup>9</sup>

Bloch also praises the inhabitants of India as “good of heart and people of kindness,”<sup>10</sup> later adding that “All their customs lead them in pleasantness and humble righteousness.”<sup>11</sup>

After recounting in a less flowery Hebrew the natural history of India, he moves to the topic of India’s inhabitants, which begins his section on Indian society and culture. Within that section, what we call “religion” accounts for 60% of it if we include his discussion of the Brahman caste.<sup>12</sup> Much of Bloch’s picture of Indian beliefs and rituals seems to reflect, in very succinct form, the picture presented in J. F. Kleuker’s book, *Das brah* *manische Religionssystem*. But small elements of Bloch’s picture come from Jewish writings such as Menasseh ben Israel’s story of the hospitals for animals and Judah Halevi’s polemical paragraph in the *Kuzari*. All in all, Bloch composed a much fuller view of Indian beliefs and practices than had appeared in any earlier Jewish texts while also including some of those earlier views.

Bloch’s picture of Indian religion (*dat*)

Beginning with doctrine, he tells his readers that “for thousands of years the sages of India believed in the oneness of the Creator, be He praised, and in the survival of the soul, and some of them spread ideas of the truth of the Name \[God\] to the rest of Asia.”<sup>13</sup> This sounds like simple monotheism, but Bloch explicates it in medieval philosophical language:

*[p. 175]* ^pg188

According to the principles of their religion (*dat*), the Brahmans believe in the reality of a sublime essence (*\`etzem nishgav*), which is Karta or Parabrahma, who is the creator and the preserver and the destroyer of everything, and who is described in sublime and true attributes like the scholars of the nations around us \[in Europe\]; but in the eyes of the masses, they divide them \[the attributes\] into three beings that compose this sublime essence. The first is likewise called Brahma and he is the creator. Vishnu is the second and he is the preserver. Shiva is the third and he is the destroyer…. The paramount foundation of their belief is a single primordial reality (*metzi’ut yah. id veqadmon*) without beginning or end, which favors justice and uprightness over burnt offerings and sacrifice, and this is the aforementioned Karta.<sup>14</sup>

Bloch explains that this one sublime essence, “the God (*ha-’elil*) Parabrahma,” is never worshipped in temples because “they say that His glory cannot be glorified in a place, The heavens cannot contain him, how much less a house, tabernacle, or tent.”<sup>15</sup> Bloch uses the term *‘elil* in a non-judgmental way, quite different from its pejorative biblical meaning,<sup>16</sup> and invents a new word, *‘elilah*, for “goddess.”<sup>17</sup>

Bloch has assimilated the Indian ideas that he found in his German source (already assimilated into European philosophy) into Jewish biblical and philosophical forms. He paraphrases [[I Kings 08#^8-27|1 Kings 8:27]], in which Solomon says of God, “The heaven of heavens cannot contain you, how much less this house that I have built,” to explain why Indians do not worship Parabrahma in temples, thus pointing to a similarity in Jewish and Indian conceptions of God’s transcendence. Moreover, he asserts that Parabrahma, like the God of the biblical prophets, demands justice rather than “burnt offerings and sacrifice.”<sup>18</sup> And the “sublime essence” and unique eternal reality of Parabrahma fit easily into medieval Jewish philosophy, as does the idea of conceding to the needs of the masses by converting pure abstract concepts of God into material and visual forms. Maimonides, for example, interpreted the Bible’s anthropomorphic descriptions of God, such as God’s arm, throne, and anger, as just such a concession.

Using the term *dat*, Bloch begins his exposition of Indian philosophy with the phrase, “principles of their *dat*.” He describes Indians as *shomrei datam*, “observers of their *dat*,”<sup>19</sup> and says that Indian scripture contains the rules of their *dat*.<sup>20</sup> He thus seems to conceive of *dat* in a way similar to the modern popular meaning of “religion,” as an institution combining doctrine and practice.

In Bloch’s account of the ancient Indian books called the Vedas, he identifies another similarity with Jewish teachings:

The first three Vedas include commandments (*mitzvot*) and duties between a man and his comrade, and the last one some duties between the created and his creator. It also appears that Manu their lawgiver 

*[p. 176]* ^pg189

like Confucius the lawgiver of China gave a law of birthright<sup>21</sup> for the duties of a man and his neighbor, and thus … both of them walked in the footsteps of the prince of God (*nasi’* ‘*elokim*) in his summation when the Gemara \[b. [[Shabbat 31#31a|Shabbat 31a]]\] says, “This is all the Torah as a whole, and the rest is its commentary.”<sup>22</sup>

Bloch categorizes the Vedas in a traditional Jewish way – as duties either to other human beings or to God (*bein adam leh. avero uvein adam la-maqom*), a categorization that began in the Talmud and became a major classification in medieval Jewish analysis.<sup>23</sup>

European scholars had been discovering the so-called “Golden Rule” in recent translations of Asian literature. In the passage just cited, Bloch finds this principle of reciprocal morality in the teachings of a Chinese, an Indian, and a Jewish thinker – namely, Confucius, Manu (whom Bloch considers the author of the ancient “laws of Manu”), and the first-century Jewish teacher named Hillel. In a Talmudic story in which Hillel instructs a non-Jew who want to convert, he expresses the essence of Torah as a type of Golden Rule. The entire sentence to which Bloch alludes has Hillel say, “What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; that is all the Torah as a whole, and the rest is its commentary; now go and learn it.” So Bloch is saying that Indian and Chinese teachings contain the same essence of Torah as taught by the great Hillel, “prince of God.”

Bloch has thus identified for his readers significant similarities between Indian teachings and Judaism (as he interpreted them). He clearly approves of these Indian teachings, which are rational and universal, and he likewise emphasizes the rational and more universal aspects of Judaism. But as Bloch proceeds to summarize the inferior theological beliefs of the masses, he becomes more critical of them. He first presents the three consorts of Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva, and then the lower gods who actually rule over the human world, including 300 million “angels.” Then comes a brief picture of transmigration (*gilgul ha-nefashot*), governed not by karma but by reward and punishment administered by the gods. The goal of Indian life, Bloch tells his readers, is to reach the Creator and gain freedom. Indians also believe that human beings may be reborn as animals, so they have built hospitals for oxen, horses, and monkeys. But here Bloch directly interposes his own strong reaction, the opposite of Menasseh ben Israel’s: “How great is the power of their foolishness! in the hearts of the children of confusion!”<sup>24</sup> Turning to God, Bloch prays, “In Your counsel let them ask no more to increase their occasions of walking against life.” He then decides to write no further about Indian beliefs:

And now I will not burden you, intelligent reader, with my story of the vanities of the opinions of the people of India because it is not profitable for an intelligent person (*maskil*), for they will endure shame and become entangled in vanities of the vacuous and fables of fabulists.<sup>25</sup>

*[p. 177]* ^pg190

As Bloch then proceeds to describe the rituals of India, his tone returns to its normal neutrality. In order to atone for sins, he writes, Indians engage in sacrifices, immersions in water, prayers, fasts, journeys to holy men and holy places, and giving charity (*tzedaqah*). Their houses of prayer (*batei tefilot*) are called “pagodas” (which he transliterates into Hebrew), and are filled with colorful statues and graven images, as well as altars for sacrifice. Bloch devotes one of his longest discussions to the topic of *sati* (a word he also transliterates), the practice whereby “the wife of a dead man throws herself upon the burning pyre of her dead husband and the two of them burn together.” He explains the widow’s action from her point of view as believing she could thereby join her beloved husband after death while avoiding a life of suffering in this world, but Bloch also notes that the Brahmans enforce this custom. He voices no explicit judgment but says that the custom is disappearing because European rulers have prohibited it.<sup>26</sup> However, he does condemn the Indian belief that bulls are holy: “The bulls there are large and good for both riding and pulling a wagon, and also for plowing and threshing. Therefore, they are called holy (*qadosh yei’amru*), <sup>27</sup> as are cows. This their way is folly for them \[[[Psalms 049#^49-14|Psalms 49:14]]\].”<sup>28</sup>

Bloch explains the four castes, and expresses sympathy for Pariahs, who “are considered as animals and abominations.”<sup>29</sup> But it is the Brahmans who interest him the most.

The Brahmans are the priests (*kohanim*), the sages (*h. akhamim*), and those possessing instruction (*tofshei torah*), and they are also the legal experts … but in farming and the craft of healing they do not extend their hands, because they are sons of the god (*ha-’elil*) Brahma who emerged from his head at the beginning of the creation, and therefore they are holy (*qedoshim*) and a kingdom of priests (*mamlekhet kohanim*) to them, whose holiness cannot be profaned, and they rule over the people’s knowledge, riches, and labor.<sup>30</sup>

By connecting the Brahmans with the phrase, “a kingdom of *kohanim* and a holy people” in [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^19-6|Exodus 19:6]], Bloch is here identifying another similarity with Judaism. The Brahmans resemble the priestly tribe of Israel, the *kohanim*, in being deemed “holy … to them,” to India’s people. Like rabbis, furthermore, the Brahmans are sages who teach and serve as legal experts.<sup>31</sup>

Bloch had to decide which Hebrew words to use in describing this foreign religion. He uses the word *qadosh* (holy) frequently to present the viewpoint of others, often without judging them, as with the holiness of the Vedas, but sometimes ridiculing Indians, as with the holiness of bulls. He calls Indian temples *batei tefilah*, houses of prayer. Brahmans teach *mishpatei torot* and *h. uqim* and *minhagim* – rulings, laws, and customs. He calls Indian beliefs *dat*; Indians are *shomrei datam*, putting their *dat* 

*[p. 178]* ^pg191

into practice.<sup>32</sup> So Indians have a concept of holiness, priests of the biblical kind, houses of prayer, and interpreters of law, and their beliefs and practices are *dat*, just as Jewish beliefs and practices are.

But Bloch views the Brahmans, despite their similarities to rabbis and *kohanim*, primarily as cruel oppressors of their own people, making their lives miserable.

Every Indian must swear by the beard of the priest or Brahman, kiss his cloak, and fulfill his will in everything he asks …. Their belief ruled in India from ancient times, even though it weighs as a very heavy yoke upon them. For it teaches them rulings, laws, and customs, but also painful ways of a life of suffering. Despite this they make it heavier and stricter and do not alter or exchange it in this faith …. And they \[the folk\] do not endeavor to remove their souls from the trap of their beliefs.

Brahmans gain people’s obedience by frightening them through prophesies, reading the stars, threatening evil consequences for disobedience, and performing magic, so that the folk “do not take a single step without asking Brahma.” Bloch thinks the Brahmans have such power because Indians believe that those who refuse their requests are wicked heretics, and because they believe in magic. They think magic can gain the love of women, protect from envy, and cure illnesses – which Bloch condemns as “things that go against the inferences of the mind and the laws of nature.” He ridicules “the greatness of their credulity to believe in vanity and chasing the wind.”<sup>33</sup> Here, the lesson for Bloch’s readers is to avoid such faulty thinking by using logic and recognizing natural law.

We find, then, in Bloch’s portrayal of Indian religion a bi-fold structure combining the philosophers’ intellectual doctrines with the priests’ oppressive mythology, both originating in ancient times and maintained by Brahmans. The philosophical religion consists of a pure monotheism conceived in ways similar to ideas held by Jews and European philosophers, conjoined with a universal ethic of reciprocal obligations. But the other part of Indian religion, co-existing with the abstract ideas, consists of anthropomorphisms of the divine, blind faith in magic, foolish ascriptions of holiness (as with cows), ridiculous ideas like human rebirth as cows and monkeys, and oppression by the Brahmans. The Brahman caste exploits the people’s credulity to increase its own power by means of a “heavy yoke of laws” and a “trap of beliefs” that bring pain and suffering to the people of India, who live wretched lives. Bloch never explains, however, how India’s rational philosophy can co-exist with its irrational beliefs and priestly power.

Bloch’s interpretation of Indian religion reflects ideas of the European Enlightenment that upheld universal rationality and individual freedom 

*[p. 179]* ^pg192

while disparaging ecclesiastical authority, ritual, magic, and mythic worldviews. But unlike most Enlightenment thinkers, Bloch expressed his conception of Indian religion in phrases taken from the wisdom and prophetic writings of Scripture.

Bloch’s sources and his originality

We can clarify more precisely what was original in Bloch’s picture of India by comparing his probable sources of information. Bloch explicitly names Johann Friedrich Kleuker as a scholar who studied India, but does not acknowledge him as a source.<sup>34</sup> Kleuker (1749–1827) was an Evangelical Protestant theologian, philosopher, and prolific author of translations and theological studies, including a book on Qabbalah, and his *Das brah* *manische Religionssystem* (The Brahman Religious System), published 25 years before the first edition of *Shevilei \`Olam*, exhibits many parallels to Bloch’s portrayal of Indian beliefs and practices. Most notably, Kleuker uses the appellations “Parabrahma” and “Karta” for the highest Being, terms found in few, if any, other presentations of Indian thought at that time.<sup>35</sup> In addition, Bloch’s definition of that Being echoes Kleuker’s definition, and Bloch’s presentation of the gods follows the outline of Kleuker’s presentation, with the three higher gods and their relationship to Parabrahma followed by the lower gods. Kleuker describes the caste system in the way Bloch does, and presents the Brahmans as an oppressive ruling class. Kleuker’s book, however, is much longer than Bloch’s section on Indian religion, is organized more systematically, and is far more detailed in presenting all aspects of “the Brahman system,” theology as well as rituals. Entertaining stories make no appearance in the book. And some of Bloch’s topics, such as *sati* and the animal hospitals, are absent from *Das brahmanische Religionssystem*.

Another book published in the same year as Kleuker’s also used the terms “Parabrahma” and “Karta,” defined as “the highest, unending, and self-existent Being.” This book was a German translation of a Latin work written by Paulinus of St. Bartholomew, who lived in Malabar from 1774 until 1789, where he studied Sanskrit and local Indian languages.<sup>36</sup> Kleuker praised the book and used it as his main source of information, quoting it often. Bloch may have discovered the German translation of this book and made use of it. But unlike Bloch, Paulinus maintained a surprising neutrality toward his subject matter, and did not portray Brahmans as ecclesiastical oppressors. Brahman theology, according to Paulinus, was essentially monotheistic because Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva are merely allegorical representations of Parabrahma’s three aspects as creator, maintainer, and destroyer of the world. Paulinus is fascinated with the concept of the *Trimurti*, the triad of these gods, which he conceptualizes in language reminiscent of the Trinity. They are “the Indian Three-in-Oneness.” “Trimurti designates here none else than three in a unique Oneness joined 

*[p. 180]* ^pg193

in bodies or persons.”<sup>37</sup> This language appears neither in Kleuker’s book nor, of course, in Bloch’s. Paulinus is also willing to consider the possibility that Brahman teachings originated from Abraham, and he names Postel as the source of this “conjecture.” (Paulinus never refers to [[Torah 05 - Chayei Sara#^25-6|Genesis 25:6]] in discussing this theory.) He concludes, however, that Brahman theology differs enough from biblical teachings that only one Brahman concept came from Abraham, the idea of a single Creator, which is why the name Brahma, the creator God, resembles the name, Abraham.<sup>38</sup> But this entire topic, so important to Manasseh ben Israel, is absent in Bloch’s and Kleuker’s books.

Kleuker’s *Das brahmanische Religionssystem*, then, and also for the reasons identified earlier, seems more likely to be the main source of Bloch’s picture of Hindu religion, although not Bloch’s only source. How did Kleuker interpret what he calls Brahmanism? Like Bloch and Paulinus, he begins his presentation of its theology with Parabrahma, but unlike them, uses concepts inflected through Neo-Platonic language. Parabrahma is “ungenerated Eternal Oneness,” “the primordial Being of Eternal Oneness” (*das Urwesen des Ewigen Einen*), and “ultimate Ground and Originator of all things” (*letzten Grund und Urheber aller Dinge*).<sup>39</sup> He explains Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva as “the qualities and effects of Godness in the state of their disclosure or recognizability,” ineffable Being turning outward toward the world.<sup>40</sup> Kleuker proposes that all the gods of the Brahmanical system derive from three central wellsprings (*Quellen*): “the realm of Ideas, Nature, and History.” Parabrahma and the three high gods exemplify the first category, the Ganges and Agni the fire-god belong to the second, and teachers and avatars such as Manu and Rama to the third.<sup>41</sup> The human goal (*Zweck*) of this system was defined by “the teaching about the high primal source of the souls, their cycles, their periods of purification and perfecting after their death.”<sup>42</sup>

This, Kleuker writes, was the pure beginning of Indian thought, felt in the hearts of its adherents, but like many religions, this one too decayed over the centuries through the usual malice, self-seeking, and falsehood of human beings, until ill-will bent the truth of its ideas into an ecclesiastical system.<sup>43</sup> As Kleuker describes the power of the Brahman priests and their “religious regulations,” some of which “must seem absurd to the unprejudiced,”<sup>44</sup> his normally disciplined objectivity gives way occasionally to outright condemnation, as in the following sentences:

So had the Brahman casuists and priests of sacrifice known, already for more than a thousand years, considered as a whole, through ignorance (*Unwissenheit*), painful superstitions (*peinlichen Aberglauben*), ceremonial service (*Cärimoniendienst*), and sanctimonious pretensions, how to dominate the folk …. This one \[a Brahman\] knows to observe everything that could serve for this, to make Hell hot for his wards and allow no possible case of conscience against himself.<sup>45</sup>

*[p. 181]* ^pg194

Further examples of Kleuker’s disapproval:

They, the Brahmans, have since early on been concerned with an endless apparatus of service to gods and images, ceremony, sacrifices, purifications, penances and pilgrimages, which are calculated for their selfishness and for the preservation of the shackles of superstition or the compulsion of the soul and conscience.

… Instead of the simple and original teachings of this belief, which the folk cannot themselves learn … they \[Brahmans\] lead them merely to the pagoda and ceremonial service, tell them gigantic fables, and keep them in one more of their pitiful superstitions (*Aberglauben*).

… This worship of images is totally set up for the needs of the common folk, who, as the priests say, for their instruction and entertainment want to have something sensory; it is also equally a sure means to maintain the superstitions themselves, and to prevent every innovation.

… \[This explains\] why the Indian nation still now is, what it was for thousands of years, and will remain, so long as it perseveres under the domination of its religious superstitions and of the priestly power of its Brahmans: – a nation that inhabits the happiest and richest land … who have been damned to this limited awareness and degradation.<sup>46</sup>

Kleuker does not apply the term *heilig*, holy, to the Brahmans, but he characterizes their revealed books, ceremonies, and pagodas as *heilig*, that is, considered holy by Indians. So are the Ganges and Mount Meru, sacrificial fire and “butter,” laws and customs, sacred knowledge, saints, and more. But for Kleuker, this perception of holiness harms Indians by protecting Brahman institutions from rational criticism and reform.

What secures the preservation of this religion is the unconditioned belief in the divine origin of its teachings and laws, the believed holiness and divinity of their written documents, their images and ceremonial service, and the power of the Brahmans, their privileges, however outrageous they may be, but as if granted from God Himself, unexamined and unweakened.<sup>47</sup>

The Brahmans refuse to alter this system in any way, clinging to the past with absolute belief. However, declares Kleuker, “There must be a change of an entirely different kind, and Providence sooner or later will give the necessary impetus.”<sup>48</sup>

Brahman religion, in Kleuker’s picture of it, began as pure rational ideas experienced deeply and sincerely by all of India’s people. It resembles Neoplatonism or even Qabbalah. But it devolved over time through normal human weaknesses into oppressive ecclesiastical ceremonies, image-veneration, and burdensome regulations, all of them associated with an unchallengeable holiness. The priests dominated the folk with 

*[p. 182]* ^pg195

superstitions, fables, and holy ceremonies, culminating in total enslavement of mind and body, a living Hell. Kleuker decries priestly oppression like a fiery Protestant preacher, but based mainly on the universal values of rationality, individual freedom, and the right to happiness, which are Enlightenment values.

Bloch embraced the same values. Both authors attacked the Brahman caste for its power to oppress people’s minds and bodies. Kleuker conceived of this oppression as shackles, compulsion, and a living Hell, and Bloch imagines it as a painful yoke and trap of beliefs. Kleuker’s attack on the Brahmans is more intellectually sophisticated and comprehensive than Bloch’s, who concentrates on the strictness and burden of the laws and on the priests’ manipulation of people by exploiting their irrational credulity. Bloch based his picture of the Brahmans on Kleuker’s but gave them some Jewish features. Bloch also copied Kleuker’s explanation of high Indian philosophy but misunderstood or revised it: Kleuker interpreted Parabrahma in a Neo-Platonic mode, as ineffable ultimate Being emerging into the world as the three highest gods, but for Bloch, Parabrahma was merely the supreme Creator whose powers must, for “the eyes of the people,” be imagined as anthropomorphic gods. Bloch also ignored Kleuker’s idea of the devolution of ancient Brahman philosophy over the centuries into an oppressive ideological system.

Kleuker’s phenomenological mode of presenting a foreign religion, setting out the reality and meaning it has for its adherents, also entered Bloch’s portrayal of Indian religion. Both writers manage to maintain, for long stretches of writing, a non-judgmental perspective on their subject matter, breaking into normative attacks only after articulating these fairly objective descriptions (although based on faulty information and preconceptions). Related to this mode of presentation, both men employ the idea of perceived holiness in describing Indian beliefs and practices: Indians consider rituals and scriptures holy even though Kleuker and Bloch do not.

But the Jewish elements in Bloch’s presentation, which we recognized earlier, are original. Bloch, as we saw, describes Indian theology through the language and concepts of medieval Jewish philosophy and biblical ideas, draws favorable comparisons with the monotheism and ethics of Judaism, employs a vocabulary of Jewish religious practice to describe Indian religious life, and expresses an Enlightenment critique in the language of biblical prophecy and wisdom writings. None of these themes, of course, appear in Kleuker’s book.

Compared with earlier Jewish images of Indian religion, Bloch seems to balance two central motifs, the philosophical with the magical. His picture of oppressive priests using magic recalls Benjamin’s image of the priests of Idlib. Like most Jewish writers before him, Bloch considers Indian religion ancient in origin. But Bloch never uses the language of idolatry to describe Indians.

Bloch has assimilated and interpreted Indian religion for his Jewish readers in furtherance of his life’s mission of saving his people through 

*[p. 183]* ^pg196

knowledge. He has used India to offer them examples of both human commonality across cultures and distances, and of intellectual and moral errors that his readers should eschew. To use the metaphors of Bloch’s introduction: he has left the dark constricted world of the rabbinic study house and traveled intellectually out upon the “paths of the world.” He has gained enlightenment for himself and is bringing back knowledge to his readers still blinded by old thinking, knowledge that will allow them to explore the paths of the world themselves. His study of India was one major segment on that journey.

### 8. David d’Beth Hillel: The hidden meanings of temple Hinduism

In the 19th century several Jewish travelers followed physical “paths of the world” to reach India, exploring the land with body and senses, but not without various preconceptions. David d’Beth Hillel from Lithuania and Palestine arrived in Bombay in 1828, Israel Joseph Benjamin from Romania visited India in the late 1840’s, Shlomo Reinman from east-European Galicia arrived also in the 1840’s, married a Jewish woman of Cochin, and settled there for a while, and Jacob Sapir arrived in 1858.<sup>1</sup> Of these 19th-century travelers, I shall examine David d’Beth Hillel (to be abbreviated here as “Hillel”) and Jacob Sapir.<sup>2</sup>

The Travelogue

David d’Beth Hillel wrote his travelogue in English and published it in 1832 in Madras, under the title *The Travels of Rabbi David d’Beth Hillel:* *from Jerusalem, through Arabia, Koordistan, Part of Persia, and India*.<sup>3</sup> In the preface he reveals about himself only that he was “born in Europe and established in Tsafath \[Safed\] near Jerusalem.”<sup>4</sup> Research by Abraham Ya\`ari in 1939 traced Hillel’s family to several generations of scholars and rabbis living in Vilna.<sup>5</sup> But was Hillel as thoroughly educated in Jewish texts as his forebears? His book exhibits very little of such erudition. While it frequently quotes the Hebrew Bible (*Tanakh*), it names only four Jewish books and only one time each: “the Jewish Talmud,” *Seder Ha-Dorot*, the *Kuzari*, and Eldad’s letter about the Lost Tribe of Dan.

This near absence of post-biblical Jewish books in *The Travels*, however, has to do with its readership, who, as the “Preface” and “List of Subscribers” show, were British officials and clergy living in Madras and Bombay, not Jewish readers. This is why Hillel had to translate Hebrew words and why he had to limit his proof-texts to the Hebrew Bible and his mention of other Jewish sources to the four listed above and to the oblique terms, “ancient histories”<sup>6</sup> and “a tradition.” When I investigated a few of Hillel’s uses of these terms, however, I discovered clear connections with the Talmud and medieval commentaries as their sources.<sup>7</sup> This demonstrates that Hillel had a deeper knowledge of traditional Jewish texts 

*[p. 187]* ^pg200

than first appears when reading his book. He probably received a typical rabbinic education in Vilna before immigrating to Palestine. But because he shaped his book for the specific Protestant audience who would be buying it, the full extent of his knowledge of Jewish texts will never be known, nor the way he would have written for a Jewish audience.<sup>8</sup>

The first scholarly studies of Hillel took no interest in his encounter with the religious communities of India. Ya\`ari analyzed Hillel’s character traits and some aspects of his rationalism, and noted his knowledge of six languages besides Yiddish.<sup>9</sup> “Rabbi David truly loved to see the world,” Ya\`ari wrote, “and found interest in everything in it …. With open eyes he observed everything that he met on his path.” Walter Fischel, an Orientalist who studied Jewish communities in Islamic lands and India, valued *The Travels* mainly as a source of factually reliable reports “of the greatest significance for the reconstruction of the Jewish Diaspora in Asia” at Hillel’s time.<sup>10</sup> Fischel criticized Hillel, however, for repeatedly trying to identify ancient groups of people with the lost Ten Tribes, drawing “hasty and unfounded conclusions” “with reckless abandon.”<sup>11</sup>

Much of *The Travels* comprises practical travel information to “benefit the public” – such as distances from one town to another, the language spoken in each locale, population numbers, the price of food and quality of the water, the climate, the cost of hiring a boat or a mule, and availability of overnight lodging. The book also reports on what Hillel learned about the manners and customs of the people he observed and spoke with during his long journey, begun in 1824, from Safed through Iraq and Persia to India, and across India overland from Bombay to Madras. His one special preoccupation was identifying groups of people who might be remnants of the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel. And Hillel was fascinated with anything “ancient,” such as tombs of Israelite prophets built in far-flung locations, and particularly with “pagodas,” his term for Hindu temples.

Asked by a rajah what was the purpose of his travel, Hillel replied “that it was only to see the world.”<sup>12</sup> This implies that like Samson Bloch, Hillel too sought to leave the intellectual confines of the rabbinic study house, but unlike Bloch, he wanted to see and interact with the outside world directly. There was something of the romantic in Hillel’s quest: having studied nothing about the lands he would visit, nothing from the work of European scholars who studied India, and possessing only his rabbinic education from Vilna, he went out to encounter “the paths of the world” using only his eyes, his mind, and his ability to converse with foreigners in their language – “only to see the world,” trying to understand it and its relation to the Jewish people.

Neither Hillel nor Sapir used the word “Hinduism.” Hillel spoke of “Hindoos” and Sapir called them “idolaters” or *hodi’im*, “Indians” (or possibly “Hindus”). But in writing about Hillel and Sapir I shall be using “Hinduism” occasionally, in a general way, to mean the whole complex of doctrines, rituals, and other religious phenomena that Hillel and Sapir encountered, and to distinguish these from Muslim phenomena. Moreover, 

*[p. 188]* ^pg201

the Hinduism that they discovered was not philosophical, scriptural, or literary, nor did they speak with swamis or yogis, or learn the ideas of Ramanuja or Shankara and their modern interpreters.<sup>13</sup> Rather, the only type of Hindu religiosity that Hillel and Sapir encountered, and only on the surface, was the devotional temple Hinduism widely practiced at that time and today by a majority of Hindus.

Interactions with Hindus and Hindu sites

Hillel visited six temples during his arduous eight-month journey from Bombay to Madras, and reported on these visits at great length (entirely omitted from Fischel’s abridgement). Typically, he began his visits by visually examining a temple’s exterior. He would call the structure “curious” and admire the “cunning workmanship” that had produced the temple’s statues and pillars. Hillel used the word “curious” frequently in his book, generally referring to things that were ancient and unusual as compared with his European background. His word “cunning” always referred to artistry, which he discovered not only at Indian temples, such as the sculpted animals and pillars of the temple at Alenjapore,<sup>14</sup> but at the many tombs of biblical prophets that he had visited earlier in his journey.<sup>15</sup> Next Hillel would enter the temple grounds and notice numerous engraved images and statues and the burning of incense. Temples were “full of images,” he frequently remarked. Then he would discover that the most hidden part of the temple, with its most important image, was located in a central room or tower. He would decide to view it as he had earlier viewed the tombs of prophets. But priests or locked doors barred his way. Although Hillel would argue with the priests, importuning them to allow him entrance, he was always refused. But he “did not listen to them” and tried to look inside.

Hillel, who had learned Hindustani, would then ask questions. He asked what a particular image meant or why people worshiped as they did. Priests or bystanders resisted answering but finally yielded. When they did, Hillel attacked their answers, usually “laughing very much” or “mocking” them. For example, when merchants near the temple at Morgaon answered Hillel’s question about images in the temple, saying they were gods, or *deva*, Hillel laughed and corrected them: no, there is only one God who created the world, and *deva* itself comes from Hebrew ([[Isaiah 01#^1-5|Isa. 1:5]], *davai*) and means sorrow because the first Hindus knew that these gods cause sorrow to their devotees.<sup>16</sup> At the temple at Bayoods, Hillel “was mocking them” for calling Hanuman a *deva* because the statue was merely insensate stone. When he heard priests recite “Barahmah,” he asked what the word meant. But when they replied, “creator,” he “laughed very much” and declared that a lifeless statue cannot create. Hillel was, furthermore, “very sorry to say” that Hindus had derived the word from the Hebrew Bible, because *bara’* in Hebrew means “creator” – which Hindus had misused by applying it to their stone statues.<sup>17</sup> Hillel never tells us how people replied, but they would 

*[p. 189]* ^pg202

not have understood his logic or been at all convinced. For this reason, Hillel’s expositions might be more usefully interpreted as arguments meant to impress his own readers.

In Hillel’s disputations, he uses Hebrew and its antiquity as an ideological weapon to deny local meanings and impose his own. In addition, employing Second Isaiah’s polemic against Babylonian worship, he falsely conflates Hindu deities with their material forms (or abodes or mediations).<sup>18</sup> Hillel displays in these meetings, as elsewhere in his travels, an aggressive, argumentative persona. His interactions with Indians were narrowly intellectual in that he grasped only people’s beliefs, not the subtle, non-rational contexts of their beliefs, such as a sense of sacredness, emotional attachment, community, the setting of boundaries. Hillel assumed the right to gaze upon whatever human beings had constructed in the world, but for those priests (I would suppose), viewing an image in the inner sanctum was not about seeing a work of human “cunning.” It was *darshan*, viewing the Deity, which required proper status, purity, and “open eyes.”

In all of Hillel’s visits to Hindu temples, he mentions only one prohibition in the Jewish laws of idolatry that he obeyed. Priests at the temple at Mowlee, in the course of venerating an image, had placed “pieces of sugar candy” on the altar, recited words over the candy, and then passed it out to the onlookers, among them Hillel. But he refused to take the candy and cited [[Torah 46 - Eikev#^7-26|Deut. 7:26–27]] as his reason. These verses command burning “the graven images of their gods” (*pesilei ‘eloheihem*) and prohibit taking “the silver and gold on them, for it is detestable (*to\`avat*) to the Lord your God … You must utterly detest (*shaqetz teshaqtzenu*) and utterly abhor it (*ta\`ev teta\`avenu*).” Oddly for the setting of Hillel’s story, these verses say nothing of candy or other food. But the Mishnah contains a law (*\`Avodah Zarah* 4.2) that prohibits taking “anything offered upon the altar” of an idol, such as “grape-clusters, wreaths of corn, wine, oil, or fine flour or anything similar,” and the Gemara (51b) to this law connects it to Hillel’s verse, [[Torah 46 - Eikev#^7-26|Deut. 7:26]]. (Again we see Hillel thinking of sentences in rabbinic literature. He carefully mentions the situation relevant to the rabbinic law, that is, the altar and offerings in the temple, but explicitly lists only biblical verses as his source.) Those biblical verses, with their words “detestable” and “utterly detest and abhor,” express a strong sense of repulsion toward idolatrous sacrifices.

Hillel’s ideas about temple Hinduism

Hillel’s conception of Hindu religion included beliefs (“All Hindoos believe in Brahmah, Vishnoo, and Seva”)<sup>19</sup> as well as worship, but emphasized the latter. *The Travels* portrays Hindu worship as calling upon gods, praying, burning incense, making offerings, blowing on conch shells, and kneeling before images. Hillel especially noticed the objects that were venerated in temples and along the streets: snakes, cows, and trees, and images of Hanuman, Rama, and other gods. He used the terms “images” and “idols” 

*[p. 190]* ^pg203

interchangeably. (Toward the end of his journey, however, after learning the concept of “a one self-existing or everlasting Being” which is “chief of the whole universe” and is never represented by images, Hillel seems to have loosened a little his view of Hinduism as solely idol worship.<sup>20</sup>)

His first encounter with Hindu worship occurred at Cochin as people were “performing Poojah.” He describes it as “the worship of Images, of all kinds of animals, and creeping thing.”<sup>21</sup> The words “creeping thing” come from the Hebrew *sheretz* of [[Torah 26 - Shmini#^11-29|Leviticus 11:29]], “creeping things that creep on the earth” (King James translation). These are an abomination (*sheqetz*), the eating of which makes the eaters themselves an abomination.

Hillel also saw that Hindus held a notion of sacred space: “the natives dwelling near this \[temple\] account it for a very holy place,” and a village, because of its many temples, is “counted by the natives as a very holy place.”<sup>22</sup> He notes likewise that Christians “account pictures to be holy” and some Muslims consider a road to the burial site of Ali ibn Talib “their most holy road.” For Hillel, however, only one space possessed true holiness: Mount Zion, which he twice calls “the most holy place.”<sup>23</sup>

Hillel’s lengthy reports about Hindu temples and worship usually conclude with theories about the real meaning of what he has seen, often introduced by the words “I conceive.” His theories are always historical, explaining origins, and this history is always Jewish history. Watching the people of Cochin “paying divine honor” to snakes and “worshipping” trees, he “conceives” that the origin of this veneration was the idolatry reported by [[II Kings 18#^18-4|2 Kings 18:4]]. This verse speaks of types of trees, namely, pillars (*matzevot*) and poles (*asherah*), that Judeans were venerating, as well as the bronze serpent fashioned by Moses. Hillel further supposed that Hindu “respect for the cow” had “probably originated in the worship instituted by Jeroboam,” as found in [[I Kings 12#^12-28|1 Kings 12:28]]. This verse recounts how Jeroboam “made two calves of gold” and told his people, “Here are your gods.”<sup>24</sup> Hillel applied biblical verses about idolatry to two other Indian festivals. He “compares” the “custom” in Bombay of throwing coconuts into the ocean with a verse in Habakkuk about Babylonians making sacrifices to their fishing nets.<sup>25</sup> And the rite of walking on coals is not what his Hindu interlocutors told him it was: a commemoration of an ancient act of *sati* performed by a virtuous wife named Nullatanga-deva. To Hillel, the true meaning of the rite was revealed by the name of the temple at which it took place, “*Darmarajah*.” Based on a theory of the corruption of words over time, Hillel “conceived” that the historical meaning of *sati* was worship of the god named in [[II Kings 17#^17-31|2 Kings 17:31]] as *Adrammelekh*, known also as *Molekh*. The Israelites “made a son or a daughter pass through fire as an offering to *Molekh*” ([[II Kings 23#^23-10|2 Kings 23:10]]), hence Hillel’s association of *Molekh* with *sati* and walking on coals. He explained that the root of *Molekh* is *melekh*, meaning “king.” He failed to complete his argument, but he seems to have thought that the name *Darmarajah* derived from the biblical *Adrammelekh* for two (inconsistent) reasons: one, that ‘*adram* in 

*[p. 191]* ^pg204

the biblical *Adrammelekh* had been corrupted over time into a Sanskrit word with the same consonants scrambled, *d(h)arma* (whose meaning Hillel seems not to have learned); and second, that the Sanskrit word *rajah* means the same as “*melekh*” in the word *Adrammelekh*. Hillel’s complete idea, then, was that from ancient biblical beginnings, the worship of the fire god *Molekh* had continued in India as *sati* under the name *Darmarajah*, a corrupted form of *Adrammelekh*.<sup>26</sup>

This clever dual-language exegesis exemplifies Hillel’s theory that Hebrew, as the primordial language of the world,<sup>27</sup> could be employed as a tool for uncovering hidden historical truths about the Sanskrit language and Hindu beliefs. I recounted earlier his assertion that the Hebrew word *davai* ([[Isaiah 01#^1-5|Isa. 1:5]]) reveals the true meaning of *deva* (god), namely sorrow, “because they pray to them many times and receive no answer.”<sup>28</sup> Thus the Hindu word for gods has built into it – covertly, in its Hebrew origin – the truth of their powerlessness. (The point of these speculations may have been apologetic. As a rejoinder to Protestants who considered Judaism inferior, Hillel’s linguistic theories would demonstrate that Jewish scripture and the Jewish holy language, Hebrew, reveal hidden truths about the world because they are ancient and influential, being the one revelation to and original language of humankind.)

In the case of the creator god Brahma, however, the name’s Hebrew origin reveals the opposite of this theory: a misappropriation that does not reflect the truth. As we saw earlier, Hillel asserted that Hindus had “drawn the word \[Brahma\] from the Hebrew” word for creating, *bara’*, “and they have wronged this word” by applying it to Brahma.<sup>29</sup> Hillel identified a similar misuse of Hebrew in the name *Ram*, “who was their ancient king.” On the contrary, declared Hillel, *ram* really comes from the Hebrew word for “high,” and is usually applied to God, “and I conceive that they have drawn this word from there.”<sup>30</sup> Hillel would probably call these two cases of Hebrew origins “borrowings.” This is the term he used for a celebration in which Muslims carried “figures of men and beasts” through the streets of Bombay and threw them into the sea. He “conceives” that this is a form of “idolatry” opposed by the Quran, “but they borrowed this custom from their idolatrous neighbors.”<sup>31</sup> Some elements that Hillel saw in Hindu worship, however, appeared Jewish. Observing an Indian festival that looked like the Jewish celebration of Purim – because Indians played the roles of a king, a queen, and a “curiously” dressed Haman, gave gifts, and dressed in costume – Hillel “conceives” that they “drew” this “feast” from a verse in the Book of Esther (8:9), in which the king’s secretaries sent letters to all the provinces in the Persian empire, including India. Hillel probably inferred that these letters told the story of Purim, and that Indians had learned about Purim from them and adopted the celebration. In addition, Hillel identifies “a strong resemblance” of Hindu purification and mourning ceremonies “to those of the Israelites” (his English term for Jews), and wonders whether Hindus are “descendants of the long lost ten tribes.” But he decides they are not 

*[p. 192]* ^pg205

because they do not practice circumcision or observe the Sabbath.<sup>32</sup> In these examples, Hillel explains Jewish elements in foreign cultures through either historical contacts with Jews or a people’s origin as a lost tribe of Israel (which was not the case for Hindus).

A last example of Hillel’s historical explanations appears toward the end of his book, after he writes, “I have been told by many learned Hindoos that the ancient Hindoos did not worship any idols.”<sup>33</sup> How, then, did this worship of images arise? What were the historical causes? Hillel offers two possible theories. One is that idolatrous “Samaritans who came to India in the time of Alexander the Great” spread their idolatry to the Indian people. (But Hillel is puzzled by the absence of any surviving trace of the Samaritans.) His other theory is that “wicked Jews who came to India after the destruction of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar” had brought with them the idolatry described in the Hebrew Bible, and Hindus had adopted it.<sup>34</sup> Hillel has thus “conceived” of four historical processes by which language and ritual practice might have spread to India – by means of linguistic corruptions, cultural borrowings, cultural survivals, and specific human migrations: idolatrous Jews in the 6th century BCE or Samaritans in the 4th century BCE.

On two occasions, Hillel voices an explicit theological judgment about Hinduism. This takes expression in the form of prayers. In the first instance, in response to a scene of people “performing Poojah” in Cochin, he “could not but humbly bless the Lord that He has given us a true law to separate us from such abomination.”<sup>35</sup> The second prayer emerges in the course of a long detailed report on the features of several temples at Conjeerveram. After devoting a page and a half to these temples, he suddenly interrupts himself:

It would take a long space to explain all of them and besides their vanity is not worthy to lose so much time on: but blessed be the Lord, who separated us from these errors and gave us a true law testifying that He alone is the one true God.<sup>36</sup>

Both prayers construct a world of opposites. On the one side is vanity, error, and everything that “abomination” connotes. This is Hindu beliefs and worship. On the other side are truth, God, blessing, revealed law, and “us.” Who is this “us”? Because Hillel associates “us” with the “true law,” this “us” is the Jewish community, not Hillel’s Protestant readers. Hillel’s word “separation” is reminiscent of verses in Leviticus (20:24–26) that speak of God separating Israel “from the peoples.”<sup>37</sup> So, although Hillel was fascinated with Hindu temples and was even tempted to identify Hindus as a lost tribe of Israel, he ultimately envisioned an eternal conflict between the principles of Hindu and Jewish worship.

This was because, as we saw, Hillel conceived of Hindu religiosity as essentially and simply idolatry of the kind described in the Bible and Talmud. It had traveled by human migrations from the Middle East to India, along with a few distorted reflections (in the words “Brahma” and “Ram”) of 

*[p. 193]* ^pg206

true Israelite belief, and then had survived unchanged. As pure idolatry, Hindu worship could only be, for Hillel, totally worthless, in conflict with Judaism, and – as expressed by his biblical quotations and his own use of the word “abomination” – repulsive, intellectually but probably also in an emotional sense. In comparing religions, Hillel’s standard was the absolute revealed truth and laws of Judaism. There was no room in his mind for understanding, as Bloch and Kleuker did, the viewpoint of Hindus, the one exception being their sense of holy space.

These ideas are mirrored in Hillel’s interactions with Hindus and their temples. Yes, Hillel was curious, as Ya\`ari pointed out, and curious especially concerning whatever he considered exotic, such as temples. And he observed well and he did ask worshipers what their statues meant. But he rejected not only their answers but their feelings, as he pursued his curiosity, his gazing, and, with mocking laughter, his own truths. His relationship with Hindus was argumentative and self-important. Judged by the Enlightenment value of religious tolerance or the more modern value of religious dialogue, the first recorded meetings between a European Jewish traveler and Hindus were a failure. Hillel, of course, does not represent all Jews, but only his own idiosyncratic ideas, his personality, and his Lithuanian rabbinic education.

### 9. Jacob Sapir: Temple Hinduism as Torah-study

Bombay, November 13, 1859. A traveler from Jerusalem browned by Yemen’s piercing sun, wearing turban and robe, disembarks after a long sea voyage from Aden and stands on the shore listening to an incomprehensible language, until some wagon-drivers recognize he is Jewish and bring him to the local synagogue. This was Jacob Sapir ha-Levi. Sixteen months earlier, he had left his beloved city, tasked with raising money from the wealthy Jews of India. Having toured Egypt and then lived with the Jews of Yemen, he stayed in Bombay half a year, observing its Jewish and non-Jewish inhabitants, and then traveled by ship to Cochin. Two months later he hired passage on a ship bound for Calcutta but monsoon rains blocked the harbor. It then took him six weeks to reach Madras, traveling by fishing barge on the Kerala backwaters past Allepey to Trivandrum, and then by ox cart around the southern cape and through the Tamil plains to Tuticorin, and eventually up to Madras. From there he sailed to Calcutta in August of 1860, where he stayed for ten more months before departing by ship on his way to Java.

After returning home, Sapir (also transliterated as “Saphir”) composed a Hebrew-language narrative of his travels titled *‘Even Sapir,* “Rock of Sapir” or “Sapphire Stone” (published in two volumes, 1866 and 1874),<sup>1</sup> in which he portrayed himself as an explorer searching out and telling the story of “our brothers and our people” in distant lands. Scholars interested in the Jewish communities of India have used Sapir’s book as a historical resource to learn about Indian Jews in the 19th century.<sup>2</sup> But no one has examined the book for its observations of Indian life at that time, its author’s view of Hindu religion, or his experience as a Jewish traveler in India. Sapir wrote at length about what he observed there, and articulated in a nuanced literary style a particular interpretation of Hindu practices which was both modern and biblical at the same time, in some ways reflecting current British and German ideas about Indian religion and in other ways as uninformed as it was original.<sup>3</sup>

Study house and travel

The introduction to *‘Even Sapir* draws the same contrast as had Samson Bloch between the rabbinic study house and outside knowledge, although 

*[p. 197]* ^pg210

for Sapir, this outside knowledge was gained through travel rather than secular literature. But Sapir portrays this travel, which he calls “my exile,” as inferior in value to sitting in a house of study. There, if he had not traveled, he could have “filled my belly with dainties from the Talmud and the legal scholars” and would have been “privileged to write about new insights into the Holy Torah” instead of producing, unwillingly, his “chattering of words” (his travelogue), which made him seem both impious and foolish, and brought neither honor nor wealth. The introduction contains two long, complicated discourses based on authoritative Jewish writings – the Talmud, Midrash, Rashi, Tosafot, and later books – which show off the author’s knowledge of these writings and defend his attempts to tell the truth about his travels. Clearly, studying the rabbinic holy books and this mode of knowledge held great value to Sapir.

But so did travel experience outside the rabbinic study hall, and on his book’s title page, he places the following list of the book’s contents to advertise that value:

The lands I wandered and explored for four years and nine months, and wrote down in a book as a remembrance. The condition and appearance of our brothers and our people, moral, Torah-related, and political, and the passing events and their origins from the past until today, accompanied with stories and matters of Torah and investigations and direct comments, and inscriptions on ancient gravestones and comments on them, and holy songs and petitions and wondrous acts, something suitable for every person. Done through the purity of our holy tongue, may many wander about in them and delight in their goodness. I hope that the multitude of his words will be pleasing to the multitude of his brothers, and for your salvation I hope to God.<sup>4</sup>

These words accord worth to the rational investigation of the non-legal aspects of Jewish life: politics, history, stories, poetry, local customs, and people’s physical appearance. Sapir expects such descriptions to be of interest and to bring pleasure to his audience. Indeed, he claims that his friends begged him to write the book because they found his words beneficial and “pleasant to know.”

Sapir presents “memory” as an important repository of knowledge. He calls his work “a book of my memories” arranged according to the sequence of his journey. Some of these memories pertain to his own personal experiences on the journey, the experiential “I.” For example, to convey the experience of departure from his beloved Jerusalem, and from his family and friends, Sapir uses the image of the soul departing from the body at the time of death, painfully and with deep groans; and his return to Jerusalem is a return to life and joy, regaining a spiritual refuge, with the wish of never leaving the shadow of its walls for the rest of his days. Even when his body traveled outside Jerusalem and his eyes viewed the passing 

*[p. 198]* ^pg211

scenery, his mind thought constantly of Jerusalem and he returned to it in his dreams and heart every night.<sup>5</sup> About his return to Jaffa at the end of his journey he wrote, “There is no expression in my language, and in my mouth no words, to tell the excitement of my soul and emotion of my heart at the moment I gazed from afar upon this small city.”<sup>6</sup>

But far more often than memories of personal experiences, Sapir records memories of his observations and critical applications of mind to what he observed around him.

My story is only about our brothers the Jews, in places that I passed through, according to what I inquired and searched out and carefully examined, in regard to their essence, quality, and quantity, from whence they came, and their manifestations and causes, and what happened to them. What became known to me from this, by clear observation or from legend and story through an investigation of their sources, only these things I recalled and recorded.<sup>7</sup>

Elsewhere in the introduction, Sapir appeals to the truth of empirical observation: “I described every place and every person in the way that it was there before my eyes, and may the God of truth guide me on the path of truth.” Repeatedly in his introduction Sapir justifies his book as “truth,” based on direct observation or critical examination of evidence. This is a truth not in conflict with the truth of holy books, but in a different sphere of thought requiring different tools of thought. But it too is guided by “the God of truth.”

How did Sapir justify remembering the non-Jews he encountered on his travels? He writes, “And after these matters \[foreign Jews\], if the subject drew me, I recalled in a very brief manner also some of the natural features of the kingdoms and the ways of the peoples among whom the Jews lived. Because even from this, there sometimes comes forth some benefit for historians or those who investigate ancient things.” Clearly Sapir means to ascribe to his descriptions of non-Jews a lower order of importance, useful only to historians and antiquarians; but oddly, he actually devotes large portions of his book to foreign geography and culture. For example, forty percent of the book’s Indian section describes India’s natural features and Indian culture, including Hindu religious life. Sapir’s careful attention to the details of Indian life implicitly claims this subject as inherently interesting and worthy of the attention of his reading audience.

The introduction to *‘Even Sapir* thus affirms not only the value of Torah study centered at home in Jerusalem, but also and at the same time, the value of traveling outward and its alternative modes of life and mind: observation, investigation of physical evidence, and personal experiences in the world “out there.” But the conflict between study hall and outer world is not as sharp as Bloch conceived it. Sapir bridges the two sides through objects that share both worlds. Foreign Jews are not only “our 

*[p. 199]* ^pg212

brothers and our people” (the familiar “us”) but foreigners who live far beyond Jerusalem and follow ways of life unfamiliar to European Jews. Their songs and prayers are beneficial to study and will inspire his readers in the service of God,<sup>8</sup> but they also differ from what his readers know. Moreover, “the purity of our holy tongue,” Hebrew, will describe foreign, sometimes impure, realities. In the introduction, Sapir applies the term “purity” to both the ritual state of staying in a study house in Jerusalem, and to the Hebrew language that describes experiences outside this pure locale. By remembering the mercies of God in his travelogue, Sapir further bridges study house and travel, through the concept of a God discovered not only in Torah study but in acts of divine intervention on behalf of pious travelers. This God, “who watches over the steps of human beings,” sent a morning wind on the day of Sapir’s departure from Jerusalem, which “drew me out into the work of the treasured holy assembly,” to raise funds for its synagogue and its education in Torah.<sup>9</sup> Hence, Sapir’s travel, his foreign journeys to collect money for his holy community at home, have a holy function. And finally, Sapir expands the concept of Torah study itself when he makes the following claim about foreign scenes he observed during his travels: “If I saw a new or strange thing, I collected it \[for my book\]. Sometimes I learned from this about a certain law (*halakhah*) or the literal meaning of a biblical verse (*peshat*) or an understanding (*havanah*), and I inscribed it for a remembrance.” The first sentence of this quotation asserts value in the novel or exotic, based merely on curiosity, while the second sentence identifies an important theoretical purpose of Sapir’s travel: to attain deeper understandings of Torah by moving outward into foreign regions beyond the study halls of Jerusalem. Repeatedly in *‘Even Sapir*, including the section on India, our author connects “new or strange” sites and behavior with words in the Torah. In this way, travel became a means of studying the Torah.<sup>10</sup>

Thus, both Bloch and Sapir espoused the value of knowledge about foreign lands and people. For Bloch, that value came from discovering commonalities between Jews and foreigners, and from learning lessons about morality and correct thinking. For Sapir, it was intellectual pleasure (inherent interest, exploring the world) and clarifying the meaning of the Torah.

Tradition and modernism

Jacob Sapir was born in Lithuania in 1822, immigrated with his parents in 1832 to Safed in Palestine, and after their deaths soon afterward, was raised and educated by the *Perushi* community of Jerusalem, most of whom had also emigrated from Lithuania. Here Sapir served as a Talmud teacher and as the community’s “scribe” whose duty was to compose poems in praise of famous visitors and benefactors; he continued to write occasional poems, several of which are included in *‘Even Sapir*. According to Yosef Yoel Rivlin, author of the first important study of Sapir (1940), his two 

*[p. 200]* ^pg213

teachers in Jerusalem, Rabbi Hillel Rivlin and Rabbi Yosef Zundl Salant, educated him in both holy and secular writings. In addition to their expertise in all the Jewish legal writings, Hillel Rivlin knew natural history and medicine, and read classical literature in its original Greek language, and Salant studied the biblical text and knew some sciences and foreign languages and was a fluent writer.<sup>11</sup> Jacob Sapir’s writings show that his education reflected that of his teachers: based mainly in the Talmud, Midrash, and later legal literature, it extended beyond them to Hebrew grammar and poetry, the biblical text, the letters of Maimonides, Ibn Ezra’s commentaries, Jewish history and travel writings,<sup>12</sup> and some geography and foreign languages, particularly Arabic, which he spoke fluently. He claimed even to have read the Quran and a few other Muslim books.<sup>13</sup> But he knew only a little English.<sup>14</sup> In addition, Sapir refers several times to “books of the explorers” (*sifrei ha-tarim*) and to “explorers of the world and travelers of the lands” without naming any of them,<sup>15</sup> and once to Samson Bloch’s *Shevilei \`Olam*, calling Bloch “the great Torah sage and scientific researcher (*h. oqer mada\`i*).”<sup>16</sup> But unlike European *maskilim*, Sapir had studied no European books of philosophy and history, and he names no books of science; and how much he learned from Bloch’s chapter on India is unclear, since his picture of Hindu religion differs from Bloch’s. Abraham Geiger (1810–1874), 19th century historian and proponent of *Wissenschaft des Judentums*, criticized Sapir for his simplistic use of evidence, such as grave markers, for determining historical dates, and the historian Simon Dubnow (1860–1941) criticized Sapir’s writings for being “very naïve” and “little versed in history and geography.”<sup>17</sup>

Yet *‘Even Sapir* reflects, in style if not theory and theology, a number of modern elements: its use of footnotes and the extensive narration of first-person experiences; its interest in Hebrew grammar and correct pronunciation; several attempts to engage in “scientific” experimentation, such as measuring the rise of the Nile as it flooded fields in Egypt<sup>18</sup>; Sapir’s skepticism about miraculous tales and his attempts to test the claims of his informants; and his collection of folk documents and his studies of grave markers for historical evidence. The book’s observations of Indian life are organized in accord with modern European geographic and ethnographic categories, generally the same ones found in *Shevilei \`Olam*. The extensive attention it devotes to non-Jewish lands and customs, furthermore, expresses a shift of values from those that shaped Jewish travel literature written before the 18th century.

Noah Gerber, in his recent history of Jewish orientalist attitudes toward Yemenite Jews, devotes one chapter to Sapir, where he calls him “the Lithuanian *maskil* from Jerusalem.”<sup>19</sup> Gerber especially points to Sapir’s long systematic ethnographies of Jews and Arabs in *’Even Sapir* as a sign of his Haskalah mentality.<sup>20</sup> Other elements of Haskalah, in Gerber’s view, are Sapir’s interest in ancient unknown manuscripts, old grave markers, and burial caves, and in material culture in general; his research method of 

*[p. 201]* ^pg214

using informants, doubting some of their claims as mere hearsay, and trying to cross-check doubtful claims; his criticism of magic and of uncritical acceptance of messianic prophecies; and his proposing that Yemenite Jews turn to Britain for protection. Sapir both romanticizes Yemenite Jewish culture as pure authentic Judaism, and finds defects in its religiosity for being centered too much on Qabbalah and not enough on study of the Talmud.<sup>21</sup> Gerber believes that Sapir’s modernist consciousness began in Jerusalem during his youth when he encountered Protestant English missionaries, although he later saw them as a threat to Jewish survival.<sup>22</sup> Gerber clarifies, however, that Sapir, though a *maskil*, was a moderate one who observed the *mitzvot* and was a rabbinic scholar. His modernism developed within the world of traditional Torah study, especially in Lithuania.<sup>23</sup>

Indeed, Sapir and his teachers fit well into the older rationalist trend in Ashkenazi Jewry, before the rise of Haskalah, exemplified by Rabbi Moses Isserles of Krakow (1525–1572) and Rabbi Judah Lowe of Prague (c. 1512–1609), who studied secular sciences in an auxiliary role that supported traditional rabbinic views. Immanuel Etkes includes Elijah, the Gaon of Vilna, and some of his disciples in this trend.<sup>24</sup> The difference between this older study of secular knowledge and that of Haskalah was partly just a matter of degree and extent, and these two types of thought often merged. Sapir had somehow absorbed modern European methods of research, but shallowly and without their philosophical framework. Compared with Bloch, Sapir showed little interest in non-Jewish writings, a key value of the Haskalah. Nor did he express dissatisfaction with rabbinic authority, another concern of many *maskilim*. In some ways, like David d’Beth Hillel, Sapir belongs more to the Romantic Era than the Enlightenment. I am thinking of his emotional exclamations of love for home and for his Jewish “brothers” whom he meets around the world, his urge to move outward and onward, and his appreciation of Jewish folk religion and ancient documents.<sup>25</sup>

Sapir’s picture of India’s temple Hinduism

Sapir wrote at length and with delight about the landscape, produce, and crafts of India. Using biblical phrases, he lauded the fertility of Indian land and the variety of its fruit. He praised the Kerala backwaters, again in ancient Hebrew phrases: this area resembled the Garden of Eden, full of branches, of every plant and herb and every good fragrance, lovely to the sight, full of joyful sounds; and his journey on its waterways was a repose and delight to the soul. He wrote admiringly about artisans who produced lovely wooden chests and chairs, the comfort of riding in palanquins, the durability of palm leaves for keeping records, ingenious ways by which Indians cool their houses, the great “wisdom” of elephants and their role in shipbuilding, and ten beneficial uses of the coconut tree. He tasted coconut juice and toddy, he learned for himself how riding an elephant felt. Sapir 

*[p. 202]* ^pg215

must have spent many long days quietly observing Indians at their work and leisure, perhaps asking his local Jewish hosts to explain further.

As examples of Sapir’s reporting on Indian culture, consider chapters 29–32 in Volume 2 of *‘Even Sapir*. The first two chapters contain a series of observations organized as units of geography and ethnology: Kerala’s language, climate, dress, writing materials; food, coconut trees and their uses, fruit, spices, and betel leaf chewing. Chapters 31 and 32 describe the Kerala backwaters (twice), beasts of prey, elephants and their use in shipbuilding, a few basic facts about the cities through which the author passed, cattle and their veneration, the landscape of the Tamil plain, and religion (images, temples, chariot festivals).

Unlike Hillel, Sapir never learned Hindustani, or any other local language. He did not argue with Hindus or enter temples. Nor did he inquire into what Hindus believed – whom their images represented or what their doctrines consisted of – though he occasionally used a vague vocabulary of belief: “the inhabitants of the land are divided in their beliefs, practices, and opinions (*‘emunoteihem dateihem vedei\`oteihem*), in venerating their idols and abominations.”<sup>26</sup> Otherwise, he described Hindu religion through the external phenomena of what its images look like and what its worshipers do in public. They worship and exalt, make offerings, burn a light before their images, and sing and dance as they carry them. Sapir’s most common word for Hindu practice is “worship” (*\`avodah*).

Sapir also noticed what Hindus considered holy. Speaking of the way that people near Trivandrum treat their cattle, he wrote, “they do not do work with them because they are holy to them” (*qedoshim heimah lahem*). He applied the same phrase to two other Hindu practices, once to explain why Hindus place corpses in the Ganges River (“one sees bodies of the dead floating on the face of the river Ganges, because this is holy to them”), and once more at the end of a paragraph about people who venerate their cows: “This worship is holy to them” (*qedoshah hi’ lahem*)!<sup>27</sup> He had earlier noticed that the Egyptian town of Tanta was “holy in the eyes of the Muslims,”<sup>28</sup> and certain Torah scrolls and saints’ tombs were holy to Egyptian Jews. For example, the Jews of Cairo frequented a large tomb outside the city because “it is very holy to them” (*qadosh hu lahem m\`od*). The man buried within this tomb, a judge named H. ayyim Kafusi, had miraculously regained his sight in the presence of his entire congregation, and now Jews poured libations over his tomb and petitioned him for help.<sup>29</sup> Sapir was reporting people’s subjective perceptions of holiness, but when he wrote about the “holy Sabbath,” “holy Israel,” and “the holiness of the religion (*dat*) of Moses,” he used the word “holy” in an absolute sense.

Why did Sapir refrain from entering Hindu temples? One likely possibility is that he was following Jewish laws of idolatry. Leviticus had warned, “Do not turn to other gods” ([[Torah 30 - Kedoshim#^19-4|Lev. 19:4]]), which had been interpreted in the Talmud as a prohibition against looking at idols,<sup>30</sup> and later by Moses Maimonides and Moses Nahmanides (1194–1270) as a ban against even 

*[p. 203]* ^pg216

thinking about idolatry.<sup>31</sup> Then how would Sapir have justified his observations and long descriptions of Hindu worship? Three codes of law which Sapir often mentioned – ‘*Arba\`ah Turim* (14th century), and *Beit Yosef* (16th century), and *Shulh. an \`Arukh* (16th century) – provide clues to his reasoning. Thus, the general prohibition against observing idolatry applies, according to *Shulh. an Arukh* (*Yoreh De\`ah*) 142.15, only “if one enjoys (or benefits from) its sight” – which Sapir could deny. Another law, based on the Talmudic interpretation<sup>32</sup> of [[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^18-9|Deut. 18:9]], permits keeping images if one’s only purpose is to study them and “to understand and to teach” (141.4); and Sapir could read this exemption as a counterweight to the general ban against observing idols, so that he was permitted to study them in order to explain idolatry to his readers. Other laws (147.1, 150.4) prohibit approaching closer than four cubits to a temple and mentioning the names of foreign deities but do permit mentioning idolatry that specifically appears in the Torah (147.4). The *Beit Yosef* permits the disparaging of idolatry (147.5) and the *Shulh. an Arukh* permits deriding idolaters and forbids praising them (147.5, 151.14). Sapir broke none of these laws, including the ban against approaching a temple.

Sapir composed four major descriptions of Hindu worship and images for his book. Here is an excerpt from his first and longest description, from a chapter on Bombay:

Many of the inhabitants of the land are divided in their beliefs, practices, and opinions (*‘emunoteihem dateihem vedei\`oteihem*), in venerating their idols (*‘elilim*) and abominations (*to\`evot*), of every host of the heavens, of every animal and beast of the earth, of the horn of the ox, of images and all works of delusion (*ta\`tu\`im*), variety upon different variety, as they were in ancient days. When I reflected on some of their worship and the days of their festivals, I comprehended several sayings, verses, and stories in Scripture and Talmud which I had not previously understood. … In every house there is idolatry (*\`avodah zarah*), the image of a woman and two children, or male and female, naked and embracing,<sup>33</sup> fashioned in stone, wood, or metal, and positioned on the wall or on the table, and an eternal lamp (*ner tamid*) burning before them. And every morning, before any act or labor, before eating or drinking, they place in front of them a heave offering (*terumah*) from their food – this is the offering of idolatry (*tiqrovet \`avodah zarah*) – and then the rest is eaten and they go forth to their labor. And likewise these gods (*‘elilim*) stand at the head of every path, and on every side and corner in the markets and roads. On the days of their festivals they make large figures (*tzurot*) like these, and dress them in garments of honor and riches. They decorate them with costly ornaments made of silver and gold and every precious stone, and in a great crowd they carry them on poles in the streets of the city, with the voice of a festive multitude, with every musical instrument, and with songs and hymns, 

*[p. 204]* ^pg217

until they come to the seashore and there they remove the mantles and ornaments from the figures and throw them naked to the sea.<sup>34</sup>

Later, in an unnamed Tamil city, Sapir is amazed at the abundance, endless array, and craftsmanship of the religious images he sees there:

In every street and market and at every corner and on roofs of the houses stand gods (‘*elilim*), diverse and strange (*shonim um’shunim*), graven images fashioned of wood and stone in wondrous forms, works of manual craftsmanship, and particularly on the roofs of the temples of their illusions (*batei ta\`ato\`eihem*), all of them covered with graven images (*pesilim*) and false gods (*‘elilim*) of all kinds of tame and wild beast, bird, loathsome animal (*sheqetz*) and creeping thing (*remes*), and everything that is on the earth.<sup>35</sup>

Sapir’s descriptions may at first sound like objective observation, but his choice of words, which come out of biblical and rabbinic denunciations of idolatry, carries the worldview in which he was raised and conveys his own viewpoint and sense of repulsion. Sapir could have used more neutral terms such as *tzurot* (forms, images) and *temunot* (pictures), as Bloch did.<sup>36</sup> For Sapir, however, Hindu gods were *‘elilim*, *gilulim*, and *to’evot*, and Hindu images *pesilim* and *ta\`tu\`im* – terms usually translated as “false gods,” “idols,” “delusions,” “graven images,” and “abominations.” Sapir’s association of these gods with abomination and delusion shows the derogatory connotation of his vocabulary, and echoes sentences from Leviticus, Deuteronomy, the Prophets, and Psalms which harshly condemn the veneration of foreign gods and statues. Consider, as just three examples, [[Isaiah 41#^41-7|Isa. 41:7]], “In that day you will reject the *‘elilim* of silver and the *‘elilim* of gold which your sinful hands have made”; [[Jeremiah 10#^10-15|Jeremiah 10:15]], “They are vanity, the work of delusion (*ma\`aseh ta\`tu\`im*)”; and Psalm 97:7, “All who worship *pesilim* are put to shame.”<sup>37</sup> One phrase in Sapir’s first description of Hindu worship, \`*avodah zarah* (literally: strange or foreign worship), used six times in *‘Even Sapir* to describe Hindu worship, appears prominently in the Talmud and Midrash as a general term for idolatry. An entire tractate of the Talmud, called \`*Avodah Zarah*, is devoted to prohibitions against contact with idols, idolaters, and objects involved with idolatry.

The words “roofs of the temples of their illusions” in the second passage quoted above, refers to a *gopuram* (temple gateway). Sapir ignores its human-like figures and applies an animal classification taken from Chapter 11 of Leviticus, especially at the chapter’s end, where certain animals are considered *sheqetz* (loathsome) and all animals are either *beheimah, h. ayah,* *\`of*, or *remes*, that is, tame beast, wild beast, bird, or creeping thing, just as in Sapir’s sentence. The phrase I have translated as “loathsome animal and creeping thing” (*sheqetz veremes*) reflects a phrase found many times in the Talmud, *sheqatzim urmasim*, which connotes “forbidden food.” Again, 

*[p. 205]* ^pg218

Sapir could easily have found other, more precise words to describe the animal images that he saw, but he instead chose specific words that turn the complex imagery of south Indian *gopurams* into a Jewish cosmology that divides the world into ritually pure and impure animals and connotes repulsion for impure food. Sapir may also have associated the animals of the *gopurams* with a sentence in the Book of Ezekiel containing the same terminology as Leviticus: “And behold, every form of creeping thing (*remes*) and abominable beast (*beheimah sheqetz*) and all the idols (*gilulim)* of the house of Israel” (8:10). Since this verse appears in the prophet’s vision of Judeans committing “wicked abominations” (*to\`evot*) in the temple, the effect of Sapir’s word choices would have been to identify the Indian images with ancient Judean idolatry.

In a chapter on Calcutta, near the end of his journey in India, Sapir openly voiced his theological judgment about Hindu religion, confirming what his vocabulary of Hinduism implied. This judgment took the form of a sentence that he constructed from four biblical phrases: he hopes that Hindus “will recognize and know that their fathers inherited a lie and that they erred from the path to make silent idols (*‘elilim ‘ilmim*), to burn incense and to sacrifice to nothingness and emptiness, and to worship the work of their hands made of wood and stone.”<sup>38</sup> This sentence associates Hindu gods with biblical assertions of the futility and error of idolatry.

Sapir’s concept of Hindu origins

In the first passage about Hinduism quoted above, there appears an odd statement about Hindu images: “as they were in ancient days”; and then Sapir makes the surprising claim that his observations of Hindu practices enabled him to understand parts of the Bible and Talmud that had before eluded him. These assertions lead us to Sapir’s theory of the origin of Hinduism. Two of Sapir’s footnotes reveal this concept.

At an unnamed city on the Tamil plains, Sapir saw a sacred chariot of huge proportions, with “great wooden wheels of the height of three men,” decorated with “all kinds of images and statues.”

And on the days of their festivals all the people of the city gather together and lift them on their shoulders, because they are an exhausting burden<sup>39</sup> even for hundreds of men, and they move it about the city on every road, with a great tumultuous voice, songs, and dances.<sup>40</sup>

Sapir comments in a footnote, “And this is what they meant about Manasseh who made an idol (\`*avodah zarah*) weighing as much as a thousand men, and likewise with the idol which Nebuchadnezzar set up.”

The scene that Sapir witnesses in southern India, a sacred car festival, leads his mind to the Talmud, which tells of Manasseh, king of Israel, making “an image as heavy as a thousand men, and every day it slew all of 

*[p. 206]* ^pg219

them,”<sup>41</sup> perhaps meaning, as suggested by Rashi, that its enormous weight crushed them. Seeing the huge sacred chariot (*ratha* in Sanskrit), Sapir now understands “what they meant about Manasseh,” that is, what the Talmudic rabbis meant. He was seeing Indians lifting an enormous idolatrous object in the same way that Israelites lifted the idolatrous image fashioned by Manasseh. Sapir thinks also of the third chapter of the Book of Daniel, in which the Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar erects an enormous gold statue for the peoples of the world to worship.

Then some sacred cattle that Sapir sees on the road from Trivandrum remind him of two more forms of idolatry mentioned in the Talmud:

The cattle … are very big and of great strength, and have a large fat back like a camel swaying back and forth … and there are some which the people of the land venerate, exalting and honoring them and covering their horns with silver, and they do not do work with them because they are holy to them, and they were idolatry \[of the type\] “*shor ‘afis*,” and the worshipers, the men and the women, would raise over their heads an image of a horn.<sup>42</sup>

Sapir’s footnote adds, “And this is what they meant by ‘Write on the horn of an ox (*qeren ha-shor*) that they would have no place in the World to Come.’”

Sapir’s use of the phrase “*shor ‘afis*” is puzzling because no such term appears in rabbinic writings. I would guess, however, that he was referring to the undefined god in the Talmud called *sar ‘afis*. He probably reasoned that the idolatry associated with this *sar ‘afis* was veneration of a *shor*, an ox, and that he was seeing with his own eyes this very same veneration in India.<sup>43</sup> Likewise, the Indian ritual of holding an ox horn over one’s head led Sapir to think he could now understand another sentence in rabbinic literature. Midrash Leviticus Rabbah asserted that Greece had “persisted impudently with its edicts against Israel and had said to them: Write on the horn of the ox that you have no share in the World to Come.”<sup>44</sup> Sapir thinks that the ritual he sees in south India explains why the Greeks chose an ox horn rather than something else when they tried to force Jews to disavow their faith. It is because oxen were sacred to the Greeks just as they are sacred to the Hindus of Sapir’s day. From the sacred chariot and the ox ceremony, Sapir has thus “comprehended several sayings, verses, and stories in Scripture and Talmud which I had not previously understood.”

Sapir’s identifications all presume that India had once been part of a vast, ancient, and idolatrous civilization that had stretched from Greece through Babylonia to India. This civilization had survived in Sapir’s time only in India, and had not changed in thousands of years, and that was why Indian culture exhibited the actual living practices of idolatry described in the Bible and Talmud. Therefore Hindu practice could elucidate obscure references to foreign customs found in Jewish holy books. This is what I meant 

*[p. 207]* ^pg220

about travel as a form of Torah study. For Sapir, travel in foreign lands among foreign people not only entertained Jewish readers with “pleasant” knowledge, but fulfilled a holy task, the act of studying the holy books – no longer limited to the study house but carried out through insights acquired in the wider world.

Moses Maimonides, as we saw, had viewed Indian rituals as a contemporary survival of the Sabian idolatry that had covered the world in ancient times. Likewise, Abraham ibn Ezra had used Arab reports about India to interpret ancient Israelite and Egyptian customs appearing in the Bible because he thought of India as a living remnant of an extensive ancient civilization comprising all the descendants of Ham. Sapir follows a similar line of thought. We learn elsewhere in *‘Even Sapir* that he had studied Ibn Ezra’s commentaries and at least indirectly, Maimonides’ *Guide*, so perhaps he learned his concept of Hindu origins from them.<sup>45</sup>

Sapir applies the same theory of origins to Indian cultural practices. He believes he has gained insight into Jewish books when he discovers Indians using iron styluses rather than ink to write letters and business accounts on palm leaves, and when he sees south Indians wearing a garment made of leaves. From the Indian styluses he thinks he has clarified what a number of Jewish texts meant by various vague terms for writing instruments.<sup>46</sup> Similarly, witnessing the “large and wide leaves” of a tree he called *‘aninas* (probably banana) enabled him (he thought) to gain a clearer understanding of [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^3-7|Genesis 3:7]], which speaks of Adam and Eve sewing leaves from a fig tree to make “girdings.” Indian Jews had told him that the fig leaves of this biblical verse were the leaves of the *‘aninas* tree (which they thought was a type of fig tree). Moreover, Sapir had seen Indians wearing a lower garment made of *‘aninas* leaves sewn together in the form of the south Indian garment called a *veshti*.<sup>47</sup> From these clues he concluded that the Bible was describing a lower garment of the same form and material as those he saw in Kerala.

Yet, though Hindu worship was essentially ancient and idolatrous, Sapir, like Hillel, identified in it certain Jewish elements and similarities, such as the Purim celebration. Writing about the Parsees of Bombay, who he thought celebrated Purim, he added, “There are other nations in India \[other than Parsees\] who also observe Purim.”<sup>48</sup> Discovering many biblical names among the Ceylonese, Sapir proposed a theory to explain Jewish customs which he thought he had discovered in India and Ceylon. These customs and names were a “trace,” a survival, from Jews who had been exiled to India after the destruction of the first Temple. Sapir thought that additional Jews had immigrated after the destruction of the second Temple, mainly to the area of Cochin, and more Jewish families had come periodically from Europe to Malabar to escape persecution.<sup>49</sup> By calculating the offspring that these many Jews would have produced, Sapir arrived at what he terms an “immense” number of Jewish inhabitants in India’s past. Nearly all of them, however, had assimilated, “and there remain for 

*[p. 208]* ^pg221

a few of them only the names of their fathers which they held onto as a mark of memory, and even a few of the customs, as I related above.” So one reason why Hindus observe a festival like Purim, burning an effigy of an evil character, is that as ancient Jews gradually became Hindus, they continued to celebrate Purim and it took on an Indian form. Sapir noticed a similar process of acculturation occurring, but in reverse direction, in the mourning customs practiced by the Jews of Calcutta: “Thus was added to the essence \[of Jewish worship\] a number of \[Indian\] accretions until the root was mixed with a large amount of vanities.”<sup>50</sup> Like Hillel, Sapir conceives of cultural diffusion occurring through migrations, accretions, and survivals.

Sapir occasionally uses the vocabulary of Jewish ritual when describing certain Hindu practices. Like Bonfils and Bloch, he had to find Hebrew words to portray a foreign religion. In his long passage about temple Hinduism in Bombay, we find three terms for Jewish rituals. Hindus keep “an eternal lamp (*ner tamid*) burning before” their deities, and every morning Hindus place before them “a heave offering (*terumah*) from their food”; and when they carry their \[gods\] through the streets, they do so “with the voice of a festive multitude,” a phrase echoing [[Psalms 042#^42-5|Psalm 42:5]]. All three terms are related to the ancient Temple in Jerusalem: the eternal lamp inside the Temple (and in later synagogues), food set aside for Temple priests, and festive crowds in Jerusalem walking to the Temple. The question, however, is whether Sapir used these terms with the explicit intent of demonstrating parallels between the two religious traditions.

Priestly power and British power

Sapir believed that Hindus do not engage in “the sciences of speculation and research” because “they walk captive after the commands and edicts of their priests and judges, and from them they turn not.”<sup>51</sup> He criticizes the priests further:

And every faction and faith has its priests (*komarim*) and temples of idolatry, to lead them captive, and they subjugate by means of them, through their festivals and celebrations, and as in former times were their path and actions, likewise even today do they hold sway over them with their impurity and rules (*dateihem*).<sup>52</sup>

These priestly rules are so oppressive that they bring “toil and weariness of soul” to the lives of Hindus, who “behave with extreme self-restraint,” abstaining from alcohol and “all food that derives from living creatures.”<sup>53</sup>

With a faith in their vanities, they live lives of sorrow and toil and do not taste at all of the goodness in the life of this world. Meat and everything that comes out of living beings, for example, are not taken 

*[p. 209]* ^pg222

to their mouths all the days of their lives, and wine and liquor they do not drink, and they abstain from the human pleasures.<sup>54</sup>

Sapir is criticizing priestly authority for mind-numbing rules and life-denying asceticism, blaming these for the universal unhappiness of the Hindu populace. (This ascetic picture is contradicted by Sapir’s reports of delicious coconut liquors and tasty roasted fish, the widespread use of spices, peppers, and betel nut quid, and the luxurious life of wealthy Indians.)

Elsewhere, he blames Indian suffering on food and weather rather than priests:

Most of them detest eating meat or anything that comes from a living creature, and they keep their souls alive with rice dishes, for wheat bread is also foreign to them. Because of this their appearance and their flesh are thin, gaunt, and feeble, and there are no mighty warriors (*gibborim*) for war.<sup>55</sup>

As to climate, Sapir claims that Indians are “docile of heart, down of spirit, limp of soul, and heavy of movement” because of “the nature of the air of this rich, warm, and moist land that stands on many waters, which weakens the strength, afflicts the spirit, and lessens the appetite for eating.”<sup>56</sup> Sapir offers a surprising solution to the problem of the soul-weari

ness caused by Hindu asceticism. He first notes that “the government of Britannia, may its glory be uplifted,” has established many schools “to teach language and literature, science and knowledge,” and has brought numerous Protestant missionaries who “try with all their might to bring the small with the great into the covenant of the Christian religion (*dat*),” but to little effect.<sup>57</sup> Then Sapir expresses a hope that “future generations \[of Hindus\] will be educated upon the blessings of the schools and these teachers, and they will study the customs and ways of Europe and will read and enjoy the books of Europe.” He is astonished, however, that Hindus, with all they suffer from the oppressive rules of their own religion,

… do not become enlightened (*lo yaskilu*) and do not cast away their abominations and idols and their images, to grasp hold of another religion (*dat*) right in front of them, which teaches them knowledge and intelligence to walk in the paths of life and to find life, in their toil and weariness of soul.<sup>58</sup>

In sum, Sapir contrasts Indian ignorance and rejection of life with European knowledge and culture and Protestant “paths of life.”

The Hebrew word *dat* appears in the last sentence quoted above, referring to Christianity. Sapir uses this term in connection with Christianity and Islam, as in *dat notzeret*, the Nazarene *dat*, and *dat ha-Quran*, the *dat* of the Quran.<sup>59</sup> Judaism is *dat ve-torat yisra’el*, the *dat* and Torah of Israel, 

*[p. 210]* ^pg223

*dat mosheh*, the *dat* of Moses, and *dat yehudit*, the Jewish *dat*.<sup>60</sup> Sapir even uses the word in relation to the people of India: “I will not expand words on the characteristics of the city and its inhabitants, the nations of the land, their *datim* \[plural of *dat*\] and their customs (*dateihem uminhageihem*).”<sup>61</sup> In all these cases, *dat* seems to mean a distinct religious system consisting of beliefs and practices and perhaps institutions. But Sapir uses the word in the plural in a rather different sense: the priests “hold sway over \[them\] with their impurity and their *datim*.”<sup>62</sup> And “the inhabitants of the land are divided in their beliefs, *datim*, and opinions (*‘emunoteihem dateihem vedei\`oteihem*), in venerating their idols and abominations.”<sup>63</sup> In these particular cases, the plural meaning of *dat* is harder to decipher, but seems to imply something closer to rules or practices, as distinct from ‘*emunot*, beliefs. I would generalize, then, that Sapir uses *dat* in the singular and sometimes plural number to mean an entity rather close to the modern popular meaning of religion as a system of beliefs and practices; in the plural, however, the word can often mean religious practices.

Sapir’s chapters on India speak frequently of the activities of the British, whom Sapir never encounters directly but whose military power provides the political context within which he travels. Sapir feels “no fear or fright” during his idyllic cruise through the waterways of Kerala because “a fear of European men falls upon the rulers of the people of the land, who are subject to the British government.”<sup>64</sup> The British appear in these chapters as conquerors of the Maharajas, as wealthy merchants, engineers, doctors, scholars of the Malayalam language, lawgivers, and missionaries. Sapir is also aware that

the good things of the land are in their hands and they are like lords of the land, knowing how to turn the prosperity of the kingdom toward themselves …. They live here a life of repose and delight, with houses full of all good things, spacious rooms, gardens and orchards, and each one has male and female servants from the Indians.<sup>65</sup>

Sapir generally approves of the British government, which he calls “happy” (*m’usheret*) and “merciful.”<sup>66</sup> He asks that “its glory be uplifted.”<sup>67</sup> Writing about Aden, he portrays England as an agent of God, who “roused the mighty heart of the English government” to occupy and build up the territory, bringing freedom and prosperity, benefits and mercies (*tovot v’h.* *asadim*), to its Jews. Applying a phrase from the Passover liturgy about God redeeming the Israelites from Egypt, he says that England delivered the Jews “from darkness to great light.”<sup>68</sup> In Bombay and Calcutta, “the merciful government of mighty England”<sup>69</sup> built up free commerce whereby people of all nations prosper, including Jews, through the “wisdom and freedom” which the British planted in these cities.<sup>70</sup> Britain banned “evil customs” like placing corpses in the Ganges, and it abolished slavery.<sup>71</sup> Sapir recounts the rebellion of 1858 at some length. He acknowledges that the East India Company had attacked Indian kingdoms under false 

*[p. 211]* ^pg224

pretexts, and that Europeans had come to India for “pride and glory, and with grand honors.” The cause of the rebellion was the understandable desire of Indians to throw off their subjugation to the Company. But then, according to Sapir, they, and especially Muslims, behaved like “beasts of the forest ripping apart their prey with anger and hot cruelty.” The English government sent skilled soldiers to “avenge the innocent blood,” until “the mighty hand and warrior’s heart of the British” overcame the rebels. Sapir compares British soldiers to “a lion among the beasts of the forest.” The Jews of Bombay and Calcutta, he adds, supported Britain in this war, praying for its success, because they depended upon the British; and the Jews called “Bene Israel” fought valiantly on Britain’s side.<sup>72</sup> It seems, then, that Sapir’s high opinion of Britain is based on his identification with Jews who benefited from British power, both in Aden and India.

Did Sapir copy Bloch?

How did Sapir learn that Hindu priests oppress people and have done so since early times? He could certainly have observed that certain men conducted rituals at temples and shrines, and he could guess or learn from Jewish informants that they were priests. He could also have observed that Hindus do not eat meat, and he may also have seen wandering ascetics. But could he have learned, from mere observation, that priests “lead people captive” and “subjugate” them through their ceremonies and rules? That people have “faith in their vanities”? That rules of asceticism came from the priests? Did Sapir really observe a general “toil and weariness of soul” among Hindu people? And how did he know that Hindus do not engage in “the sciences of speculation and research”?

I propose that Sapir inserted certain claims into his picture of Hindu religious life that did not derive from his direct observations. I will explore two possible sources – Bloch’s chapter on India, and the political and missionary ideologies that circulated among the British in India at that time.

Turning first to Bloch, we recall that like Sapir, he too portrayed the priests as oppressive rulers who had been controlling their followers “from ancient times.” The Brahmans’ rules are strict, he wrote, and cause “painful ways of a life of suffering.” Bloch explains Brahman power through false beliefs and people’s credulity, and Sapir writes of people’s “faith in their vanities.” Bloch uses two metaphors in his portrayal of the priests that show up in Sapir’s picture. Bloch speaks of “the trap of their beliefs,” and Sapir, of captivity: the priests “lead them captive.” Bloch asks God to stop Hindus from “walking against life,” and Sapir wishes Hindus would adopt a religion that teaches them how to walk in the paths of life. So could Sapir have learned these ideas and drawn these metaphors from reading *Shevilei* *\`Olam*, a book he mentions once in a footnote?

But Sapir’s concept of the priests also differed in some ways from Bloch’s. Bloch thought that Brahmans gain their power mainly through people’s 

*[p. 212]* ^pg225

credulity and false beliefs, especially magic, whereas Sapir focuses on ceremonies as the source of their power. For both authors, the priests’ heavy rulings and laws cause misery, but Sapir goes further and imagines these laws as harsh rules of asceticism that prevent Hindus from enjoying “the goodness in the life of this world.” Sapir also associates the rules with “impurity,” a category absent from Bloch’s account.

Their larger pictures of Hinduism differ even more. Both think that Hindu religion is ancient, but Sapir identifies it with ancient Near Eastern idolatry and considers it entirely false. In contrast, Bloch considers Indian philosophy monotheistic and ethical, and sees it as a force that spread monotheism to Asia. Its gods represented concessions to the masses, and only when these gods multiplied and became millions of angels and the veneration of oxen did he deplore Indian beliefs. Sapir, however, knows nothing about Hindu gods, much less a “sublime essence” or the Trimurti or the Veda’s ethical teachings or a Hindu concept of transmigration. His reports mention nothing about castes, pilgrimages, or priests performing magic.

I can nevertheless imagine Sapir coming across *Paths of the World*, glancing at its section on Brahmans, and reworking the section’s main ideas for his own book in a way that made sense to him. From Bloch’s emphasis on wrong ideas and misbeliefs, Sapir saw mainly wrong laws. This is what we would expect from a thinker who saw the world through rabbinic legal categories.

*Shevilei \`Olam* and *‘Even Sapir* exhibit a number of intriguing literary similarities. Each book begins its section on India with lavish praise for the land and its variety of fruit, expressed through phrases from the Bible. Both books recount in detail the way Indians use coconut trees and write on leaves. Both describe Indian men as weak; Bloch says “Indians are not as strong in the build of their bodies as European men”<sup>73</sup> and Sapir describes them as “thin, gaunt, and feeble.” In addition, Bloch asserts that Indians possess a low level of knowledge about the natural sciences (*yedi\`ot ha-tiv\`iyot*),<sup>74</sup> and Sapir that they neglect “the sciences of speculation and research.” As to religious beliefs, both imagine Indians speaking a phrase from Jeremiah, “Our fathers have inherited a lie” (16:19).<sup>75</sup> And both books use a phrase from Isaiah, *qadosh yei’amer lo*, “it is called holy” (4:3), in the same ironic way to deride people’s misguided sense of holiness. Both apply the phrase to the veneration of oxen and cows.75a

What these similarities mean is hard to determine. Are they coincidental, or simply elements of the geographical literature and Jewish vocabulary of the time? Were books of Haskalah such as *Shevilei \`Olam* available to Sapir in Jerusalem before he began his travels, or in 1864, after his return, when he began to write Volume 1? Or more likely: did he discover the book while traveling in Europe in 1865,<sup>76</sup> so that *Shevilei \`Olam* influenced the final form of his report on India in Volume 2 before its publication in 1874?

It is clear, however, that Sapir had not read David d’Beth Hillel’s book. He did share some important ideas and values with Hillel, such as believing 

*[p. 213]* ^pg226

that Hindu religion was identical with the idolatry described in the Bible. But Sapir never mentions Hillel, even as he lists many other Jewish authors who had written earlier accounts of what Sapir observed on his travels. In particular, Sapir devotes several footnotes to an earlier traveler’s account of India, that of Israel Joseph Benjamin, whom he attacks for his sloppy investigations. Moreover, Hillel himself had published very few copies of his books, and they were in the hands of his British subscribers. Sapir also tells us that he found the English language difficult. I would instead explain the similarities in the thought of Hillel and Sapir from their common Lithuanian rabbinic background.

Echoes of British ideology

Some of the same ideas about Hindu religion that Sapir might have learned from Bloch, were also important features of the ideology of British political theorists and Protestant missionaries, and those ideas may instead have come to him from them. Furthermore, while Bloch never recommended a solution to the problem of Hindu misery, British thinkers did, and their solutions approximate Sapir’s – namely, his proposal that Hindus learn European literature, knowledge, and customs and that they convert to Christianity, a religion of enlightenment and knowledge, to gain a happier life. These two ideas surely originated from British sources.

From the early 19th century through mid-century, British imperial governance in India was shaped by an ideology of political liberalism. I turn to Thomas R. Metcalf’s masterful synthesis of recent research, *Ideologies of the Raj*, in order to sketch certain liberal ideas relevant to Sapir’s thought. British liberalism, writes Metcalf, conceived that human nature could be changed through “the workings of law, education, and free trade,” liberating “individuals from their age-old bondage to priests, despots, and feudal aristocrats so that they could become autonomous, rational human beings.” In India, these values led to British reforms of Indian law, commerce, ownership of property, and education.<sup>77</sup>

The philosopher John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), for example, believed that Britain had a duty to lead India out of its low state of civilization and modernize it. Good government and education would cultivate liberty and self-reliance.<sup>78</sup> Charles Trevelyan, colonial administrator in India from 1826 to 1840, recommended the enactment of legal reform and free trade, and improvement of Indian character through education and conversion to evangelical Christianity.<sup>79</sup> Thomas B. Macaulay (1800–1859), historian and Whig politician, was more specific about the type of education needed. He had studied Sanskrit literature but scorned it, and instead wanted to make Indians into modern Europeans by educating them in English literature, morals, laws, and art.<sup>80</sup>

Liberal theorists viewed Hindu religion as an oppressive obstacle to the independent progressive thought that was the goal of Liberalism. Influenced 

*[p. 214]* ^pg227

by Orientalist scholarship carried out by William Jones, H. T. Colebrook, and others, they viewed India as a society controlled by scripture and the despotic rule of Brahmans.<sup>81</sup> In the opinion of James Mill (1773–1836), political and economic theorist and author of the influential *History of British India* (1817), the caste system created by the Brahmans was the main cause of India’s “degradation.” This system of guile and “tyrannical oppression,” including ceremonies that kept people in abject submission, “enchained” people’s minds and bodies. It was “despotism and priestcraft” empowered by “enormous and tormenting superstition.”<sup>82</sup> Similarly, Sir Alfred Lyall, who served in the Indian Civil Service from 1856 to 1902, expressed despair over the “boundless cruelty and grotesque invention” of Hindu worship and beliefs.<sup>83</sup>

British thinkers of the time held to the Orientalist theory of India’s glorious Aryan past, a time of institutional and philosophical greatness, but a greatness that had been arrested and degraded into the “barbarism” of the present. Lyall thought the transcendent ideas of ancient Brahman philosophy had been distorted by “a tangled jungle of disorderly superstitions.”<sup>84</sup> Nevertheless, both James Mill and Lyall saw elements of contemporary Hindu religion as survivals from the ancient world. Mill claimed that conversing with Hindus was like conversing with ancient Babylonians and Egyptians, and Lyall considered popular Hindu worship akin to Roman polytheism, so that one could understand Roman religion better by studying “Hinduism.”<sup>85</sup>

Some of Sapir’s ideas about Hindu religion echo the thinking of these liberal British writers. He criticized the harsh rules of the priests that brought suffering to their followers, applauded the free trade established in Bombay and Calcutta by the British government, agreed with British laws against slavery and disposing of corpses in the Ganges, and recommended a European education consisting of language and literature, science and other modern knowledge, which he called “the blessings of the schools.” He thought he was learning about ancient idolatry by studying contemporary Hindu worship. Also like some British authors of the time, he espoused a concept of climate determinism, namely, that heat and humidity subverted manliness and courage. Hot climates, in this British theory, caused “an enduring degeneration of mind and body,” and even despotism and fatalism.<sup>86</sup> But most liberal British thinkers refrained from supporting missionary efforts to convert Indians, adhering instead to the principle of religious neutrality.<sup>87</sup> James Mill and Macaulay envisaged a secular, literary education to transform Indian character instead of the religious education taught at schools run by Protestant missionaries.<sup>88</sup> So Sapir’s proposal that Hindus convert to Christianity does not likely reflect British political thought.

However, for the British missionaries serving in India at this time, converting Indians was their central goal. Geoffrey A. Oddie analyzed the numerous writings produced by British Protestant missionaries in the 19th century, and I have examined additional works, mostly by Baptist 

*[p. 215]* ^pg228

missionaries, archived in the library of Regent’s Park College. Intriguing parallels to Sapir’s view of Hinduism appear in these books.

To begin with, these missionaries, like Sapir, often used the word “idolatry” in their portrayals of Hinduism. We find the term used by three famous missionaries whose writings shaped the thinking of many others: Reginald Heber, William Carey, and William Ward. Heber (1783–1826), an Episcopal Bishop, wrote of “Brahmanical idolatry,” meaning images used for “idol worship,” and of “idolatry” invented by the Devil.<sup>89</sup> Carey (1761–1834), a Baptist pioneer of missionary work in India, expressed his reaction in 1794 to a ceremony called *chakra puja*, in which men swing from hooks piercing their backs:

Their horrid and idolatrous transactions have made such an impression on my mind…. Who would grudge to spend his life and his all, to deliver an otherwise amiable people from the misery and darkness of their present wretched state.

This was wretchedness caused by ceremonies imposed by the priests.<sup>90</sup> Ward (1769–1823), another Baptist and missionary pioneer, wrote repeatedly of “the folly of idol worship.”<sup>91</sup> The same term was voiced by many other missionaries, for example, “idolatry rampant” and “their idols” (Elijah Hoole, 1844), “abominable idolatry” (Joshua Russell, 1852), and “the most barbarous idolatry” (Andrew Fuller, 1808).<sup>92</sup> Letters written by the Baptist missionary John Chamberlain (1777–1821) convey a sweeping portrayal of Hinduism as nothing other than idolatry. Ceremonies were “the works of idolatry and blind superstition,” a temple was “an idol’s house,” Krishna was “the idol Krishno,” the life of “the natives” was “superstitious idolatry and delusion.”<sup>93</sup> At one point in his travels, Chamberlain tells villagers (in a way reminiscent of Hillel), “Why are you so ignorant? Worship him who made the heaven and the earth …. Destroy these idols, and worship them no more!”<sup>94</sup>

Also like Sapir, British missionaries used the word “abominable” (in their English interpretation of the word). Joshua Russell, writing in 1852, called a festival of Kali “abominable idolatry.”<sup>95</sup> And the editor of the London Missionary Society’s *Juvenile Magazine* characterized the worship of Kali as “full of cruelty and abomination.”<sup>96</sup> Chamberlain, observing funeral practices, wrote of “all this filthiness and abomination they call holiness!”<sup>97</sup>

False belief was another major theme of British missionary writings. John Murdoch, in his book of advice titled *The Indian Missionary Manual* (1864), emphasized this idea with phrases, such as “false religion,” “blinded by idolatrous creed and prejudices,” “their superstitions and false notions,” and “erroneous doctrine.”<sup>98</sup> Most missionaries used the concept of “superstition” to characterize Hindu beliefs. Chamberlain employed the terms “delusion,” “blindness and ignorance,” and “darkness.” John Foster 

*[p. 216]* ^pg229

in 1821 described Brahman theory as “fantastic,” “this stupendous delusion.”<sup>99</sup> Missionaries also accused Hindu beliefs of irrationality. Carey, writing in 1796, characterized Hindu mythology and Scripture as “absurdities,” and speaking to a participant in a *chakra puja* ceremony, called him a “fool.”<sup>100</sup> Ward, writing in 1817, criticized Hindu thought and ceremonies as irrational, absurd, and inhumane.<sup>101</sup> And James Hough in 1839 declared Hindu ceremonies unworthy of “a rational mind,” and its myths about gods “extravagant, absurd, ridiculous, and incredible.”<sup>102</sup>

Missionaries did not write as often about the Brahman priesthood as secular thinkers did, but some missionaries took note of it. Carey and Ward had studied and adopted Orientalist ideas about Hindu religion. Ward called it “the greatest piece of priest-craft” (intended pejoratively), seeing Brahmans as the source of “the whole fabric of superstition” and the “system” of popular ceremonies.<sup>103</sup> Hough (reminiscent of Johann Kleuker) called Brahmanism a “complete scheme devised to enslave mankind” and keep the Indian people in deepest ignorance.<sup>104</sup> Chamberlain wrote of “the craft, avarice, and pride of the Brahmans.”<sup>105</sup>

The missionaries’ solution to what Carey called “the misery and darkness of their \[Indians\] present wretched state,” including enslavement under the Brahmans, was, of course, conversion to Protestant Christianity. Not only because it was the truth, but because it was rational and humane. Most of these Evangelical missionaries, although espousing proselytization and biblical literalism, studied science and literature as part of their English education and often part of their missionary training.<sup>106</sup> They preached to Indians in various settings, but they also founded numerous schools, where they taught literature and science along with Scripture. William Ward, for example, was a strong proponent of Enlightenment ideas. He compared “the European mind,” invigorated by centuries of illumination, with “the Hindoo still walking amidst the thick darkness.”<sup>107</sup> He believed that Britain had the responsibility to civilize Indians through a rational scientific education:

Our present duties to this people seem to be comprised in imparting to them, first knowledge, and then sacred principles; and in this God-like work, Schools, as well as the extensive circulation of elementary works on the first principles of science, and of the Holy Scriptures.<sup>108</sup>

One particular idea expressed by a few Christian missionaries set them apart from Hillel and Sapir: the concept of Hinduism as a religion of the Devil, perhaps influenced by [[1 Corinthians 10#^10-20|1 Corinthians 10:20]]. Bishop Heber declared that the Devil had invented Hindoo concepts, ceremonies, and acts of uncleanness and cruelty.<sup>109</sup> Carey wrote that the Hindoo holy books had been “manufactured by Satan,” who had “dictated those vile and destructive fables.”<sup>110</sup> And according to Elijah Hoole, Hindoo worship was the Devil’s work, who was “worshipped under various forms by idolatrous 

*[p. 217]* ^pg230

Hindoos.” “The Hindoo system,” he maintained, “proceeds from the father of lies, the old serpent.”<sup>111</sup> No Jewish interpretation of Hindu religion connects it with Satan.

Jacob Sapir’s view of Hindu religion echoes, though inexactly, some central ideas of these British missionaries. They all reduce Hindu practice and thought to the concept of idol worship, associating it with the strongly pejorative term, “abomination” (although the precise meanings of the word differ between languages). Sapir, like the missionaries, characterized Hindu religion as falsehood and priestly oppression. Finally, like the missionaries, he proposed a European education and conversion to Christianity as solutions to Indian suffering. But Sapir recommended conversion not because he thought Christianity was true but because he saw it as a religious system (*dat*) that would cultivate European rationality and offer freedom from harsh ascetic practices.

Of course, Hillel and Sapir arrived at their central idea, Hindu worship as idolatry, independent of the missionaries. The Jews and the Evangelicals all started with the idolatry they found in Scripture, which they received through their separate Jewish and Christian histories of interpretation. But both groups thought similarly enough that when they arrived in India, they all seized upon the term “idolatry” to categorize the religious life they encountered there.

It is understandable why Sapir would have agreed with many points in the British ideologies I summarized. But how might he have come upon these ideas? Hillel had conversed with British officers and missionaries in India,<sup>112</sup> but Sapir never mentions speaking with any British citizens there, and he admits that (at least in the early part of his journey) his English proficiency was very weak. How did he learn about the curriculum taught in British schools? Perhaps this information came to him through conversations with the Jews of India, with whom he spent most of his time there. But would these Indian Jews be expected to agree with British civil servants and missionaries in the matters of educating and converting the Indian populace? All I can say for now is that a few sentences in Sapir’s account of India, sentences not based on direct observation, exhibit an intriguing similarity to contemporary British political and religious ideologies, and that aspects of these ideologies surely in some way influenced Sapir’s account of Hindu religion and his evaluation of it. In particular, his promotion of European education and Christian proselytizing, two specific British goals, go beyond what he could have found in Bloch’s *Shevilei \`Olam*.

One further idea that both Sapir and Hillel shared with British thinkers of their time was the concept of cultural diffusion and survivals as ways of explaining Indian culture. This appears in British texts in the form of the Aryan invasion into the Subcontinent (cultural diffusion occurring through migration) and an original Aryan form of Brahmanism which became distorted over time but was still partly visible today (cultural survival). Sapir, 

*[p. 218]* ^pg231

it may be recalled, thought that cultural diffusion had occurred through migrations, accretions, and survivals, resulting in a Hindu version of Purim, Jewish names held by Indians, and Indian accretions in the Jewish rituals of Calcutta. Hillel had earlier written not only about survivals, but corruptions and borrowings. For Hillel, rather than Aryans bringing foreign cultural elements to India, it had been Jews who had done so.

Sapir’s picture of Indian religion, like Hillel’s, resembles Ha-Levi’s image of Indians as idolaters rather than philosophers or moralists. Hillel and Sapir’s Indians, however, lack the magic and the quarrelsome, devious traits of Ha-Levi’s Indians. Like most Jewish writers before them, Hillel and Sapir consider Indian religion ancient in origin. They discern certain Jewish elements in Hindu practice but this is quite different from Bloch’s assertion of an essential agreement in matters of theology and ethics. 

The writings of Samson Bloch, David d’Beth Hillel, and Jacob Sapir exhibit a new burst of Jewish interest in India and Hindu religion in the 19th century. They represent new literary paths to India, as secular geographical knowledge and personal travelogue, and a deeper encounter than ever before.

But a flawed one, too, or only a beginning. Hillel and Sapir, encountering the world essentially through categories and values rooted in the traditional thinking of Lithuania Judaism, fundamentally failed in understanding and evaluating Hindu religious life. Despite their curiosity and interest, their views of Hindu religiosity were poorly informed and prejudicial. Even when they perceived a Hindu sense of holiness, they usually reacted to it with ironic scorn. In contrast, Bloch’s comparisons between Judaism and Hinduism (the common ethics of the Vedas and the Torah, the philosophical monotheism, parallels between *kohanim* and Brahmans, Hindu beliefs and practices as *dat*) were tolerant and generous. But his knowledge was superficial and intellectually undisciplined, and scorn and harsh judgment came easily to him, too. Yet as we saw from German and British examples, the Jewish encounters were not alone in these flaws.
