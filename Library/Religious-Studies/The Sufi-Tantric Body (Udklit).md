Michaelmas Term In Hindu tantric traditions there is a distinctive understanding of the body; an

Introduction

esoteric anatomy founded on an internalization of cosmology and tradition. Often, the esoteric anatomy is based on hotspots in the body called *cakra*s. This esoteric understanding of body and tradition is also found in Bengali Ṣūfīsm. There the hotspots are called *maqām*s and the anatomy is founded on an internalization of theology and tradition. I will investigate whether the practice of Bengali ṣūfīs can be denoted as tantric. This will be done on the foundation of Gavin Flood’s theory on the tantric body (2006). As a reference frame for this investigation I will use the Hindu Ṣaṭcakranirūpana \[SCN\]. Therefore, this essay will contain a short introduction to the two esoteric anatomies through concrete examples from the Hindu SCN and the Ṣūfī Yoga Kalandar \[YK\]. Through an examination of differences and similarities between the two texts, I will apply Flood’s theorization on both. Lastly, I will determine whether Ṣūfīsm and the body of YK is tantric.

Since I expect the reader of this essay to have a greater knowledge in Hindu terminology than Islamic, I will be elaborating much more on the terminology used in YK than in SCN. Furthermore, it should be noted that the SCN is used only as a frame of reference, hence the focus on YK will be greater in his essay. In the appendix is a scheme on both the *cakra*-system of SCN by Sir John Woodroffe, and the system of *maqām*s by me. I have also found it necessary to have a Bibliography beyond References, since a lot of my knowledge has been gained through literature not actively used in this essay. I hope for this essay to be the beginning of a greater study on yoga and Ṣūfīsm and the interaction of different religious systems and understandings.

Michaelmas Term The term ’the tantric body’ is developed by Gavin Flood in his book of the same title

The tantric body

(2006). I have chosen to use this term as a foundation for my comparison of tantric Hindu traditions and Bengali Ṣūfī traditions, because I find that the term embraces all aspects of the relationship between body, tradition and text that we find in these traditions. The theory Flood sets on the tantric body is this:

“The theory I wish to present is simply this. The tantric body is encoded in tradition-specific and text-specific ways. The practitioner inscribes the body through ritual and forms of interiority or ascetism, and so writes the tradition on to the body. Such transformative practices are intended to create the body as divine. This inscribing the body is also a reading of text and tradition” (Flood 2006, 13)

To many modern people the practice of yoga might seem highly individual, but it must be emphasised that the practice of tantric yoga builds on an interaction between tradition and individual, text and practice (ibid., 11). Without an interplay between these the tantric body cannot be created. Via the tantric body the yogī inscribes himself into the shared memory and imagination of the tradition. He becomes text and tradition and is part of creating and reconstituting it (Flood 2004, 8).

To become a part of the tradition the yogī must understand his relationship to the cosmos he exists in; a cosmos built on a religious understanding of the world revealed in texts. The texts are often direct words from God, that being Śiva, Viṣṇu or others, and theory on cosmos is legitimized by its divine source. Between God and yogī, as well as yogī and tradition, is the guru. The guru must guide the yogī on practice and deliver esoteric tradition-specific knowledge, which the yogī must internalise to become part of a ‘*shared memory’*, and thus tradition and text himself (ibid.). This implies that the yogī must internalise cosmos by mapping it on to the body. Via this practice a correspondence between micro- and macrocosm is created. This divinises the body and the tantric body is created. The divinisation is essential to the tantric traditions, since it is perceived that to worship a god, one must become a god. According to Flood, the divinisation of the body means that the yogī’s subjectivity is transcended and becomes coterminous with the subjectivity of the deity. It must be underlined, that these practices and

*[p. 4]* ^pg4

Michaelmas Term cosmological understandings are always text and tradition-specific (Flood 2006, 11).

As explained, the yogī must create a correspondence between micro- and

Cakras

macrocosm in creating the tantric body and thus becoming a part of both tradition and text. The tantric microcosm often entails the perception of an esoteric anatomy, which can be built on a system of *cakra*s.

The origin and development of such a system is widely debated. This particularly, because the system might differ from tradition to tradition. It may vary in relation to numbers of *cakra*s, their design, location in the body and the like. Widely known is the 6 +1 *cakra*-system. This system is thought to be firstly presented in the 11th chapter of the Kubjikāmatatantra, from the 11th century CE. This system contains a dualistic cosmology incorporating the union of Śiva and Śakti in the body. But, in a later chapter of the same text we also find a *cakra*-system of 5 *cakra*s, which does not include Śakti, nor Kuṇḍalinī or Kubjikā, but with a focus on the five elements. David Gordon White claims that the 6 *cakra*-system can be traced back you the Bhāgavatapurāṇa, however with no use of the word ‘*cakra’* itself (Flood 2006, 158). There is no doubt that a system of hotspots or points of energy in the body has been referenced early in the history of esoteric anatomy, but according to Sanderson, Flood and others the 6+1 *cakra*-system is not to be found before the Kubjikāmatatantra. Here we find a clearly defined esoteric anatomy with 6 *cakra*s on a vertical axis in the body.

The number of *cakra*s on this vertical axis may variate between different texts and traditions, and also within texts as we see with the Kubjikāmatatantra. But, texts like the Ṣaṭcakranirūpana and the influential Nāth yogīc practice has been a factor in standardizing the system of 6+1 *cakra*s (ibid., 159).

SCN is a text from the Srīvidyā tradition; a southern Kaula-tradition with focus on

Ṣaṭcakranirūpana

the goddess Tripurasundarī (Hatley 2015, 5; Flood 2006, 159). It is presented in *The Serpent Power – Being the Ṣaṭ-cakra-nirūpana and Pādukā-pañcaka* written by Sir John Woodroffe, originally under the pseudonym Arthur Avalon. SCN is the 6th

*[p. 5]* ^pg5

Michaelmas Term chapter of Srītattvacintāmaṇi by Pūrṇānanda from around the 16th century (Woodroffe, ix-x; Hatley 2015, 5). I have chosen the SCN as frame of reference for this essay, because it has had a great influence on the modern and global understanding of the *cakra*-system; here consisting of the 6+1 *cakra*s (Hatley 2015, 5; Flood 2006, 230 n48). Due to the length and reason for this essay, the examination on the *cakra*s of SCN will be kept simple. For an overview on all the attributes of the *cakra*s a scheme is found in the appendix.

The *mūlādhāracakra* is the lowest placed of the six *cakra*s, hence called the root *cakra*. It is placed under the genitals, over the anus and at the end of *suṣumṇā*. It has four petals with the letters va, śa, ṣa and sa written in gold (v. 4). Its *bīja* is *Laṁ*, its element earth and in it is Brahmā, Ḍākinī and Traipura (v. 5-8). Inside the Traipura is *svayambhuliṅga*, with Kuṇḍalinī three and half times around it. Meditating on this, the yogī becomes lord of speech and free of all diseases (v. 13).

Next *cakra* on the vertical axis of *suṣumṇā* is *svādhiṣṭhāna* and is placed at the genitals. It has six petals with the letters ba to la in golden colour (v. 14). Inside the *cakra* is a half moon and the *bīja Vaṁ* (v. 15). Its element is water and within it is Viṣṇu and Rākiṇī who is drinking ambrosia (*cittā*). The one who meditates on this *cakra* will be free from his enemies and his words will become nectar-like (v. 18).

At the region of the navel, above *svādhiṣṭhāna*, is the *maṇipūracakra*. This lotus has 10 petals with the letters ḍa to pha. Inside it is a triangle with the *bīja Raṁ*, and its element is fire (v. 19). Above the *bīja* is Rudra and Lākinī. She drinks ambrosia (*cittā*) (v. 20-21). By meditating on the lotus, the yogī will obtain the power to destroy and create the world (v. 21).

Above *maṇipūra*, at the region of the heart, is *anāhata*. It has the colour of the Bandhūka flower and 12 petals with letters ka to ṭha. Inside it are two triangles on top of each other forming a star with six corners (v. 22). This has the colour of smoke and contains the *bīja Yaṁ*. Kākinī is here, bearing noose and skull and drinking nectar (*sudhā*) (v. 24). Śakti is in the middle of the *cakra* in a *trikona* together with the *bāṇaliṅga* (v. 25). The yogī meditating on this *cakra* will be like the Lord of Speech and capable of protecting and destroying all worlds (v. 26). He will become dear to women, wise and of noble deeds. His senses are under control, his mind in concentration and he is able to enter another’s body (v. 27).

Michaelmas Term In the throat is *viśuddha*. It has 16 petals with the 16 vowels on. It contains the *bīja Haṁ* and symbolises the element of ether. In the lap of the *bīja* is Sadāśiva (28-29). Śakti Sākinī is also here, in the area of the moon (v. 30). To the yogī with pure and controlled senses this is the gateway to *mokṣa* (v. 30). He who meditates on this will become wise and reach peace of mind. He will see the three periods, benefactor of all, and be free from disease and sorrow. He destroys endless dangers (v. 31).

Above *viśuddha* is the *ājñācakra*. It has two petals with the letter ha and kṣa. In here is Śakti Hākinī and a *yoni* with the *itaraliṅga*, that shines like a chain of thousands of lights and its *bīja* is *Oṁ* (v. 33). Meditating on this the yogī will be able to enter another’s body, become the most excellent of Munis, and all-knowing and -seeing. He will realise his unity with Brahman, becoming the Creator, Destroyer and Preserver of the three worlds (v. 34). He will reach uninterrupted bliss, see Bhagavān and will after death enter “that Supreme, Eternal, Birthless, Primeval Deva, the Puruṣa” (v. 38).

With this practice, and help from his guru, the yogī will see above the *ājñācakra* (v. 39). He will reach *sahasrāra*, the thousand petalled lotus. This lotus is bright and shining, entails all the letters and is eternal bliss (v. 40). Here the yogī will realise the oneness of *jīvātmā* and *paramātmā* (v. 43). The experience of *sahasrāra* is divided into three stages; *amākalā*, *nirvāṇakalā* and *nirvāṇaśakti*. From *amākalā* drips the nectar as result of union of Śiva and Śakti. In *nirvāṇakalā* divine knowledge is given (v. 47). *Nirvāṇaśakti* is the abode of Śiva and can only be accessed by yogis. It is pure knowledge and the place of liberation (v. 49).

As part of the esoteric anatomy in SCN we find Kuṇḍalinī, a serpent female energy. She rests in the *mūlādhāracakra*, and must pierce through all *liṅga*s and *cakra*s, so she can unite with Śiva in *sahasrāra*. This leads to bliss of liberation (50- 51). Kuṇḍalinī will drink from the *amṛtakuṇḍa* and spread through the body on her way down to *mūlādhāra* again (53). Kuṇḍalinī and a system of *cakra*s are not always described together. Sometimes we have *cakra*s without Kuṇḍalinī and sometimes we have Kuṇḍalinī without *cakra*s; as in Haṭhapradīpikā, for instance. The SCN ends with a verse on the importance of the yogī’s devotion to his guru. It is stated that this text is faultless, pure and secret. It is a supreme source of knowledge on liberation (v. 55).

Michaelmas Term The religion of Islam is generally very different from Hinduism. It is younger,

Maqāms

monotheistic and dogmatised on one holy scripture; the Qurʾān. But, as many highly dogmatised religions Islam also has a mystical side; Ṣūfīsm. Early in the development of Islam Greek philosophy, and especially Plotinus and Neoplatonism, played a significant role (Sedgwick 2007, 31). Together with early ascetic tendencies the emanationist understanding of the world came to be significant for Ṣūfīsm (ibid., 38). Amongst the first to be called ‘ṣūfī’ (woollen) is al-Junayd, a Baghdadi ascetic in the 9th century. He too had a very emanationist understanding of the world and Allah (ibid., 40). To him, the goal of the ṣūfī had to be *fanāʾ*; annihilation into God (Sedgwick 2007, 40). Together with the notion of *fanāʾ* goes *baqāʾ*; perpetuation – an eternal life in God (Schimmel 1975, 59). According to al-Junayd these practices were only for the few and one had to be very careful on it. Secrecy was essential (Sedgwick 2007, 40).

Thus, to many ṣūfīs the goal is *fanāʾ*; annihilation. Therefore, it is said that the ṣūfī must follow a spiritual path of discipline, the *ṭarīqa*, with help and guidance from a master, a shaykh (Geoffroy 2010, 8). Here we find the emanationist approach; the ṣūfī is moving closer and closer to God (ibid., 12). On this path the ṣūfī reaches both stations (*maqām*) and states (*hāl*) (ibid., 10). *Maqām*s are station that the ṣūfī reaches by discipline and aspiration. The ṣūfī has an active role in reaching these (Schimmel 1975, 100). What defines the *maqām*s is very text and tradition specific; often it is repentance, trust in God and poverty (ibid.). The states (*hāl*) is a kind of divine present or favour that one cannot strive for and therefore also not influence (Geoffroy 2010, 10). This could be love, desire for God, intimacy, but too varies among traditions. The same is for the number of both *maqām*s and *hāl*s (ibid.).

There is a lot more to be said on ṣūfī practice and beliefs, as there is with tantrism. I have explained the main terminology to understand the religious world view and phenomena that is to be found in the ṣūfī text included; YK. I will elaborate further on the ṣūfī orthopraxy and orthodoxy in the comparison between YK and SCN.

Michaelmas Term According Das Gupta, Islam and Ṣūfīsm spread to the regions of Bengal around the

Yoga Kalandar

11th century. At the 12th century Ṣūfīsm began to settle and influence the Hindu world view; at this time with the two orders Chishti and Suhrawardi (Das Gupta 1946, 191). At the 17th century seven different Ṣūfī orders was in Bengal. They had had a great influence on the people there and succeeded in attracting many Indians to the Ṣūfī practice (ibid., 192).

YK, a Bengali Ṣūfī text, is thought to be written around the 17th century. The author is unknown, and it is thus not possible to say which group it belonged to (Cantú 2015, 16). I have chosen this text as focal point of this essay, because it seems to entail a fusion between a tantric understanding of the body and Islamic theology and world views. As far as I know three translations of this text can be found; Enamul Hak’s English translation from 1957, France Bhattacharya’s French translation from 2003 and the English translation of Keith Cantú as part of his master’s thesis *Theurgy and the Snake – the Yoga Kalandar and Bengali Sufism* (2015), submitted to University of Washington. Last-mentioned is the only translation I have been able to get access to. Cantú’s is a non-annotated translation from Ahmed Śarīph’s Bengali version of YK, based on France Bhattacharya’s French annotated translation (Cantú 2015, 66 n17). I am aware that there might not be much authority in the translation of Keith Cantú, but I think it shows the main tendencies of this text, and Ṣūfī world view just fine. YK entails many interesting religious phenomena, but as with the SCN I will here examine the formation of the *maqām*s, as a pendant to *cakra*s. I will elaborate further on cosmological and theological aspects of both texts in the comparison.

The *maqām* called *nāsut* is YK’s pendant to the *mūlādhāracakra* (v. 8). This is the

Nāsut

place of the Three Points and is guarded by the angel Azrael, who takes the appearance of a tiger (v. 7; 104). In the Three Points is a fire with no sound, that must be carefully kindled every day, since it makes the body immortal (v. 10-13). The existent-self (*jībattamā*) is the lord of *nāsut* (v. 8). The doors of this place are the ears, which are of great importance (v. 17-18). Pressure must be put on to “the hidden root” (this *maqām*); if done every day this will destroy all illness (v. 14; 16).

It should be clear by now, that some main similarities are found between the *cakra*-

Comparison

system presented in SCN and the *maqām*s in YK; some differences are also clear. I will use the theory of Flood’s tantric body (2006) to compare these two systems, but also to discuss whether the body in YK is tantric. As I see it, the creation of a tantric body consists of four main parts; 1) A divine theory on cosmos, that the yogī with help from 2) a guru must 3) internalize through practice 4) to deify the body and become a part of text and tradition (Flood 2006, 11). I will be comparing and discussing on the basis of these four parts.

In his book *The Tantric Body* (2006)*,* Flood puts an emphasis on the Tantras as texts

Cosmology

of revelation for the tantric traditions. Tantras are perceived as revelations, a direct word from God and is hence thought of as divine (Flood 2006, 8). The texts included in this essay, YK and SCN are not Tantras. YK, obviously, is a Ṣūfī text and exists within a tradition, that does not have Tantras. The SCN is a chapter from Srītattvacintāmaṇi; an unpublished work on tantric ritual (Woodroffe 1972, x). Woodroffe argues for the classification of this text as tantric (ibid., 2), but there seems to be no doubt when in a preliminary verse a legitimation via the Tantras are made in SCN; “according to the Tantras” (ibid., 359). Since the Tantras are thought of as revelations, the practice of the SCN is divinely legitimized.

The case of YK is somewhat different. The YK does not describe itself as a Tantra. Furthermore, it does not consist of a revelation from a supreme being in

*[p. 12]* ^pg12

Michaelmas Term the sense of a direct word from God (Flood 2006, 8). In Islam the only text containing a direct word from God is the Qurʾān (Vikør 2012, 32). The YK could have been legitimized via an *isnād*; a chain of transmitters, used in the *Ḥadīth*- literature and thus very common to Islamic literature (ibid., 39). But it is not. There is no divine legitimation of YK, though the text does start off with a salute to Lord Nirañjan, Allah, the Prophet and Fatima – in that order (v. 1-4).

Both SCN and YK entail a theory on cosmos. It is generally known that a system of *cakra*s can be used in mapping the cosmos onto the body (Flood 2006, 157). In both text we find an emanationist cosmology, in that the *cakra*s and *maqām*s are used in tracing a route back to the divine source. In SCN this emanationist cosmology is shown in the tracing a route back to the divine source of the universe; the merging of Śiva and Śakti. In YK the emanationist cosmology entails a tracing back to Allah as the divine source of creation; but not back to creation itself. Furthermore, the names of the *maqām*s seem to entail all of cosmos. The terms *nāsut, malakut, jabarut* and *lāhut* - in Arabic *nāsūt, malakūt, jabarūt,* *lāhūt* (which I will use from now on) – can be understood as terms describing different *ʿālam*s (worlds); spheres of existence (Boer 2012). These terms are not dogmatized, and some are more well-known than others. The lowest *maqām* is that of *nāsūt*. It is mostly seen in pair with the term *lāhūt*, in YK the *maqām* of the heart. The meaning of the pair is interpreted differently by various Ṣūfī authors and is well known to Shīʿi imamology (Arnaldez 2012b). In the thought of al-Hallaj the pair of *nāsūt* and *lāhūt* is that of the human sphere and the divine, respectively. *Nāsūt* is the human substance and thus the antonym to *lāhūt* (ibid). Following *nāsūt* is *malakūt* in the ṣūfī perception of *maqām*s in YK. As *nāsut* and *lāhut*, *malakūt* and *jabarūt* also goes in pair (Boer 2012). *Malakūt* is the sphere of “immutable spiritual truths” (ibid.). This meaning the sphere angels, the spiritual reality of man, and some authors includes the souls as well. Over the *maqām* of *malakūt* is the *maqām* of *jabarūt*. In the Encyclopaedia of Islam *jabarūt* is stated as “the world of (divine) Omnipotence” (ibid.). According to al-Ghazali it contains the “impressionable and imaginative faculties of the human soul”. Al-Ghazali’s understanding of the relationship between *malakūt* and *jabarūt* is founded in a very Neoplatonic understanding; the *malakūt* being the shadow of the idea and *jabarūt* being the archetypical idea itself (ibid.). As the last *maqām*, but not the highest in vertical

*[p. 13]* ^pg13

Michaelmas Term sense, is that of *lāhūt*; placed in the heart. As explained, *lāhūt* is the antonym of *nāsūt* and so the divine sphere. Thus, the whole cosmology of Ṣūfī thought is implicated in the YK.

The Tantras are the direct word from God and thus thought of as having a present

Transmission

force. Therefore, it maintains its relevance. This divine voice can only be accessed through initiation with help from a guru (Flood 2006, 19; 63).

The importance of the guru and transmission is also underlined in the SCN. In verse 36 it is mentioned that the yogī must use the knowledge he has gained “by the service of the Parama-guru”. The *paramaguru* is the guru of the yogī’s guru. Woodroffe argues, that this verse emphasises the practice knowledge transmitted via *paramparā*, and not knowledge derived from books (Woodroffe 1972, 454). This underlines the importance of the guru’s knowledge and help to the yogī. Furthermore, it is stated that the yogī must serve and be devoted to the guru (v. 39; 52), and that some practices can only be learned from the guru and his tradition (v. 50; 53). In YK we meet somewhat same understandings of the guru. Three times it is stated that the ṣūfī must listen to “the mouth of the guru” (v. 70; 90; 200). The idea of a guru is also not unfamiliar to Ṣūfīsm. When initiated into Ṣūfī traditions, the student must travel on the initiatory path, the *ṭarīqa*, with direction from a master, in Ṣūfī traditions normally called a shaykh. The shaykh is a mediator between God and man and is indispensable for the student seeking union with God (Geoffroy, 142).

These spiritual masters, gurus or shaykhs, plays an important role in transmitting tradition and memory. To Flood memory passed through generations is central to the concept of tradition (Flood 2004, 8). In his *Ascetic Self* he argues that “Tradition is not passively received but actively reconstructed in a shared imagination and reconstituted in the present as memory” (ibid.). In tantric traditions the (tantric) body is used in reconstructing tradition and recreating memory; the body is thus both representation and lived experience (Flood 2006, 74). This understanding of tradition and body also underlines the relationship between theory and practice; theory being the shared imagination and practice

*[p. 14]* ^pg14

Michaelmas Term being the reconstitution of the memory entailed in the shared imagination (Flood 2004, 8). According to Flood, this can be done via yogīc practice by mapping cosmos or tradition on to the body and thus internalizing both text and tradition. The theory on cosmos, as presented in the tantric texts, must be internalized

Internalization

through yogīc practices and thus codified on the yogīc body (Flood 2006, 11).

In SCN this is done by meditating on the *cakra*s, containing a shared imagination on cosmos. Through this practice the body will be entextualised (ibid., 6). Via the *cakra*s an emanationist understanding of cosmos is expressed. Through the yogic practice one moves closer and closer to the divine source of creation; the merging of Kuṇḍalinī and Śiva in *sahasrāra*. On the way more and more of cosmos is internalized. This is especially expressed via the Sanskrit alphabet being mapped on to the petals of each lotus (*cakra*). Each letter of the alphabet is also a *mantra* and divine in origin; it is a manifestation of the universe via sound (Woodroffe 1972, 135). Basic worldly elements of creation are also internalized via the *cakra*s; water, fire, air, earth, ether. When the yogī reaches the state of *sahasrāra* and liberation, the whole of cosmos is in the body. The body thus becomes an index of the cosmos as presented in the text and understood in the shared imagination of the tradition of the text. The cosmos is internalized, the body entextualised and tradition reconstituted and shared (Flood 2006, 118).

I would argue, that the same can be said of YK, though with an extreme focus on theology over cosmology. As explained the names of the *maqām*s; *nāsūt*, *malakūt*, *jabarūt* and *lāhūt*, are terms identifying different spheres of existence, *ʿālam*s, and thus entails an Islamic understanding of cosmology. This cosmology is clearly internalized via the *maqām*s, as it is seen in SCN. But, besides this cosmology a huge emphasis on theology is found in YK. In the practice of each *maqām*, the ṣūfī must internalize key Islamic and Ṣūfī theological terms, the *mañjil*s (Ar. *manzil*); śarīya, tarikat, hakikat, māraphat – in Arabic; *sharīʿa* (the Islamic law), *ṭarīqa* (the ṣūfī path), *ḥaqīqa* (truth), *maʿrifa* (knowledge); which I will use from now on. These four terms are essential to classic Islamic orthopraxy but is also very central to the ṣūfīs. These four domains constitute the way to God. Explained by Schimmel in *Mystical dimensions of Islam* (1975), the ṣūfīs normally perceive this way as

*[p. 15]* ^pg15

Michaelmas Term tripartite, consisting of *sharīʿa*, *ṭarīqa* and then either *ḥaqīqa* or *maʿrifa*. But in YK we find all four, all defining different and important aspects of Ṣūfī orthopraxy and orthodoxy. In YK these four terms also come to emphasise an emanationist understand of theology and cosmology. This emanationist understanding is very clear in the names of the *maqām*s; showing a movement from *nāsūt*; the human sphere, to *lāhūt*; pure divinity. The same can be said of these four *manzil*s. The *sharīʿa* is found in *nāsūt* and should thus be internalized firstly. The *sharīʿa* is the divine law mainly based on the Qurʾān and authoritative *ḥadīth*s, and mostly concerns Islamic orthopraxy. In YK the five pillars of Islam are mentioned; prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, profession of faith and alms (v. 127). The dimension of *sharīʿa* reflects the understanding of *nāsūt*; the clearly human sphere as the lowest of emanations, where right practice is important.

The *manzil* of *malakūt* is *ṭarīqa*; the Ṣūfī path of discipline. The *ṭarīqa* is above *sharīʿa* because it is the path to God. In YK the ṣūfī is expected to detach himself of everything of this world, along with showing *bhakti* to people (v. 128; 131). This clearly emphasises the ascetic dimension of this Ṣūfī practice. As I see it, this is connected to the sphere of *malakūt*, because the yogī must entail some of the same qualities as the Prophet Muhammad; as a shadow of the idea itself.

In the *maqām* of *jabarūt* is *ḥaqīqa*, the truth or divine Reality (Geoffroy 2012). As mentioned the notion of *ḥaqīqa* is sometimes interchangeable with *maʿrifa*. But, a difference can be outlined. According to al-Hallaj the *ḥaqīqa* is what leads to the Real but is not the Real itself (Gardet 2012). Ibn Arabi presents a different perception of this notion; he sees the *ḥaqīqa* as the ultimate reality of the real itself (ibid.). In relation to the YK the notion of al-Hallaj fits the best. Firstly, because the stage of *ḥaqīqa* is obviously not the last since we have that of *lāhūt* and *maʿrifa*. Furthermore, the requirement of the ṣūfī on this stage seems to be an extension of that of the *ṭarīqa* stage. The ṣūfī must “bear hunger, thirst, and loss of sleep” (YK, v. 134); an underlining of the ascetic requirement of a ṣūfī on the spiritual Ṣūfī path of discipline. Continuing this practice will take the ṣūfī to the *maqām lāhūt* and stage of *maʿrifa*.

The main meaning of *maʿrifa* among ṣūfīs is that of cognition (Arnaldez 2012b). In terms of orthopraxy the stage of *maʿrifa* in YK entails the perfect behaviour of a ṣūfī; one who reads the Qurʾān constantly, loves and is loved, is

*[p. 16]* ^pg16

Michaelmas Term altruistic and indulgent (YK, v. 139-142). The cognitional experience at this stage is also explained; one will see the Lord on his throne in *lāhūt* (v. 80). If one is successful in removing Iblis and his servants, the vision of the Lord will be consistent. This agrees withal-Junayd’s understanding of *fanāʾ* and *baqāʾ*; the annihilation in God, but also the remaining in God. The concept of *fanāʾ* entails an understanding that the ṣūfī becomes a mirror in which God transpires (Geoffroy 2010, 14). This perception is very common to Ṣūfīsm and is also used in the YK (v. 85; 138).

It is clear, that we have an emanationist cosmology that is internalized in the YK. As part of this emanationist interpretation of cosmos an emphasis on the importance of theology is evident. In this cosmology, theology has its importance, because orthopraxy and orthodoxy are inseparable in Islam.

In the SCN the emanationist cosmology is also clear, but no theological terms in relation to orthopraxy are mentioned, such as *karma*, *dharma*, *ahiṃsā* and so on. No requirements of the yogī is settled. This also underlines the differences relating to the goal of the yogīc practice in SCN and YK. In YK the goal is to reach cognition of God, to live in his picture and unite with him after death, whereas in SCN the goal is liberation from this world and bodily immortality.

By inscribing the text onto the body, as exemplified above, the body becomes

Divinisation

entextualised, cosmos and theology internalized, tradition is reconstructed, memory reconstituted, and the experience of the practitioner becomes part of a shared memory and imagination. Flood argues, that in this process the body is divinised. Classic to tantric tradition is, that to worship a god one must become a god (Flood 2006, 11). Flood writes:

“The practitioner in ritual contexts becomes divine such that his or her limited subjectivity is transcended or expanded and that subjectivity becomes coterminous with the subjectivity of his or her deity, which is to say that the text is internalised and subjectivity becomes text-specific” (Flood 2006, 11).

The divinisation of the body is done by entextualising the body. Flood emphasises the physical ritual of imposing *mantra*s with the fingers on to the body, before the inner practice. SCN and YK are not ritual manuals of physical ritual, but only

*[p. 17]* ^pg17

Michaelmas Term describing the inner worship and meditational practice – besides the requirements of the ṣūfī in relation to the *manzil*s. I do not know the physical ritual of either SCN nor YK, but it is clear to me that these texts calls for divinisation of the body via inner practice. In SCN we see that *mantra*s are imposed on to body, both as the Sanskrit alphabet and the *bījamantra*s. In YK *mantra*s are not as many as in SCN. Nevertheless, it is mentioned several times, that “the Name” must be repeated and memorized (YK v. 8; 104; 108; 112; 116; 160). But, there is no doubt that this “*mantra*” is not mapped on to the body in the same way as we see in SCN. But, I will still argue that an inner divinisation is done.

As shown, the practice of YK leads to a cognition of God; one sees himself in his picture via the mirror and, via *fanāʾ*, annihilate himself into God. I argue, that exactly this means that the subjectivity of the ṣūfī becomes transcended and expanded; one comes to understand all emanations of God by tracing a route back to the divine source via yogīc practice – and that the subjectivity of the ṣūfī become coterminous with God; there is an understanding and annihilation, extinction of the self into God – “māraphat is my unveiling” (v. 242). But, to say that the ṣūfī becomes a god, would be paradoxical because in Islam ‘there is no god, but God’. Instead the term of *baqāʾ* is used; after the annihilation with God the ṣūfī subsist in and by God, lives through Him and sees Him in everything (Geoffroy 2010, 15). The subjectivity of the ṣūfī becomes coterminous with that of God, the text is internalised, and the subjectivity is text-specific.

Both the SCN and YK entails so many religious phenomena, terms and understandings as part of the esoteric anatomy. One could have mentioned the dualism of SCN, the understanding of death in YK and so on. But since this essay is on the tantric body, which is highly text and tradition specific, I believe that the above comparison is sufficient in discussing whether the body in YK can be said to be tantric or not.

So, is the body of YK a tantric body? Yes, I would say. If we take a look at Flood’s

Tantric Ṣūfīsm

theory on the tantric body as stated in the beginning of this essay, it surely is. He writes:

*[p. 18]* ^pg18

Michaelmas Term “The theory I wish to present is simply this. The tantric body is encoded in tradition-specific and text-specific ways. The practitioner inscribes the body through ritual and forms of interiority or ascetism, and so writes the tradition on to the body. Such transformative practices are intended to create the body as divine. This inscribing the body is also a reading of text and tradition” (Flood 2006, 13)

Is the body encoded in tradition- and text-specific ways? Yes, it is. We see a tradition- and text-specific understanding of an esoteric anatomy consisting of *maqām*s and *manzil*s. Does the practitioner inscribe the body through ritual? Yes, he does – if he follows the practice as described in YK. It is also very clear that the tradition is mapped on to the body; both the cosmology and theology of this tradition is internalized. These practices of YK are indeed intended to create the body as divine; but not in the same sense as we see in SCN. It is important to understand, that though the annihilation of *fanāʾ* could be interpreted as a merging or union of the practitioner with God, this would be paradoxical. The body is divine in the sense that God acts through the ṣūfī and that the ṣūfī realizes his origin as being a part of God’s emanations.

This one example of YK does not make it correct to say, that Ṣūfīsm in general is tantric. But, Flood puts a lot of emphasis on the specificity of text and tradition, and with that in mind, I would argue that Ṣūfī practices can be tantric, and that they use the tantric body in passing on tradition, reconstituting memory and reconstructing the shared imagination of tradition, as we see in traditions of the Tantras.

Furthermore, YK is not the only Ṣūfī text consisting tantric yoga practice. Carl Ernst and Kazuyo Sakaki show the influence of a Sanskrit text in parts of the Muslim world (Ernst 2003 & 2005; Sakaki 2005). The text should allegedly have been called Amṛtakuṇḍa in Sanskrit, and has been translated into Arabic, Persian, Turkish and Urdu (Ernst 2003, 202). Unfortunately, the original Sanskrit manuscript does no longer exist. Ernst is working on a critical translation of these texts and has been for over 20 years, but it has not yet been published[^1]. But, in many of his articles he writes on the Amṛtakuṇḍa and its relation to Indian yogīs and especially Nāth yogīs (Ernst 2005, 21). The Islamic translations of Amṛtakuṇḍa

[^1]: I know this from a mail correspondence with Carl Ernst, November 2017

*[p. 19]* ^pg19

Michaelmas Term (Arabic: Ḥawẓ al-ḥayāt; Persian: Baḥr al- ḥayāt) seems to entail both Islamic theology and cosmology together with tantric yoga practices and ideas, such as *cakra*s, *mantra*s, yoginīs, *khecarī* and clear correspondences between micro- and macrocosm (Ernst 2003, 211-213). I will not go into a further investigation of the yogīc elements of these Persian and Arabic texts in this essay. But, I want to underline, that YK is not a unique case of intermingling between classic tantric ideas and Islam and Ṣūfīsm. Furthermore, the existence of these texts seems to emphasise that it is possible denote Ṣūfī practices as tantric, but maybe not Ṣūfīsm as tantric. It is of course text- and tradition-specific. It would be very interesting to investigate the translations and Islamic interpretations of Amṛtakuṇḍa, when Carl Ernst publishes his translations.

This essay was set to investigate the body of Yoga Kalandar as tantric; this meaning

Conclusion

the body as a vehicle to transmit a shared memory of tradition via internalisation, entextualisation and divinisation. There seems to be no doubt, that with the definition and theories of Gavin Flood, the body of YK can be said to be tantric. This does not mean that Ṣūfīsm itself is tantric, but that the practices of some Ṣūfī traditions surely is. As Flood emphasises over and over again in *The Tantric Body* this is very tradition- and text-specific. This essay will be the foundation of further studies on the same area. I wish to investigate the relations between Nāth yogīs and ṣūfīs as done to some extend by Carl Ernst and Kazuyo Sakaki. Their work is very extensive but seem to lack an understanding of both tantric Hindu traditions and Islamic mystic tradition at the same time; both in terms of phenomenology and language. Hopefully, this essay will be the beginning of a greater comparative work on yoga and Ṣūfīsm, an intermingling of interesting traditions and religious world views.
