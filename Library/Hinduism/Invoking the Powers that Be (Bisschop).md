## Introduction

In a recently published article, Marko Geslani has drawn attention to the incorporation of śānti rites in the Purāṇic rājyābhiṣeka and, in this connection, discusses the Mahāśānti mantras of the Bṛhatsaṃhitā (BṛS 47.54cd– 60ab) and the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa (ViDhP 2.22).<sup>1</sup> ViDhP 2.22 contains an extensive mantra of one hundred and eighty-five verses in total, requesting all deities and cosmic powers to consecrate the king. Beginning with Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva, the text takes us through a host of cosmic powers, ranging from the four Pāñcarātra vyūhas

(Vāsudeva,

Saṃkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna, and Aniruddha) to various sets of deities and sages, as well as astrological conjunctions, abstract principles, texts, places of pilgrimage, and rivers. The list is a veritable encyclopedia of cosmic power. It shows similarities to a much smaller list of sixteen verses given in the context of the puṣyasnāna ‘bath of prosperity’ in Varāhamihira’s Bṛhatsaṃhitā (BṛS 47.54cd–60ab). This invocation is uttered by the purohita at the king’s puṣyasnāna. It also starts with Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva, before moving on to the Sādhyas, Maruts, and other deities. This invocation also includes astronomical principles, such as the Nakṣatras, the Muhūrtas, and other units of time. The two invocations are clearly related and form a powerful conclusion to the consecration rite. Noteworthy in the mantra of the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa is the inclusion of the four Pāñcarātra vyūhas, immediately after the invocation of the trinity of Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva. This

\*Email: p.c.bisschop@hum.leidenuniv.nl feature is in line with the Pāñcarātra background of the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa and indicates how mantras like these could be used to express sectarian notions.<sup>2</sup> The present paper introduces another extensive Mahāśānti mantra, found in the Śivadharmaśāstra, a text still awaiting critical edition. The Śivadharma’s Mahāśānti mantra is a rich invocation that has much to offer to scholars interested in the development of the pantheon of deities and their iconographies. The Śivadharma’s Mahāśānti mantra also yields important evidence for the way Śaivism came to occupy a central role in creating ‘empires of faith’ in South Asia and beyond. Indeed, the influence of the Śivadharma is attested not only in India but also, for example, in inscriptions of the Khmers in Cambodia.<sup>3</sup> Like BṛS 47.54cd–60ab and ViDhP 2.22, the Mahāśānti mantra in the sixth chapter of the Śivadharmaśāstra invokes an array of divinities and cosmic powers. It illustrates how this kind of ritual invocation was adopted in the strong sectarian context of Śaivism. A study of this mantra may thus add to our understanding how different Hindu religious traditions developed their own competitive models by, on the one hand, harking back to earlier Vedic rituals, but, at the same time, developing them in new directions.

All quotations from chapter six of the Śivadharmaśāstra in the present article are cited from this draft edition.<sup>12</sup> The Śivadharmaśāstra, along with the other texts belonging to the Śivadharma corpus, addresses the lay community of Śiva worshippers. The very first chapter starts with a praise of Śiva worship and the characteristics (lakṣaṇa) of the śivabhaktas. The Śivadharmaśāstra and the Śivadharmottara are both free from tantric influences, which hints at a relatively early date of composition, although the exact date is still open to discussion.<sup>13</sup> The third text in the corpus, the Śivadharmasaṃgraha, by contrast, shows the influence of tantric models, and has borrowed extensively from the Niśvāsamukha, the opening Śaiva work of the earliest surviving tantra, the Niśvāsatattvasaṃhitā.<sup>14</sup> A diachronic study of the corpus can yield important insights into the development of Śaivism and the ways in which the Śaiva religion incorporated ideals reflecting the concerns of the Śaiva laity, in particular those of local rulers and kings. The Śivadharmaśāstra consists of twelve chapters in total. The teaching is spoken by Nandikeśvara to the sages on mount Meru at the request of Brahmā’s son Sanatkumāra, who asks Nandikeśvara about the supreme dharma called ‘Śivadharma’. According to Nandikeśvara, the teaching was originally spoken by Śiva Pārvatī, (Ṣaṇmukha), himself to Skanda Nandikeśvara, and other gods. In other words, the Śivadharma gives rules for the worship of Śiva that ultimately stem from the mouth of the deity himself. A Śaiva can thus be identified as someone who worships Śiva in accordance with the system set forth by Śiva.<sup>15</sup> In the chapters that follow, different ways of worshipping Śiva, in particular in the form of the liṅga, are discussed, including such practices as setting up liṅgas, constructing temples and hermitages, making donations, observance of fasts, and other activities to be performed by the lay devotees of Śiva. There are indications that the work was conceived in a Pāśupata environment, for there is a long section devoted to the merits of bathing in ashes (chapter eleven), while making donations to Pāśupata-type yogins is recommended in particular (chapter twelve). A hitherto unidentified verse quoted by Kauṇḍinya in his commentary on the Pāśupatasūtra can be traced in chapter eleven of the text. The verse praises the bath in ashes:

yaḥ snānam ācaren nityam āgneyaṃ saṃyatendriyaḥ|
kulaikaviṃśam uttārya sa gacchet paramaṃ padam||<sup>16</sup> He who constantly bathes in ashes, with his senses controlled, he rescues twenty-one generations and reaches the supreme state.

The reference to rescuing twenty-one generations expresses a lay ideal at odds with the strictly ascetic

*[p. 135]* ^pg3

type of Pāśupatism described by Kauṇḍinya. A Pāsupata background of the earliest parts of the Śivadharma corpus is indicated also by the mention of an icon of Lakulīśa in the second chapter of the Śivadharmottara. The image of Śiva to be installed is described in the following words:<sup>17</sup>

tatra mṛddāruśailaṃ vā sthāpayed vidhivac chivam|
sarvavidyāvidhātaraṃ sarvajñaṃ lakulīśvaram||
vṛtaṃ śiṣyapraśiṣyaiś ca vyākhyānodyatapāṇikam|
padmāsanasthaṃ deveśaṃ prasannavadanaṃ gurum||
There, made of mud, wood, or stone, one should install, according to precept, Śiva, the arranger of all sciences, the omniscient one, Lakulīśvara, surrounded by his pupils and pupils’ pupils, with his hand raised in the act of teaching, seated on a lotus, the lord of gods, with a pleasing face, the teacher.

## Śivadharmaśāstra 6: The Mahāśānti

Amidst the descriptions of ritual and religious activities of the śivabhaktas, the long Mahāśānti invocation takes up the sixth chapter of the Śivadharmaśāstra. It is by far the longest chapter of the text. In my draft edition it covers two hundred and fifty-six verses in total. The invocation is introduced by Nandikeśvara in three verses:<sup>18</sup>

ataḥ param idaṃ guhyaṃ rudrodgītaṃ<sup>19</sup> mahodayam|
mahāvighnapraśamanaṃ<sup>20</sup> mahāśāntikaraṃ<sup>21</sup> śubham|| 1||
akālamṛtyuśamanaṃ<sup>22</sup> sarvavyādhinivāraṇam<sup>23</sup>|
paracakrapramathanaṃ<sup>24</sup> sadā vijayavardhanam<sup>25</sup>|| 2||
sarvadevagrahānīkaṃ<sup>26</sup> samabhīṣṭaphalapradam<sup>27</sup>|

śāśvatam<sup>30</sup>|| 3||

Following this, the mantra moves on to other deities in the inner circle around Maheśvara: Nandīśa, Vināyaka, Mahākāla, Ambikā/Gaurī, Mahāmahiṣamardinī, Bhṛṅgiriṭi, and Caṇḍeśvara. This group of deities, along with Kārttikeya, is reminiscent of the circle of eight Gaṇapatis found in tantric Śaiva literature,<sup>66</sup> the only one missing being Vṛṣabha. A later manuscript tradition in fact inserts a three-verse invocation of Vṛṣabha, perhaps with this set of eight Gaṇapatis in mind.<sup>67</sup> Each deity is described briefly. These descriptions provide valuable information on the mythological and iconographical identity of the mentioned deities and

may help in dating the text. Thus, we find that Maheśvara is invoked as four-faced, which is a relatively archaic feature in line with the epic tradition, and not yet as five-headed, which would indicate the influence of tantric Śaivism.<sup>68</sup> On the basis of the descriptions of Bhṛṅgiriṭi and Vināyaka, however, I have concluded that it is unlikely that this part of the text stems from before the sixth century.<sup>69</sup> The extent to which this also applies to other parts of the text remains to be investigated.

After the inner circle of Śaiva deities, the text moves on to a long list of other deities, starting with Brahmā and Viṣṇu, followed by the mother goddesses. It is noteworthy that practically all these deities receive an epithet indicating their devotion to Śiva. Thus, Brahmā is described as being Śiva’

‘solely

(śivasadbhāvabhāvita), while Viṣṇu is said to be ‘endowed with the favour of Śiva’ (śivaprasādasampanna) and ‘engaged in contemplation of Śiva’ (śivadhyānaparāyaṇa).

This characteristic sets it apart from the Mahāśānti mantras of the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa and the Bṛhatsaṃhitā, where the various deities are all invoked in their own right. In the Śaiva universe of the Śivadharma, by contrast, all powers that be are ultimately dependent on and devoted to the worship of Śiva.

In between the two great gods Brahmā and Viṣnu and the eight mothers, Naraharinatha’s edition inserts five more interesting invocations, dedicated respectively to the Arhat, the Buddha, Vijayā, Dharitrī, and cows. These verses do not occur in the manuscripts that I have consulted, with the exception of the Śāradā manuscript, which displays significant variants for these verses. They reflect the inclusive nature of the text and the possibility of extending the list, aimed at including as many powers as can be imagined, including non-Śaiva deities such as the Arhat and the Buddha.

As is to be expected, they are again all said to be devoted to Śiva.<sup>70</sup>

Following the eight mothers (Brahmāṇī, Rudrāṇī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Aindrī, Vārāhī, Cāmuṇḍā, and Lamboṣṭhī),<sup>71</sup> each listed and described in detail, the text invokes various other groups of mother goddesses (Ākāśamātṛs

Lokamātṛs, Rudramātṛs

and and Saumyamātṛs) as well as the Gaṇas in the ten directions.

Then the sages, starting with the Saptaṛṣis, including also their wives and daughters; Siddhas, Vidyādharas, Grahas, and Garuḍas; Daityas Śaṅkukarṇa, Mahānāda, Mahājambha, (Namuci, Hayagrīva, Prahlāda, Tāraka, Agnimukha, Kālanemi, Virocana, Hiraṇyākṣa, Suparva, Suloma, Mucukunda, Sukunda, Revataka), their wives, daughters, and sons; the eight Nāgarājas (named as Ananta, Vāsuki, Takṣaka, Karkoṭaka, Padma, Mahāpadma, Śaṅkhapāla, Kulika) and other Nāgas, their wives, daughters and sons. Next come the sacred rivers (Gaṅgā, Yamunā, Narmadā, Gomatī, Kāverī, Varuṇā, Devikā, Candrabhāgā, Godāvarī, Sarayū, Gaṇḍakī, Kauśikī, Sarasvatī, Nairañjanā, Śoṇa, Mandākinī, Saṃnihitā), both on earth and elsewhere, Yakṣas (Mahāvaiśravaṇa, Maṇibhadra, Suviroma, Vibhāṇḍaka, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Pūrṇabhadra, Pañcika, Virūpākṣa, and others), as well as their wives and daughters, mountains,<sup>73</sup> oceans, and finally, Rākṣasas, Yoginīs/
Ḍākiṇīs, Bhūtas, Pretas, Piśācas, and Grahas, in sum all divine powers (devādayaḥ sarve).

The last mentioned seven groups of beings, all of them wild and terrifying by nature, viz. the Rākṣasas, Yoginīs, Ḍākiṇīs, Bhūtas, Pretas, Piśācas, and Grahas, are invoked in a special manner. This indicates that the worship of Bhairava had become a feature of Śaivism by the time of composition of the text and that his worship was specifically connected to these groups of deities. ## Features of the Śivadharma’s Mahāśānti

While I cannot go into detail here, I would like to draw attention to a few significant features of this lengthy invocation, in particular when we compare it with the

*[p. 137]* ^pg5

Mahāśānti mantras of the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa and the Bṛhatsaṃhitā. First of all, the continuous stress on devotion to Śiva by all these powers stands out: thus, even the rivers are said to ‘delight in the worship of Śiva’s feet’ (śivapādārcane ratāḥ), the mountains are ‘devotees of Śiva’ (śivabhaktāḥ), and the oceans are ‘devoted to the worship of Rudra’ (rudrapūjāparāḥ). In the end, one is led to the conclusion that the powers that be are those deities that recognize the authority of Śiva and are loyal and devoted to him. There is none in a position of power who is not dedicated to Śiva. In this respect, the treatment of the individual powers in the śāntimantras of the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa and the Bṛhatsaṃhitā is strikingly different. In these texts each deity is invoked on its own, without reference to devotion to another deity. In the Śivadharma, however, we are in an exclusively Śaiva universe, where all authority ultimately rests in Śiva alone.

Secondly, the Śivadharma’s invocations are far more detailed in providing iconographic descriptions, mythological attributes, epithets, and so on of the various deities invoked, in contrast to the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa and the Bṛhatsaṃhitā, which are, in the end, no more than lists of names joined with the imperative of the verb abhi-ṣiñc, ‘let them consecrate’. This not only makes the Śivadharma’s mantra a rich source for studying the development of the individual deities and their attributes, but it also gives the mahāśānti a different character. The mantra can be read like a veritable iconographical programme. It is not just an invocation, but it is simultaneously a source of information on the deities for those who recite it or hear the recitation. It is a means of invoking the deities in person, by visualizing them and making their appearance manifest in the mind. All of this is done with emphasis on the fact that all these deities are in fact devoted to Śiva.

This brings me to a third feature of the mantra, which relates to its use. While the mantras of the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa Bṛhatsaṃhitā

and the are clearly meant to be recited at the royal consecration, the Śivadharma’s mantra is meant to ward off dangers in general and bring about welfare to the land and the people. One might say that in the Śivadharma it is the pacification of the deities and their devotion to Śiva that is central, instead of the abhiṣeka of the king, as repeatedly stressed in the Viṣṇudharmottarapurāṇa and the Bṛhatsaṃhitā with the refrain: ete tvām abhiṣiñcantu, ‘let them consecrate you’. The Śivadharma’s refrain, by contrast, is connected to an unspecified first-person subject: śāntiṃ kurvantu me sadā, ‘let them always bestow comfort on me’, with variations. At the end of the chapter it is said that the one who recites or hears the mantra gains different benefits depending on the person in question: one who desires a girl obtains a girl, one who desires victory obtains victory, one who desires wealth obtains wealth, one who desires a son obtains a son, and so on.<sup>88</sup> This fits with the general lay environment, the concerns of which the Śivadharma addresses. ## The use of the Śivadharma’s Mahāśānti according to the Śivadharmottara

The use of the Śivadharma’s Mahāśānti according to the Śivadharmottara

Chapter two of the Śivadharmottara is dedicated to an elaborate ritual complex called vidyādāna, ‘gift of knowledge’. This concerns first of all the instruction of the Śivadharma by the teacher to Śiva’s devotees, but more importantly it refers to the ritual donation of a manuscript to a Śaiva guru along with the support for its recitation.<sup>89</sup> The chapter gives a fascinating account of how to copy a manuscript, a sort of Sanskrit philology avant la lettre, in which one is allowed to make corrections on the spot if one sees fit to do so, and it describes a lengthy ritual in which the donor is a king who has the copied manuscript paraded through the royal capital before he finally hands it over to the Śaiva guru at the Śiva temple (ŚiDhUt 2.59–60). The scene that follows is relevant to putting the Mahāśānti mantra of the Śivadharmaśāstra into context. According to the Śivadharmottara:<sup>90</sup>

śāntyartham ekam adhyāyaṃ gobrāhmaṇamahībhṛtām|
rāṣṭrīyanagarāṇāṃ ca vācayed vācakottamaḥ||
chandolakṣaṇatattvajñaḥ satkavir madhurasvaraḥ|
gāndharvavid vidagdhaś ca śreṣṭhaḥ pustakavācakaḥ||
śāntitoyena rājānaṃ samutthāya gurus tataḥ|
śirasy abhyukṣayed īṣat tatrasthaṃ ca janaṃ tataḥ||
To secure pacification (śāntyartham) for the cows, brahmins, the kings, and the towns in the kingdom, the most excellent reciter should recite one chapter \[from the book\]; the best reciter of books is proficient with the characteristics of metre, a true poet, has a sweet voice, is a knower of the art of the Gandharvas and clever. Rising up the guru should then sprinkle the king a little with appeasement water (śāntitoya) on the head and then also the people present there.

There can be little doubt that the manuscript not mentioned further by name from which a chapter should be read is in fact a copy of the Śivadharmaśāstra and that it is chapter six of the Śivadharmaśāstra which should be recited on this occasion. The ritual described does not appear to be a rājābhiṣeka as such, but is a more general pacification rite meant for the entire population of the kingdom headed by the king. Central to the ritual is a book, a copy of the Śivadharmaśāstra, handed over to the Śaiva guru. The welfare and success of the kingdom

*[p. 138]* ^pg6

have come to be grounded in the king’s donation of a copy of a Śaiva scripture to the Śaiva guru.

The copying, study, and recitation of the Śivadharma is attested in several medieval inscriptions from South Asia.<sup>91</sup> Most revealing is a bilingual twelfth-century Cālukya inscription from Karnataka,<sup>92</sup> which reports a grant of land made to support, among others, the performance of the recitation of the Śāntyadhyāya itself, the sixth chapter of the Śivadharmaśāstra. This was not a one-time event, for the text was to be recited ‘from month to month, on the two 8th and two 14th days, new moon and full moon, and the vyatîpâta and sankramaṇa conjunctions’.<sup>93</sup> The inscription then moves on to describe other occasions at which the Śāntyadhyāya was to be recited: ‘on the mahâ-parvvas, the two equinoxes and the two solstices, on the eclipse of the sun or moon, on the full moon days in Ashâḍha, Kârttika, Mâgha, and Vaiśâkha’.<sup>94</sup> The Śāntyadhyāya was thus singled out as a central piece for recitation purposes and used at various ritual times of the year. A testimony to this use is the existence of manuscripts that only transmit this particular chapter of the Śivadharma. Thus, the online catalogue of the NGMCP not only records a host of Śivadharma manuscripts, but also mentions a number of paper manuscripts of the Śāntyadhāya/Śāntikādhyāya of the Śivadharmaśāstra alone.<sup>95</sup> This indicates the use and importance of the sixth chapter also in later times.<sup>96</sup>

More research is needed to trace the place of the Śivadharma’s Mahāśānti mantra in the history of Hindu kingship. For this it is first of all necessary to have access to a reliable, critical edition of the Śivadharmaśāstra and the various texts that follow in its wake. A simple search for the title Śivadharma in the online catalogue of the NGMCP alone already yields fifty-four hits, which can be easily extended by other search criteria. Manuscripts of the Śivadharma also survive from South India, Bengal, and Kashmir. The work is referred to in several inscriptions from South and Southeast Asia. Sanderson has shown that some of the inscriptions identifying those who are punished in the hells depicted on the gallery of hells at Angkor Vat go back to the text of the Śivadharmottara.<sup>97</sup> There can be little doubt that the Śivadharma corpus has played a key role in the success of Śaivism through the ages. It is about time that these texts receive the attention they deserve.
