The historicity of psychoanalytic categories and 

the illusion of desire as a natural state 

The question posed by Herbert Marcuse in Eros and Civilization is not, as The New Spirit of Capitalism would have us believe, that of authenticity, but of ‘instinct’, or, more precisely, of Trieb, usually translated into French as ‘pulsion’ and into English as ‘drive’. And in this respect, Eros and Civilization is a more interesting and important book than One Dimensional Man. There is no doubt, moreover, that this discourse on the Trieb and, through it, the question of the relation between capitalism and libido, shaped the events of May 1968 in France and elsewhere. It also influenced French thought after 1968, including in particular the denunciation of the oppositional discourse on alienation by so-called ‘French theory’. The problem with Marcuse’s thought — in its impact on the international student movement, and especially the French student movement, at the end of the 1960s — lies in his reading of Freud. This problem, however, is not identified in The New Spirit of Capitalism. 

Marcuse’s central thesis is that the reality principle has, in the age of industrial capitalism, become the performance principle, in the service of what he calls surplus-repression. And he proposes a critique of the Freudian theory of libido on the grounds that it treats as natural a situation that is in fact historical, and therefore contingent. Marcuse calls for a perspectival change in support of 

*[p. 44]* ^pg52

a struggle for the ‘liberation of instincts’, on the basis of a change of theoretical perspective. This change would consist firstly in distinguishing two planes of the reality principle: the structural plane, which is in this way a kind of ‘nature of desire’; and the historical plane, that is, a non-permanent plane — the performance principle being the configuration of the reality principle proper to the industrial age. 

This distinction is interesting because it is premised on the principle that the forces through which psychoanalytic categories are formalized are themselves formed through an historical process. It is unclear, however, how it would be possible to isolate \[dégager\] a structural plane that would not be always already historical, that is, historically or protohistorically or prehistorically engaged \[gagé\]. A plane, in other words, that would not be always already artefactual, to the extent that the artefact is precisely the operator of processuality and of the historicization of these categories, engaging desire precisely as a process of individuation — but this is the very thing that escapes Marcuse. That the artefact is the operator of the process, that is, its condition, means that it is not possible to isolate a pre-processual basis of this process: the ‘structural’ is elementarily ‘historical’ and as such ‘accidental’, and this is neither guided nor contained by any ontology that would permit description of the constitutive elements of the process. The elements are always already supplements: elementarity is an elementary supplementarity. 

What I am here calling the process of formation of psychoanalytic categories, through which elements are always already becoming supplements (taken up into a mechanism that is always already, phantasmatic and fantastic, that is, “fictioning? the real, including as technical invention), is the process of psychic, collective and technical (that is, artefactual) individuation. Within such a process the elements are not as important as the relations between them. If there may be elements that maintain their form, or rather maintain themselves through metastable rather than stable forms, this is only because forces are constituted through relations. These relations occut in at least pairs, which seem to form oppositions but in reality compose — and which cannot, contrary to Cartesian. method, be de-composed into simple elements without being destroyed by this very decomposition. And the common becoming 

*[p. 45]* ^pg53

of these relations, within such a three-stranded individuation process, falls within the purview of genealogy, itself conceived on the basis of organology. 

General organology’ is the theoretical framework of the genealogy that accounts for this processuality: it describes this process as a series of defunctionalizations and refunctionalizations of living, artificial and social organs. This process constitutes a genealogy of the noetic sensible.\* In this genealogy, it is technics that, itself constituting a process of individuation, comes constantly to reconfigure psychic individuation, which falls on the side of the pleasure principle, and collective individuation, on the side of the reality principle — and it does so by rearticulating the transductive relation in which they are constituted. This means that one does not precede the other ~ the pleasure principle does not precede the reality principle. The reality principle, as circuit, is on the contrary the realization of the pleasure principle, to the extent that it is the horizon of transindividuation of psychic individuation, governing the pleasure principle as its différance - a différance that passes through the circuit of collective individuation or, obviously, may fail to do so. 

Now, the reading of Freud postulated by Marcuse presumes, on the contrary, in its stiff, ambiguous and hesitant way, that the pleasure principle precedes its domination by the reality principle. It presumes, that is, that the pleasure principle precedes the reality principle itself, as a kind of lost paradise from which the desiring subject will have fallen, after some kind of fault. Perhaps this fault would consist in technicizing life, that is, killing it through the internalization of that deadness that is technics. The desiring subject would then have fallen into a fate consisting in the repression of the ‘instincts’ and the ‘domination of the reality principle’ over the pleasure principle, as if the history of desire must take the form of a negative eschatology, of an apocalypse. The time would now have come, however, for us to rise up and escape from this apocalypse, via the messianic leap of revolutionary struggle. 

Writing and reading these words, I cannot help but be struck by the resemblance, in its general outline, between this process and what I myself have described as the path leading from the prehistoric human group to control societies. These societies have now become uncontrollable, and therefore require what in The 

*[p. 46]* ^pg54

Decadence of Industrial Democracies | referred to as a jumpstart, which I believe to be revolutionary. Nevertheless, the profound difference between my discourse and Marcusian thinking lies in his presupposition of a kind of natural state of desire, whereas I posit that desire is constituted through its artefactuality. Furthermore, the path that has led societies to become uncontrollable does not seem to me to be a continuous straight line to the destruction of individuation, that is, desire. Rather, it consists in a series of oscillations, admittedly of increasing amplitude, alternating between disindividuating tendencies and a reconstruction, on the basis of these same tendencies, of new types of individuation processes, that is, new forms of psychic and collective individuation. Herein lies the play of what I call the doubly epochal redoubling of the historical epochs of individuation: the technological epokhé is always disindividuating, and therefore requires an individuating redoubling formed through practices of individuation, and in this case, through hypomnesic practices of otium, which I also refer to as care. 

It is a matter, therefore, of thinking care, care that must be sought within each historical or genealogical stage of a series of individuations as that which enables a psychopathology to be articulated with a sociopathology — that is, that enables individuation to be thought in terms of a political economy, itself conceived on the basis of a libidinal economy. 

From psychopathology to sociopathology 

2 Marcuse’s distinction between the structural plane and the historical plane is, from the perspective of a theory of psycho-sociotechnical individuation, invalid. Nevertheless, he does utilize this distinction in order to pose psychopathological problems in sociopathological terms, that is, in political terms. It is true — and this is so despite the fact that no structural plane can be isolated as an element from the relation that is the reality principle - that the reality principle unfolds historically, or, more precisely, that there is an historical becoming of the relation between pleasure and desire. This being the case, this relation, if not this element, may indeed become an object, not just of psychic care, but of collective struggle. 

*[p. 47]* ^pg55

This is why it appears that the distinction between the structural and the historical is what enables the articulation of the psychic and the collective, that is, psychoanalysis and politics: ‘\[P\]sychological categories . . . have become political categories. . . . \[T\]he cure of personal disorder depends more directly than before on the cure of the general disorder.’” 

The goal is laudable: the present work has a similar objective —and I argue that blaming parents and children, and medicalizing their psychic suffering, serves only to conceal the historical processes destroying libidinal energy. Marcuse, however, fails in his undertaking because, instead of studying relations, he prefers to define elements that he imagines can be referred back to an ontology, and he believes this because he does not perceive the place of the artefact in the constitution of desire. This failure, which will contribute to the persistence of the ambiguities of ‘artistic critique’, must today be meticulously analysed, given that a new critique must be formulated to confront capitalism’s loss of spirit. 

Psychoanalytic categories have a genesis, and to this extent psychopathology is a sociopathology. In this genesis it is the artefact, and therefore the accident — that is, exteriorization — that sets things in motion. Marcuse tries to reconstitute the genesis of the reality principle so as to ground his critique of Freud. But this critique relies on an erroneous and above all regressive reading: Marcuse would have us turn back to a discourse of the opposition of tendencies, whereas there are only processes of the composition of tendencies. It was one of Freud’s major achievements to have established a form of thinking that, after Plato, was completely new, and that was first rediscovered by Nietzsche. This way of thinking was characteristic of Heraclitus, but more generally constituted the Presocratic, tragic style of thought. 

Marcuse’s critique is regressive to the extent that the constitutive elements of the tendencies, once they have been decomposed by his critique, no longer seem to be tendencies at all, that is, forces composing in a process through which they co-constitute and co-individuate themselves. Rather, they seem to be hypostasized powers, and as such principles. Freud’s great accomplishment, however, was to have shown that only the play of forces is open to analysis: forces must be distinguished, but they can no 

*[p. 48]* ^pg56

longer be opposed, since it is the relation through which they compose that constitutes them. Forces, which are also processes, may indeed appear to be principles, but this merely reflects the fact that they may be metastabilized in historical forms that seem stable but which are in fact metastable. It is inherent to every epoch to naturalize processes that are in reality, as social and historical organizations of the play of forces, artefactual, and as such simulacra. 

In his wish to inscribe the becoming of forces in an historical process through which he will be able to oppose them, Marcuse fails to see that the question is the ‘and’ through which the psychic and the collective compose. Nor does he see that this ‘and’, which constitutes the simultaneously conjunctive and disjunctive relation of the psychic to the collective, is technics. Marcuse is unable to perceive that technics conjoins and disjoins two planes that form, in their tension, a process of individuation, that is, a genealogy that transforms the play through which the pleasure principle and the reality principle compose. Marcuse did introduce the legitimate goal of thinking psychoanalytic categories as a becoming, and as an always already psycho-social becoming, rather than as a pre-established, fixed, psychic structure. Despite having done so, however, the distinction between the structural and the historical plane ends up contributing to the generation of the ambiguities that befell ‘artistic critique’, ambiguities that continue to close off opportunities for critique. On this point, the elaboration of a new critique passes through the critique of Marcuse, and, in so doing, through a general critique of psychoanalysis, of which it is here possible only to sketch the outlines.’

Contradictions between Marcuse’s Marxism and his Freudianism in relation to struggle (eris) against the risk of decomposition. Moving beyond guilt Marcuse tends to oppose what in fact composes, and this means that he tends to reintroduce a Marxist, that is, a dialectical, way of thinking, whereas Freudian thought constitutes a decisive rupture with and advance upon dialectics, that is, on Platonism.’ As a result, he has little choice but to pose the question of the 

*[p. 49]* ^pg57

relation between the pleasure principle and the reality principle in terms of a struggle in which one must dominate, defeat and overthrow the other, in order to bring the struggle to an end. The alternative is to think the interplay between the pleasure and reality principles, to reflect on their past and future becoming, to conceive this future as a struggle without end, if not without motives, between tendencies for which we, the current players, are responsible. And in this case, it is a responsibility to ensure that the struggle does not end with the decomposition of its tendencies. 

Marcuse thus reasons by analogy with what he believes to be the necessary class struggle of the proletariat, who, in this class struggle, must overthrow capital: the pleasure principle must in the same way impose its law on the reality principle. This reasoning in terms of revolutionary overthrow or inversion is grounded in the unconscious idea of an original fault, a guilt that constitutes, for Freud, the origin of libido - a question that is never raised by Marcuse. Marcuse was indeed justified in wanting to denaturalize the Freudian categories and thereby to bring the psychopathological (that is, psychoanalytic) level together with the sociopathological (that is, political) level. This failure to raise the question of guilt means, however, that his way of doing so was doubly erroneous: on the one hand, his thought is a regression from Freud’s achievement of thinking in terms of tendencies; and, on the other hand, he repeats Freud’s own error, which consists in failing to think, in the play of forces, the artefactuality of desire itself - as desire for what does not exist but which, incalculably, consists (while making exist, bringing into existence, that which does not exist: namely, artefacts). 

Marcuse knows there is a problem with his form of reasoning, a form that opposes what in fact composes, and it is to justify his reasoning that he distinguishes between a structural reality principle and an historical reality principle. This is a seductive distinction, because it allows history to be introduced into the evolution of psychoanalytic categories, but it is dangerous, because it leads back to thinking in terms of principles. But it is thinking in terms of principles that, precisely, we must project beyond, so as to reach a relation, and in this case a relation that is a liaison. Doing so makes it possible to think desire beyond pleasure, and to think 

*[p. 50]* ^pg58

this as a transductive relation generating infinite struggles that can never be won because there is no guilty party, no-one at fault - there is no fault but only default, which opens play. This default that opens play is necessary, and it is this tragic thought that enables thinking to go beyond not only the pleasure principle, but beyond original guilt. 

Marcuse’s ‘Freudo-Marxist’ synthesis works, then, by identifying the overthrow of the domination of capital with the overthrow of the domination of the reality principle. On one side we find the class struggle, and the revolutionary horizon wherein the proletariat, that is, labour, must overthrow the power of capital. On the other side, there is the struggle of principles, and the revolutionary horizon wherein the pleasure principle must overthrow the reality principle, which here also means the moral order that justifies it, and the super-ego that arises within it as the domination of the father, Now, the default of mortals, that is, their prostheticity, stems from the fault of Epimetheus, that is, of a god, opening the horizon of their eris, that is, of their competitive emulation \[émulation), but which can always become their discord. But in Marcuse we see the disappearance in a single blow of everything that, in this mortal default, this prostheticity, necessitates dike and aidos as consistences that do not exist, a dike and aidos that we must thus distinguish from law (which does exist), and from the translation of ethics into morals (which does exist). And all this disappears into what, for Marcuse, is little more than a super-egoistic apparatus that represses the ‘instincts’, instincts that it is then a matter of ‘liberating’. 

Technics, super-ego and desublimation 

4 Ignorance of the question of technics — that is, of the role of tertiary retention - combines here with regression in relation to the Freudian theory of the composition of tendencies. Technics is then viewed as what conveys, through the industrial apparatus, the domination of the reality principle. Marcuse comes in fact to see Freudian theory as legitimating repression in the service of this domination. Confronted with this, and against it, Marcuse sets as his political agenda, and as a psycho-sociopathological question, the ‘liberation of instinctual needs’ through the establishment of 

*[p. 51]* ^pg59

Jl 

‘4 non-repressive mode of existence’ constituting ‘a new stage of civilization’.'° Marcuse, in other words, does not see technics as what conveys both synchronization (as the condition of transindividuation, and as tertiary retentional milieu), that is, the reality principle, and diachronization, that is, the pleasure principle. But also as what conveys, more profoundly, the death drive and the life drive. Technics is not conceived as the condition of conjunction and disjunction in individuation, but solely as an instrument of control, one that may certainly be overthrown through the pleasure principle becoming dominant, but not as what constitutes pleasure as such, that is, idividuation as such. 

If the agenda today is more than ever a matter of asserting not just the possibility but the absolute necessity of shifting to a new stage in the history of civilization, it is certainly not a question of positing that this new civilization will be a ‘non-repressive’ mode that liberates our ‘instinctual needs’. The price of the intermittent character of the passage to noetic actuality is that regression and repression are the tragic conditions of the noetic, which can only distinguish justice from law and ethics from morality insofar as there exists Sittlichkeit (that is, manners, savoir-vivre) and Moral-itat. How, in any case, could savoir-vivre be isolated from manners, or manners from all morality? How to isolate existence from an ethics that would be the experience of aidos, that is, from shame as being ashamed in the face of ignorance of the necessary difference between what exists and what does not exist yet consists? Infinite justice, which is an object of desire, cannot be reduced to finite law. But desire can desire its object, which is structurally infinite, only insofar as it undergoes the experience of its default (which is not a lack, which is more than a lack, and which is the consistence of its non-existence) in the existence of a law that is necessary. 

A tendency is constituted in relation to what ties it transductively to its counter-tendency, and life is what accomplishes (realizes) death (as the dead), and conversely (this is why life is full of phantoms, that is, revenances of the dead in the form of spirits and fetishes, objects that become animated and constitute mystical forces — including commodities). Pleasure, on the other hand, is what constructs reality (insofar as fantasy arises through the individuation of psychic beings who, incomplete, pursue themselves 

*[p. 52]* ^pg60

in their fantasy, and who, through this transindividuating pursuit, trans-form reality, and are themselves conversely and transductively trans-formed through it): pleasure cannot be opposed to reality, nor life to death, freedom to repression, justice to law, ethics to morality, necessity to the accident, and so on. 

It is the relation between the two great drive-based tendencies that evolves over time, as a series of compositions, and with the evolution of this relation so too the principles evolve. The most interesting aspect of Eros and Civilization, despite its limits and in these limits, is that it understands the drives as ‘material’ or, more exactly, as the potential for evolution (which should be described as the preindividual stage, but this is very far from Marcuse’s thinking''): ‘\[The\] primary “drives” of the human organism . . . are subject to historical modification.’ 

Hence is posed the problem of a genealogy of desire. This becomes very explicit when the reality principle (that is, for us, the pleasure/reality relation) becomes the performance principle. The reality principle reaches a point where, as industrial capitalism, and as we shall see for Marcuse himself, it coincides with a process of desublimation that is also a diversion of libidinal energy. Libidinal energy thereby becomes mortified, leading to a fall in libidinal energy, in turn necessitating the revolutionary organization of a new form of sublimation. 

Marcuse calls this new form ‘non-repressive’: 

I propose in this book the notion of a ‘non-repressive sublimation’: the sexual impulses, without losing their erotic energy, transcend their immediate object and eroticize normally non- and anti-erotic relationships between the individuals, and between them and their environment." 

5 Processes of adoption and diversions of libido: Marcuse and the tendency of libidinal energy to fall l argue in Uncontrollable Societies of Disaffected Individuals that hyperindustrial capitalism diverts infantile libido, normally invested in parents, towards objects of consumption.'\* But the possibility of such diversion is inscribed in a process of adoption 

*[p. 53]* ^pg61

that defines any process of psychic, collective and technical indiyiduation. This process of adoption is itself founded on an originary and constitutive capacity to divert libido from its objects, which are less ‘natural’ and ‘spontaneous’ than they are configured by the state of organization of collective individuation — which is also responsible for the historical character of perversion. This diversion is an arrangement between the terms of transductive relations through which tendencies come together and compose with one another. I thus also argue, on the other hand, that the current stage of this diversion is unusual in that it leads to a decomposition of these tendencies, that is, to their destruction as tendencies composing and co-positing desire, both individual and collective. This most recent stage is, therefore, an organization of psychic and collective individuation that favours a regression to the drives, through which tendencies become unbound, and at which point perversion may become decomposition. 

This diversion has therefore always existed. Marcuse paraphrases Freud: ‘The methodical sacrifice of libido, its rigidly enforced deflection to socially useful activities and expressions, is culture.’\* Or, quoting Freud directly: ‘\[Society\] must see to it that the number of these members is restricted and their energies directed away from sexual activities on to their work.’!® But Marcuse remains ambiguous on precisely this point. He seems suspicious of Freud and ultimately to oppose this perspective, to the point that we may well wonder if he does not identify this diversion with a fall, and with guilt: the culpability of capital, of which technics would be the harbinger and the condition, as the disposition to control. This would be something like a resurgence and a reinterpretation of that aspect of religion that has always defined work as a fall, whereas in Greek mythology it is a matter of vengeance between the gods, mortals being merely the victims, not the authors, of a divine fault arising from a divine conflict. 

As for what Marcuse describes as the overcoming of the erotic object by erotic energy, that is, as generalized erotization, this passes through narcissism as a source of sublimation and investment. This overcoming moves from the self-centred object of love towards those objects that constitute the ego as the sedimentation of its identification processes, that is, as the sedimentation of the series of libidinal diversions and the successive projections and 

*[p. 54]* ^pg62

adoptions, more or less perverse, in which it would consist. And it is in order to oppose this movement of sublimatory erotization, whose source lies in narcissism, that Marcuse refers to ‘repressive desublimation’. The latter is a very interesting concept, because it illustrates the way in which the diversion of libido towards objects of consumption consists in subjecting the criteria for this diversion to the performance principle, that is, to a reality principle itself totally subjected to the imperative of gaining a return on industrial investments, that is, of obtaining the best possible performance: 

Conversely, one can speak of ‘repressive de-sublimation’: release of sexuality in modes and forms which reduce and weaken erotic energy. In this process too, sexuality spreads into formerly tabooed dimensions and relations. However, instead of re-creating these dimensions and relations in the images of the Pleasure Principle, the opposite tendency asserts itself: the Reality Principle extends its hold over Eros.’ 

This ‘release of sexuality in modes and forms which reduce and weaken erotic energy’ is in a certain way already — 13 years before 1968, and long before The New Spirit of Capitalism — the identification of a ‘recuperation-implementation’ of the struggle against the paternal super-ego for the benefit of a reorganization of capital. 

In other words, Eros and Civilization succeeds in identifying - in the evolution of capitalism and in the organization of the pleasure/reality relation under the auspices of the performance principle — the problem that the tendency of libidinal energy to fall is inherent to the performance principle. This principle seems, at a single stroke, to be the operator of identification for a singular epoch, and to constitute a limit in the history of libido: what I myself analyse as the hyperindustrial epoch of the decomposition of the tendencies constitutive of desire, concretely expressed as cultural capitalism. Cultural capitalism deploys and systematizes what, at the beginning of the twentieth century, American capitalism identified as the issue of consumption, and this leads to the destruction of desire itself and, with it, to the destruction of sublimation: to ‘repressive desublimation’. 

The problem is that Marcuse’s reasoning, despite being highly prescient, nevertheless consists in opposing sublimation and 

*[p. 55]* ^pg63

5S 

repression and in identifying repression with the reality principle. Marcuse never analyses the question of whether there could be a tendency to regression that is constitutive of the noetic soul. The noetic soul passes into actuality (‘acts out’) both noetically and destructively, that is, through processes of negative sublimation, which is something altogether different from desublimation, though it is among its worst effects. This tendency to regression is exploited by a logic that diverts libido to the best-performing objects possible. This new process of adoption, controlled by management and marketing, consists in selecting objects of libidinal investment in accordance not with what reinforces the transindividuation process, that is, psychic and collective individuation, but rather with what provides the best rate of return on investment (financial investments, but via diverted libidinal investments). And this return is expected on a very short-term basis, in the context of a global capitalism that has largely ‘liberated’ itself from its attachment to industrial capitalism, that is, to objects of technical knowledge.

‘Liberation of instincts’, technesis and the passage of desire — the thrust of the knife Marcuse, by establishing that the performance principle is only the culmination of the growing domination of the reality principle, tends to turn the latter into a less effective version of the performance principle, but a principle of the same nature, given that this principle, as a principle, bas a nature, and is thus not simply a tendency: he tends as a general rule to oppose pleasure and reality, rather than seeing them as two ends of a continuum. Marcuse himself paves the way for the discourse of desublimation through a critique of the super-ego that consists in opposing the super-ego to the ‘liberation of instincts’, as a result of which this critique tends to become less a critique than a denunciation. And hence whatever progress there might have been towards a reinterpretation of sublimation is immediately reverted, sending the Marcusian position on a regressive path in relation to Freud’s own achievements. The problem is that by defining this new sublimation as ‘non-repressive’, oppositional logic is immediately introduced, and much of his reasoning is thereby invalidated. 

*[p. 56]* ^pg64

There is no sublimation, nor any individuation, no concretizing or fixing of libidinal energy on this or that object, without the occurrence of a process of repression - if only as the circuit of différance that extends a pleasure and realizes a desire. Libidinal investments always lead to processes of appropriation (of which the law is the exappropriation, as shown by Derrida'\*), but they are also diachronizing phenomena that play out only within horizons of synchronizations. As such, they contribute to the evolution of these synchronizations, but they exist only in tension with that which they are causing to change. These tensions are potentials contained in the supersaturated milieus that form the horizons of synchrony, and when an individuation passes into actuality, that is, diachronizes itself, these potentials are revealed in synchrony 

itself 

— or, more precisely, they are revealed as being contained by it as its repressed contents.'? This is the process I described in De la misére symbolique 2 as the play of stereotypical and trauma-typical secondary (psychic or collective) retentions.”° 

The constitution of a new form of sublimation, therefore, occurs through the socialization of a new reality principle, and not against the reality principle. As was shown clearly by Derrida in The Post Card, the reality principle is the pleasure principle, but as its différance — that is, its writing, which according to my own analysis also means its technesis. Technesis is effectively realized only to the extent that it is inscribed in collective individuation. It is precisely this question of technesis, however, that is, of the role of technical individuation in psychic individuation, that Marcuse ignores. But it is technesis that forms the three-stranded process of individuation, a process that can emerge only from what Leroi-Gourhan described as a process of exteriorization (that from the outset lies outside the relations between the ‘structural plane’ and the ‘historical plane’ of the reality principle), through which what Marcuse refers to as the ‘mutability of the instincts’ takes place: 

The animal drives become human instincts under the influence of the external reality. Their original ‘location’ in the organism and their basic direction remain the same, but their objectives and their manifestations are subject to change. All psychoanalytic concepts (sublimation, identification, projection, repression, introjection) connote the mutability of the instincts.” 

*[p. 57]* ^pg65

This mutability falls outside the history of the relations between the ‘structural plane’ and the ‘historical plane’ of the reality principle, and this distinction is therefore unworkable: what is structural is not some norm for a reality principle defined in some unknown way. Rather, what is ‘structural’ here is the play of two principles, insofar as these operate via technesis, which is precisely what opens up this play. It does so, firstly, as fetishism and projection: it is elementary supplementarity, which reaches the point of being a principle only as an historically determined epoch of a tendency, a tendency that is itself indeterminable other than in its relation to a counter-tendency. The reality principle, like the performance principle, is concretely expressed in social organizations that are historically particular, but what must be thought, beyond this principle, is the tendency as drive, or ‘instinct’. Furthermore, social organizations can be conceived only on the basis of the artificial organs that support them, collections of tertiary retentions that they appropriate through the constitution of retentional systems. These systems define the criteria through which the relations between pleasure and reality are regulated, that is, the rela- . 

tions between fiction and truth.” 

And the ‘mutability of instincts’, through which animality becomes humanity, is the passage of desire, which begins by deferring the satisfaction of the drive, or in other words by saving it up, economizing it. Libido is libidinal energy, and this energy is produced by an economy, the economy of différance, through which the drive becomes an energy invested in objects that are highly variable, and are so because they are fantasies, that is, supports of the projection of what does not exist: the singular, that is, incalculable, object. This projection — which is therefore not pathological, except in the sense that love is a pathology, a good pathology, as was suggested, for instance, in Plato’s Symposium ~ is called individuation. For this différance itself presupposes a supplement, which makes it the prosthetization of the living, that is, the artificial and artefactual becoming of the organ, and of psychic organization as well as social organization. 

‘All psychoanalytic concepts’ are thus concepts wherein the psychic is constituted only in the social, to the strict extent that life ex-sists, that is, that it seizes the dead and is seized by it in return. The dead: that is, tertiary retention, the inorganic

organized as spatialized time and temporalized space — technics. This is what from the beginning is left out of Marcuse’s analysis, and for this reason his account of the relations between the structural and historical planes of the reality principle never gets off the ground. Whereas, in fact, it is clear that the passage from the psychic to the collective is also the passage from pleasure to reality — that is, desire as such, and as passage. 

Even though the wish to think psychopathology as sociopathology requires it, Marcuse does not think individuation insofar as it is psychic and collective. This ‘and’ is both conjunctive and disjunctive — as I argue in my analysis of the Antigone complex” and in my analysis of artistic actualization \[passage a l’acte artistique|, where the artist appears as the great transindividuating transductor. But Marcuse is unable to see that, as conjunctive and disjunctive, this ‘and’ is precisely the question of the passage into action, of acting out, insofar as it is both, on the one hand, transgressive and elevating, and, on the other hand, that which connects pleasure to reality, and does so beyond what opposes them ~and by passing through the death drive.\*\* Now, these are the issues involved in the Freudian fable of the murder of the father, which ties together these two, seemingly utterly opposed, dimensions of the passage to the act. It composes these two dimensions as a process, and turns this knot into a weapon, given that murder is involved: in this case, the murder knife, which opens the age of the super-ego by imposing the authority of the father in the form of his ghost. And I argue that this opening is equally that of the passage of desire itself — the thrust of a knife.

Yet is this ‘sense of guilt’ not just the fixation, or the idée fixe, of a Judaeo-Christian reading of the question of murder, in relation to which the fundamental question would be less the gesture of acting out, of the passage into action, than the condition of its possibility, which is technics as necessity (ananke)? 

In a note preceding what will turn out to be, then, a genesis of guilt, Freud refers to J. J. Atkinson to argue the hypothesis that after the murder of the father, war breaks out between the sons. And what is striking here is the homology of this structure with the myth of Epimetheus, where, after the theft of fire (as divine attribute, that is, attribute of the father) by Prometheus, mortals find themselves at war with one another, forcing Zeus to equip them, via Hermes, with dike and aidos. But in the Freudian myth, through which he believes he will be able to discover the foundations of the Oedipus complex, there is an obvious aporia of origin that turns into a hermeneutic, but very vicious, circle. What the murder of the father is supposed to do is provide the horde with an exit from its bestiality. This is shown by Freud’s reference here to the behaviour of herds ‘of wild oxen and horses’ observed in New Caledonia by Atkinson, who found that ‘the conditions which . . . prevail in the primal horde . . . regularly lead to the killing of the father’,?° the young bulls or stallions being in league against the dominant male, as frequently occurs in animal hordes (which suggests this is a very strange use of the words ‘murder’ and ‘father’, since what is being referred to here are the struggles between males in general). But even though the murder of the father is supposed to provide such an exit from bestiality, it nonetheless remains the case that these sons already love, and hate, fear and admire, the father, who is therefore already a father. Hence the murder could not be what founds this love and this hate, constituting the father as a father, that is, as a figure of the father. It is a matter here, not of what founds the father, but of the foundation of guilt. 

*[p. 61]* ^pg69

In other words, the passage of desire is here defined by guilt, and the latter is the result of a weapon, which is also to say, of a use of technics. I have argued elsewhere that the whole problem lies in the presumption that technics existed prior to the murder:” Freud wonders how the sons found the courage to take action, and suggests it may have been ‘command over some new weapon’.?® He does not see that to presume that the weapon existed before the murder is a serious problem. It is precisely this repression of technics, however, that lies at the origin of this obsession with guilt, and this is what neither Marcuse nor Freud manage to think: the sons must precede the constitution of the sons insofar as the sons are constituted as sons by killing the father. And the father is himself only constituted as the father after the event, and insofar as he returns as a ghost. This implies that he was already there as father before his constitution as father — unless, indeed, his image is only formed on the condition of producing, through his murder, the illusion or the simulacrum of his existence before the murder. Such an illusion, however, would involve the same false problem as that of knowing what comes first in the hylemorphic scheme: form or matter? The chicken or the egg? 

Simondon’s answer is clear: neither one nor the other, neither form nor matter, neither father nor son, neither structural principle (formal or material, depending on whether one is an idealist or a materialist) nor historical principle. It is not a matter of thinking that this or that tendency or principle precedes some other. Rather, it is a matter of thinking the differentiation of a potential in two tendencies in the course of a process through which a preindividual milieu, supersaturated with potential, individuates itself. And, in this case, this is a potential supersaturated by the fact that life, that is, vital individuation, projects itself through its technical exteriorization. ‘This is why Marcuse goes astray in wanting to identify a structural reality principle before the historical reality principle (which in any case he never actually manages to do: we are unable to discover, by reading Eros and Civilization, what this structural reality principle could possibly be). Marcuse fails to grasp that technics, far from being the principle of a fall and a very long downward spiral \[débandade\], is the process through which desire is constituted as default of origin, that is, the process constitutively causing the (de) fault |faire-défaut\] of the 

*[p. 62]* ^pg70

desired object, and is not the fault through which desire would be diverted from its nature. 

The Greek tradition, as I have recalled in relying on the works of Jean-Pierre Vernant, is tragic to the extent that it is constituted through the question of the struggle between Prometheus and Zeus. But the Greek tradition thinks not in terms of fault or guilt but rather in terms of tragic fate, that is, in terms of the struggle between tendencies that can be understood only in terms of their play, without being able to choose between them. And this is so to the extent that the Greek tradition posits technicity — as originary facticity, that is, as default of origin — as constituting the lot of mortals. This is why death, desire (as sexual difference, embodied in the figure of Pandora, wife of Epimetheus) and technics constitute the fabric onto which the motives of aidos and dike come to be projected. Aidos and dike do not themselves exist, but bring about the collective movements that form the law and manners as social organizations of the relations between tendencies and principles, between life and death, pleasure and reality. Furthermore, aidos and dike presuppose an appropriation of the supports of production, through which they are translated, socially, into the forces and relations of production. 

Revenance is what already haunts me, me who did not arrive until after this murder, very long after this dreadfully old murder that is, according to the fable told in Totem and Taboo, the very origin of desire: all mortals, that is, all those who desire, are its heirs, and as such they inherit a feeling of guilt. And yet, I suggest, in truth this guilt may be only an historical but recent version of a much more deeply woven feeling, a revenance resulting from the projection of the possibility of causing death constituted by this weapon that is all technics, all horizon of technicity, that is, facticity. And this already haunts me, and firstly as the instant of my death, that I hallucinate from a young age, as the very experience of time. I project fantasies reminiscent of this murder that is, in reality, the default of origin in general, but as what opens the door for eris, which is also to say, what necessitates prohibition and the law. And this murder is also, again, the fantasy par excellence of what does not exist insofar as it is above all a matter of the relation to my death, through which alone I can conceive, fear or want the death of the other: for the instant of my death is the only event 

*[p. 63]* ^pg71

that counts, the only one that truly happens, but also the only event that never truly happens, since at the moment it arrives, I disappear. 

The instant of my death is therefore, in some way, what, ‘always pending’ \[toujours en instance\],” and not just in potential, par excellence consists. It is what both does not exist and never will exist, and yet it insists, and resists every event because it exceeds, precedes and succeeds every event: it is by and in pending that everything consists. Such an instant is consistent more than it is non-existent, like the ghost of the father, and it is as such essentially indeterminate and incalculable. It is a simulacrum, the simulacrum par excellence, and is so as projection. And this simulacrum | 

is the escapement of the time of consistence — in the horological sense where the escapement, in the anchor or the wheel, is what constitutes a clock’s system of beats, its basic rhythm, the principle of its movement. 

The instant of my death is incalculable and, as such, the ‘escapement’ of ‘différance’, which is also to say, as we shall see, of consistence, and it is the dynamic of individuation as the individuation of singularity — a question that, as we shall also see, is more complex than that of ‘authenticity’. Techniques of calculation enable the past to be determined, that is, ‘durably fixed’? Such techniques, in other words, retentionalize the past by tertiarizing it. And yet, these techniques may also intensify the indetermination of the future. It is in this way that primary retentions, filtered by secondary retentions, presuppose tertiary retentions, that is, epiphylogenesis as support of the preindividual. Now, it is in the light of such a composition of calculation and the incalculable that we must weigh the ‘structural’ relations, which are in fact always already ‘historical’, that operate in the composition of the pleasure principle with the reality principle. 

8 Diversions and decompositions 

As for the history of relations in which this composition consists, this is the history, according to Marcuse, of ‘the transformation of the pleasure principle into the reality principle. ... The reality principle supersedes the pleasure principle.’\*! This way of presenting historical evolution tends to lead, in a way that is not made 

*[p. 64]* ^pg72

explicit, to the ‘structural’ presupposition that the pleasure principle would lie at the origin of the process, or is the origin itself, while the reality principle would lie at the end of the process, or is the end itself, in the double sense of purpose and termination. This would mean that the reality principle would be the beginning of the fall outside the origin, salvation from which would arise from a revolutionary leap, an eschatological redemption. And it would mean, furthermore, that politics would be the restoration of the reign of pleasure through the liberation of the instincts. 

Hence Marcuse describes a chiasmus within which pleasure turns or degrades into reality. Extrapolating, this in turn implies that the one, pleasure, could occur without the other, reality, and reciprocally. This is clearly contradicted by the fact that it is the relation between them that grounds these principles — but to fully understand this it would be necessary to move beyond a thought that thinks in terms of principle. There is a pleasure principle only to the extent that it is contradicted or dictated \[(contre)dit\] by the reality principle. So, when Marcuse quotes Freud saying that society ‘must see to it that the number of these members is restricted and their energies directed away from sexual activities on to their work’,® he presumes the existence of libido, posited here as quasi-synonymous with sexual activity, before its diversion to work. Whereas in fact it is precisely this diversion — which is also, as I have explained, an adoption (and the possibility of all perversions) — that means the ‘animal drives’ become, not what Marcuse calls human instinct, but rather desire, of which libido is the energy. It is not merely that civilization is the diversion of libido: it is libido that is its ‘own’ \[propre\] diversion, that is, its im-propriety — its elementary supplementarity. 

The error here lies in the notion of succession, including in Freud himself, and the roots of the resulting confusions can be found in Totem and Taboo. This is why, when Marcuse reproaches Freud for considering the pleasure and reality principles to be ‘eternally’ antagonistic, he himself nevertheless fails to see that it is a matter of tendencies. He does not see, that is, that a composition of tendencies is involved, such that it is the impossibility of the elimination of one by the other that constitutes their play. 10 The opposition of Narcissus and Prometheus Marcuse may well understand that what is at stake is the corresponding destruction of primordial narcissism,” leading him to a re-evaluation of the figure of Narcissus. Unfortunately, however, he fails to identify the causes of this destruction, because he lacks a correct analysis of the process of the control of conscious time, that is, the control of the arrangements of primary, secondary and tertiary retention. Marcuse’s re-evaluation of Narcissus is carried out in the first place against the figure of Prometheus. Narcissism is therefore not conceived as the capacity for the psychic individual to project protentions via those mirrors that are the tertiary retentions in which artifices consist. It is for this reason that Marcuse here introduces Baudelaire (whose point is, however, more about civilization as the ‘reduction of the traces of original sin’ than about the development of modern technics, that is, disenchantment, other than as that which enables this reduction, and as a reaction to which there arises the spiritual devotion to ‘turn-tables’\*\*), and that he states:

Narcissism, as the diversion of energy in the context of decomposition, may indeed lead to regression, but what this misses is that narcissism is also the condition of belief ‘in this world’, as otium. This logic of opposition between Prometheus, on the one hand, and Orpheus and Narcissus, on the other, is consistent with the approach that opposes tendencies (and principles) that actually compose. Marcuse, however, grounds his thought of a new form of sublimation on: 

the notion \[appearing late, with the appearance of narcissism in Freud’s second topology\] of an undifferentiated, unified libido prior to the division into ego and external objects. . . . Primary narcissism ... engulfs the ‘environment’, integrating the narcissistic ego with the objective world.\*! 

And here he quotes from the opening chapter of Civilization and Its Discontents: 

Originally, the ego includes everything, later it detaches from itself the external world. This is why, contrary to the current point of view, ‘narcissism, usually understood as egotistic withdrawal from reality, here is connected with oneness with the universe’.®? And it is on this inversion of the meaning of narcissism that Marcuse grounds his thought of a new age of sublimation that would no longer be a diversion of libidinal energy, nor a repressive system: 

all sublimation would begin with the reactivation of narcissistic libido, which somehow overflows and extends to objects \[and constitutes\] a non-repressive mode of sublimation which results from an extension rather than from a constraining deflection of the libido.

It is at this point that his reasoning goes astray. For if the goal is to constitute a new form of sublimation, which is also to say, another relation between pleasure and reality, and thus between the psychic and the collective, and also between the diachronic and the synchronic, the way to do so would certainly not be to deny their difference and their tension. It is this difference and tension that is the very mechanism of individuation as escapement and eris, as a process of emulation or competition. Constituting a new form of sublimation, on the contrary, requires rearticulating narcissism on the basis of the current supports of production, and doing so in the hyperindustrial stage of hypomnemata, without which there can be no sublimation — a new form of sublimation that will take various forms of otium and negotium, that is, of the social organization of the life of the spirit. 

It is in the context of the drive-based becoming of control technologies that these questions can and must be posed. This becoming occurs on the terrain described in The Ego and the Id, that is, as the inherent tendency of sublimation to generate an instinct to destroy, especially when sublimation reaches the stage of the automatization of the super-ego. But if this is so, it is due to the systematic exploitation, by industrial populism, of the regressive tendency of the intermittency of being. But the historical realization of these tendencies is organological, that is, the result of a stage of grammatization in which the synchronic and the diachronic tend to technically (grammatically) decompose. It is therefore a question of singularity more than of ‘pure Eros’ (or, a fortiori, of ‘authenticity’). For singularity must be thought, at the level of the process of psychic, collective and technical individuation, as idiomatic constitution, that is, precisely, as an individuation that is immediately psycho-social and, as shown by Derrida, grammato-logical, or in my own terms, techno-logical.

Libidinal ecology 

One aspect of the discussion with Freud opened up by Marcuse focuses on the relations between work and sublimation. I will 

*[p. 78]* ^pg86

reserve a detailed commentary on this for the fourth volume of Disbelief and Discredit, which will be devoted to the question of the otium of the people and to the relations between otium and negotium. But while these may be names, too, for pleasure and reality, it is also the case that: (1) otium and negotium are themselves already composed from the drives; and (2) their conflation, invoked by The New Spirit of Capitalism,” is on the contrary precisely what, as the culture industry and the submission of knowledge to calculation, generates the decomposition of the drives. And this means, as well, the decomposition of libido, this being the plague specific to uncontrollable societies. 

I will try to show in The Aristocracy to Come, and at the end of this volume, that Marcuse may be correct to pose the question of the invention of a new form of sublimation and of another relation between pleasure and reality, but this can be thought only as the question of a new form of political economy proper to the industrial age of the spirit. And we shall see that, in addition to the fact that Laplanche and Pontalis note the absence of a complete theory of libido in psychoanalysis, Freud himself drew attention to the absence of any in-depth psychoanalytic account of work. 

All questions with respect to work are overdetermined by questions concerning nature — work being conceived as the trans-formation of nature. The state of nature does mot exist, but is nevertheless necessary to Marcuse’s reasoning, albeit always in an occluded way, just as occurs in the fable of the murder of the father and the paradox of the weapon in Totem and Taboo. But in this state of nature we again find Rousseau’s problem, which I examined in Technics and Time, 1 by commenting on this magnificent passage in A Discourse on the Origin of Inequality: 

It has not even entered into the heads of most of our writers to doubt whether the state of nature ever existed; but it is clear from the Holy Scriptures that the first man, having received his understanding and commandments immediately from God, was not himself in such a state, and that, if we give such credit to the writings of Moses as every Christian philosopher ought to give, we must deny that, even before the deluge, men were ever in the pure state of nature; unless, indeed, they fell back into it from some very

extraordinary circumstance; a paradox which it would be very embarrassing to defend, and quite impossible to prove.” 

I would like here to recall my earlier commentary on this passage, a passage that to me seems to illuminate the aporias and paradoxes of Freud and Marcuse: 

Hence the pure nature of man is paradoxical and improbable, doomed to fiction and to artifice. Rousseau’s aim, the absolutely equal origin of man, is prior to the first real man. ‘Let us begin then by laying all facts aside, as they do not affect the question’ and could never teach us anything. We are not in the historical. But we are not for all that in simple fiction either, for there is evidence dictating the account of such a fiction. Evidence dating from before all facts - transcendental evidence. We were admiring the extreme prudence of Rousseau: now must we admire his extreme audacity. Pure nature, man in his essence come before creation. The fall outside of pure nature is creation. This is certainly an audacity that remains faithful to the spirit of religion, of original sin and of babelization: it is through an accident provoked by divine will, by Providence, that the process of the fall and the precipitation into disastrous inequality can be explained in detail, as, in the Essay on the Origin of Language, God’s finger, tipping the axis of the planet, introduces seasonal variations and along with them idiomatic differences. But there remains the task of thinking what precedes creation.”

The conclusion reached by Rousseau in his search for the essence of man is that the man of pure nature, of the pure origin, purely wild, ‘perhaps never did exist, and probably never will exist’.\*’ I often refer to this to show that the status of non-existence must be thought — so as to become a consistence, and in this case, the political consistence of a social ‘contract’, that is, of an individuation — on the basis of the fiction and artificiality of technics. This being the case, Rousseau is less than clear in stating that this is not a matter of rediscovering some man who truly exists, but rather of reconstituting the conditions in which what does not exist consists in giving the very movement of individuation. Which would be a simulacrum — hardly more than a fagade. 

At the same time, we should recognize that the question posed by Marcuse — but which he does not pose in the right way, namely, as an organological question — is that of the status of what does not exist: pure pleasure, beyond all reality. But as this non-existence is not proclaimed and examined as such, it functions as a lure and a denial, and as a mystical and naive repetition of theological and messianic schemes. It should instead be raised to its actual rank, which is that of the question of consistence. This is less origin than motive, projecting in mirrored form, and as vanishing point, a default of origin, as an end projected from the irrepressible fantasy of this origin, but the tragic experience of which is the default of origin, that is, technics as artifice and fiction, and, as such, as the necessary default. 

It is nevertheless important to read and re-read Marcuse, because the question of consistence unavoidably and immediately involves the question, not only of sublimation, but of the super-ego in the epoch of the automatization of the super-ego. And this situation then becomes toxic, just as the products of disassimilation become poisonous for protists if the liquid in which they are cultured is not kept fresh. This toxicity is what we live through in contemporary industrial society, and especially in France, in a time of spiritual misery maintained and exploited by industrial populism and political populisms. The question of the super-ego expressed in this way, however, is not and never will be resolved by simply re-elaborating questions of work and sublimation. For the question of the super-ego is our political question, the question of our age. The evolution of capitalism — in particular as it has 

*[p. 81]* ^pg89

become financialized and totally deterritorialized — has striven to see that this question receives no critique (contrary to what ‘artistic critique’ believed), and instead becomes the subject of fantasy: May 1968 will have been an extraordinary fantasy and, as such, a seismic event, no doubt still barely thought, however much it has been fantasized about. As for critique, the effort remains to be made — and in the form of a new critique in an age where the absence of critique, that is, of judgment (for which denunciations are substituted, and in all camps, and on all registers), is no longer sustainable. 

Conceiving a new industrial model means conceiving a new process of individuation that will reconstitute its conjunctive and junctive properties by re-elaborating the question of the social tie on the basis of a redefinition of the play of the drives in contemporary technological space. In this, Prometheus and Narcissus, far from being opposed, co-individuate in a libidinal economy conceived on the basis of a libidinal ecology that reconfigures the contours of savoir-vivre, that is, of manners and law as social concretizations and existences, in default of justice and ethics as consistences, feelings for what does not exist. Through this, the process of super-egoization — always already split (into justice and shame, Eros and Thanatos) — is formed into the tragic necessity of a new critique, if not as its categorical imperative. 

What Boltanski and Chiapello have failed to grasp, as have the proponents of ‘artistic critique’, is that the super-ego is the law of the simulacrum. They have not understood that the super-ego is the simulacrum par excellence, that which leads to automatism and toxicity as much as to invention, and that there is an economy and a politics of simulacra, for example, an American industrial strategy of ‘soft power’. This country, America, invented a new process of adoption, and of individuation, and of diversions of libido and of various perversions, a sociality and a way of life entirely reconfigured by the industries of calculation and the imagination, by cultural and cognitive technologies. But such a politics of simulacra has today become highly toxic for America itself. 

The American industrial model, which was the true mode of production of the super-ego in the age of disenchantment, is becoming obsolete and dangerous, even though it is being exported throughout the entire world. The current challenge is to invent
