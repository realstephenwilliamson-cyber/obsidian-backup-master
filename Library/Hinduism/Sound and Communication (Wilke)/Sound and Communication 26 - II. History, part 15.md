We find it particularly interesting that the Nāda-Brahman - which already in Śarṁgadeva contained a latent transreligiosity, because it absorbed many Hindu traditions — opened up a field that attracted more forms of transreligiosity, inspired them, adopted them, and was overlapped by them, or entered into a more or less (non-)transparent continuum with them. Here one should understand transreligiosity as a conscious fusion and transcending of traditional religious boundaries. This religious style has ancient roots in India and was a good strategy for perceiving a deeper unity in the real religious diversity (such as seeing all the Vedic gods than “only the One,” as it says in the Rgveda, or the many goddesses as manifestations of the one Śakti, etc.). The Nāda-Brahman reinforced and intensified this tendency, as already became clear in Tyāgarāja’s discussion (17th cent.). Yet Tyāgarāja’s intensified Smārta trans-culture and his ecumenical Nāmasiddhānta were neither the first nor the last example of this tendency. They appear absolutely harmless and traditionalistic when compared with the transreligiosity of the Northern Indian Sants (15th cent.) and their marked Nirguṇa-Bhakti for the “Nameless One” that included Islam and at the same time went beyond it. The (Muslim) weaver Kabīr was the most prominent representative of the Sant movement, which did not accept traditional religious boundaries, and when Kabīr died both Hindus and Muslims claimed him as źheiíir saint. The transition from the Sants to (what was later called) Sikhism is seamless and fluid. The Sikh practices (from the 15th cent.) fused Islam and Hinduism, gave song and contemplation of the greatness and sweetness of the divine name top priority in religious practice and centered on the transcendency, the “featureless” and “nameless” God. Similar to the Hindu bhakti parallels, the fifth Sikh guru Arjan (1581-1606), who compiled the Guru-Granth (Adi Granth), taught that this practice led to joy, heavenly bliss, inner peace and deliverance, because God was immediately present in the sung name. There is an equivalence between the Nāda-Brahman idea that the whole world is filled with the “cosmic, primordial sound,” and monodic, extremely microtone-laden Indian modal music. In particular when over-tonality

is stressed, as in the above example, a dense, constant sound presence arises that translates the Nāda-Brahman so to speak into sensory perception and arouses a “feeling” of unity in plurality. 

Berendt was therefore not all that far off the right track when he claimed that for the Nāda-Brahman, the cosmic primordial sound, there were comprehensible acoustic forms that “immediately” evoke the idea of a cosmic unity in the sound. According to Berendt, this had something to do with the holistic nature of sound itself. His key examples were overtone singing, the chanting of OM, and the oscillation of a 81111182 .[^141] The vibrating string is a truly good illustration of the “unique nature” of sound and central complexes of ideas that are associated with the Nāda-Brahman. When a string is plucked or struck, first of all a vibration arises in the whole string. However, this vibration immediately causes a second, somewhat quieter, oscillation, that of the two halves of the string. Then the three thirds of the string start to vibrate, followed by four quarters, and so on ad infinitum. But succeeding partial vibrations are always quieter than their predecessors and at some stage they become completely inaudible. Even though we think we are hearing one single tone, acoustic theory tells us that we are really also hearing many different tones, as each “individual tone” contains within itself all the other “individual tones” as a weak additional sound scape. Therefore, in such cases acoustics experts speak of “harmonic sound” and not of “tone.” The epithet “harmonic” refers to the whole harmonic spectrum of co-vibrating individual tones. Genuinely “single” sounds, that is, tones without such a harmonic spectrum, are certainly mathematically conceivable, but can never be fully realized by physical means. Instead, every vibrating body produces a harmonic spectrum through its vibration. The number of overtones and their proportional relations are directly responsible for the timbre of the sound. In general it is true that the sound formed is perceived as harsher and more penetrating, the more audible overtones it has in the higher frequencies. 

Interestingly, instruments that have an especially rich overtone spectrum, e.g. shawms, seem to have disappeared in Europe when modality went out of fashion at the end of the Middle Ages. With their high proportion of overtones these instruments present the ear with the unicentric structure of the overtone scale as a definite modal framework. In the overtone scale, structured as 1:2:3:4 ..., ultimately every tone is defined in relation to the fundamental. This means that even one single tone from a shawm makes a hundred other frequencies sound at the same time, all of which are pre-defined by the fundamental. However, since the Renaissance the aim has been polycentric organization

Diametrically opposed to this is the typical classical Indian instrumental ensemble consisting of a soloist who usually plays a instrument with a strong overtone spectrum, a drummer providing the rhythmic framework, and a third musician playing a bourdun instrument (harmonium or Tāmpūra). Instead of de-emphasizing the fundamental, as in Western music, in Indian music it actually became emphasized. Now, Western music also had similar bourdun instruments such as the bagpipes and the hurdy-gurdy. Church organs in particular had an important bourdun function (which explains the use of the German word “Or-gelpunkt” for “bourdun” or “drone”). Yet bagpipes, hurdy-gurdies and organs are primarily used for playing melodies. This is not how the Indian Tāmpūra is used, though, and musicians playing the harmonium (imported from the West) try to eliminate this possibility as far as possible. Therefore, of the bourdun instruments, the Indian Tāmpūra, a four-stringed lute, is especially remarkable because it is the only instrument that was designed for playing the bourdun and cannot be used for playing melodies as it has no frets. The four metal strings are tuned to the fundamental, the fifth and the octave of the Rāga and are plucked continuously as open strings. The function is to set up the fundamental as the bourdun, a permanent background of sound against which all the intervals should be heard. The key factor for the listeners, however, in the case of the Tāmpūra is that this constant monotonous reiteration of the fundamental does not sound static. A special curved bridge ensures that the overtones fluctuate constantly, and so the resulting sound from the four strings of a well made Tāmpūra no longer sounds like that from a plucked instrument. Instead a steady but internally moving sound is generated (similar to that of a hurdy-gurdy). Despite its superficially static nature, in a sense the overtone-rich sound of the Tāmpūra also oscillates, shimmers and is very much alive. 

The Tāmpūra is a “modern” stringed instrument and was probably developed only in the 16th/17th century to accompany classical song in the Khyāl style that was in vogue at that time, possibly at the court of

The harmonium was introduced even later, imported from the West by missionaries, and it was rapidly adopted in India as a new invention of the 19th century, being adapted to the Indians’ own needs. They provided it with buttons for the bourdun and built instruments to be played with only one hand, which means that a singer can accompany him or herself with scintillating bourdun sounds. Yet the Tāmpūra remained the definitive instrument for concert performances, reinforcing and perfecting an existing musical ideal. The ideal of the wavering drone can be found as early as in the 13th century, in Śārṁgadeva. The Tāmpūra’s predecessors included the Rudra-Vīnā and the one-stringed Ektar, an instrument that is still popular in ascetic circles even today and also has a rhythmic function that the Tāmpūra no longer fulfills. The perfection of the Tāmpūra lies in the fact that it was obviously designed especially so that the individual strings not only sound at the frequency to which they are tuned, but at the same time primary partial tones of this basic frequency become audible as individual tones. The fact that individual tones in music are actually an acoustic illusion is exploited, for every tone physically consists of a (potentially) infinite series of individual vibrations. One could go as far as to say that every tone contains every other tone. Good Tāmpūra instruments can generate within one individual tone a complex spectrum of many tones, in which even unmusical listeners can hear five to six tones quite clearly, although only a single string is being played. In this context, self-less-ness is a conscious, intentional attitude. In their total devotion to music Indian musicians are probably not so very different from many classical European musicians. The key fundamental difference remains that between the Western harmonics and polyphony, and the Indian microtonal modal music, whose sensory sound impression already triggers different qualities of perception and feeling, i.e. different sensory knowledge is produced, similar to the ways that composition technique here and improvisaton technology there place quite different requirements on composers and musicians.

They reflect a “struggle between the value spheres” (Weber) within one’s own, Western culture. On one side of the divide is the sociologist who finds Weber confirmed and styles himself a typical European scientist with a pen in his hand, keeping his distance, and taking a dialectic approach to the encounter with India; on the other side is the modern seeker who wants to break away from Western rationalism and find something in India that is missing at home.
