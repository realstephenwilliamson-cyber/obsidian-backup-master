## About

A short dialogue by Plato on courage. Two fathers ask the generals Nicias and Laches whether their sons should learn to fight in armour; Socrates turns the question to what courage is, and the talk ends without an answer.

- **People:** Lysimachus, son of the statesman Aristides, and Melesias, son of Thucydides (not the historian); their sons, named after their grandfathers; the generals Nicias and Laches; and Socrates.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** after a display of fighting in heavy armour by Stesilaus. Jowett places the scene between the battle of Delium (424 BC), where Laches had seen Socrates' courage, and Mantinea (418 BC), where Laches died. Britannica sets the fictional date before Nicias' defeat in Sicily.
- **Composition and authenticity:** treated as genuine. The IEP lists it among the early dialogues, written after 399 BC and before Plato's first trip to Sicily in 387; Copleston puts it in the Socratic period and sums it up: "On courage. No result."
- **Argument:** Laches proposes that courage is a kind of perseverance of the soul; Socrates replies that prudent withdrawal is sometimes wise. Nicias proposes knowledge of what is to be feared and hoped for; Socrates argues that this would be knowledge of all virtue, not of one part of it. The dialogue ends in aporia.
- **Place among the dialogues:** in Thrasyllus's fifth tetralogy as *Laches, or On Courage*, classed as obstetric (midwifely), with *Theages*, *Charmides* and *Lysis* (Diogenes Laertius). Stephanus pages 178a–201c. Jowett compares it with the *Charmides* and *Lysis* and ties its account of knowledge and virtue to the *Protagoras*.
- **Why it matters:** scholars read its ending differently: Iain Lane takes the Socratic elenchus as an end in itself, while Gregory Vlastos traces the failure to flaws in the particular definitions (Wikipedia). Wikipedia adds that the later deaths of both generals in the war give the dialogue added weight.
