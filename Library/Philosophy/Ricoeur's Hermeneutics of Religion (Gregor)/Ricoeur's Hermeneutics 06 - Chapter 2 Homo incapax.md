## Chapter 2

## Homo incapax

*Radical Evil and the Bound Will*

Ricoeur’s project in the Philosophy of the Will led him to confront the problem of the evil will, that is, the bondage of the will by sin. Ricoeur frames this evil with the concept of “fault,” a term he borrows from Nabert to identify a fundamental incapacity at the heart of the human being. This is not the incapacity that is essential to being a finite, fragile, fallible, and vulnerable human. Instead, it is a corruption, an incurvature, a bondage that the will inflicts upon itself. 

In this chapter we will see that the enigma of fault requires a fundamental reorientation of philosophical reflection. When consciousness turns back on itself and reflects upon fault, it finds that it has posited evil through its own act. But it also finds a deep incomprehensibility, since fault is inexplicable, unintelligible, a fundamental irrationality at the heart of the human being. Reflection cannot grasp fault directly through concepts; instead, it must proceed indirectly, taking a detour through the manifold symbols and myths that figure the meaning of sin and evil. This is one of the main insights that prompts Ricoeur’s hermeneutical turn.

Ricoeur’s detour through the symbolism of evil is just that—a detour. The long path leads back toward philosophical reflection, and so this chapter will also examine Ricoeur’s own approach to thinking fault philosophically. For reflection, one of the most perplexing aspects of fault is its self-inflicted bondage. The will binds itself. Freedom makes itself unfree. The *I can* turns itself into the *I cannot*. This is a deep enigma that reflection cannot fathom. The goal of reflection is to understand the self as the origin of its acts, but the act that posits evil is not itself the radical origin of evil. Instead, the self finds itself participating ontologically in a reality that is prior to its own act of sin. The challenge, then, is to understand fault in terms of both act and being. 

*[p. 36]* ^pg53

Ricoeur’s attempt to do so leads him to a mediation of Augustine’s concept of original sin with Kant’s concept of radical evil.

This mediation is not Ricoeur’s final word on the topic, however. Ricoeur will also expand his hermeneutics of *homo incapax* to incorporate the themes of suffering and vulnerability. Religion aims not only to regenerate the capacities that are bound by evil, but also to address the self who suffers and to increase capability in those who are vulnerable. Religion is also addressed to the suffering, vulnerable self.

### Fault and Fallibility

If the phenomenology of the body exposes the limits of traditional reflexive philosophy, in *Freedom and Nature* Ricoeur also delimits the boundaries of eidetic phenomenology. His project there is an eidetic phenomenology, insofar as his analysis seeks to identify the essential structures of willing. But as eidetic, this phenomenology needs to bracket two important but contingent (rather than essential) factors that influence the will: fault and transcendence.

*Fault* refers to the corruption of the self in sin or evil, which binds the will and plunges freedom into bondage (FN 21–28). Ricoeur borrows the term from Nabert, who takes the experience of fault as his starting point in *Elements for an Ethic.* For Nabert, reflection encounters fault as a past act of transgression. Consciousness identifies that act as something it caused, and from which it cannot dissociate itself. Through its self-judgment, consciousness makes a prisoner of itself: “There appears a causality which has affected itself, which does not know how to deliver itself from itself” (EE 4–5). The experience of fault is not, however, limited to remorse over one particular act, since it extends and applies to the entire being of the self (EE 6). As reflection pushes further, it discovers that it is impossible to identify the absolute beginning of fault. The self may have initiated this act, but prior to this act was a “secret complicity of a much more remote past which opposes an absolute limit to any ambition one might have of understanding it or of recapturing its genesis” (EE 11). The experience of fault is therefore not the mundane moral experience of a guilty conscience, but a “fundamental metaphysical experience”—a kind of negative participation, a “participating in a structure of being which cannot be reduced to the operations of individual consciousness.” More precisely, it is a participation in *non*being.

Ricoeur takes up Nabert’s treatment of fault as a participation in nonbeing, but he is careful to distinguish his account of nonbeing from then-current discussions of nothingness and negation in existential philosophy. Ricoeur contends that there are two different sorts of negation that must be kept separate. First, there is the negation that characterizes finitude: “the lack of need, 

*[p. 37]* ^pg54

the gaping hole of possibility opened by a project, the refusal inaugurating all voluntary affirmation, . . . the impotence indicated by death and even birth itself” (FN 23). All of these aspects of finitude are characterized by a certain negativity, a *not-*. This negation is, however, fundamentally different from the Nothing of *fault*, or what Ricoeur calls *vanity*—the futile chasing after the wind described in the book of Ecclesiastes. This Nothing is, paradoxically, a destructive force, an active wound, a power by which the self binds itself (FN 23–24). By failing to distinguish the negativity of finitude from the Nothing of fault, existential philosophy thereby “contaminates fundamental ontology with a sort of diffuse absurdity” (FN 25n.11). In short, where finitude is an essential feature of human being and willing, fault is a contingent event that should not be. For Ricoeur, fault is the delusion of being a self-positing, radically autonomous “Self,” separated from others and estranged from being (FN 29). The condition of fault is one of bondage, and Ricoeur describes this with the Old Testament symbolism of Israel’s captivity in Egypt.

This image of bound subjectivity aptly describes the self-positing I of reflexive philosophy, but Ricoeur argues that all philosophies that revolve around subjectivity, even existential variants like that of Jaspers, are captive to this vanity: 

The culpability of philosophy takes refuge in the concern for *self* (*Selbst*) which animates all of existentialist philosophy. A strange shriveling-up, a frenzied preoccupation with the self, besets every such philosophy. It is hidden in the proudest pronouncements on choice, which is choice of myself . . . . Is not this bitter vindication of autonomy a kind of agitation within the enchanted circle of some sort of captivity? .  .  . Perhaps in the last analysis, all illumination of *Existenz* remains burdened by the “vanity” of a freedom which is less enamored of Being than of its own power to choose and its own glory. (RJPR 635–36)

Despite its differences from the reflexive tradition, the philosophy of existence also tends to offer a model of subjectivity curved in on itself.<sup>1</sup>

Just as Ricoeur brackets fault in his eidetics of the will, so he brackets *Transcendence.* Transcendence opens up questions of the true origin of subjectivity and the liberation of freedom from fault (FN 3, 29). It is the presence of a grace that precedes one’s own power and regenerates freedom in the death and rebirth of the self (FN 30–33). But given the eidetic nature of *Freedom and Nature*, Ricoeur temporarily keeps this discussion in brackets, as he does with fault. Thus we too will keep our discussion of Transcendence if not in brackets, then at least on hold until chapter 3. 

The second part of Ricoeur’s trilogy is a two-volume work entitled *Finitude and Guilt*. In the first volume, *Fallible Man*, Ricoeur offers a description of “the constitutional weakness that makes evil possible” (FM xliii). Note that 

*[p. 38]* ^pg55

he is only talking about the possibility, not the contingent actuality, of evil. This possibility is neither remote nor abstract, but is “inscribed in the innermost structure of human reality” (FM 1). Ricoeur identifies this constitutional weakness in an ontology of disproportion, by which he means our paradoxical non-coinciding with ourselves. What does it mean to say we do not coincide with ourselves? On one level this refers to the gap or hiatus between me and myself, or the *I* as subject and *me* as object. I, like you, am a self-relating being, in whom consciousness can become self-consciousness. The primary goal of reflexive philosophy is to close this gap by knowing myself. 

There is, however, another level of disproportion: We are constituted such that we can be both greater than and less than ourselves. Plato identifies something like this paradox in Book IV of the *Republic,* where Socrates asks how it is possible to be stronger than oneself (430e). That is Socrates’ definition of moderation (*sophrosyne*), in which the better part of the soul (prudence) has mastery over the worse (appetites and irrational desire) (431a). The philosophical tradition has often taken this kind of discrimination between the higher/lower, better/worse to require a faculty psychology in which reason, the senses, imagination, passions, and will all have their proper rank and relation. We find this in the classical view that reason should rule over the passions, as well as in the Humean inversion that would have reason be the slave of the passions. We see a similar hierarchy in Descartes’ fourth *Meditation* and its account of the possibility of error (or sin) in the disproportion between the finite intellect and the infinite will, and the will’s propensity to outstrip the intellect and make judgments on an epistemically inadequate basis. Ricoeur agrees with Descartes that we are subject to error because of a kind of disproportion of the finite and infinite, but for Ricoeur these are not linked with specific faculties or functions (FM 2). Instead, the disproportion plays out in all parts of the self.<sup>2</sup>

Ricoeur also agrees with much of the philosophical tradition that the human is an intermediate being. This is not, contra the tradition, because the human is an intermediary between angel and animal, or god and beast, or being and nothingness, but rather because “he is intermediate within himself, within his *selves*” (FM 3). We are intermediary because we are mediating beings; we “bring about mediations” within and outside of ourselves. It is within these mediations, in our ontological disproportion, that Ricoeur locates the possibility of fault. With this point, Ricoeur removes the brackets applied in *Freedom and Nature*, moving from an *eidetics* to an *empirics* of the will—in particular, the finite will characterized by fragility and susceptibility to evil.

Ricoeur undertakes an *empirics* of the will because fault is a historically contingent event—an actuality distinct from the structural *possibility* of committing evil. For this reason, *Fallible Man* remains a work of “pure reflection,” a “direct exercise of rationality,” and as such, it excludes our concrete, 

*[p. 39]* ^pg56

everyday reality. This condition of being enslaved by the passions is one such reality, and so it is inaccessible to pure reflection. Evil is a “sealed book” that reflection cannot open (SE 347). The only way to understand something about evil in its concrete actuality is to listen to the history of believers and the language in which they confess fault. And as Nabert recognized, the experience of fault extends back behind any particular act to a prior nonbeing, as a negative participation: “The riddle of the slave-will, that is, of a *free will that is bound and always finds itself already bound*, is the ultimate theme that the symbol gives to thought” (FM xlv). A large part of the difficulty with thinking the servile will is that it involves two seemingly contradictory notions—free will and servitude—coexisting in the same person (SE 151). This paradox is the reason why it cannot be thought directly, but must be expressed indirectly through symbols. Thinking these donative symbols is the task Ricoeur takes up in *The Symbolism of Evil*. 

### Ricoeur’s Hermeneutical Turn

In *The Symbolism of Evil* Ricoeur aims at a “concrete reflection,” bringing together the dual aspects of his intellectual formation—the Greek and the biblical, the reflexive and the symbolic (H 4; SE 20). The book comprises a phenomenology of confession—a “re-enactment” of the experience of fault in the sympathetic imagination (SE 3–4, 19, 161). This re-enactment cannot take place directly, whether through philosophical reflection, religious psychology, or phenomenological description. Instead of a shortcut to direct description, philosophy must take the long way around, on a detour through the symbolic language of confession (SE 266, 274).

Philosophical reflection on fault—like philosophy in general—cannot begin without presuppositions. It has to find the right point of departure, and reflecting on fault means beginning with the concrete language of confession, since that is where fault comes to consciousness and into speech. Philosophy must begin in the fullness of living, existing language (SE 7, 19, 348). This point is related to Nabert’s principle that reflection can only recognize the subject in its objects. In *The Symbolism of Evil*, Ricoeur takes this principle and shows what it looks like in practice.

In the experience of fault, the religious believer experiences a vexing perplexity, “existential eruptions” of self-alienation, incomprehensibility, and meaninglessness. Confession is the original mode in which these experiences are articulated in language. The language that gives voice to this experience is not, however, direct or literal. While we can speak more directly about such phenomena as purposes, motives, and the *I can*, Ricoeur observes that talk about evil requires metaphors like “estrangement, errance, burden, and 

*[p. 40]* ^pg57

bondage” (RM 316), as well as symbols like “stain or blemish,” “trespass” and “missing the mark” (SE 9). The symbolism of evil is a central feature of cultural imaginaries around the world. As Ricoeur observes in *Memory,* *History, Forgetting*: “No theme outside of death and love has given rise to as many symbolic constructions as evil” (MHF 465). These primary symbols are then given secondary symbolic expression in myths, which tell stories that tie the symbols into a larger meaningful whole about the cosmos and our place therein (SE 170–71). Only at a relatively advanced level are they given tertiary expression in theological and philosophical concepts, which Ricoeur refers to as *speculation* (SE 9).<sup>3</sup>

A direct phenomenology of fault is therefore impossible because we have no direct, immediate, or literal access to the original consciousness of fault. Confession is always mediated by symbols and myths, so if we hope to understand the phenomenon of fault we must follow the indirect path of interpreting the symbols and myths through which fault comes to language. Without that symbolic language, the primal experience of fault “would remain mute, obscure, and shut up in its implicit contradictions” (SE 161). Ricoeur’s phenomenology of the will therefore takes a hermeneutical turn, following an interpretive detour through the symbols and myths that figure our understanding of the bad will.

### “A Symbolics of Liberty in Bondage”

The *Symbolism of Evil* marks the beginning of Ricoeur’s hermeneutical turn. While his earlier works contain insights that point toward hermeneutics (such as the opacity of self-consciousness and the need for mediation), this was Ricoeur’s first overtly hermeneutical work because of its explicit attention to deciphering the diverse symbols and myths that bring the experience of evil to language (IA 17). Ricoeur credits Mircea Eliade’s *Patterns in Comparative Religion* with helping him to recognize the primacy of the symbol as the “fundamental structure of religious language” (IA 17). Ricoeur’s journey through the symbols and myths of evil is expansive and rich in detail, covering symbols and myths from Babylonian, Jewish, Greek, and Christian texts; it can therefore be easy to lose sight of what *The Symbolism of Evil* is trying to do and how it fits into the larger project of the Philosophy of the Will. In Ricoeur’s words, the book aims to “give reflection a new start by means of a symbolics of liberty in bondage” (SE 19). He focuses on three primary symbols: evil as defilement, as sin, and as guilt.

*Defilement* is the most archaic of the symbols of evil. It is expressed in the language of contagion, infection, impurity, stain, spots, blemish, and revolves around “a quasi-material something that infects as a sort of filth, that harms 

*[p. 41]* ^pg58

by invisible properties,” and yet has a power that works on our “psychic and corporeal existence” (SE 25–26). It has an objectivity to it, so that those who come in contact with it become infected. The subjective posture toward defilement is thus one of anguish and dread—dread of contamination and the vengeance it demands (SE 33, 42). The aim of confession is a recognition that justice demands some kind of punishment, the aim of which is not merely payback but restoration—to re-establish and re-affirm the order that has been violated, in the hope of finally eliminating the fear and pain associated with the punishment.

The second schema of the bound will is that of *sin*. The symbolism of sin is oriented to the idea of existence “before God,” particularly in the Hebrew model of the covenant between God and Israel. Sin is not a violation of an abstract moral code or command; commands and prohibitions are given as part of YHWH’s constitution of Israel as his people. Sin is therefore a violation of this “personal bond” between YHWH and his people (SE 52); it is a rupture of relation (SE 58), a lost bond, root, or ground of one’s being (SE 71).<sup>4</sup> Ricoeur observes that the Hebrew scriptures have no abstract word for sin, but instead a “bundle of concrete expressions”: *chattat* refers to missing the mark, which would find its Greek counterpart in *hamartema* and Latin in *peccatum*; *‘awon* indicates going astray, wandering on “a circuitous, curving, torturous way”; *pesha‘* refers to revolt or stiff-neckedness; and *shagah* is the situation of being lost after going astray (SE 71–73).

What these relational terms have in common is an imagination of sin as negation, nothingness, nonbeing rather than a position or being. Thus Ricoeur also highlights those symbols of sin and evil that have a certain *positive* ontological status (SE 81). If sin is not a being in the sense of a spiritual or physical substance, this does not mean it simply does not exist. It is “real” in the sense of existing objectively beyond my subjective consciousness of guilt. Moreover, it is not only personal or individual because it also manifests at the communal level in group responsibility and shared complicity in guilt. And, finally, sin is real because God sees it. “God—and not my consciousness—is the ‘*for itself*’ of sin” (SE 84). Ricoeur cites [[Psalms 139#^139-23|Ps. 139:23–24]] as an example: “Search me, O God, and know my heart; test me and know my thoughts. See if there is any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting.” God sees the self with an absolute regard, and this regard gives the self a new task, of seeking to know oneself as one is also known ([[Psalms 139#^139-23|Ps. 139:23–24]]) (SE 84–85).

The other major symbol of sin is that of bondage or captivity. Ricoeur finds this in Babylonian, Hebrew, and Greek sources, as well as in the New Testament, where Paul depicts slavery: “Therefore, do not let sin exercise dominion in your mortal bodies, to make you obey their passions. . . . Just as you once presented your members as slaves to impurity and to greater and greater 

*[p. 42]* ^pg59

iniquity, so now present your members as slaves to righteousness for sanctification” ([[Romans 06#^6-12|Romans 6:12]], [[Romans 06#^6-19|19]]). In these verses, Ricoeur discerns a symbolism of a “sinful being who is at the same time *act* and *state*” (SE 154), with the act being the “‘yielding’ of the body to servitude” and the state being the reign of sin that ensues: “A ‘yielding’ *of* myself that is at the same time a ‘reign’ *over* myself—there is the enigma of the servile will, of the will that makes itself a slave” (SE 154).

The third schema of evil is *guilt*, which marks the increasing internalization of sin consciousness. As we just saw, sin is an objective reality of the human before God. Guilt is the subjective awareness of this reality. Sin and guilt are therefore two moments in fault: sin is the objective, ontological moment while guilt is the subjective, self-conscious moment (SE 101). This heightened self-consciousness is a phenomenologically new stage, since it is a recognition of one’s own responsibility for misusing one’s freedom to commit evil (SE 102). The movement from the objective reality of sin to the “phenomenalism” of guilt also complicates things, since it opens the “play of illusions and masks” as the human becomes his own measure: “Man is guilty as he feels himself guilty” (SE 104). One danger here is that the seriousness of sin might be deflated: I’m not guilty so long as I don’t get caught! Or I can develop my own therapeutic methods for managing my guilty feelings. But another, opposite danger that arises with the increasing interiority—and individuality—of guilt is that of the scrupulous conscience, which can create a “hell of condemnation” (SE 104). 

Ricoeur cites a culturally and theologically formative example with the apostle Paul. For Paul this guilt begins with the impossibility of satisfying the demands of the law, and thus the impossibility of being right, or just, before God. The law cannot give life; it only gives the knowledge of sin and, in so doing, provokes further sin, so that sin forms a circle with itself and leads to death (SE 140–41). The law sets up an ontology of self-justification, and with it a new existential category: the flesh (*sarx*), set in opposition to Spirit (*pneuma*). Paul is not presenting a metaphysical dualism in which two substances are in conflict, one as the principle of evil and the other of good. Flesh and Spirit are basic existential orientations of the whole person. Flesh is not the body, but the existential posture of willing to live and justify oneself according to the law (SE 142). What follows is a fundamental self-alienation, a “cleavage” within the self. The I acknowledges itself as “a being of flesh, sold into the power of sin” (7:14), yet, in acknowledging itself, disowns itself: “It is no longer I who perform the action” (7:20). “Now if I do that which I will not, it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me” (7:20). 

The self is alienated from itself. It is I, yet not I. Ricoeur advances a basic principle to understand this phenomenon: “To become the tribunal of oneself is to be alienated” (SE 145). This idea plays out in the subsequent history of 

*[p. 43]* ^pg60

Christianity in theologians like Augustine and Luther on the question of justification by works. Ricoeur also observes that this Pauline theme plays out in Hegel, Marx, Nietzsche, Freud, and Sartre, all of whom examine the dark depths of self-alienation (SE 145).

To further intensify this predicament, the guilty conscience is unable to understand the meaning of its own condition in the present. It is only in retrospect, from the condition of justification by God—a justification that overcomes alienation and liberates the self—that the conscience can understand its earlier condemnation (SE 148–50, 275). Ricoeur sees the same retrospective understanding in Israel, who understand their bondage in Egypt only in hindsight after being liberated.

Ricoeur spends a lot of time in his detour through the symbols of defilement, sin, and guilt, so it is helpful when he reconnects with the larger project of the Philosophy of the Will by seeking to “recapitulate” these symbols conceptually. As he writes in the conclusion to Part I of *The Symbolism of Evil*: “The concept toward which the whole series of the primary symbols of evil tends may be called the *servile will*” (SE 151).

Ricoeur offers a threefold schematism of the servile will. The first feature is the positivity of evil. This symbolism expresses the sense of the positivity of evil—it is not nothing, a lack or absence, but is posited and has a dark power to enslave the will that posited it. The second feature expresses the sense of evil’s externality: “Evil is both something brought about now and something that is always already there.” The third feature expresses the sense of evil as infection. This is the most archaic element of the symbolism of evil, and seems most connected to magic, so it initially appears the most outdated and unsalvageable. Yet Ricoeur continues to find significance in the way these images show that “seduction *from the outside* is ultimately an affection of the self by the self, an autoinfection, by which the act of binding oneself is transformed into the state of being bound” (SE 155–56). That is the paradox expressed by the symbolism of evil. The self freely makes itself unfree. The *I can* turns itself into the *I cannot*.

This schematism mediates the experience of fault as it is expressed in the language of confession, and it has a quasi-teleological drive toward conceptualization. The symbol gives rise to thought. Ricoeur wants to carry this semantic abundance into the domain of philosophical reflection, to gain understanding of fundamental existential truths about the human being. In the symbolism of the bound will, he is seeking to understand fault.

Few philosophers have really taken fault seriously, partly because it can be addressed only at the level of historical contingency and not at the level 

*[p. 44]* ^pg61

of abstract, universal structures. Even philosophers seeking to think in an existential key—Heidegger, Jaspers, Sartre—tend to reduce fault to a structural feature of finitude. Sin is something of an embarrassment to philosophy, since it is impossible to render intelligible. This is the fundamental mystery of the *I cannot*. One thinker who does, however, attempt to grapple with this mystery is Immanuel Kant, whom Ricoeur cites as his example for the philosophical interpretation of sin and the paradox of the bound will. Kant will prove to be a programmatic thinker for Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, so it is to Kant that we now turn.

### Destined for the Good yet Inclined to Evil: Kant on Radical Evil

In *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason*, Kant presents his project as a contribution to philosophical theology, as distinguished from *biblical* theology. This separation of disciplines is important for Kant, who is keen to show that his philosophical inquiry into religion does not encroach on the faculty of theology. Kant’s philosophy of religion is not a work of natural theology or apologetics, but rather a proto-hermeneutics of religion. This is evident in the way Kant draws on the “history, languages, the books of all peoples, even the Bible, in order to confirm and explain its propositions” (RBMR 61), and then interprets these sources within the boundaries of reason alone. Kant therefore differentiates between ecclesiastical *faiths*—that is, Judaism, Christianity, Islam (what is often called “positive religion”) and the pure religion of reason.

This philosophical interpretation does not presume to carry its “propositions over into biblical theology” or to “modify its public doctrines.” Philosophy may borrow from biblical theology, but Kant insists that he is putting these resources to strictly philosophical use and not making any demands on biblical theology (RBMR 61–62). The goal of this philosophical interpretation is to establish a “pure *rational system* of religion,” which could then be compared with the “*historical system*” of positive religion and its fragments of divine revelation. Kant seeks to show that these two modes of religious discourse are not only compatible but result in a fundamental unity, which becomes evident when we transform religious doctrine into moral concepts (RBMR 64–65).

For Kant, morality does not in itself need religion. Morality can discern the objectives of the will through pure practical reason: I know what my duties are because of the categorical imperative. There is, however, still a question of the ultimate outcome of my dutiful actions. Although duty is a purely formal concern and should not concern itself with consequences, at some level 

*[p. 45]* ^pg62

we expect that duty should lead to a happy ending. This desire for happiness should not be our reason for doing our duty, but it can register at the level of hope for the “highest good in the world,” such that moral goodness is united and harmonized with happiness. In order to hope for this highest good, “we must assume a higher, moral, most holy, and omnipotent being who alone can unite the two elements of this good.” This orientation toward the highest good as our final end is not a mere contingency of religious piety, but arises naturally out of moral reason (RBMR 58–59). “Morality thus inevitably leads to religion, and through religion it extends itself to the idea of a mighty moral lawgiver outside the human being, in whose will the final end (of the creation of the world) is what can and at the same time ought to be the final human end” (RBMR 59–60).

The first thing to note is that Kant’s project is a philosophical interpretation of *religion* rather than theology, so its primary focus is the human being rather than God. God does appear in the book as the source of the highest good in which moral goodness and happiness coincide, but as Ricoeur writes, this “adds nothing to what had been said about God” in Kant’s critical philosophy, as this postulate comes straight out of the *Critique of Practical Reason*.<sup>5</sup> Instead, Kant’s philosophical interpretation of religion revolves around the question of freedom—that is, how religion in its representations, beliefs, and institutions helps to restore the subject’s freedom to act in accord with the moral law (FS 75–76). For Kant, religion is about the regeneration of the bound will, and this will prove to be a central theme that Ricoeur takes from Kant in his description of religion as the rebirth of the capable self.

It may come as something of a surprise that Kant gives religion this role in moral regeneration, since he argues in the Preface to *Religion* that the moral subject does not need religion to be capable of doing its duty (RBMR 57). Ought implies can. This is soon complicated, however, by Kant’s observation that human nature is divided, a mix of good and evil dispositions (RBMR 63). Kant is not satisfied with views that present human nature as purely good or totally corrupt, insisting on the contrary that it is partly good and partly evil. Humans have an original predisposition toward the good. This predisposition is grounded in the human desire to preserve itself, to propagate the species, and to form community; it is also grounded in the rational capacity for comparison and consideration of the opinion of others. Kant readily admits that these desires can and often do turn vicious, but their deepest nature is an original goodness, which predisposes us to the good. This is especially true of the human attunement to respecting the moral law and recognizing it as sufficient in itself to orient our power of choice (*Willkür*) (RBMR 75–76).

In using the term *human nature,* Kant is not suggesting a static essence independent of human choice and responsibility. For him, *nature* designates the “subjective ground” by which we adopt the maxims or principles of our 

*[p. 46]* ^pg63

actions. The subjective ground “*precedes every deed*, and hence is itself not yet a *deed*” (RBMR 79). This is important for understanding the human propensity to evil. It is not simply that we sometimes make bad choices. The propensity to evil runs deeper, and it plays out in successive stages: first, in the frailty of human nature, when the good maxim in my power of choice is weaker than my inclination; further, in the impurity of the heart, which often needs additional incentives to move it to the good; and finally, in human depravity or perversity. The first two stages are unintentional guilt (*culpa*), but the third is deliberate guilt (*dolus*), in which the power of choice adopts maxims that undermine the moral law, subordinating its incentives to others. Kant is not talking about an outright malice that diabolically chooses evil for evil’s sake, because deliberate guilt often involves a heavy dose of self-deception regarding the goodness or evil of the maxim (RBMR 77–78, 84). Still, Kant calls this propensity “radical evil,” *radical* recalling the Latin *radix*, for “root.” Evil is radical when it corrupts the roots or the ground of all maxims, and cannot be rooted out or “extirpated by human forces” (RBMR 83). As Ricoeur puts it, radical evil is “the a priori principle of every bad maxim” (FS 80).

Kant’s concept of radical evil is his rational reinterpretation of the doctrine of original sin. In his view, this propensity is universally human, yet it is neither innate nor a work of nature. Each individual is responsible for it (RBMR 70–71). With this concept, Kant identifies the paradox in the confession of evil: it is at once an individual choice and deed and a something that was already there prior to our act (FS 79). Kant rejects, however, the theological idea of original sin as *inherited*, since evil does not originate from a propensity but in a freely chosen fall (RBMR 86–87).<sup>6</sup> Despite his attempt to approximate original sin with his notion of radical evil, though, Kant’s interpretation is ultimately Pelagian. While Ricoeur does not embrace Kant’s Pelagian interpretation of sin as freely chosen, he is still sympathetic to Kant’s critique of original sin, and he attempts to find a position that mediates between Pelagianism and the Augustinian account of original sin.

### The Trouble with Adam (and everyone else): Augustine and the Hermeneutics of Original Sin

Augustine is a crucial figure for the hermeneutics of evil. His works are central to the cultural memory of Western Christianity, and the West in general, because of his influential treatment of sin, grace, will, and the interior life of the soul. Augustine’s work is also a rich resource for any phenomenology of confession that wants to let the believing soul speak. Augustine’s *Confessions* 

*[p. 47]* ^pg64

is a major case-study for the hermeneutics of the religious self, since it consists of Augustine’s self-interpretation through the Bible. In almost every paragraph Augustine configures his past experiences with reference to biblical texts—not to mention pagan classics like Virgil and Plotinus. 

One of the most vivid expressions of Augustine’s believing soul comes in Book VIII of the *Confessions*, where he describes the conflict between two wills. Augustine has heard about the conversion of Victorinus, a famous professor of rhetoric and scholar of Platonism, and he longs for a conversion of his own that he might experience the courage and freedom of Victorinus. Yet all he knows is a self-imposed bondage: “I sighed after such freedom, but was bound not by an iron imposed by anyone else but by the iron of my own choice. The enemy had a grip on my will and so made a chain for me to hold me a prisoner.” Augustine is not simply blaming the devil, since he himself is complicit in his own bondage. He outlines the stages by which this bondage takes hold: “The consequence of a distorted will is passion. By servitude to passion, habit is formed, and habit to which there is no resistance becomes necessary. By these links, as it were, connected one to another (hence my term a chain) a harsh bondage held me under restraint.”<sup>7</sup> 

Augustine feels a new will beginning to form within himself—a will to serve God freely and enjoy Him as “the only sure source of pleasure”—but this nascent will is too weak to overcome the binding power of his old will.<sup>8</sup> He then describes the experience of being internally divided between the spirit and the flesh, and he notices a shift insofar as he identifies more with the new spiritual will than the old will of the flesh, the “no-longer-I” of sin dwelling within ([[Romans 07#^7-17|Rom. 7:17]]), since his sin was now more “passive and unwilling rather than active and willing.”<sup>9</sup>

“What is the cause of this monstrous situation?” Augustine asks later in Book VIII. “The mind commands the body and is instantly obeyed. The mind commands itself and meets resistance. . . . The mind orders the mind to will. The recipient of the order is itself, yet it does not perform it. What causes this monstrosity and why does it happen?”<sup>10</sup> Here Augustine’s answer to the question is more existential phenomenology than substance metaphysics: The mind is “uplifted by truth” and a new will comes into being, but this will is incomplete because it is weighed down by the habit of the old will, so it fails to engage. There are two wills—both incomplete—within one divided self.<sup>11</sup> What Augustine will not say, however, is that these two wills are rival substances at war within the self. He explicitly forecloses that interpretation,<sup>12</sup> which is the view of the Manichees. The struggle in this passage is twofold, for alongside the struggle between the two wills, Augustine is also doing battle with Manichaeism.

As Augustine recounts in the *Confessions*, the metaphysics of evil was a long-standing obstacle to his conversion to Christianity. He spent nearly a 

*[p. 48]* ^pg65

decade studying Manichean teaching, which offered a Gnostic concept of evil as a substance or contagion that had contaminated the entire cosmos (CI 272–73). One of the intellectual breakthroughs that allowed Augustine to break from the Manichees was the realization that evil is not a substance, that it has no nature or being of its own but is rather a wound in being, a privation of the Good that occurs with our freely chosen turn away from God. The opposite of conversion, it is an *aversio a Deo*:

I inquired what wickedness is; and I did not find a substance but a perversity of will twisted away from the highest substance, you O God, toward inferior things, rejecting its own inner life ([[LXX Sirach 10#^10-10|Ecclus. 10:10]]) and swelling with external matter.<sup>13</sup> 

According to Ricoeur, by reconceiving evil as *doing* rather than *being*, Augustine reorients the problem of evil from ontology to ethics. Against the tragic vision of the cosmos, we suffer not because of fate, fortune, or divine caprice but because of our free choice. We are responsible (CI 273–75). 

By rethinking evil in this way, Augustine also unwittingly enabled a new misunderstanding—that of Pelagius, who took Augustine’s emphasis on the free choice of the will to a thoroughly voluntarist conclusion: just as sin arises from the free choice of the will, so it is up to each individual whether or not he or she will sin. Each of us is individually responsible, and it also seems to follow that it is within every individual’s power *not* to sin (*posse non peccare*). The Pelagians viewed freedom as unencumbered by habit, history, or acquired nature (CI 279). Yet, such an optimistic anthropology was incommensurable with Augustine’s own experience of the captivity of sin. Augustine was acutely aware of the iron bonds that are imposed, paradoxically, by one’s own free choice.<sup>14</sup> This is why Augustine does not write that he converted himself to God—something he was unable to do—but rather that God converted him.<sup>15</sup>

The Pelagians also maintained that a just God would not punish us for the sins of others, which entails that we cannot inherit sin or guilt from Adam. When Paul writes in Romans 5 that all sinned in Adam, this indicates “a relation of imitation. *In* Adam means *like* Adam” (CI 278). In response, Augustine amplifies his concept of original sin,<sup>16</sup> taking it to its “bitter end” by interpreting it simultaneously as “a taint inherited by birth” and a personal guilt deserving death. Augustine thereby inconsistently combines a quasi-juridical concept of inherited guilt and a quasi-biological concept of sin as a contagion passed in sexual generation (CI 270, 280). Consequently, while Augustine’s doctrine of original sin was “anti-gnostic in origin and intention,” it ends up leading to a “quasi-gnostic” rationalization in which sin acquires the status of Gnosis, a concept of theological speculation.

*[p. 49]* ^pg66

Augustine’s concept of original sin became immensely influential throughout Western Christianity. Ricoeur cites a passage from the *La Rochelle Confession of Faith*, which asserts: “We believe that the entire descent of Adam is infected with this contagion. The contagion is original sin and a hereditary vice. It is not just an imitation, as the Pelagians, whose errors we deplore, have held” (CI 269). This conception of sin plays out at more popular levels, as in Bob Dylan’s lyrics: “Temptation’s not an easy thing, Adam’s given the devil reign. Because he sinned I got no choice, it runs in my veins.” Similarly, in Kendrick Lamar’s song: “I got dark, I got evil, that rot inside my DNA.”<sup>17</sup> While imagery of contagion may express the ease with which sin spreads, it also reinforces the conception of evil as a quasi-biological *thing*, a substance that has a being or nature of its own. Such is the danger of Augustine’s account of original sin.

Ricoeur’s interpretive work on original sin is therefore an attempt to get back behind some of this theological speculation and encounter the text and its symbolism with fresh eyes. Ricoeur does this by offering a series of clarifications regarding our tendency to import later ideas back into the text of Genesis. For example, while it is traditional to refer to the Adamic myth as a myth of “the Fall,” this symbolism derives more from Plato, Gnosticism, and Plotinus than the text of Genesis itself, which is more a myth of “deviation” or “going astray” (SE 233). For another example, while the free will defense (owing no small part to Augustine) tends to reflect on the Fall as a consequence of misused freedom, the Adamic myth is not primarily oriented around the concept of freedom. What is in play is “the power of the human creature to undo (*défaire*) and to unmake himself (*se défaire*).” *Fallibility*, not freedom, is the issue here (SE 233–34). A third example is that the Adamic myth is not the starting point for the Jewish experience and articulation of sin. Adam’s role in the Old Testament is negligible, and even Jesus in the New Testament never says anything about Adam’s sin. The figure of Adam comes into prominence in the writings of Paul, who juxtaposes Adam and Christ as types representative of larger wholes: the old humanity and the new humanity, death and life (see Romans 5 and in I Corinthians 15). Thus Ricoeur contends that “it was Christology that consolidated Adamology” (SE 238).

Ricoeur’s most significant objection to the Augustinian reading of the Fall is the way it moves from the level of the symbol to speculation, taking the symbolism of the Adamic myth and rendering it as a quasi-gnostic concept. The concept of original sin, as articulated by Augustine and subsequent theologians, is not originally biblical but rather the result of theological speculation. In Augustine’s case, this developed in the controversies with the Manichees and then the Pelagians. The images, parables, and narratives of captivity are present in the biblical texts, in preaching, and in confessing 

*[p. 50]* ^pg67

sin, but they are “more descriptive than explanatory,” and it is precisely in the attempt at causal explanation that Augustine’s doctrine of original sin goes astray. Theological speculation wants determinate knowledge of sin, and so tries to fix the image in a rational concept. Ricoeur therefore proposes to deconstruct (*défaire*) the dogmatic concept of sin as “false knowledge”—that is, quasi-juridical or quasi-biological knowledge—in order to recover the surplus of meaning intended by the symbols.

Does this mean Ricoeur sides with Pelagius? Not really. Despite the flaws of Augustine’s concept of original sin, Ricoeur still argues that it conveys a depth of meaning that Pelagius “completely misunderstood” (CI 281). Once it is broken apart as a speculative concept, the doctrine of original sin can once again reveal its “dark analogical riches”—that is, the surplus of meaning that the concept intends, and which is originally expressed in such biblical symbols as wandering, revolt, the missing of the target, the curved and torturous path, and captivity (CI 282). The biblical symbolism is not so much a causal explanation as a description of the “obscure and obsessive traits” we experience in the confession of sin. Ricoeur highlights three such traits in particular: first, the “*realism* of sin”—that is, the recognition that “consciousness of sin is not its measure.” Sin is not merely what happens to come into consciousness, but “my true situation before God.” Sin is a reality that is independent of my own conscience or consciousness; it runs deeper and spreads wider than I recognize, which is why the external perspective of the prophet is often necessary to make me aware of sin (CI 282). 

The second trait of penitential experience is the communal solidarity of the human race in sin—that it is not a matter of individual choice and sole responsibility, but that sin unites humanity in a “transbiological and transhistorical solidarity” (CI 282–83). This also means that the individual subject is not the origin of sin. As Ricoeur writes, the Adamic myth reveals this meaning, the mystery that whenever anyone chooses evil, he also *discovers* evil as “already there, in himself, outside himself, and before himself.” In this regard “I do not begin evil; I continue it” (CI 284). Ricoeur argues in *The Symbolism of Evil* that in this sense, the Adamic myth preserves some aspects of the tragic myths of evil. To be sure, the Adamic myth is “anti-tragic” in its rejection of the ideas of both fate and a wicked god complicit in human sin. The figure of Adam “thematizes a mystery of iniquity which is not reducible to clear consciousness of actual evil, of the evil beginning in the instant” (SE 311). This is the point we saw in Nabert at the beginning of this chapter, namely that it is impossible to locate myself as the absolute origin of fault. Instead, reflection discovers a negative participation in something prior to my own choice. As Ricoeur puts it, I confess that evil is “already there in the very instant in which I avow that I put it there. This other side, not posited, of an evil that is posited, is the ‘radical’ in radical evil” (SE 311). 

Ricoeur sees this meaning in Genesis 3, in the enigmatic figure of the serpent that symbolizes the way we find evil already there (SE 257). But the 

*[p. 51]* ^pg68

serpent also deepens this meaning because it represents the otherness of the Adversary who seduces, the source of sin depicted in images of the demonic and diabolical (SE 313). Ricoeur does not want to render the symbolism of the demonic in speculative concepts, and generally seems to have a demythologizing approach to the question of the Devil.<sup>18</sup> Yet, for Ricoeur, demythologization does not mean demythicization, so at this stage of his work he does attempt to listen to the symbolism of the demonic and let it be, rather than following the facile psychologizing of so much modern thought.

The idea of sin as *inheritance* is still compelling because we experience sin as already there, along with an inclination to sin that runs deeper than individual choice or acts. We find ourselves captive, bound, incapable of acting the way we should. This is the third trait Ricoeur identifies: our experience of fundamental impotence in the face of sin—the gap between “I want” and “I can” (CI 282–83). Augustine had intense firsthand experience of this gap, and he saw it in the Pauline letters. This is what he wants to defend against the Pelagian optimism of free will. In Ricoeur’s words: “Evil is a kind of involuntariness at the very heart of the voluntary, no longer facing the individual but within the voluntary; and it is this which is the servile will” (CI 286).

For Ricoeur, then, one of the basic problems with the Pelagian view is this: Despite offering a commonsense, intuitively attractive picture of free will and individual responsibility, it does not have Augustine’s phenomenologically profound insight into human nature—the human being’s perplexing, perverse capacity for incapacity, to freely make itself unfree. Ricoeur wants to preserve this aspect of the Augustinian concept of original sin, unpacking its insight into penitential experience while discarding its speculative, explanatory aims.

This is where Ricoeur draws on Kant’s concept of radical evil. While he does not offer an outright endorsement of Kant’s interpretation of sin as individual choice, Ricoeur claims “it has as much right” as the Augustinian interpretation of sin as a quasi-biological inheritance (FS 81). To put the issue in different terms, it is a question of how to think sin in terms of *act* and *being*.<sup>19</sup> Should sin be understood as act—as freely posited, conscious choice? Or should it be understood as being—as an ontological reality that precedes and underlies the act? Ricoeur’s point, which has strong phenomenological warrant, is that we need to include both aspects in some way. In order to think sin, we need to reckon with the experience of sin as external, as a reality I did not initiate with my own act. I am not the radical origin of sin; my act continues the long, destructive history of sin. Augustine recognizes this, which is why the Pelagian perspective is not a profound enough picture of human nature. This is the sense of the Pauline language of being *in* Adam. My ontological participation in Adam runs deeper than mere imitation. Yet at the same time, sin is also an act in which I posit evil. I am responsible for my act. Ricoeur is suggesting that Pelagius and Kant get this much right.

*[p. 52]* ^pg69

The trick, then, is to find a way to think sin as both act *and* being. What Kant shows us, Ricoeur contends, is the impossibility of a speculative victory that renders the mystery of radical evil intelligible. By highlighting these limits, “Kant completes Augustine,” since he gets rid of the Gnostic aspects of Augustine’s concept of original sin and then proceeds with a “transcendental deduction of the ground of evil maxims.” The result of this deduction is the realization that “the search for a ground of the ground” will always return to a plunge into the depths of unknowing, as the real origin of evil is impenetrable to our thought (CI 308). 

Kant’s transcendental deduction of radical evil clearly does not give philosophy all it needs to think the bound will—otherwise Ricoeur would not find it necessary to undertake a hermeneutical detour through the symbolism of evil. The symbols give rise to thought, but thought must remain in constant contact with the symbol, which is both the source of nourishment and the insurmountable limit of thought. The next question for Kant is what religion can do about the problem of the bound will. As we will see in chapter 3, both Kant and Ricoeur argue that religion taps into resources of goodness that lie within the self—resources that are more originary than evil. Victor Hugo, reflecting on the character of Jean Valjean in *Les Misérables*, writes:

Can human nature be so entirely transformed from top to bottom? Can man, created good by God, be made wicked by man? Can the soul be changed to keep pace with its destiny, and become evil when its destiny is evil? . . . Is there not in every human soul, is there not in the particular soul of Jean Valjean, a primitive spark, a divine element, incorruptible in this world, immortal in the next, which can be developed by good, kindled, lit up, and made resplendently radiant, and which evil can never entirely distinguish?<sup>20</sup>

For Kant, Hugo, and Ricoeur, the answer to these questions is *yes*, there is such an unextinguished goodness. What all three have in common is a very modern confidence in the fundamental goodness of the human being—a goodness that is never fully erased by evil. “As radical as evil may be, Kant states . . . it is not original” (MHF 491). For Ricoeur, the propensity to evil may be radical, but the predisposition to good is original.

### Fragility and Suffering

Ricoeur’s primary concern in the Philosophy of the Will is with fault as evil committed, and the bondage that is self-inflicted. Ricoeur later observed that this focus was due to his early intellectual formation, which led him to think about fault in dialogue with Paul, Augustine, and Luther. But he came to 

*[p. 53]* ^pg70

see that lineage as too limited insofar as it reduces the problem of evil to the interiority of guilt. His thinking expanded to include the problem of violence in its many forms, including evil in the form of suffering.<sup>21</sup> If fault concerns the evil that men do, there is also the evil we suffer. Ricoeur makes this distinction in his 1985 lecture “Evil—A Challenge to Philosophy and Theology,” and it reflects his point that the self is both active and passive, and that human being must be understood as both acting and suffering \[*agissant* et *souffrant*\].<sup>22</sup> 

Ricoeur draws this dialectic of acting and suffering from Hannah Arendt’s book *The Human Condition*, which points out that doing and suffering are two sides of the same coin: “The story that an act starts is composed of its consequent deeds and sufferings.”<sup>23</sup> There is some precedent for this insight in Ricoeur’s earlier work, which recognizes the self is both active and passive. The dialectic of the voluntary and the involuntary continues in the dialectic of acting and suffering, power and impotence, *praxis* and *pathos* (DB 27–28). Suffering, however, is a specific form of passivity. Both words—*passion* and *passivity*—have their root in the Latin *patī*, which means to undergo, endure, submit, or suffer. But not all passivity is suffering. As Ricoeur writes in *Oneself as Another*: “Undergoing and enduring are, in a sense, revealed in their complete passive dimension when they become suffering” (OA 320). The complete passivity that is suffering “is not defined solely by physical pain, nor even by mental pain, but by the reduction, even the destruction, of the capacity for acting, of being-able-to-act, experienced as a violation of self-integrity” (OA 190).

Part of the ontology of the self is its fragility, its vulnerability to various forms of suffering. Ricoeur also finds this point in Arendt, who draws our attention to the frailty of human action, and the ways we can be “deprived of the capacity to act.”<sup>24</sup> Fault is therefore not the only thing that threatens to undermine the capable human being; fragility and vulnerability are also essential to our experience of ourselves as capable and incapable. Human beings are vulnerable to pain, illness, death, and natural disasters, as well as violence and injustice inflicted by others. One of the questions we will need to ask is not only how religion regenerates capability by liberating the will bound by radical evil, but also how it can address those who suffer and who are vulnerable to suffering.

This is especially important because a phenomenology of human capability could suggest a story of human triumph and success. But the capable human being is also the vulnerable human being. Capability and vulnerability are counterparts, alternate aspects of what it means to be human, co-existing in the same human being. Capacity can only be understood in relation to incapacity, just as action can only be understood in relation to passion—in the senses of both passivity and suffering.

*[p. 54]* ^pg71

Ricoeur discusses this point in detail in his 1995 lecture “Autonomy and Vulnerability.” There he argues that the fragile, vulnerable human being is “called on to become autonomous” (AV 72–73), and he considers how the law facilitates the exercise of autonomy by human beings who are characterized by capability and vulnerability. These questions are also pertinent to the hermeneutics of religion, which is concerned with how religious discourse cultivates the capability of vulnerable human beings. 

Radical evil is thus not the only factor that hinders the self in its capability. There is also the reality of vulnerability. To be sure, evil and vulnerability are distinct phenomena. Whereas evil is contingent rather than part of the essential structures of the will, vulnerability is part of the ontological constitution of what it is to be human. Evil and vulnerability do, however, intersect. Vulnerability is a site of interest for evil—something to exploit. Evil sees vulnerability as an opportunity to gain power, a chance to oppress and inflict suffering. Some forms of vulnerability are also exacerbated by evil that we inflict on each other, and which we inflict on ourselves. Thus while evil and vulnerability are ontologically distinct, phenomenologically we need to recognize the ways they are often interwoven. We also need to recognize the place of fragility, or vulnerability, in the four fundamental capacities of the self. 

I can speak, I can act, I can narrate, I can assume responsibility. But my experience of this capability is not that of uninterrupted, unimpaired power. These capacities are fragile, partial, and their exercise often inconclusive, such that I am also aware of the many ways in which I *cannot*. The capacity to speak is always fragile in the sense that it is limited, “never complete, never fully transparent to itself.” The capacity to speak effectively also requires a mastery of language, and those who lack the ability or opportunity to speak well—to explain, argue, discuss, and defend, and to do so with appropriate confidence—are excluded from “the sphere of discourse” (AV 76–77). The capacity to act—that is, to *do* something—also has a counterpart in incapacity because of illness, age, and infirmity. The ability to act is distributed unequally among persons, creating a more pronounced vulnerability in “societies based on efficiency and competition like our own” (AV 77). The capacity to *narrate* is crucial to the identity of the self, for its continuity and constancy through time. We need to learn how to tell our stories: how to tell them in new ways, how to allow them to be told by others, and how to subject them to critique—whether by historians or by our loved ones. These stories are never finished and always subject to revision; they may have plot holes and be marked by self-deception and misunderstanding. In this regard, narrative identity is also marked by fragility, or vulnerability.

Finally, the capacity of imputability, to take responsibility for one’s actions, is also fragile. It depends on a capacity to understand one’s acts as subject to 

*[p. 55]* ^pg72

a symbolic order of moral norms and obligation, through commands, counsel, customs, institutions, founding narratives, exemplary lives, and the praise of moral sentiments. The capacity to subject one’s acts to this order is a condition of being responsible for the consequences of one’s actions (AV 82, 84). This symbolic order establishes the strongest connection between the self and the moral norm, and it provides the figures by which the rule of law can take shape (AV 90). Yet there is also fragility here, in the difficulty we have inscribing our actions in a symbolic order, and in the difficulty of this inscription being understood by others. In other words, this is the challenge of legitimating moral and religious authority (AV 85–87).

In order to understand the liberating power of religious discourse, we need to consider the way it regenerates the will that is bound by radical evil, as well as the way it addresses the vulnerable human being, in order to reconstitute and restore those capabilities where *I cannot*.
