### 4.1–6: Climax of Paul’s self-defense

*[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-1|Text]], p. 358* · Paul returns to questions of competence (see 2.16). ^4-1

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-1|1]]:** *God’s mercy*, see 1.3–7n. *Ministry*, service guided by faith. ^4-1-2

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-2|2]]:** *Renounced . . . shameful things*, Paul develops the contrast between his teaching and that of Moses, who hid the temporary nature of his ministry. Paul is unlike those who *falsify God’s word* for their own advantage. *Cunning*, lit., “crafty”; see 11.3; 12.16. *Conscience*, see 1.12n. ^4-2

> [!note] Reading of the Old Covenant *(p. 358)*
> Paul’s reference to the people of Israel who “hear the reading of the old covenant” reflects the practice of reading successive passages of the Pentateuch in the synagogue on Sabbaths (as well as on Mondays and Thursdays; see *m. Meg.* 3.4–6). While the number of segments in which the reading took place on weekdays, Sabbaths, and festivals was known in Mishnaic times (*m. Meg.* 4.1–2), in this period, the specific length and sequence of readings was debated (*b. Meg.* [[Megillah 31#31b|31b]]). Only by the fourth or fifth centuries were the weekly readings fixed. The tradition in the land of Israel was that the community would hear the entirety of the Pentateuch read sequentially over a period of three years, while in the Babylonian Diaspora the reading took place over a single year (*b. Meg.* [[Megillah 29#29b|29b]]). Since Aramaic was the language of everyday speech, the Mishnah prescribes that the Scripture in its original Hebrew would be followed, verse by verse, by an Aramaic translation and interpretation, provided by an individual known as a
>
> *Meturgemon*, that is, an official translator (*m. Meg.* 4.4). In this way, the content of God’s demands and promises as well as their significance and meaning in the current day were made clear.
>
> Within some Christian interpretations, Paul’s reference to “the old covenant” (3.12–18) is taken to refer to Pentateuchal law, which Jesus is understood to have abrogated (see [[Mark 07#^7-19|Mk 7.19]]), but not to the other biblical materials, e.g., Psalms and Prophets. In this understanding, Paul’s point, referring specifically to Scripture’s ritual obligations, is that the Jews’ veiled reading makes it impossible for them to see that the law was always meant by God as a transitory step toward the true covenant of those who are in Christ. An alternative reading holds that Paul speaks not of the end of the law but rather of the removal of the “veil” that, in his understanding, prevents the Jews from seeing the Christological references in their own Scripture and being transformed by the glory of the Lord (v 18). In this reading, Paul does not speak here about the end of the law at all.

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-3|3]]:** *Gospel is veiled*. . . *perishing*, that some people do not see the Gospel’s truth is not a failing of Paul’s teaching but of people who are spiritually blind. ^4-3

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-4|4]]:** *This world*, see [[1 Corinthians 03#^3-19|1 Cor 3.19]]; [[1 Corinthians 05#^5-10|5.10]]; [[1 Corinthians 07#^7-31|7.31]] (twice), Paul’s synonym for “the present evil age” ([[Galatians 01#^1-4|Gal 1.4]]) and “the present time” ([[Romans 03#^3-26|Rom 3.26]]; [[Romans 08#^8-18|8.18]]; [[Romans 11#^11-5|11.5]]). *God of this world*, see “rulers of this age” ([[1 Corinthians 02#^2-6|1 Cor 2.6]],[[1 Corinthians 02#^2-8|8]]; [[Ephesians 02#^2-2|Eph 2.2]]) and 2.11n. Early Jewish sources similarly distinguish “this world” from a messianic “world to come” but without the negative attitude that Paul expresses toward current life. See, e.g., *m. Avot* 4.17: “Better is a single moment spent in penitence and good deeds in this world than the whole of the world to come.” *Light* as a metaphor for God’s teaching (e.g., [[Isaiah 02#^2-5|Isa 2.5]]; [[Psalms 043#^43-3|Ps 43.3]]; [[Psalms 118#^118-27|118.27]]; [[Proverbs 06#^6-23|Prov 6.23]]) and later Jewish sources (e.g., *Eccl. Rab*. 11.6; 12.7). Paul reflects a deterministic ideology. God’s mercy (v 1) accounts for his ministry, and God’s will also explain the inability of unbelievers of *seeing the light*. Josephus (*Ant.* 13.171) attributes determinism to the Essenes, and it is found in some Dead Sea Scroll texts (e.g., 1QS 1.9–11); such thinking appears as well in rabbinic texts that explain all future history as established by God at the time of creation (*m. Avot* 5.6; *Gen. Rab.* 1.4). *Image of God*, [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-26|Gen 1.26–27]] and 9.6 refer to humankind’s creation in God’s image. But the idea is not explicit elsewhere in the Tanakh and, except for in references to these verses, does not occur in the rabbinic literature. When Eleazar b. Simeon calls a man ugly, the person responds not that he was created in God’s image, but that Eleazar should accuse the craftsman who made him of fashioning an ugly vessel (*b. Taʾan.* 20b). ^4-4

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-6|6]]:** Paraphrase of [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-3|Gen 1.3]]; [[Isaiah 09#^9-1|Isa 9.2]]; [[Psalms 112#^112-4|Ps 112.4]]. ^4-6

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-7|4.7–5.10]]:** **Affliction and consolation,** Themes of weakness and suffering, raised in 1.3–11, are elaborated to signal God’s empowering presence; *b. Ber*. [[Berakhot 5#5a|5a]]-b following [[Proverbs 03#^3-12|Prov 3.12]] depicts suffering and affliction in the absence of sin as evidence of God’s love. ^4-7

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-7|7]]:** *Clay jars*, our human bodies, which are weak and impermanent and so cannot be the source of the power of the *treasure* found in knowledge of the Gospel. The idea of the human body as a vessel of clay, which ultimately returns to dust, is common in Job (4.19; 10.9; 33.6) and Isaiah (e.g., 64.8), which depicts God as the potter and humankind as God’s creation. ^4-7-2

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-8|8–9]]:** Catalogue of hardships formulated as antithetical pairs to show the incomparability of God’s glory. See 6.4–10; 11.23–27; 12.10; [[Romans 08#^8-35|Rom 8.35]],[[Romans 08#^8-38|38–39]]; [[1 Corinthians 04#^4-11|1 Cor 4.11–13]]; [[Philippians 04#^4-12|Phil 4.12]]. Stoics used such catalogues to demonstrate their indifference to adversity; for Paul, adversity demonstrates the vessel’s unworthiness, and the overcoming of adversity documents divine power. Rabbinic Judaism carried the idea a step farther: suffering in this world assures those God loves the greatest reward in the world-to-come (e.g., *Sifre Deut*. 32.10: R. Simeon b. Yohai says, “Suffering is precious. For through suffering three good gifts were given to Israel . . . the Torah, the land of Israel, and the World-to-Come”). ^4-8

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-10|10–11]]:** *Carrying . . . the death of Jesus*, Jesus’ death is replicated in Paul’s bodily sufferings, and thereby the *life of Jesus* becomes visible; cf. [[Galatians 02#^2-20|Gal 2.20]]. ^4-10

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-11|11]]:** [[Psalms 116#^116-10|Ps 116.10]]. *Given up to death for Jesus’ sake*, believers’ suffering and persecution were counterpart to the suffering of Jesus, which was made manifest in the believers’ flesh. ^4-11

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-13|13]]:** *“I believed, and so I spoke,”* a citation of [[Psalms 116#^116-10|Ps 116.10]]\[LXX\], which makes Paul’s point: his suffering increased his confidence in proclaiming the Gospel. ^4-13

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-15|15]]:** *Grace*, see 1.12n. ^4-15

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-16|4.16–5.10]]:** Paul uses dualistic language to express the tension between present afflictions and ongoing renewal. ^4-16

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-16|16]]:** *Lose heart*, see 4.1. O*uter nature*, that part that undergoes sufferings (vv 8–9) and carries Jesus’ death (vv 10–11). *Inner nature* . . . *renewed* daily; cf. [[Romans 12#^12-2|Rom 12.2]]. ^4-16-2

**[[NRSVue 2 Corinthians 04#^4-18|18]]:** *Temporary and eternal*, a distinction familiar in Jewish eschatological thinking; see, e.g., *b. Shabb*. [[Shabbat 10#10a|10a]]; 33b; *b. Taʾan*. 21a. ^4-18
