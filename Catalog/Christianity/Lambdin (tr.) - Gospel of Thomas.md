## About

A collection of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, with no narrative frame. It survives complete only in Coptic, in a codex found near Nag Hammadi, Egypt, and no major Christian church accepts it as canonical.

- **Manuscripts:** the full text is the second of seven works in Nag Hammadi Codex II, found in December 1945, dated about 340 and held in the Coptic Museum, Cairo. Three Greek fragments from Oxyrhynchus (P. Oxy. 1, 654, 655), dated 130–250, preserve parts in markedly different wording.
- **Date:** Wikipedia says most scholars place it in the second century, with proposals from about 60 to 250. Britannica gives the mid-second century.
- **Author:** the opening names Didymos Judas Thomas as the one who wrote the sayings down. Britannica says it is traditionally ascribed to the apostle Thomas; Wikipedia says most scholars do not think he wrote it.
- **Contents and debates:** almost two-thirds of the sayings resemble sayings in the canonical gospels; 13 of its 16 parables are also in the Synoptics. Most scholars, per John P. Meier, think it depends on the Synoptics; Helmut Koester and Stevan Davies argue for independence. Britannica calls it grounded in gnosticism; Wikipedia notes that it lacks the full Gnostic myth and that scholars disagree.
- **Early witnesses:** Hippolytus of Rome (c. 222–235) quotes a "Gospel according to Thomas"; Origen lists one among heterodox gospels.
- **Lambdin's translation:** the Gnostic Society Library credits it to the Coptic Gnostic Library Project at Claremont, whose texts Brill publishes. That project's edition of Codex II, 2–7, edited by Bentley Layton (Leiden: Brill, 1989), gives Thomas in volume 1.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says some count it among the earliest accounts of Jesus' teaching, and it figures in debates about Q. Britannica says some scholars think parts preserve some of the oldest records of his words, but that its theology keeps it from being a major source for the historical Jesus.
