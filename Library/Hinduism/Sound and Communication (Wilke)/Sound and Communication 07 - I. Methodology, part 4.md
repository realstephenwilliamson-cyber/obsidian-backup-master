We investigate participation as a form of “habitus” in various cultural spheres and their overlappings. The basis is a phonocentric life reality in which semantic and emotional messages and even scientific messages are materialized in a sensory aesthetic manner. This life-world included many cognitive blends of profane and sacred views of language and interpretation of reality. It produced a fair number of holistic world-constructions, and performative fusion was not only one of the most cherished religious goals, but also an important aspect of cultural reproduction. The participatory approach in philosophy has been called metaphysical monism elsewhere. However, Sanskrit Hinduism has more than one version of cosmic unity, including more dualistic ones within theistic and devotional movements, not forgetting sonic cosmologies. Irrespective of this multiplicity, the ideal of participation in and merger with the supreme deity or higher unity pervaded practically all systems. The performance types included not only ascetic exercises and fasting, or intellectual contemplation and pure gnosis, or complete devotional surrender and feats of humility, but more often than not sound rites. Whether combined with other practices, or performed individually, the idea that one can merge with the higher unity, participate in the divine and tap divine power by means of sound (of the mantra, the divine name, or music) is all-pervading, and extends into public performances. Even today the text and its sound are often themselves viewed as a sacred, numinous substance. Essentialist objectivization is crucial to acoustic piety. In India this includes conviction of the holiness and power of sacred sound, and the belief that deities are immediately present in Tantric and devotional mantras. 

Participation in the case of Indian phonocentricity means participating in the sounds, allowing oneself to be tied into their sensory aural nature, to enjoy the “rasa” of their aesthetics, but also social synchronicity, mythical embeddedness and sacrilizing one’s life. In the case of the natural scientists mentioned, participation means inclusion of the cryptic, athmospheric, poetic and non-verbal into the scientific proposition and thus stronger emotional fusion with the scientific subject. In the case of acoustic piety it means participating in the life of the texts and the numinous substances communicated in them.

#### 1.6 Orientation on Scriptures and Orientation on Sounds -Religions Compared

In the West we generally forget just what a fundamental role the audible and spoken word often plays in everyday religious practice. In the academic approach, and for the image of holy texts, the focus has been on the written form and the discursive reading of it. The model of the “book religions” dominates. In the 1980s William Graham challenged this view by introducing the term “oral scriptures” to denote the ritual function of the sacred books and the reception of the Quran in daily religious life.” However, the oral-aural aspect of holy scriptures is not much of a theme in academic studies until to date and the public opion in Western countries of what religion is about is scriptural (besides institutional): everything is laid down in the holy “scriptures” and should be individually realized in the “act of faith.” It has been widely discussed in the past decades how much Christian protestant discourse dominated the very definition of religion within scholary approaches to religion. And it is well known that the scripturalist model of the “book religions” has been the very basis for constructing the image of “world religions.” This scripturalistic bias and the book religion model have a whole package of reasons. Amongst them are the common Western linkage of scholarship, writing and higher education, the common neglect 

of everyday piety within scholarly discourse, and the self-representations of the so called “book religions” themselves.

In India in the early Middle Ages there was already an exceptionally lively culture of intellectuals that exhausted its options for pluralistic domains of meaning in quite varied ways, either independently of, or tied to, religion. In the context of the Veda hermeneutics, a Hindu scholasticism developed from about the seventh century AD onwards, several centuries before Christian scholasticism in Europe. The Veda Mīmāmsā schools opened up to philosophical and linguistic argumentation, and began to use terms from epistemology and logic, in order to provide a rational explanation of why and how the Veda was an extra “means of knowledge” of reality (besides sense perception and logical conclusion, etc.).™ By contrast, the philosophy schools of the Nyāya and Vaiśesika, which arose originally independent of the Veda, developed a rational defense of the Vedic revelation, thereby at the same time defending the relevance of human reason and 10216. Alongside them, Veda-independent revelatory texts and theologies also developed, as did a broad spectrum of “profane” academic literatures. 

Yet it is important to note that a scientific approach and belief in magical efficacy of holy texts and sacred sounds were never necessarily mutually exclusive. And acoustic piety stands alongside thoroughly reflected theology. Magic was not something bad, but quite helpful in all types of difficulties. Mythical embedding was actually a cultural value. Even scholars did not want to do without it, as is shown below in the chapter on the literati. They actually preferred a certain amount of aesthetic, atmospheric and cryptic elements; a dry rationalism, clear, simple statements without any aesthetic quality appeared uninteresting even to the natural scientists. The mathematicians and astronomers underpinned (at least secretly) their pithy, emancipatory “habitus” with participatory elements. It was a fashion in Southern India to use the sounds of the alphabet as numerical codes, which was a more aesthetic device than digits and tables. Interestingly, the sonic world orientation is reinforced precisely among those theologians and philosophers who are closest in time to Angenendt’s schoolmen. At around this time we find sound cosmogonies and expressive fictionalizations such as the Language Goddess in whose body the syllables of the alphabet are inscribed (chap. 1.10) - this goddess (who also holds a book in her hand) is a typologically characteristic aesthetic product of sonic culture that is also in possession of writing. The European late medieval transformation into a “rational” theology and new systematic terminology that Angenendt outlined and which he interprets as a necessary consequence of book culture and as the replacement of a “simple religion of magical mythical embedding,” obviously have no equivalent (in this form) in Sanskrit Hinduism. Why is this? Media practice as cultural practice was different, i.e. the use of writing and the value attached to it. Even the whole cultural system was different. In the participatory mode of the mythical embedding or its active, creative production one can recognize a continuity and correspondence between acoustic piety and academic styles. They were not seen as opposites. On the contrary, “universal myth” was sought in almost all philosophical/theological systems. These systems provided rationalized frames for synthetic world-designs and symbolic destabilizations of boundaries. 

The literacy thesis as formulated by Angenendt is not applicable to Sanskrit culture. His blueprint disregards cultural contingency. In other

Up to modern times fewer blatant breaks are to be found in scholarly work and scientific reasoning in India than in Europe. A sea-change and simultaneous new beginning in Europe was marked by the Renaissance, which revived the heritage of Antiquity; the “Gutenberg Galaxy,” which eventually allowed the culture of writing to reach the masses; the development of modern sciences that outstripped the scientific cultures of Hindu India and Islamic Arabia that up to that time had been far more advanced; and the European colonialization of the world, which also affected India. And yet a modern, historicizing historiography (which both Angenendt and Goody attribute to literate culture) did not develop either in Europe or in India until the nineteenth century. General literacy (the ability to read) also begins in Europe only in the eighteenth century. This is not to deny the profound effects of the “Renaissance of the twelfth century,” which were largely limited to scholars still dominated by churchmen. It was the time when universities and modern urban culture emerged and when, in addition to the “book as a new tool,” Aristotle was rediscovered and when not only scholasticism flourished, but also medieval mysticism. All this had political, social, ecclesiastical, mental historical and anthropological consequences, as Angenendt and other medievalists have shown. For a historical anthropology, it is not only of interest how (not least in mysticism) modern individuality formed, but also the marked separation of the inner and outer worlds that was then emerging and would later be sealed by Descartes’ Meditationes. 

However, what is equally interesting for a historical anthropology, and particularly revealing for the period of Indian religious history when writing was definitely in use, is the fact that we find rarely a strict separation between mythos and logos, ritual and teaching, inner world and outer world, emotion and intellectuality, word and sound, or supernatural and worldly realities. On the contrary, we frequently find consciously synthetic systems. The sonic life-world itself was promoting this integrative cultural pattern by not separating the voice from the written text. And conversely, probably due to this cultural pattern, the sonic culture never disappeared, but instead was re-modeled over and over again. It is certainly not possible to name only one single cause for the Hindu predilection for holistic world-view constructions and the “identificatory habitus” (Michaels) that some authors found typical of this culture. The predilection has to do with the doctrine of unity of the Upaniṣads and the metaphysical monism that continued more or less

Just how greatly the religious notion of harmony was used, above all since the nineteenth century, not only for efforts at religious reform, but also for the formation of the Indian nation, has been pointed out in a lot of research on neo-Hinduism/neo-Vedānta. Each of these factors mentioned added fuel to the Hindu predilection for holistic world-view constructions and furnished it with many faces, functions and modalities of expression. However, this predilection also had to do with the media situation that reinforced the holistic “habitus” and facilitated interference between different cultural sectors. The life-world centered on words and sound was a collective training ground where at no time did a really fundamental gap ever open up between mythical magical embedding and rational thought systems or literary culture, even if one can identify some historical developments of consciousness toward more abstract thought. Yet logos and mythos were also reunited in new, rational systematic syntheses, and precisely this appears to us to be typical of Hinduism and is possibly a product of the fact that orality and literacy were never separated. Goody (and later Angenendt) did not consider a “third space” of this kind as a media-related, aesthetic and cultural continuum. This points up the necessity of giving more weight to the cultural construction conditions and symbolic orders than the strict literacy proponents did.

In general we can say that a separation of the inner and outer worlds and radical internalization with the Upaniṣads begins as early as the seventh or eighth century before Christ. The changes also correlate with the emergence of a culture of writing and increasing scripturality/literacy in the field of religion. Yet scripturality never extinguished the “viva vox” any more than interiority and emotionalization broke away from the texts or understanding and inner comprehension made external forms of practice obsolete. It is possible to find this in certain groups, principally in monastic communities, but it never developed into a mass culture. On the contrary, even in ascetic traditions total internalization was again followed by a sensualization and materialization in the form of distinctive sound rites and sound contemplation, and the discovery of the inner body and inner sounds. This became effective even in popular culture. However, what determined the popular culture and a major part of actual practice was primarily that the Vedic ritual was replaced by the Hindu pājā and a theistic piety became established that participated in the Vedic sound paradigm and in the pantheistic, non-dual (inaudible) “knowledge” of the Upaniṣads and transferred this world-view onto the great deities. In this “theification” (which was mono-theification as a rule) there simultaneously developed a similarly rich, positively gushing range of different media and ritual culture (religious images, sacred architecture, processions, etc.) as in medieval Christianity. The inner training was pursued in many variations, starting with the Yoga-Sūtras of Patañjali and the various Vedānta schools, and also in the Tantra and the devotional movements. With the possible exception of Śaṁkara and early Yoga, one cannot however speak of any complete break between the outer and inner world due to the inner training. Not only was the Vedic ritual replaced by the sensory pūjā ritual, but also the inner world was ritually opened up and shaped.
