INTRODUCTION 

The *Phenomenology and the Human Sciences* was originally intended to be, and its first volume appeared as, an annual supplement for *The Philosophical Topics.* With the demise of *The Philosophical Topics,* the supplement had to suffer the same fate. It is at this time, that the editors of *Man and World* (as well as Mr. Alexander Schimmelpenninck of the Martinus Nijhoff) very kindly offered to publish the articles that I had solicited for the planned second volume of the *Phenomenology and the Human Sciences* in a special issue of *Man and World.* As the volume goes to press, I must place on record my gratitude to them. To the authors who have borne with me the long delay and uncertainty to which their contributions have been subjected in this process, I owe both gratitude and sincerest apologies. 

These essays, by the very nature of the original project of *Phenomenology and the Human Sciences,* are of varied nature, and are devoted to a variety of themes. They range from interpretation of classical Husserlian phenomenology (Harlan and Rockmore) to deconstructionism in its various aspects (Silverman, Schilrmann, and Shapiro). In between fall two essays on Merleau-Ponty (Casey and Edie), two on hermeneutics (Gadamer and Seebohm) and two on political philosophy (Dauenhauer and Umphrey). 

Rockmore takes up the idea of "crisis," not merely as it appears in Husserl's late famed work, but in all of Husserl's thinking, and isolates a whole series of crisis to which that thinking responds: the crisis of reason as threatened by psychologism, the same crisis deepened by the challenge of history, the crisis of subjectivity, and the crisis of the objective sciences. Apart from providing some commentaries on Husserl's use of "Krisis," Rockmore's account also establishes a certain continuity in Husserl's problematic as against attempts to see in it radical discontinuities. Harlan takes up a different challenge: how to bring together a (Husserlian) theory of mind with the theories of cognitive psychology and AI research. He finds them not only compatible, but well suited for each other - together, they point to what Harlan calls "computational phenomenology." 

Of the two essays on Merleau-Ponty, the one by Casey concentrates on Merleau-Ponty's (and Bergson's) idea of habitual bodily memory. As a part of Casey's ongoing work on memory, this essay, by way of commenting on Merleau-Ponty, develops the idea of memory as an experience of depth in time. Edie describes the conflict between 

*[p. 242]* ^pg6

structuralism and dialectics in Merleau-Ponty's writings between 1949 and 1959, and argues that dialectics eventually prevails over structuralism in so far as Merleau-Ponty comes to emphasize the existential and experiential roots of the structures which structuralism wants to absolutise. 

Gadamer's essay was originally presented as his response to this editor's critique of hermeneutics. True phenomenology, he argues, is not foundationalistic. If it is to remain close to real givenness, it must be based on "participation" in concrete life experiences, and thus would become hermeneutics. Seebohm renews his questioning of modern hermeneutics and argues for a mutual dependence between methodical hermeneutics and hermeneutics as a general theory of pre-methodical understanding, and from this vantage point gained by a study of Dilthey and Boeckh asks Gadamer, if one could reach a consciousness of efficient history as a consciousness of situatedness without passing through a stage of methodically grounded interpretation? 

While Silverman elaborates upon the concept of "logocentrism" and traces the route from Heidegger to Derrida in the process of deconstruction of *logos,* Schurmann shows how with the demise of all "first ultimate representations," i.e. of all putative transcendental sources of justification, normative legislations and their transgression become inseparable. Phenomenology, then, can become a tool for what Schurmann calls "discursive intervention" in order to exhibit the pretensions of all remaining "idols" of transcendental, metaphysical thinking. 

One such is taken up by Shapiro. If the classical phenomenologists viewed the text as an organic unity, as a unified structure, Shapiro suggests the possibility of reading a text in order to *disperse* it. Nietzsche's aphoristic works are studied by Shapiro in order to show how these works frustrate the classical reading of a work as a unified structure. Inseparable from it is a theory of the reader (as a traveller) and a theory ofreading (as interrupted). 

The two remaining essays are devoted to political thought. Umphrey's essay, originally given as a Hannah Arendt memorial lecture at the New School for Social Research, New York, questions the validity of the very idea of political philosophy. Neither as a mode of philosophizing nor as characterizing a subject matter of philosophy is the adjective "political" unproblematic. Without giving a definitive answer to this deep question, Umphrey does however insist upon the need for meta-political, philosophical justification of the concept of politics. 

Dauenhauer's essay is more positive and constructive in its orientation

*[p. 243]* ^pg7

. Distinguishing between the politics of vision (where politics can be a science) and the politics of might (for which, as with Hobbes, politics is the expression of a radical human autonomy), Dauenhauer argues that both leave room for tyranny, and suggests a third conception of politics - one based on hope - which succeeds in making tyranny impossible. The importance of his conception - derived from Merleau-Ponty - lies in the author's subtle formulation of the idea of hope. The paper also develops a fascinating concept of freedom as being essentially relational and of experience as interrogatory. Dauenhauer also argues for the centrality of the concept of hope in both western and oriental thinking, which adds a new dimension to his argument for a "politics of hope."

THE CONCEPT OF CRISIS AND THE UNITY OF HUSSERL'S 

POSITION 

TOM ROCKMORE 

*Fordham University* 

A key problem to be faced in the interpretation of any philosophical position concerns its essential continuity or discontinuity over time. There is, of course, no single way of responding to this concern. One kind of strategy, which in principle seems to me to be nearly as misguided as it is frequently employed, consists in the emphasis on discontinuity. It is this kind of approach which elsewhere in the literature has given rise to interpretative anomalies, in which different positions are allegedly distinguished within a single philosophical corpus, such as Wittgenstein I and Wittgenstein II, early Marx the philosopher and late Marx the economist, Heidegger before and after the *Kehre,* and the four phases of Russell's thought as radically distinct but closely related to his different marriages. 

Although common, an interpretative strategy of this kind is not to be recommended, if only because no philosopher ever begins again to philosophize wholly anew, either in respect to his own position or the previous tradition. It is indeed possible to uncover layers in a given philosophical position in a manner akin to that in which an archeologist uncovers geological strata accumulated through the ages. But a would-be interpreter would be ill advised to regard the phases of a given philosopher's thought as wholly external to each other, as totally devoid of an underlying theme which is stated and restated and further developed at different periods in varying ways. For every philosopher always deepens, transforms, and otherwise reworks his views in the context of present and preceding phases of his thought. And (s)he participates as well, if only through its rejection, in the dialogue carried on in the wider philosophical tradition, which precedes, succeeds, and hence eventually will inevitably include his or her own view as part of the ongoing debate which stretches temporally from pre-Socratic thought into the future. 

*[p. 246]* ^pg9

The problem of continuity or discontinuity in the interpretation of a philosophical position is crucial to the understanding of Husserl's thought. Here it concerns the unity of his position over time as well as its relation to the subsequent phenomenological movement to which his thought gave rise. It is usual to divide the evolution of Husserl's position into a series of conceptual and chronological periods and hence to stress difference as opposed to similarity in the interpretation of conceptual change over time. In the introduction to a recently published collection of papers on the concept of the life world, Elisabeth Stroker, for instance, writes : "Damit sei der Leser noch einmal an jene vielerorte 'Wende' erinnert, die Husserl - nach selbstkritischer Abkehr von fruher erprobten 'cartesischen' Verfahren zum Einsteig in die Kor-relationsproblematik von wissenschaftlicher ObjektiviHit und transzendentaler Subjektivitat - spat noch auf einen neuen Weg in die Phano-menologie gefUhrt hat."! But although there is certainly evolution in the thought of all active thinkers, namely those who are not content merely to repeat in unaltered fashion their earlier ideas-, which is nowhere more clearly the case than for Husserl, one should not therefore overlook the extent to which later developments in his thought are already "contained" or "implied by" its earlier forms, from which they hence follow as a "natural consequence." Indeed, one of the themes which emerges from the different contributions to this volume, written from widely different perspectives, is that the concept of the life-world, found most prominently in Husserl's last, unfinished work is not a sudden innovation. It is rather the result of his preoccupation with similar themes, under both the same and other headings, in published and unpublished manuscripts, dating mainly from the 1920's, and is further consistent with and perhaps already implicit in the original phenomenological breakthrough a generation earlier. Hence, one must be careful in according too much weight to change at the expense of continuity in Husserl's position with respect to its evolution over time if the sense in which it is continuous with respect to its central core, its essence as it were, is not to be obscured. 

A converse difficulty, which arises when continuity is perhaps accorded too much weight, concerns the relation between the positions of Husserl and of his phenomenological successors. It is notorious that even during Husserl's life time, later phenomenology, perhaps most visibly in Heidegger's position, but in others as well, began to diverge in fundamental ways from its Husserlian inspiration. In this sense, the phenomenological movement resembles so many others in the history of philosophy which derive from and claim allegiance to the ideas of a 

*[p. 247]* ^pg10

seminal thinker, but within which the relation of the epigones to the central intellectual figure is often difficult to interpret. As can be seen, for example, in Cassirer's position, certain forms of neo-Kantianism are heavily contaminated with Hegelian elements and hence differ widely from Kant's own thought, despite the continued invocation of his name and, presumably, his authority. This last point is even. more evident in respect to Marxism. It is of fundamental importance, as has become ever more obvious in recent years, to avoid the facile conflation, asserted by Marxists on mainly political grounds, between Marx and his Marxist acolytes. By analogy, if the "phenomenological movement" is to be more than an empty name, it is necessary both to distinguish between Husserl and his followers at the same time as their specific positive relation, in Husserlian language the essence of phenomenology, is identified. 

My intention in noting these problems of interpretation is not to propose their resolution here, even in outline, but rather to situate the present endeavor. My immediate purpose is to call attention to the concept of crisis as indicative of the continuity in Husserl's thought, both early and late and as one dimension of its continuing relation to the post-Husserlian phenomenological movement. Although this concept has received relatively less attention than that of the life-world, it is, I believe, not less important. Nor is its consideration confined exclusively to Husserl's last phase. As Merleau-Ponty has perceptively noted: "Husserl's philosophical endeavor is basically directed to the simultaneous solution of a crisis in philosophy, a crisis in the sciences of man, and a crisis in science as such which we have not yet passed through."2 But although he is aware of the importance of the concept of crisis for Husserl's thought, neither Merleau-Ponty, nor indeed to the best of my knowledge anyone else has so far attempted to study this idea within Husserl's position as a whole or as concerns its relation to later phenomenology. Yet it should immediately be noted that my aim is not to contribute to the phenomenology of crisis as such, although this concept is not less relevant now to our daily life than it was a half century ago. Rather, I shall reflect on the idea in question, from another level, in order to study its central role, both in Husserl's thought, early and late, as well as in that of his phenomenological successors. 

It is perhaps appropriate, despite Hegel's criticism of Kant, to begin with a reference to the nature of the task at hand, which cannot, of course, be resolved either in the short compass available here or through a reductive approach. Certainly one of the considerable merits of Herbert

*[p. 248]* ^pg11

Spiegelberg's monumental history of *The Phenomenological Movement* is that it consciously avoids a reductive response to this kind of concern and seeks instead to provide a detailed description, from the meta phenomenological plane, of the different themes which in fact constitute the phenomenological movement and of Husserl's place within it. And although I am aware of the limits of the present discussion, to the extent that the concept of crisis is, as I believe, a fundamental theme in both the Husserlian and post-Husserlian discussion, it will perhaps be possible to "escape the triviality of a mere common denominator"3 against of which we are warned. 

Spiegelberg's pioneer history of phenomenological thought can aid in attaining methodological clarification of the present task. It does not of course follow that because the topic here is phenomenology itself, that an inquiry of this kind cannot be carried out in a "genuinely phenomenological manner," provided, of course, that agreement can be reached as to the proper interpretation of that controversial phrase. Husserl, as is well known, spent several decades of intense intellectual labor in an ultimately unavailing attempt, as judged by his own rigorous standards, to arrive at a satisfactory formulation of the phenomenological method. It is hence significant that in his insightful discussion of the essential characteristics of that method, Spiegelberg places reduction, which Husserl regarded as the keystone of his phenomenological arch, in sixth place in a list of seven characteristics, of which only the initial three are putatively accepted by all those associated with the phenomenological movement. <sup>4</sup>

It is, to be sure, extremely difficult to specify necessary and sufficient conditions of phenomenology as such. Spiegelberg suggests that the non-reductionist approach, including intuiting, analyzing, and describing particular phenomena can constitute a common program. <sup>5</sup> But he also admits that from a piecemeal perspective phenomenology cannot be considered to display a novel approach, since none of its methodological precepts is unique in the tradition. <sup>6</sup>With this in mind, in the present case, although perhaps arbitrarily, stress will be placed mainly on Spiegelberg's first two criteria, the investigation of particular phenomena and general essences, in other words on careful description, as well as on his seventh criterion, the interpretation of the meaning of the phenomena, since these characteristics seem most useful for the interpretation of the role of the concept of crisis in the thought of Husserl and his epigones. 

If we turn now to the topic, we can immediately note that the term "krisis" can be traced etymologically to the Greek *krisis,* which means 

*[p. 249]* ^pg12

choice or selection, judgment or trial, crisis or critical moment.' It is this latter pair of meanings which is best conserved in modem European languages, in which cognate terms appear, both in the English crisis, which translates Husserl's unusual usage of the Greek transliteration *Krisis,* as well as in the latter's more usual German synonym, *Krise.* Husserl employs the word 'Krisis' in both the latter senses. Speaking broadly, he uses the term to refer to a problem constitutive of one or more areas of human concern, especially, but, as I shall show, not only, as concerns the concept of rationality. I shall now identify and relate together a series of related forms of crisis which occur and recur in the conceptual evolution of Husserl's position over time, as a fundamental and enduring concern. 

The idea of crisis, to be sure, only emerges into full view in Husserl's last, unfinished work, where it assumes its most developed, but still fragmentary form. But it is already present, lurking as it were in the conceptual shadows of his thought, in prior writings, in the attacks on naturalism and historicism in 'Philosophy as Rigorous Science' and still earlier in the lengthy discussion of psychologism in the *Logical Investigations.* 

It is well known that Husserl was doubtly influenced by Kant, both directly and through his study of German neo-Kantianism. In the *Logical Investigations,* Husserl elaborates an initial, phenomenological defense of the concept of reason in quasi-Kantian fashion. <sup>7</sup>In the *Critique of Pure Reason,* Kant distinguishes psychology, which is that domain occupied with the study of the actual operations of the human mind, from logic. He further differentiates three forms of logic, namely as applied or concerned with what in fact occurs, as transcendental or related to the conditions of knowledge whatsoever, and as pure or directed solely to the rule of the pure thought of an object as distinguished from empirical modes of knowledge. 

In a manner similar to and clearly in part influenced by Kant, although by others as well, in his approach to epistemology in the first volume of the *Logical Investigations,* entitled in Kantian fashion *Prolegomena to Pure Logic,* Husserl defends a concept of logic understood as the idea and theory of science in order to found the special sciences and defend the possibility of apodictic knowledge. <sup>8</sup>The relevant difference is that, whereas Kant's basic concern is to demonstrate the a priori possibility of knowledge as such, Husserl's main emphasis in this work is on the refutation of psychologism in all its forms. 

Although Kant had dealt with psychologism in rapid fashion, none other than Cassirer has reminded us that the possible confusion between

*[p. 250]* ^pg13

transcendental and psychological idealism, as it appeared in the Carus/Feder review of the *Critique of Pure Reason,* was the principal reason for Kant's revision of it. <sup>9</sup>Despite his belief that Kant's attempts to deal with this problem were perhaps insufficient, HusserI devoted a major portion of the first volume of the *Logical Investigations* to this difficulty which, as he recognized, threatened the goal of securing the foundations of philosophy, science, and knowledge itself. It is precisely this quasi-Kantian perspective which lies behind his critical review of such writers as Lipps and Mill. For psychologism opens the door to sceptical relativism, all versions of which share an affinity for a conception of knowledge as contingent. But in opposing relativism, Husserl believes he is countering the psychologism from which it derives. 'In our attacks on relativism, we have of course had psychologism in mind. Psychologism in all its subvarieties and individual elaborations is in fact the same as relativism, though not always recognized and expressly allowed to be such.'lo Thus, like Kant, HusserI opposes psychologism in order to shore up the ramparts of the possibility of certain knowledge, a task also contributed to, in his opinion at this time, by Herbart and Leibniz, as well as Lotze, Lange and Bolzano, and in later texts by Descartes and Fichte. 

In subsequent writings, prior to the *Crisis,* Husserl's view of the crisis of reason threatened by psychologism remained substantially the same, although the earlier emphasis is partially modified, for instance in 'Philosophy as Rigorous Science,' by firm opposition to naturalism, of which psychologism now appears as a mere variety, and historicism, including romanticism and *Lebensphilosophie.* But it would be incorrect to construe this change in emphasis as a basic shift in HusserI's position, since his goal is and remains the fulfillment of the age-old Platonic quest for certainty, despite the relative eclipse in this text of the privileged position that logic had earlier been held to enjoy with respect to other sciences. Just as HusserI earlier urges that the proponents of the psychological foundation of knowledge are not genuinely critical with respect to epistemology, so he now suggests a correlative point about modern philosophy's fascination with the as-yet-unrealized idea of rigorous science. Despite the important efforts expended in the modern tradition by such talented individuals as Descartes, Kant, and Fichte, no genuine progress towards the scientific goal has been accomplished and even the goal as such has apparently been abandoned in the contemporary forms of relativism which have arisen on the heels of romanticism's unselfcritical epistemology. 

It would be interpretative folly either to overIook or to minimize the 

*[p. 251]* ^pg14

important alterations which occur in Husserl's position in later writings, such as the *Cartesian Meditations* and the *Formal and Transcendental Logic.* But it would seem that even at this later point, Husserl's conception of the underlying crisis of reason which falls short of, or even rejects the traditional goal of apodicticity, remains substantially unaltered. There is, to be sure, at least one fundamental difference which appears in the former text, since Husserl, for what may be the first time in both his published and unpublished corpus, makes a determined, largely successful effort to understand transcendental phenomenology as the necessary and in fact completed version of the Cartesian philosophy and of the philosophic quest in general. But although there is a sense in which in his later turn towards Descartes Husserl 'moves beyond' Kant, it does not follow that his own position therefore undergoes a radical change. For as Hegel shows in his criticism of the critical philosophy and as Heidegger presupposes in his so-called destruction of the history of ontology, there is in fact an important measure of continuity between Kant and Descartes, on whose position Kant's own philosophy largely depends. An analogous point can also be made in terms of the *Formal and Transcendental Logic,* a text from the same period, in which Husserl's earlier quasi-Kantian view of logic is again emphasized in the style of the *Logical Investigations.* 'Transcendental logic,' Husserl writes, 'is an ultimate theory of science, an ultimate, deepest, and most universal, theory of the principles and norms of all science."11 

A more developed, in part novel concept of crisis, which is still continuous with earlier treatment of this idea, appears in Husserl's last work. The precise relation between this book and Husserl's prior writings is a topic of continuing controversy whose resolution is not necessary for present purposes. But I would like to comment on a least one facet of this problem, as concerns the degree of continuity between this text and Husserl's earlier thought, since this is germane to my remarks on the concept of crisis. 

In the 'Translator's Introduction,' David Carr suggests that there is at this point a fundamental shift in Husserl's position, in particular with respect to the emergence of the problem of history, a claim that others have also madeP This interpretation is in part supported by the important Appendix IX in the translation. 'We must distinguish,' Husserl writes, 'between a broader and narrower concept of self-reflection; pure ego-reflection and reflection upon the whole life of the ego as ego; and reflection in the pregnant sense of inquiring back into the sense or teleological essence of the ego.'13 Now clearly it is 

*[p. 252]* ^pg15

the latter, teleological and historical approach which is so prominent in the *Crisis* as opposed to preceding texts, although it would be wrong therefore to infer that this represents a break in Husserl's thinking. For the distinction in question corresponds to that drawn, in writings prior to the *Crisis,* between pure ego reflection and the life of the ego as ego, which in the *Crisis* becomes the teleological essence of the ego. But these distinctions are, I believe, complementary since, in both cases, the intent - a goal from which Husserl never wavers - is to ground philosophy as a science of absolutely certain knowledge, necessary for the life of man. 

There is an undoubted change in Husserl's understanding of crisis in his last work, where the idea is more prominent and better developed, with easily distinguishable sub-forms. But it should be stressed that the difference to be observed in this text is the result of further working out of an underlying viewpoint, which here undergoes evolution within the framework of a wider, constant perspective. I shall now differentiate various forms of crisis referred to within this text. 

The problem of reason which attempts to ground itself, so prominent in earlier texts, is still present, although the emphasis has been somewhat altered. On the first page, in language which recalls earlier writings, Husserl says: '\[T\] he crisis of a science indicates nothing less than that its genuine scientific character, the whole manner in which it has set its task and developed a methodology for it, has become questionable.'14 In this passage and its continuation, we meet familiar themes concerning the relation of science and method, the contemporary rise of sceptical, non-absolute epistemologies, and the relation of philosophy and psychology. 

The relevant difference concerns the attempt here to relate the crisis of reason to history. Above I referred to the denotative meanings of 'crisis' as constitutive or momentary. These meanings are neither logical disjuncts, nor even exclusive alternatives. Rather, they are largely compatible, as witness Husserl's belief that the crisis of reason was apparently thought by him at this time to have entered a particularly dangerous stage. Here we can note that the *Crisis* was apparently written during the period 1934-1937. Thus the time of its composition coincides roughly with the period immediately following the rise of National Socialism in Germany. This event, which some saw as a possibility for authenticity, was viewed by others, evidently including Husserl, as further deepening the difficulty faced by those who would defend the traditional form of reason. 

As the book develops, Husserl further focusses his attention on 

*[p. 253]* ^pg16

other, particular kinds of crisis. There are at least two senses in which his use of the term concerns his own, personal situation. An initial, obvious sense is provided by his situation at the time the book was written, as an old man, perhaps Germany's most important living philosopher, who suddenly, for 'reasons' relative to his religious heritage, was denied further permission either to teach or write in the country which he had hitherto graced as a central jewel in its intellectual diadem. In this context, the term has a pathetic ring, somehow inadequate to what it is meant to convey. 

A similar comment can be made about the other, personal sense of the word here, in association with the pathos of Husserl's efforts singlehandedly to ground knowledge as such, and thereby to bring to a close one of mankind's greatest, most significant, and most enduring intellectual projects. 'Philosophy as science,' Husserl writes in an of ten-cited passage, "as serious, rigorous, indeed apodictically rigorous, science - *the dream is over.* "15 Now there is a problem here as to whether this statement is meant in a personal sense, or rather as a description of the prevailing *Zeitgeist.* Some have held that Husserl is here attempting to characterize the present age, whereas others have interpreted this passage as indicative of Husserl's conscious despair as the result of his inability to complete satisfactorily the single task to which he has devoted a life of unremitting intellectual toil. But although both interpretations are compatible with the goal animating the entire Husserlian corpus, namely to ground philosophy as a certain science, one should not therefore neglect another, perhaps incompatible, element which surfaces here and which can perhaps best be described in quasi-Kantian language. Instead of the stronger claim that certainty must be constitutive as such, which corresponds closely to the traditional conception of the discipline, Husserl now suggests in this last text, and indeed at times perhaps in earlier writings as well, that although apodicticity must indeed be sought in unyielding fashion, it has the status of a regulative idea which can never in fact be fully attained. Hence, rather than an admission of personal failure or a negative characterization of contemporary culture, it seems perfectly permissible to construe the same passage as an indication of personal satisfaction on Husserl's part that he had now accomplished all that was in the power of any single individual towards the realization in his own time of the philosophical goal of perfect knowledge. 

The personal forms of crisis distinguished here are novel relative to Husserl's earlier writings which, at least in the published material, apparently contain little explicit discussion of the personal dimension. 

*[p. 254]* ^pg17

These fonns of personal crisis further point beyond themselves to the more general problem of subjectivity. HusserI now turns to this problem in a third sense of the tenn "crisis" in which subjectivity itself is in question. Although it might be thought that the problem of subjectivity would arise and be confined within psychology, Husserl is here less interested in this particular science. Rather, he is concerned to show that the problem of subjectivity, from a different perspective, unavoidably emerges in the course of any and all attempts to shore up the foundations of the so-called exact sciences, in particular as concerns their claims to objective knowledge. 

Since the suggestion concerning the positive relation between subjectivity and objective knowledge might appear mistaken, it is worth dwelling on this point for a moment. At least since Descartes, and indeed even earlier, there has been an increasing emphasis, both in philosophy and elsewhere, on the elimination, or at least the irreducible minimalization, of the role played by subjectivity as a necessary condition of the maximalisation of the claims to epistemological objectivity. This tendency, which is already apparent in Plato's view of philosophy as a fonn of dying, is visible in both the Cartesian concept of the cogito and the Kantian view of the transcendental unity of apperception, neither of which ought to be confused with human being, and again in the Husserlian grasp of the phenomenological subject as distinguished from the person who lives within the natural attitude. But, from another perspective, Plato also held - unlike Aristotle and many later thinkers, at least since Augustine - that knowing and doing are inseparable, which in tum implies that pure theory represents the best and indeed indispensable guide to the highest fonn of human life. Thus the insistence on the reduction of the subjective component in objective knowledge is balanced by the equal stress on its relevance for subjectivity. It is in this sense that HusserI writes, here underlining the practical significance of his quasi-Platonic search for theoretical certainty at the same time as he brings out the pragmatic intention implicit in his earlier criticism of relativism: "Skepticism about the possibility of metaphysics, the collapse of the belief in a universal philosophy as the guide for the new man, actually represents a collapse of the belief in 'reason,' understood as the ancients opposed *episteme* to *doxa."16* 

Although Husserl focusses here in a new way on the problem of subjectivity, his general analysis is already at least implicit, if this interpretation is correct, in prior discussion of rigorous science and objective knowledge. The significance of his present, more explicit 

*[p. 255]* ^pg18

concern with subjectivity can perhaps be brought out through comparison with Heidegger's view, against which he may here have been reacting. There is, to be sure, a generally Heideggerian tone to Husserl's language in this text, as in his suggestion that man *(Dasein)* must confront the problem of the meaning *(Sinn)* or lack of meaning *(Sinn-losigkeit)* of human destiny *(Schicksal).* But Husserl's view of the relation of subjectivity to objective knowledge differs basically from Heidegger's. Despite its allegedly radical intent, the attempt in *Being and Time* to go beyond human being to knowledge of Being is basically similar to traditional ontological views. In this respect Husserl, who renews with the Platonic view, which has long been out of fashion that philosophy is inseparable from the good life, is more radical in his concern to renew an impulse which, if not entirely absent in later thought since its origin in the Greek tradition, has often been obscured. But it is only if such pure knowledge is in fact possible that philosophy can fulfill its traditional role, clearly apparent in Husserl's simple statement, which expresses so well his belief that the responsibility of the philosopher to his fellow human beings is constitutive of philosophical activity as such: "In *our* philosophizing, then, - how could we avoid it? - we are *functionaries ofmankind."17* 

The preceding form of crisis concerned the relation between objective knowledge and subjectivity, and raised the problem of the significance of rigorous knowledge for man. Now, in a fourth meaning of the term, Husserl poses the problem of the claim of the special sciences to yield knowledge at all. Unlike other senses of the word "crisis" which have been mentioned so far, and whose treatment in this work is mainly implicit, this final meaning of the term is the acknowledged central theme of the book, as is evident both in the title and in the important discussion of Galilean science. 

The relation of this form of crisis with those already distinguished requires some commentary. The present form of crisis, namely that of objective science, resembles that which immediately precedes it, the crisis of subjectivity. In both cases, Husserl's strategy is to attenuate the subject/object distinction in order to reveal the interrelation of su bjectivity and objectivity. The relevant difference is that the crisis of subjectivity is concerned with the relation of objective knowledge to subjective being, whereas the crisis of objectivity requires a demonstration of the relation of scientific knowledge to everyday experience. But although the present difficulty recalls the quasi-Kantian concern in the *Logical Investigations* to ground mathematics and science, at this point Husserl no longer turns to a quasi-Kantian form of logic, 

*[p. 256]* ^pg19

but towards life. In particular, rather than to provide a fonn of pure logic which can function as the idea of science as such, HusserI provides a brilliant application of the phenomenological method as he understands it, indeed one of the rare extended indications of its proper employment in writings after his phenomenological breakthrough, in a description of how such knowledge relates to ordinary experience. 

The discussion of this problem, which is continued elsewhere in manuscript fonn, especially in the important Appendix VI on "The Origin of Geometry," here centers on Galileo, whose application of mathematics to nature, according to HusserI, alone made modem science possible. It is common knowledge that the concept of rigorous science engaged the Greeks, and this is further a central theme of modem times. But in post-Cartesian thought, this idea undergoes a fundamental transformation, through the application of this science, not to a realm of unchanging reality, putatively located beyond the realm of appearance, in some conceptual nether worId", but to experience itself. "What is new, unprecedented," HusserI writes, "is the conceiving of this idea of rational infinite totality of being with a rational science systematically mastering it. "18 

The problem arises out of the difference in kind between the geometrically precise and descriptively simple, ideal entities which are the content of modem mathematical and physical sciences but never met within experience as such, and the very different kinds of entities, "laden with rough edges" and complicated beyond any simple account, which we in fact encounter in the everyday world. The contribution of Galileo was, HusserI believes, to understand that through a method of approximation the world as experienced could be converted into a simpler, ideal form which pennits description and study with the exact techniques of mathematics and yet retains a link with the experiential world. Otherwise stated, the significance of Galileo's seminal insight is that it made possible the development of the mathematical approach to nature, despite the naivete of Galileo's self-understanding, further characteristic of natural science as a whole. 

Husserl's phenomenological discussion significantly clarifies the claim of modem science to provide objective knowledge. In the first place, if we recall the Platonic distinction between reality and appearance, it follows that modem science is indeed concerned with the real or, in Husserlian terminology, the ideal, and not with the lower realm of appearance, the everyday experiential plane. But, and this is the second point, knowledge gathered on the plane of scientific 

*[p. 257]* ^pg20

reality only has meaning for us because it is the result of an abstractive process, whose original source is frrm1y anchored in the experiential world, from which its objects are derived through a process of mathematical approximation. In this respect, it can be noted, Husserl's view parallels in a significant manner Whitehead's slightly earlier position, in that what he called the "bifurcation of nature" is resolved through a theory of extensive abstraction in which the continuity between the planes of science and life is established through a theory of events linked by the relation of extension, comparable to a nesting set of Chinese boxes. 

So far in this paper, I have been considering forms of crisis, not only in Husserl's last work, but in his position as a whole. My aim throughout has been to demonstrate that, despite the change and development in his thought over time, which should not be minimized or otherwise discounted, there is nonetheless significant continuity which confers upon it the unity of a single position. It would, of course, be incorrect to exaggerate this unity in order to portray it as a seamless web. For Husserl's thought is never given in static or final form, but is always, as it were, underway and thus unfinished. Nevertheless a form of unity, perhaps indeed even an essence, is readily apparent in the belief, to which one mayor may not subscribe, that the subjective and objective forms of crisis distinguished within Husserl's writings are aspects of the crisis constitutive of western rationality, now manifest in an acute form due to the rise of fascism, which has not yet become a rigorous science. 

At the beginning of the discussion, I indicated that it would concern both Husserl's position as such and its relation to post-Husserlian phenomenology, toward which I would now like to cast a brief glance. The concept of crisis, to be sure, is confmed neither to Husserl's writings nor to phenomenology as such. Certainly Marxism, to take merely one example, has long been concerned with social crisis and its implications, and even perhaps with the crisis of rationality under the heading of "idealism." But beyond the general relation created by an interest in Husserl's thought, one of the conceptual ties that binds together the post-Husserlian phenomenological tradition is an interest in forms of crisis. Although the term is rarely and perhaps never employed by Heidegger, certainly a chronic form of crisis is prominent in his understanding of philosophy which, since the pro-Socratic beginning of the tradition, has lost its way. Sartre, on the contrary, is concerned with forms of social crisis, due, in his early writings, to the inability of human beings to achieve an unmediated form of real community which, 

*[p. 258]* ^pg21

in his later thought, can be brought about in mediated form in a revolutionary situation. Suffice it to say that other forms of crisis can be discerned in the positions of such phenomenologists as Jaspers and Merleau-Ponty. 

I would like to conclude by calling attention, albeit in briefest terms, to yet another form of crisis, that in the relation between Husserl and his- intellectual heirs. As early as *Die Idee der Phti'nomenoiogie (1907),* Husserl formed the conviction, from which he never wavered, that the concept of reduction was the keystone of the phenomenological arch. It is, however, common knowledge that, despite the continued influence of Husserl upon later phenomenology, his belief in the importance of reduction has been mainly honored in the breach, and even explicitly rejected by such major phenomenologists as Heidegger, Sartre, and Merleau-Ponty. But although there is hence a crisis within the Husserlian phenomenological tradition, which opposes Husserl to many of the most significant, later representatives of the phenomenological movement, one should not therefore infer that this basic disagreement is indicative of the conceptual demise of Husserlian phenomenology. For although in their zeal for phenomenological orthodoxy some Husserlian acolytes have clearly thought otherwise, a certain irreverence for philosophical dogma, although coupled with profound respect for basic insights, is a necessary element of the philosophical process without which it degenerates into a form of religious devotion. I conclude therefore that a virtue of this last form of crisis, or *remise en question* of Husserlian phenomenology by its closest students is that it indicates the continuing vigor and interest of Husserl's thought, as reflected in its hold upon the contemporary philosophic mind.

TOWARDS A COMPUTATIONAL PHENOMENOLOGY \[1\] 

ROBERT M. HARLAN 

*St. Bonaventure University* 

The past twenty years has witnessed a revolution in psychology, one that may come to be regarded as comparable in scope to the one accomplished by Freud at the turn of the century. This revolution consists of the reemergence of the concept of mind, understood as a functional system responsible for the construction of the behavioral environment within which an organism's behavior takes place, within the explanatory framework of psychology. In contrast to behaviorism, with its insistence that psychological explanations of behavior be based upon laws connecting behavioral responses to specific variables in the stimulus array, psychologists are once again permitted to "look beneath the skin," to posit functions responsible for the transformation of the stimulus array into a behavioral environment, the environment experienced by the organism, in order to explain behavior. Mind, thus, is viewed as an information processor, a functional system that operates on physical stimuli and transforms them into objects that are meaningful for the organism. 

This reemergence of mind can be attributed to two distinct developments, both of which are significant for the shape the information-processing or computational (Fodor, 1975) theory of mind has taken in current cognitive psychology. The first is the Chomskian revolution in linguistics. Chomsky shifted the focus of linguistic research from linguistic performance, the types of sentences produced by speakers on given occasions, to what he called linguistic competence, the set of rules (phonetic, syntactic, semantic) that a language speaker must possess in order to assign meaning to the physical speech tokens. The motive for this shift in focus is easily stated: Human beings are capable of both producing and understanding an infmite number of sentences in their native language, a fact which reflects the creative aspect of human speech. Speaking a language, thus, involves more than simply 

*[p. 262]* ^pg24

learning the appropriate responses to physical speech tokens (Chomsky, 1965). Indeed, even perceived properties of utterances such as stress lack a corresponding physical property (Chomsky, 1972). To account for the strictly infmite number of sentences the speaker is able to produce and understand, thus, we must posit a functional system by means of which meanings are related to physical speech tokens, a system that can be described in terms of a finite set of rules responsible for the generation and interpretation of meaningful speech. The object perceived by the hearer, thus, is the meaning of the sentence 'The dog is in the garden,' an object the formation of which is based upon an interpretation of the physical signal. The laws governing this interpretation are what the linguist attempts to describe. 

The second development responsible for the computational theory of mind is the digital computer (Pylyshyn, 1979). Quite apart from the question of whether or not a properly programmed computer could be developed that functions "just like" a human being, the advent of the computer gave rise to the impetus to so break down human cognitive abilities such as playing chess, solving problems and understanding sentences so that they can be performed by the computer, and performed in such a way that the ascription of intentional predicates to the computer's behavior is both meaningful and justified (Dennett, 1978). For the cognitive psychologist, thus, the computer simulation of human cognition (henceforth AI or artificial intelligence) provides a model in terms of which cognition can be analyzed, an analysis which, of course, must meet constraints both in terms of psychological reality and brain "hardware." Most important, however, AI shows how a system can represent physical imput from an environment in such a way that behavior analogous to that of humans can be produced. 

The approach to cognition that has resulted from these two developments can be formulated in the following way. Instead of commencing with a categorization of types of behavior and attempting to relate these categories to variables in the physical environment, the cognitive psychologist begins with the question, given that any system (computers, human beings, stray dogs) is capable of manifesting specific types of behavior (answering questions, solving problems, recognizing chairs), what type of processing, by which information provided by the environment is represented, connected to knowledge the system already has, and transformed by procedures possessed by the system, is required for the observed behavior to take place. Once such a computational model is developed attempts can be made to test the model in terms of what is known about the physiology, behavior, etc. of the system in question. 

*[p. 263]* ^pg25

While a more detailed presentation of the computational theory of mind (CTM) will be undertaken below, this cursory presentation of it enables a comparison to be made between the CTM and a conception of mind that arose from entirely different considerations, the phenomenological conception of consciousness developed by Edmund Husserl. Husserl argued (1962, sect. 31) that consciousness, the functional system responsible for the world as it is experienced by human subjects, must be understood as a "region of being" that operates in accordance with laws peculiar to it. The functioning of this system is concealed by its results; that is, human awareness is focused on the object of awareness (the intentional object), not the processes responsible for the production (constitution) of that object. To make the functioning of the system itself an object of investigation, thus, a special type of analysis is required. Under the guidance of the object as it is intended by the subject an attempt is made to construct the various operations of consciousness in virtue of which this awareness and its corresponding object have come about. In the mature formulation of his conception of this functional system Husserl (1960, chpt. 4) conceived of consciousness as a system that, at any given time t, consisted of a set of connections (accomplishments) stored in memory that, in accordance with laws governing what he called passive genesis, enabled information provided by the physical environment to be intended (become an object of awareness) as having a specific meaning or sense, a sense that owed its formation precisely to the availability of the accomplishments sedimented in and accessible to the system. 

This presentation of the phenomenological theory of mind (PTM), like that of the computational theory, must be developed below. Nonetheless, the cursory presentation of the two theories enables me to state just what I shall be arguing in this paper and to indicate how my thesis will be supported. My thesis is this: I believe that the development of the phenomenological theory of mind should make use of advancements made in contemporary cognitive science, especially by those working in AI. My reason for this belief stems from the emergence of questions in AI (e.g., how can programs designed to understand natural languages be made context sensitive) that parallel questions that have emerged in the phenomenological tradition. These questions have resulted in the proposal of novel ways of structuring background knowledge to make it accessible to the cognitive tasks being simulated. I am certain that we can learn from these proposals. Further, I submit that not only can the development of the PTM benefit from insights that have emerged in AI but that the best insights

*[p. 264]* ^pg26

of the PTM can be used to guide simulation in AI. 

But are the two theories of mind really compatible? That they are and that similar questions arise for both theories will in part support my thesis. My procedure will be somewhat unorthodox. I will first state what I regard to be the common positions held by the two theories of mind as well as a major difference between them. The difference, I will maintain, should be removed, hence calling for a change of the phenomenological theory. Having established their common positions, I will then tum to a discussion of both theories to support my claim. 

I. What the CTM and PTM share in common 

That the concept of mind that has emerged in cognitive science generally and in AI in particular, especially following Minsky's proposal of the concept of frame for the appropriate means of structuring background knowledge, has important parallels with Husserl's version of the PTM is not a novel thesis. Dreyfus (1981) has pointedly drawn a parallel between Husserl's concept of noema and Minsky's concept of frame and has used criticisms of the former that emerged in the phenomenological tradition as a basis for criticizing the latter concept in AI. I shall not attempt to answer Dreyfus' challenges here, challenges which should be addressed. Rather, I am more interested in establishing precisely what commitments the CTM and PTM share in common and in delimiting the challenge posed by the CTM to the PTM. In so doing I hope to support my claim that the development of the PTM lies in the careful consideration of results obtained in AI. 

The cursory presentation of the two theories of mind in the last section suggests four important commitments they share in common in their approach to the study of mind, especially as the latter plays a causal role in human behavior. The points are these. 

1\. Both conceptions share the belief that the environment which gives rise to behavior is what the old Gestaltists called the behavioral environment, the environment as interpreted (represented, intended) by the subject, not the geographical environment, the physical environment that exists independent of its being an object for a subject. 

2\. The distinction between the behavioral and geographical environment, in tum, points to a system within the subject by means of which the behavioral environment acquires its interpretation (rep-presentation

*[p. 265]* ^pg27

, status as an intentional object) for the subject. 

3\. The functioning of this system is not accessible to the subject. As a result the functioning of the system (e.g., how it represents input from the geographical environment, how background knowledge available to the system is structured and is made accessible to the system in specific cases, etc.) must be inferred on the basis of the differences between the behavioral and geographical environment and on the basis of the accomplishments of the system itself. Thus, the construction of the functioning of the system must be guided by a careful description of the behavioral environment. 

4\. The description of the functioning of the system that results is independent of the descriptions of the physical mechanism in which it is realized. 

I shall not argue for the first two points for they are shared not only by the CTM and PTM but by other conceptions of mind in philosophy and psychology as well. Nor shall I argue for the fourth point: I will accept as decisive the argument of Fodor (1967) that design-functional explanations are both legitimate and are irreducible to (though not incompatible with) physical explanations. What I wish to demonstrate here is the methodological parallel between the CTM and PTM asserted by the third point. This will need some work and will receive attention in my discussion of the two theories of mind. Against many in the phenomenological tradition I must establish the claim that Husserl's construction of the functions responsible for the constitution of intentional objects is not a descriptive enterprise, as though the inner workings of consciousness were accessible to some type of reflection. What I wish to stress for the present, however, is what the CTM and PTM share in common with regard to the way the functional system=mind is to be investigated. 

For the linguist the child's understanding of and subsequent behavior towards the utterance 'The scissors are for cutting' is mediated by his ability to assign the appropriate meaning to the physical token he encounters, an ability that is described by the cyclical application of phonological, syntactic and semantic rules. These rules, in turn, are discovered not by introspection but by examining the language itself and speakers' intuition regarding what sentences are perceived to be grammatical. Likewise, the child who simply pick up a pair of scissors and cuts with them exhibits behavior the explanation of which rests upon his possessing a system capable of assigning a previously learned value to the physical stimulus. To explain this occurrence an 

*[p. 266]* ^pg28

attempt must be made to describe how knowledge is stored by the child and how it is accessed and used in this particular case, a description once again guided by the environment produced by the system and not accessible by reflection to the child himself. In both cases the description of the functional system responsible for the formation of the behavioral environment is independent of the descriptions of the physical transactions that take place in his brain. Rather, the question addressed is simply what representations and procedures must be posited in order to account for the behavioral environment which engenders the observed behavior, independent of the cash value these representations and procedures may have in terms of neurophysiology. 

II. The difference between the CTM and PTM 

If the methodological parallel between the CTM and the PTM is granted, a parallel that I believe can be supported through a detailed analysis of both, there remains a fundamental difference between the two conceptions once we turn to the type of explanation aimed at in the computational and phenomenological theories of mind. 

For Husserl the functional system responsible for the construction of the behavioral environment is irreducibly mental; that is, the fundamental concepts upon which the explanation of human awareness rests, the concepts of apprehension and motivation, are primitives that are not subject to further analysis. Thus, to continue with the example used above, Husserl would argue that the child's having "scissors - for cutting" as his intentional object rests upon his having previously apprehended this connection between the phenomenally presented scissors and their practical function, an apprehension motivated by, e.g., his seeing the scissors being used for this purpose. His having acquired this meaning, in turn, enables the currently perceived scissors which are not now being used in this way to motivate their being intended (apperceived) as "for cutting." If, however, we ask the question, just what is apprehension, how can we explain how the child connects the phenomenally given scissors with their practical function, explanation ceases. For Husser! it is simply that apprehension occurs that is the mark of human intelligence. Further, it is in virtue of these connections between events, objects, properties, etc., all the product of apprehension, that the behavioral environment is constructed. 

The cognitive psychologist, however, is not content to stop at this point, for his goal is not simply to point to but to explain how it is 

*[p. 267]* ^pg29

that phenomena like apprehension come about. The necessity that he do so, moreover, was pointedly formulated by Dennett in his essay "Intentional Systems" (1978): if one argues that the child forms the intentional object "scissors - for cutting" on the basis of his connecting the phenomenal object with the use that was made of it, or that he connected the scissors he currently sees with his previous experience of scissors, the question remains of who or what does the connecting. Since it is the child's intentional object we wish to explain the only alternative is to posit within the child (more precisely, within the child's functional system=mind) a second, smaller child (or, as Husserl did, an ego) who does the connecting. And of course how this smaller child does the connecting is just as much a mystery as how the larger child did it. Dennett's point, thus, is this: the psychological explanation of intelligent behavior cannot appeal to intentional words like "apprehension," "motivation," "recognition," etc., for it is just how "the accomplishments that these words describe take place within the functional system=mind that the psychologist wishes to explain. 

I believe that this argument is decisive and that it necessitates a major modification of the PTM: Phenomenology must become computational phenomenology. In particular the development of the phenomenological conception of mind must abandon the notion that apprehension and motivation are primitives in psychology and must propose models that enable us to explain how new concepts are learned and, once learned, remain accessible to the system and play a causal role to constructing the behavioral environment. 

As will become clear below in my presentation of the CTM, the work now being done that should be considered is in AI. Let me add, however, before considering the PTM and CTM in detail that I do not envision nor am I proposing a passive borrowing from the concepts that have emerged in AI. On the contrary, I believe that some of the best work in the phenomenological tradition (e.g., Heidegger's insistence that the practical concerns of the subject playa decisive role in organizing the behavioral environment, Schutz's study of types of motivation, Gurwitsch's analysis of noematic systems, Merleau-Ponty's analysis of space) can provide the basis for new approaches to the representations of knowledge. Further, this interchange is possible precisely because of the methodological approach to the study of human cognition shared in common by the two conceptions of mind. 

*[p. 268]* ^pg30

III. The PTM and the CTM 

To this point I have attempted to state my main thesis, viz., that the development of the phenomenological theory of mind lies with an Auseinandersetzung with recent developments in cognitive science, especially the work in AI. This exchange, I have argued, demands that the phenomenologist abandon the notion that apprehension and motivation are psychological primitives and that he carefully examine attempts made in AI to simulate concept learning and natural language understanding. To both defend the claim, however, that the PTW and the CTM represent compatible approaches to the study of mind and to indicate precisely how work in AI can be used to model apprehension and motivation, it is necessary to examine both theories of mind in some detail. My presentation of the two conceptions of mind, thus, will be guided by the following question: What role do apprehension and motivation play in the PTM and how are these roles accounted for in the CTM? 

IV. The phenomenological theory of mind 

Hussert's contribution to theoretical reflection on human cognition rests upon his attempt to analyze the intentionality of human awareness, more specifically, with his attempt to specify the nature of the functional system=consciousness that is responsible for the way in which the objects of human awareness are presented to (intended by) subjects. I shall attempt to characterize his mature conception of this system, paying particular attention to the roles played by apprehension and motivation. 

It is a commonplace in both philosophical and psychological reflections on perception, for example, that we are aware of (perceive) more than is actually or evidently given of an object at any given time t. If I walk into an office with an arm load of books and see a table in the comer, I simply go over and place the books upon it, behavior which reveals that the table was presented to me as having the proper-ty "capable of supporting books." Further the table has this significance for me even though there is no direct evidence that it has this property. If we generalize this observation it can be stated that objects in the perceptual field are presented to awareness in accordance with more or less articulated categories (physical object, object having this or that practical use, etc.) that prefigure the way in which further 

*[p. 269]* ^pg31

experience of them will continue. That such prefiguration of the course further experience is involved in ordinary awareness is revealed by the surprise one experiences upon an unexpected occurrence in experience. It is only at this point (e.g., the books placed on the "table" falling to the ground) that the implicit reference to subsequent experience of the objects in the perceptual field becomes explicit (I realize that it was not a table at all, a realization based upon the failure of subsequent experience to conform to the implicit expectations I had upon seeing a "table" in the corner). 

The tradition in both psychology and philosophy against which Hussed proposed his theory of mind had attempted to explain the surplus of information involved in the articulation of the perceptual field by evoking some type of mental operation (quick judgments or associations) that is performed on the truly given in perception, sense data. The reason for this move was straight-forward: Given that, on the one hand, perception does involve a causal interaction between the physical environment and the subject and, on the other, what we are aware of at any given time t goes beyond information that could be provided by this interaction, it was held that the causal interaction provides the subject with sense data, the lowest level of awareness, upon which either inferences can be made or with which associations could be made. 

For reasons that lie beyond the scope of this paper (but see Gurwitsch (1964) for a survey of the conceptual and. empirical grounds supporting Hussed's move) Hussed argued that this approach to human awareness should be reversed. Instead of beginning with a theory of perception (Le., perception = sense data + mental operations) he proposed that we begin with a thorough-going description of the perceptual field as it is given to human awareness and attempt to discern the functional system responsible for this field. Thus, the "given" in perception was no longer taken to be what are in fact unobserved sense data but, in contrast, simply the object as intended by the subject. This object, in turn, became the guiding clue for an analysis of the functional system responsible for its being intended in just the way that it is intended by the subject. 

Hussed's inversion of the traditional approach to perception resulted in what would today be termed an expectation-driven theory of perception. Abstracting from the physical imput of the sensory mechanism (and this is what the "phenomenological reduction" is) and turning instead to the intentional object as presented to the subject, Hussed argued that the awareness of the object could be analyzed in terms of 

*[p. 270]* ^pg32

two, co-constitutive moments, a perceptive and an apperceptive moment (1966, pp. 336ft), both of which correspond to aspects of the intentional object. If we take as my intentional object the "table-ascapable-of-supporting-books," these conceptual distinctions can be illustrated as follows: corresponding to the perceptive moment of awareness would be that part of the table that is actually present to me; corresponding to the apperceptive moment would be all references beyond the actually presented table, its unseen sides, its capability to support books, its being something I can't walk through, etc. It is, of course, these implicitly contained references which I need not be (and for the most part am not) conscious of that account for the prefiguration of further experience mentioned earlier. Equally important, however, is the fact that these references are co-constitutive of the intentional object itself, as the expression "actually present PART of the table" reveals: It is only insofar as these references beyond what is "actually given" have already been established that we can make the distinction between what is actually given and what is merely apperceived. To use language developed by Gurwitsch (1964), the conceptual distinction between the perceptive and apperceptive moment presupposes the prior assignment of a functional character to that given in the perceptive moment by the context in which it is perceived. Thus, there is a reciprocal determination between the actually given and apperceptively given aspects of the intentional object, the former determining the types of apperceptive aspects with which it can be combined, the latter determining the role the former plays in such a combination. 

With this consideration of Husserl's approach to the analysis of perception I can now present his mature conception of consciousness, one arrived at in the '20's especially by his asking questions regarding the functioning of the system below the conscious level (the level of "passive synthesis"). What is interesting is that these are questions that are now the focus of attention in cognitive science. 

The image I (1978) have used to characterize this conception of mind is that of a field. Stored in the system are perceptual patterns characteristic of object types, information about the values of objects (e.g., practical uses), the roles objects play in various contexts, etc. The information stored in the system is the product of experience (Husserl, against Kant, sided with the empiricists here). In particular the information is the product of the way the system organized imput from the environment. 

This organization takes place on two levels, the active and passive 

*[p. 271]* ^pg33

(conscious and non-conscious). Much structuring of environmental imput occurs on the latter level. For example the linking together of the perspectival appearances on one object such that, once linked, the appearance of one side is intended as the appearance of an object (Le., with implicit expectations of further appearances), is the product of passive genesis. Indeed Husserl was forced at this point to appeal to the old Humean notion of association to account for this organization, though the concept was altered to take into account the role the system plays in patterning imput, patterning that makes association possible. Built upon this patterning is organization that takes places on the active or conscious level. For example the learning of the concept "scissors-for-cutting" requires that the connection between the scissors and the use to which they are put be noticed and connected. This type of connection is apprehension, an accomplishment Husser! attributed to the ego. 

My metaphor of a field was selected to characterize the system once this organization has been achieved. At a given time t objects can be intended as having values, properties, relations etc. that go beyond what is immediately given from the environment (e.g., the table is intended as capable of supporting books) thanks to information stored in and accessible to the system. Likewise, how this system functions is not itself something I am conscious of - I see the table as something to put my books on, not the processes that make this intentional object possible. 

It is this interrelation between information stored in the system and the environmental imput in the formation of the intentional object that is captured by the concept of motivation. The table-appearance can be intended as the appearance of a table only if information regarding the patterns characteristic of tables has been stored; i.e., the table-appearance motivates the intentional object "table" in virtue of one's having learned what tables look like. Likewise the child's learning the use of scissors presupposes that he has come to understand the purposive behavior of subjects, the causal properties of objects in the scene, etc. In both cases it is the organization of environmental imput achieved by the passive or non-conscious functioning of the system that is responsible for what is intended. 

With this presentation of the PTM and the role apprehension and motivation play in it I can return to the question that guided the presentation, viz., can developments in cognitive sciene point the way to the development of the PTM. More specifically, have developments within cognitive science provided a means by which apprehension, the establishment of new connections between objects, events, etc., can be 

*[p. 272]* ^pg34

explained without appealing to an homunculus? How does the CTM handle concept-Ie~rning? Secondly, what approaches have emerged in cognitive science to the representation of knowledge, representation that can account for the expectational character of human experience? Can these approaches provide a means of specifying the way in which the functional system = mind stores past cognitive accomplishments and enables environmental imputs to the intended in the way that they are? To answer these questions we must now turn to a presentation of the computational theory of mind. 

V. The computational theory of mind 

In the introductory discussion of this paper I argued that the PTM and the CTM share in common the belief that some system within the subject is responsible for the behavioral environment, the set- of objects, values of and relations between objects and events, etc. experienced by the subject. Thus both approaches attempt to describe the functioning of the system responsible for transforming physical imputs from the geographical environment into the environment experienced by the subject and responsible for eliciting his behavior towards it. At first glance, largely because the terminologies are so radically different, it appears that the descriptions of mind that have resulted in the two traditions share nothing in common: whereas the phenomenologist speaks of apprehension, motivation, active and passive genesis, sedimentation and habitualities, the cognitive scientist speaks of symbols and expressions, data bases, control structures and searches. For those familiar with work in cognitive science generally and AI in particular, however, I trust that my presentation has at least established the points in common shared by both the approach to and questions raised by human cognition between phenomenology and cognitive science. In this section I shall attempt to accomplish the reverse. That is, I hope to show, jargon aside, how work in AI has approached questions of interest to the phenomenologist. In particular I hope to show how the simulation of concept-learning and how knowledge has been represented in AI point the way to developing the phenomenological concepts of apprehension and motivation respectively. 

Like Hussert, who viewed mind as a unique functional system with its own primitives and own mechanisms, so too the computationalist begins with his own conception of mind: the mind must be an information-processor, capable of representing the environment and making calculations

*[p. 273]* ^pg35

based upon this representation. Why this conception of mind is advantageous was best formulated by Fodor (1975). Human beings, when faced with a given need (e.g., to eat lunch, get a point accross, express displeasure at an event), have the ability to decide how best to accomplish it. This decision rests upon their ability to represent certain behavioral options, the consequences arising from each, and the desire ability of each of the several consequences. As a result a psychological theory that aims to account for such behavior must be computational, for decision-making rests upon calculating which behavioral option best meets a given goal. Computation, in turn, requires an ability to represent each of the behavioral options and the consequences of each. 

A means of practically realizing this conception of mind is found in the digital computer. On this view the mind is understood to be a physical symbol system, the symbols and strings of symbols (expressions) providing the means of representing the environment, the various procedures or operations that can be performed on the expression providing the computational ability of the system. 

Two distinct and general questions have emerged for the CTM as it has emerged in AI that run parallel to questions faced by the PTM discussed earlier. The first question concerns the structure and form of the representation of knowledge in the program. Given that the behavioral environment of the program depends both upon imput from the environment and upon the way that this imput is represented by the program, a representation, that we will see, depends upon the way knowledge already stored in memory is structured, what is the best way to organize this background knowledge such that it remains accessible to the demands posed on the program from the environment? This question, I submit, is relevant to the development of Husserl's concept of motivation, for like Husserl the cognitive scientist is interested in determining how background knowledge determines the program's "model of the world" (Winograd, 1976) or behavioral environment. Further, it provides a means of concretely demonstrating how background knowledge shapes physical imput to form Husserl's intentional object. 

The second question raised concerns just how knowledge (new expressions) is added to memory, how previously stored expressions are modified and at what point they must be removed entirely, all in light of new experience. This question clearly corresponds to the one answered by Husserl's concept of apprehension, a concept which, as argued above, should be abandoned as a psychological primitive of the 

*[p. 274]* ^pg36

PTM. I shall discuss these two questions and how they have been addressed in AI separately. 

Since the publication of Minsky's "A Framework for Representing Knowledge" (1975) the focus of research in AI on the structuring of data bases has undergone a shift of emphasis of great importance for phenomenology. The reason why is easy to state. Minsky argued that the aspect of human awareness that had not been captured by previous work in AI was its expectational character. That is, whether one is concerned with ordinary perception or with understanding a story that is read, both reveal fairly strong expectations on the part of the perceiver or reader with regard to what types of things might be encountered. Equally important, these expectations determine the significance of the particular aspect of the scene perceived or the semantic value of the sentence read. As experience or reading continuous his general expectations are filled in, revisions where required are made, etc. The goal of the computer simulation of human cognition, he argued, must develop a way of representing background knowledge that makes the simulation of this expectational aspect of human experience possible. 

The concept he proposed for structuring background knowledge was the frame. A frame is simply a data-base which represents a typical scene (in perception) or scenario (in the case of events). To take an example a house frame contains a list of the parts of a house together with their interrelation. The parts (e.g., living room), in tum, contain pointers to more detailed frames about the part (e.g., what to expect in a living room), and these to even more specific details about the details (e.g., furniture, couches, etc.). 

What is interesting about the concept is this. Like Husserl Minsky argued that the information we are directly given from the environment is always limited and perspectival. As a consequence, to assign meaning (the appropriate representation) to this limited bit of information we must have stored in memory information that not only tells us the role played by this limited bit of information in a larger context (the living room is a certain part of a house, along with others, and has certain uses) but provides fairly strong expectations regarding what types of things will be encountered and what their significance is. 

An important realization of Minsky's concept of frame can be found in the work of Roger Schank (1977). Like Minsky Schank's attempt to model language understanding begins with the premise that understanding rests upon the ability of a system to properly embed 

*[p. 275]* ^pg37

a limited bit of information in a larger context that reveals the significance of that information. He has developed two different strategies for providing this context, scripts and plans. 

In the former background knowledge is structured in accordance with a typical sequence of causally connected events (e.g., the events that occur while at a restaurant). This enables the program to associate imput with a context, thereby enabling the program to determine the significance of the imput. Further, the program has determinate expectations of what types of events are to follow, enabling it to infer that events not stated have occurred and to recognize when something out of the ordinary has gone wrong. Schank's script-based program (SAM), thus, is able both to summarize stories and to answer questions about them. His plan-based program, on the other hand, uses goals that individuals might have together with a sequence of actions that would enable them to realize those goals as a means of connecting events that occur in a story. Like the script-based program, however, the aim is to enable the program to recognize the connections between events, a recognition founded upon background knowledge that enables the program to embed sentences it encounters in a context that determines their significance. 

I t is not the place here to critically analyze either Minsky's theoretical concept of frame or Schank's development of it. What I do wish to establish is simply the importance of this work for the development of the phenomenological concept of motivation. As argued earlier Husser! was correct to insist that it was the interworking of both environmental stimuli and background knowledge that was responsible for the subject's behavioral environment. The work of Minsky and Schank in AI have begun to develop ways of storing and structuring background knowledge to account for this interworking. Equally important, the best work in the phenomenological tradition (e.g., Merleau-Ponty's critique of the geometrical representation of space based upon his description of spatial organization) can be used as a basis for proposing new structures for background knowledge. 

Work in AI that is most relevant to the explanation of how apprehension comes about is found in the area of the simulation of concept-learning. Winston's approach (1975) took concept-learning to be essentially hypothesis-formation and testing, the result being that he devised a program capable of representing a scene, comparing it with a model of a given concept stored in memory, and revising the model depending on whether or not the scene was an example of the pertinent concept. 

Though I believe that concepHeaming is correctly understood as 

*[p. 276]* ^pg38

hypothesis-fonnation and testing, it is clear that Winston's approach is inadequate to account for apprehension in the phenomenologist'S sense on two counts. First, as Dreyfus (1980) has pointed out, Winston was forced to give the program a means of representing scenes, a meqns that in essence "told" the program what aspects of the scene are important for the specific learning task it was assigned. Thus, what is lacking is the simulation of how attention is directed to the relevant aspects of a scene or sequence of events. Secondly, the program had to be infonned of whether or not the example was successful or not, a decision that (at least for anything beyond elementary concept-learning) should rest with the program itself. Thus, his approach could not account for the transition from what Piaget called assimulation to accomodation. Indeed, if we take these two objections together it is understandable why Husserl insisted that the establishment of new connections between objects and events and the revision of previous connections in light of new experience was a primitive function of mind, one accomplished by an ego. 

A way of overcoming these types of objections and of enabling apprehension to be accounted for without appeal to a homnuculus, however, is suggested by Heidegger's classic example of the farmer and the west wind. He noted that the two facts, a change in wind direction and the coming of rain, would be associated only by a subject with a whole set of practical concerns and goals. Thus, it is precisely the fanner's background concerns about getting the crops in that enables him to connect the two events. 

The suggestion I would make, thus, is the following. The answer to the first objection lodged against Winston would be to recognize the role context plays in making a given set of events, objects, etc. relevant to the program. Whether this could be done with Schank's plan-based representational scheme or with one yet to be envisioned is a question I shall leave open. The answer to the second objection, on the other hand, is suggested by Minsky. The ability to recognize the necessity of revising a concept in light of new experience could be handled by the defaults of the frame. If experience reveals that one of the expectations arising from the frame is violated, and yet too much infonnation has already received a tenninal assignment within the frame to abandon the frame, a Winston-type mechanism for revising the nodes of the frame could make the required modifications. 

My point in this paper, however, is not to propose new approaches to either the structuring of data bases or to concept-learning. Rather it is the more modest goal of defending my main thesis that the develment

*[p. 277]* ^pg39

of the phenomenological conception of mind rests with an active exchange with researchers in both cognitive science and AI. This aim, at least, I hope to have accomplished here.

I t was Bergson who first attempted to distinguish "habit memory" from "image memory." By the latter he meant any form of representation of past experience, typically via visualization; it is what we normally term "recollection." Before Bergson made the pointed suggestion that there are at least two fundamental forms of memory, it had been widely assumed by philosophers and psychologists alike that there is only one basic kind of rem em bering, namely, recollecting. This was the case whether recollection is conceived in a transpersonal setting (as by Plato, who made it essential to all eidetic knowledge) or in a strictly personal context (which is how we tend to think of it today). Either way, recollection is considered to be reproductive in operation, proceeding by isomorphism - whether this be an isomorphism between dianoetic diagrams in the soul and the Forms, or between "ideas" that resemble "impressions," or between mind and its own past being. The premise at work throughout this redoubtable tradition is that remembering, if it is to work at all, must replicate past events in an explicitly representational format. These events in turn make up the life history of the individual rememberer (this holds true even for Plato insofar as the history of a given soul includes episodes of viewing the Forms in a previous life). From the standpoint of this premise, it does not matter whether the replicative representations are mental in status (e.g., the notion of "ideas" in Locke, Hume, and Berkeley) or physiological in being (as Descartes was tempted to think and as "trace" theories have presumed from the nineteenth century onwards). These options complement and mirror each other, and they accomplish essentially the same work.
