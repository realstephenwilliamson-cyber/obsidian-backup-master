## Part IV The Same God: Rethinking Hinduism

 Overview 

 In Part IV the argument takes a turn by redefining our lead question. Part III dealt with the problem of Avoda Zara and accordingly focused our attention on the other god. Part IV asks the question of how we might come to regard the God of another religion as the same God. The criteria for defining Avoda Zara and for affirming the same God are not the same and the difference between them will yield different views of Hinduism. Furthermore, once we are able to affirm the same God, a series of theological questions, pointers for dialogue serving the goal of better understanding, emerges. There was no room for these questions as long as the guiding question was that of Avoda Zara. Once the question is redefined, new horizons of inquiry open up. 

 Chapter 11 makes the move from framing the question in terms of Avoda Zara to terms of the “same God.” All the positions encountered in Part III are here reviewed through the new inquisitive lens. The discussion suggests that different criteria apply for each question. Accordingly, a religion could be both Avoda Zara and an approach to the same God. If so, this would certainly neutralize the negative emotional and attitudinal by-products of the declaration of another religion as Avoda Zara. 

 For each of the views surveyed in Part III , it is suggested that it is possible to identify some aspect of recognition of the same God, if not in full, then at least in part. Particularly interesting is the view of Meiri, who might himself be understood as having reframed the question from terms of Avoda Zara to terms of the “same God” question. 

 Affirmation of the same God requires criteria by means of which the affirmation is made. A review of “same God” affirmations in relation to Christianity suggests that belief in creation and faith in various stories and miracles, founded upon the more fundamental common faith in revelation, provide the means for recognizing the same God. In the case of Hinduism we would have to identify criteria that do not depend upon a common revelation. These could include creation. They could also appeal to common metaphysical understanding. Finally, 

*[p. 128]* ^pg134

they could draw on the fruits of the religious life, including morality, spirituality, and mysticism, as ways of indicating an approach to God that may be recognized as fundamentally one and the same as known in Judaism. 

 Chapter 12 builds on the premise that the same God may be affirmed in Judaism and Hinduism. Such affirmation changes the rules of the game in terms of attitudes and of what is made possible in the relationships and interactions between believers of the two faiths. Scorn is replaced by acceptance; rejection and delegitimation are replaced by the possible need to listen to the spiritual testimony of the faithful of another tradition who approach the same God in a different way. This leads us then to consider the particularity of Hindu gods. Rabbinic positions we saw allow us to accept Hinduism by highlighting commonalities, but at the same time ignoring the particularity, the strangeness, the otherness of Hindu faith. But if we affirm belief in the same God, we must also be able to account for what is different. Is the only possible approach one that ignores particularity and specificity of faith or is there a way of making sense of much of the real, lived, expressed faith of Hindus? Put differently, how do we make sense not only of the Hindu God, but also of the Hindu gods. 

 The discussion opens by exploring the possible difference of meaning between two affirmations—“there is no god besides God” and “all gods are God.” The conceptual gap may represent what is at stake in the discussion and suggest why Judaism cannot simply resolve the difficulties it has with multiple gods by subscribing to the latter formula, typical of a Hindu view of God and gods. 

 In order to make sense of the particularity of the gods we need to consider these in their specificity. Here the discussion divides into three. The first part discusses forces of nature, considered divine. Here we encounter one important suggestion, recognized already by Muslim authors, that distinguishes gods from angels, viewing Hindu gods not as “gods” in our sense, be it cosmically, metaphysically, or devotionally, but as equivalent to angels in our worldview. This strategy, combined with several of the strategies identified in Part III , would allow us to consider these gods in a more positive light. 

 The second part discusses the view of humans as gods. While biblical and rabbinic opposition to the view of humans as gods was related to the political dimensions of such designations, the question remains whether we can find an approach that could make sense of such designation, even if it is limited to the spiritual sphere. Several notions are put forth as ways of advancing the discussion. The permissibility of Shituf, discussed earlier, suggests a way of accepting such worship. Nachmanides’s discussion also provides an interesting precedent for acceptable worship of humans. Finally, the notion of the image of God may be put to the service of this inquiry. 

 The third part focuses on all those gods that cannot be subsumed under the first two categories, and these are some of the major Hindu gods. Some work can be done using strategies of abstraction and translation, wherein these gods correspond to principles of philosophical value that can be appreciated across religious traditions. But there are limits to the power of translation, requiring us to suggest another strategy. 

*[p. 129]* ^pg135

 In order to make sense of their particularity and of what seems to us foreign, against the background of a recognition of faith in a common God, I develop here a notion of the religious imagination that seeks to legitimate the workings of the imagination in the religious domain, independently of its truth value. This theory is drawn from the resources of traditional Jewish spiritual literature. What is here attempted is both the formulation of such a theory and its extension from an internal discussion to an externally oriented view of other religions. 

 This theory would have to meet three requirements:

 A. A dissociation of the content of imagination from an “out-there” truth component, so that what is produced by the imagination need not be considered true in a descriptive sense, or correspond to spiritual reality as it is, even though some degree of abstraction and translation into philosophical terms seems unavoidable. 

 B. Recognition of the instrumentality of religious imagination in reaching God and making His presence real, such that it enlivens the spiritual life, producing beneficial fruits. 

 C. Appreciation of the fruits of religious imagination as expressions of a divinely given capacity, grounded in the notion of the image of God. 

 To these discussions of the positive workings of the religious imagination is added, at the end of the chapter, one prominent rabbinical voice, which affirms that Avoda Zara must apply to a created being, thereby upsetting the proper balance of creator and creature. Imaginary beings do not fall within the scope of Avoda Zara. Working with this position, especially when couched in a broader theory of religious imagination, would further remove the concern for Avoda Zara from the particular way in which the Hindu religious imagination presents its gods. 

 Chapter 13 tackles the other thorny question that impacts a Jewish view of Hinduism: the worship of images. As we have seen in Part III , none of the authorities we studied considered image worship to lie at the heart or to provide the definition for Avoda Zara . Once we affirm recognition of the same God, we may also revisit the worship of images and ask how we might understand it. This discussion takes the form of a dialogue, present or future, where different spiritual understandings are given a hearing, and where the potential relevance of the Hindu position receives a hearing, founded on notions of legitimate otherness, supported by faith in the same God, leading us to seek the testimony or spiritual understanding of what informs the worship of idols. 

 Several insights are featured in this section. The first concerns the notion of permanence of the idol and how it is conceived in relation to “God.” Some analogies from Jewish sources make the practice more understandable. One analogy is from the Temple. Thinking of the idol as equivalent to the Temple, a (ultimately temporary) dwelling place, brings the practice within reach of our understanding. A second strategy brings us back to biblical ideas of divine embodiment and notions of a fluid divine body. The fluidity of the divine body, manifesting here 

*[p. 130]* ^pg136

and there, simultaneously, taking on multiple expressions, makes us query the very notion of divine embodiment. This query allows us to revisit notions of embodiment and how they might relate to the embodiment of Hindu deities, both in the abstract and in the concrete idol. Finally, there is room to consider the psychological usefulness and instrumentality of the idol as a stage along the path to a higher recognition, as affirmed by Hindu voices. 

 The problem of divine embodiment and its relation to the absolute has also been of concern to Hindu thought. A study by Rabbi Herzel Hefter seeks to explore the meaning of the prohibition on idolatry in terms of stasis and dynamism. The problem is larger than the worship of images and is relevant to how we approach our own religious values, especially our view of Torah. Therefore, the Hindu concern for avoiding stasis in relation to idols can serve as a lesson for the deeper meaning of what is to be avoided in idol worship. 

 The chapter’s concluding reflection touches on the psychology of idolatry and the religious worldview it inculcates. If built into the worship of images is the recognition that these must ultimately be transcended and the recognition of the relativity of various religious forms, this inculcates a patient and tolerant attitude. The Jewish view and its absolutist approach to the same problem could inculcate the opposite attitude. In pedagogical terms, the questions of religious violence and tolerance are important expressions of the psychological orientation and the price paid for specific religious choices made. We must also consider these, when thinking of the relative values of different religious approaches to God.

### 11 Do Jews and Hindus Worship the Same God?

 Reframing a question is often a very helpful way of moving a discussion forward. Having identified multiple positions concerning what constitutes 

Avoda Zara and having ref lected on how these positions might be relevant to Hinduism, I would like to now approach these same sources from a novel angle, after reframing our lead question. Part III revolved around the question of whether Hinduism is Avoda Zara, mostly based on the precedent of Christianity. This is a classical Jewish formulation, and it grows out of a halachic frame of reference, providing a guideline to practical issues and setting certain attitudes in place. What would happen if the question was redefined from the essentially negative formulation of Avoda Zara to a more positive formulation? In Part IV I would like to revisit the above discussions by recasting them through the following question: Do Jews and Hindus worship the same God? The positions encountered in Part III would provide the building blocks by means of which we might be able to construct new conceptual edifices, around the guiding framework of the “same God.” The “same God” question allows us to seek commonality and recognition, rather than to focus on negativity, which would then have to be limited in practical or theoretical ways. It allows us to identify common ground, which in turn could lead to the sharing of spiritual insight, based on the recognition that we are approaching the “same God.” The “same God” question seems to me a powerful way of moving the discussion forward. It allows us to revisit earlier positions and to identify their potential for advancing theological discussion as well as relationship building. It even allows us to revisit those positions that we would have thought showed no promise in terms of a positive appreciation of Hinduism. Let us then explore how new horizons might open up through a novel theoretical formulation. 

 The “same God” discussion is a novel discussion. <sup>1</sup> The “same God” question is actually the flip side of the foundational biblical perspective, forbidding the worship of other gods. “Other gods” is how the ten commandments refer to gods of other religions. In a polytheistic or henotheistic context, reference to other gods is self-evident, and the issue of “same God” does not even arise. It requires 

*[p. 132]* ^pg138

 further theological evolution, <sup>2</sup> and the clear recognition of a single divine being to raise the question of the same God. <sup>3</sup> 

 A discussion of the “same God” question in relation to Avoda Zara allows us to revisit what is Avoda Zara and how it is construed. In juxtaposing the two perspectives we may discover full or partial overlap between the issue of Avoda Zara and the “same God” question. The “same God” question could conceivably uncover the implicit assumptions of a discussion of Avoda Zara, provide a counterpoint to it or redefine it. It is thus a very helpful heuristic device, by means of which age-old questions of Avoda Zara may be revisited, appreciated anew, and possibly redefined. 

 The title of this section, “Do Jews and Hindus Worship the Same God,” will seem familiar to readers conversant with the Jewish-Christian dialogue. It echoes the opening statement of the famous Jewish statement on Christianity, issued in 2000, according to which Jews and Christians worship the same God. The affirmation is far from trivial and was, indeed, itself subject to further debate. <sup>4</sup> The need to affirm the identity of God as known in Judaism and Christianity grows out of a tradition of considering Christianity as Avoda Zara, but more broadly, out of a long history of competition and tension that required a statement of what the two religions share in common as a way of advancing their relations. The authors felt, correctly in my view, that framing the theological issues in terms of the “same God” question is a way of advancing discussion, as well as of advancing relations. Now, as the authors of the statement are well aware of the history of viewing Christianity as Avoda Zara, there are two ways of understanding their statement. The first is that they have chosen to affirm the position according to which Christianity is not Avoda Zara. If so, their statement would be opposed by all halachic authorities who maintain the view that it is. The alternative would be that even if Christianity is considered Avoda Zara, Jews and Christians worship the same God. This seemingly paradoxical statement could be variously understood. We have noted that the strangeness, which makes Avoda Zara strange or foreign worship, could address either the God or the means of worship. If the strangeness is indeed one of worship, which is executed by means, including theological means of conceiving and approaching the Divine, foreign to Jewish understanding, then the religion may be considered Avoda Zara even if it approaches the same God. If so, something significant is gained by reframing discussion in terms of the “same God” question. Such reframing provides the basis for attitudinal change. Avoda Zara excludes; recognition of the same God unites. Avoda Zara functions as a means of rejecting the other; recognition of the same God limits differences to the ritual or theological realm, while maintaining a core common approach to God. Ultimately, Avoda Zara runs the risk of othering its worshippers to the point of losing sight of the common humanity, even if the consequences of the halachic proclamation of Avoda Zara do not necessarily entail such a position. By contrast, affirmation of a common God goes a long way toward establishing a common humanity and its common and often related approach toward the Divine. 

 The “same God” question hinges to a large extent, certainly in the first instance, on the question of whether a religion is viewed through its ritual expressions or 

*[p. 133]* ^pg139

through its philosophical positions. The question is as much an internal question as it is a question between religions. One way of establishing the fact that all Jews worship the same God is that they share the same rituals. As these rituals point to God, who is their ultimate reference point, this functions as a way of establishing the identity of the God worshipped. By extension, those who do not share in these rituals cannot, till proven otherwise, be assumed to be worshipping the same God. This is then further compounded by the worship of idols and images. If worship is addressed to an idol, and that idol is not recognized by Judaism as God, then the intuitive and instinctive conclusion is that the worship is being directed to a god who is other than God. 

 The positions of the sages of the Middle Ages, especially as articulated in relation to Christianity, all make a view of another religion more complex. Let us then review the positions discussed above in light of the “same God” question. I would like to open with a discussion of Maimonides’ position. Maimonides represents the view that Christianity is Avoda Zara, without any equivocation. It would seem this position leaves little room for recognition of the same God. Yet, upon closer scrutiny things may turn out to be more complex. As already noted, Maimonides does not offer us a clear accounting for why Christianity is considered Avoda Zara. If Maimonides is following a possible talmudic precedent, an unlikely possibility in my view, then we do not really know what it is that makes Christianity Avoda Zara. The decision would be formal and would leave little room for additional reflection. If, however, Maimonides’ working definition, as it emerges both from his historical narrative and from the core definition he offers for Avoda Zara in Chapter 2 , 1 of the Laws of Avoda Zara , is considered, then the problem with Avoda Zara is the worship of a being other than God. This definition would put the same God question right at the heart of the discussion. Thus, Maimonides’ definition of Avoda Zara allows us to approach the discussion not from an analysis of worship, but by tackling directly the question of whether or not one is worshipping the same God. Exploring this question leads us to the realms of intention and of philosophy. We thus return, once again, to the question of the intention of the worshipping person. Once again we ask whether the ultimate criterion for how to view a situation is that of the (Jewish) observer or that of the (non-Jewish) worshipper. While this approach is useful, inasmuch as it builds on Maimonides’ core definition, it is also problematic in some ways. To apply it to Maimonides requires us to assume that Maimonides did not fully understand Christianity, either because he did not live in a Christian milieu, <sup>5</sup> or because his rabbinic culture, both ancient and contemporary, precluded more serious engagement with its theoretical understanding. This approach uses the criteria suggested by Maimonides while assuming these could or should have led to alternative conclusions. Maimonides might not recognize himself in such a presentation. 

 There is another way of considering the Maimonidean view. According to this approach, the “same God” question may be distinguished from the halachic status of Avoda Zara. Even if a religion is considered Avoda Zara, we may still be able to affirm that Judaism and that religion worship the same God. If so, the affirmation of the authors of *Dabru Emet* concerning Christianity may be upheld 

*[p. 134]* ^pg140

even according to a Maimonidean view of Christianity. Several lines of reasoning could lead to this conclusion. The first, already suggested above, would be to consider Avoda Zara a consequence of wrong worship, a false approach to God, while the identity of the God remains the same. While Maimonides’ core definition of Avoda Zara does involve worshipping another being, it may be argued that the images of stone and wood are themselves such an other being. <sup>6</sup> While this may be a simplistic view of the practices of another religion, it would nevertheless provide us with a coherent understanding of why the worship of images is forbidden. It is not simply a mistaken form of approach. The homage paid to images is tantamount to worshipping a being other than God, the physical images themselves. Now, while the worship might be considered as directed to another being, this does not lead to the conclusion that the core understanding of God in another tradition is of another God. We recall that Maimonides’ narrative of Avoda Zara failed to mention the most basic and classical sense of Avoda Zara, namely the worship of another God. All Avoda Zara is the outgrowth of the worship of or the misdirected application of the will of the one true God. If we return to the initial stages of the history of Avoda Zara, the generation of Enosh, the worship of another being—stars and planets—is the heart of the sin, while the understanding of God remains correct. Maimonides affirms in Laws of Avoda Zara 2,1 that the worship of another being, even while acknowledging God’s reality, still constitutes Avoda Zara. If so, it is easy to see how one could be guilty of Avoda Zara, while at the same time holding on to a recognition of the true identity of God. The root of the theological error concerns God’s will, not God’s identity. If so, we may legitimately distinguish the “same God” question from the question of Avoda Zara. Even though Christianity, and by extension many forms of Hinduism, may worship God in ways that are inappropriate, involving the worship of other beings, this does not necessitate the conclusion that the core understanding of who is God is different. <sup>7</sup> 

 Support for distinguishing between the two questions may be brought from Maimonides himself. In a famous responsum, Maimonides permits the teaching of Torah to Christians, while upholding the talmudic prohibition of teaching Torah to non-Jews in relation to Muslims. <sup>8</sup> The reasoning Maimonides offers is that Christians believe in the same revelation as us, and will therefore treat the Torah taught them respectfully, whereas Muslims do not show the same respect to our Scriptures, claiming they have been falsified. Now, the acknowledgment of the validity of revelation is not simply a respectful attitude. It is a theological statement, involving a sense of who God is, the fact that He addresses us and what His revelation consists of. To claim that Christians recognize the same revelation as us and that their recognition is of halachic consequence is also to make an implicit statement concerning the faith that Christians have in the same revealing God. <sup>9</sup> Maimonides’ ruling is thus tantamount to recognition of a common faith in God in Judaism and Christianity. <sup>10</sup> 

 The distinction between the question of Avoda Zara and the “same God” question is of great significance. Distinguishing between these two issues could open a space for recognition and respect for Christianity, even while upholding a variety of practical stringencies arising from the recognition that in technical 

*[p. 135]* ^pg141

and legal terms it is Avoda Zara. The possibility that Maimonides can be enlisted in support of such a distinction is particularly interesting. If Maimonides conditions much of present-day halachic attitudes to Christianity in ways that would seem to be negative and not supportive of respect and tolerance, the recognition that his rulings allow us to draw the distinction between the question of Avoda Zara and the “same God” question is very significant for contemporary relations. Entering the discussion through the “same God” portal significantly narrows down the gap between Maimonides and the halachic authorities we are about to discuss, who consider that it is permissible for non-Jews to worship God alongside other beings. From the perspective of Avoda Zara there is a radical difference between them, with the Maimonidean position leading to rejection of the legitimacy of Christianity. Seen, however, from the perspective of the “same God” question, the gap between these positions may disappear. Both positions may be able to recognize that the God of Judaism and the God of Christianity are one and the same. 

 Let us consider how this line of reasoning might be extended from Christianity to Hinduism. Even if Hinduism were considered Avoda Zara, this does not necessarily preclude recognition of the same God, worshipped differently in both religions. The argument would have to be constructed differently. The argument from common scripture would have to be replaced by a philosophical/theological argument, concerning God’s Being, and the recognition of significant commonalities in terms of metaphysics. In fact, Maimonides is probably as good a candidate as any for establishing such commonalities. Maimonides’ philosophical view of God has recourse to a negative theology, which precludes making positive affirmations concerning a God who is beyond the power of our speech and thought. Maimonides’ entire philosophical oeuvre is geared at harmonizing his own philosophical view with the religious language and practices of a tradition that speaks of an anthropomorphic and anthropopathic God. Structurally, Maimonides is positioned in relation to tradition in much the same way that the great philosophers of the vedantic school are positioned in relation to their own. On the one hand, an apophatic understanding of God is espoused. On the other, a tradition that espouses other dimensions (even if these differ between Judaism and Hinduism) is upheld. The project of reconciling philosophical views with ritual practices and a variety of scriptural expressions is common to Maimonides and the Hindu philosophers. I think it is fair to speculate if Maimonides had had the possibility of encountering philosophers such as Sankara or any of his followers, he would have felt great commonality with their understanding of God. <sup>11</sup> In all likelihood he would have rejected the attempted integration of their philosophical worldview with prevailing cultural and scriptural norms of worship. But the kind of rejection that is founded upon the recognition of a common God is qualitatively very different from the blanket rejection of another religion under the label of Avoda Zara. This is precisely what the distinction between the question of Avoda Zara and the “same God” question seeks to achieve, and I think it is likely that this distinction can be upheld even in relation to Maimonides and his possible views on Hinduism. 

 One must, of course, distinguish between different dimensions of the Hindu notion of God. While Maimonides might feel very comfortable with 

*[p. 136]* ^pg142

the philosophical God, the object of the philosophical and mystical quest of the Upanishads, the Vedanta, and other philosophies, he would surely not be able to recognize divinity in the various Hindu gods, who are said, by that same philosophy, to be manifestations of the one unchanging being. Thus, while we are able to identify a possible area of mutual recognition, it is clear that for Maimonides it is possible at best to speak of a partial recognition of the same God. How significant such partial recognition might be depends on how we view metaphysics in relation to ritual and myth. In other words, to the extent that we privilege the absolute Brahman as the ultimate referent of “God,” the area of possible recognition of the same God broadens. 

 If for Maimonides we have to argue for the possibility of a partial recognition of the “same God,” such partial recognition seems to underlie Nachmanides’ understanding, especially as explicated by the Rashba. According to this position, it is permissible to worship another being, and to recognize it as divine, provided one maintains awareness of the ultimate metaphysical and spiritual reality, according to which God is at the top of the metaphysical pyramid. This position makes some interesting options available. A nation may worship its celestial minister, and for that matter any being other than God may be worshipped, without it being considered Avoda Zara. This creates the possibility that worship may be offered that is not Avoda Zara and that is nevertheless still directed to a god other than our own. This is one of several points where the discrepancy between the status of a religion in terms of Avoda Zara and the answer to the “same God” question emerges. According to this perspective, it may indeed be that Hindus worship various gods who are not the same as our God. Nevertheless, such worship may be legitimate and appropriate for them. While it is not Avoda Zara, it also does not point to the same God. Rather, a higher sense of God is recognized as common to Judaism and Hinduism and it is this dimension that would allow us to recognize the validity of Hinduism for its faithful and to recognize some dimension of a common God. Thus, when Jews and Hindus speak of God they mean both the same thing and something different. When it comes to the higher aspects of God, those that are expounded by philosophy, and where some commonality might be identified, Jews and Hindus may be able to recognize the same God. When it comes to the various forms of worship of gods, who are understood as expressions of divinity, paralleling the *elohim* of which Nachmanides speaks, <sup>12</sup> Jews could no longer recognize the Hindu god/s as their own. This dual perspective is grounded in how God governs the world and is therefore legitimate and not a sign of something having gone awry. <sup>13</sup> 

 What this means is that in approaching the notion of God in Hinduism, from a Nachmanides-based position, one would have to reply with a “yes and no” to the “same God” question. Both the “yes” and the “no” may be legitimate for Hindu believers. However, in terms of the “same God” question, the response would vary according to the religious understanding of a given system, group, or practitioner. If one maintains a position that all worship is directed to the supreme God, who “comes as” individual beings, then the ultimate focus of attention is God the absolute. If so, then the range of religious phenomena that can be incorporated within a the recognizable common ground of “God” broadens. If, on the 

*[p. 137]* ^pg143

other hand, the Hindu believer is aware of the metaphysical gap between God the absolute and the gods of the manifested, created order, then we find ourselves with a position parallel to that of Nachmanides, where the only possible common ground concerns God the absolute. 

 While Nachmanides’ position does allow meaningful recognition of the same God, it does require several moves that may meet with opposition. Moving from the divinely ordained heavenly order, known by earlier generation, to the manifestations of the religious mind, spirit and imagination of India requires the will power to broaden the categories and to extend the structure developed by Nachmanides to another spiritual reality. The nature of God would also be subject to further consideration. Do Hinduism and Judaism speak of the same thing when they refer to God, in His ultimate being? Nachmanides’ God is juxtaposed with celestial ministers, but remains nevertheless deeply implicated in creation and history. While the ministers have relations with the nations, God or, better yet, the God of Israel has a particular relationship with Israel, a relationship that governs Israel’s history. If we look at the biblical image of Israel’s God, the features it shares in common with the Hindu Brahman are minimal at best. The fact that there is a similar structure, wherein lower and higher expressions are recognized as divine, is not sufficient common ground for identifying the Hindu God and the Jewish God. In order to make the case, we would need to enter a philosophical or theosophical discussion regarding the nature of God and to compare Hindu thought, as expressed in works such as the Upanishads and in the vedantic philosophy, with the work of Jewish philosophers and kabbalists. Nachmanides himself is a kabbalist, and therefore his own understanding of the Divine is far more complex than the anthropomorphic image of God that emerges from the biblical narrative upon which he comments. It may therefore be possible to find strong common features between a view of God that Nachmanides would subscribe to and a Hindu view of God. 

 To speak of the same God does not necessitate an identical theology. The kind of legitimate difference between theological positions that is tolerated within Judaism provides sufficient proof for this claim. Differences in theology between a Jewish and a Hindu view of God the absolute may therefore be explored in the way similar differences are explored within Judaism. Such exchanges could be theologically enriching. They could help restore interest in theological discussion and in reflection on God in Judaism. Some of the vibrant and engaging discussions that characterized earlier historical periods have all but disappeared from Judaism’s spiritual horizons. Engaging another religious worldview, especially one that puts God and the quest for God at its center, could be helpful in rediscovering the passion for theological reflection. I believe that even though various theological nuances will emerge, the effort to clarify the relations and differences between a Jewish and a Hindu view of God, in the highest, will lead to the conclusion that both traditions seek to address the same reality. 

 One of the ways in which we can establish that indeed we are speaking of the same reality is to identify key features of that reality. Rather than seeking full theological accord, something that does not exist even within our own tradition, we may seek a statement, a quality, an awareness, or a realization that captures 

*[p. 138]* ^pg144

what we consider definitive of God and locate it in both traditions. This would affirm the identity of God, as known by both traditions, as the same God. This is very much the strategy that Jews have applied to Christianity for hundreds of years. We move at this point from Nachmanides’ perspective to that of proponents of the view that Shituf is permissible for non-Jews. Both Nachmanides and the proponents of the permissibility of Shituf share the common assumption that the standards of Avoda Zara are different for Jews and non-Jews. Accordingly, for both views the answer to the same God question would be “yes and no.” However, certainly in the case of followers of the Shituf view, the “yes” seems to far outweigh the “no.” This may be because Shituf requires worship of the same God, not merely recognition of His existence. It may also be because the position was articulated in relation to a religion that builds upon and extends the common foundations of the biblical view of God. Throughout the history of the development of this position, we find it alongside a complementary strategy, which addresses the “same God” question directly. 

 If we go back to the initial statement of the Tosafot, from which the entire Shituf construct emerged, we note that at its very initial stages the permissibility of Shituf is accompanied by an important “same God” statement. <sup>14</sup> The Tosafot struggle with the possibility that a Christian who is taking an oath may be taking an oath involving Avoda Zara, and that the Jew, through his legal involvement, would be implicated directly or indirectly. In the discussion, the affirmation is made that the “intention is to the Creator of heaven \[and earth\].” This affirmation can be understood in one of two ways, depending on the variant readings. According to one reading, <sup>15</sup> there is no concern about taking an oath in God’s name, even though Jesus is included in the Christian understanding of God, *because* their intention is to God. This is an important reading. Even though the notion of divinity is mistaken and includes Jesus, the ultimate intention of the worshippers is directed to God. We know it is the same God that we worship because He is understood as the creator. Two important lessons emerge from this reading. The first is that the faith of the worshipper is what determines whether He is appealing to Avoda Zara or not. Here, the intention is to God, even though the notion of God includes Jesus. Intention prevails over the theological error of considering Jesus as God. <sup>16</sup> We have struggled earlier with the problem of whether our perspective or the perspective of the believer should determine whether he or she worships God or something else. The Tosafot are an important source in support of the understanding that intention is the ultimate source of meaning. The second lesson is that we have here a way of identifying that we mean the same thing when we say “God.” God’s role as creator functions as an indicator that we are indeed talking about the same God, despite the differences in religion. <sup>17</sup> Creation is only one way of referencing God. If it is deemed the most fundamental common ground for recognizing the same God across traditions, we could equally apply it to Hinduism. <sup>18</sup> While the theory of creation is different and the narrative accounts vary widely, it is legitimate to boil faith down to some broad and abstract proclamation of commonality and to see God the creator as the means of identifying the same God. Upon further reflection, the choice of “Creator” as the identifying attribute of God makes a lot of sense. God’s great 

*[p. 139]* ^pg145

acts, as captured in Scripture, memory, and ritual are creation, the giving of the Torah and the continuing involvement in Israel’s fate, particularly as this finds expression through great acts of deliverance from exile and oppression. Of these, only the first is of universal significance. The relationship that all humanity, and all beings, have to God is by virtue of the fact that He is their creator. Therefore, “Creator” could and should serve as the most fundamental common ground between religions. Indeed, those religions that deny God’s role in creating the world may, by this reasoning, be beyond the pale of recognition. Hinduism is not one of those religions. Thus, one way of establishing the identity of the same God is by appealing to what is deemed most basic about God, and in this case it is the fact that he is the creator, as proclaimed by Judaism and Christianity. 

 The Tosafot’s view should be complemented by reference to another passage of the Tosafot, again referring to Christianity in terms of Avoda Zara. The Tosafot struggle with the fact that talmudic prohibitions on commercial relations with idolaters are not practiced. Several answers are offered. They finally opt for the answer that “concerning the non-Jews who are among us, we know them to not be idolaters.” <sup>19</sup> This is a striking statement. It appeals to a very basic instinct of recognizing what is the religious reality of the other. It implies that one can recognize that the other religion is not worshipping idols or, in this text literally, stars. The “same God” argument is not explicit. However, it seems to me that it informs this statement. Contrary to earlier rabbinic positions that attempted to resolve the conflict between law and custom by seeing the Avoda Zara as mere external gestures, devoid of true religious passion and zeal, <sup>20</sup> what we have here is a statement that affirms our own ability to recognize the other as not worshipping another God or, in other words, as worshipping the same God as us. It seems to me that this statement very much complements the previously quoted position of the Tosafot that recognizes the fundamental intention and approach to the common God, creator of heaven and earth, as more significant and as overriding theological and, possibly other ritual, differences. 

 One must realize that such statements do not yet amount to blanket recognition of Christianity, exonerating it from the charge of Avoda Zara. Had the logic of intention had the upper hand over the theological and ritual dimensions that led to the prevailing attitude that Christianity was Avoda Zara, there would have been a much more principled statement with regard to Christianity’s status as non-Avoda Zara. In fact, Meiri may be understood as someone who affirms overall intention and orientation, in relation to God, thereby recognizing the same God in the God of Judaism and the God of Christianity. Meiri may indeed be the product of a gradual maturation of the Tosafists’ position. <sup>21</sup> But other than this remarkable exception to the rule, most authorities continued to regard Christianity as Avoda Zara despite the striking statements from the Tosafot, quoted before. These statements remained small autonomous arguments that found their place within a broader halachic argumentation that upheld the view of Christianity as Avoda Zara, in accordance with prevailing norms up to that time. <sup>22</sup> If, indeed, we recognize that the Gentiles among us (note: rather than the Gentiles among whom we dwell) are not recognized as worshipping idols, why did this not yield a broader and more principled statement that Christianity was not Avoda Zara? 

*[p. 140]* ^pg146

 As is implied in the previous paragraph, ideas take time to mature. An original intuition expresses an insight that is revolutionary in relation to previous understanding. It does not enter broader Jewish consciousness in a flash. Its import takes time to unfold. Time is needed because of how deeply entrenched the attitude to Christianity as Avoda Zara is. The deep attitude, bolstered by the sight of an alien worship amid an often hostile society, cannot be transformed overnight, simply on account of an important recognition. For this recognition to become definitive, time is required. The process whereby Christianity was affirmed to not be Avoda Zara and the God of Christianity was recognized as the same God we worship is thus a gradual process that grows out of the texts and insights quoted above. The idea is first born as an attempt to justify practice, be it the practice of doing business on Sundays or the need to accept oaths from Christians. The theoretical formulations gradually gain force, as a different image of Christianity as a valid religion emerges. Perhaps more positive relations between Jews and Christians also played a role in the willingness to let these insights flourish into a full-scale recognition of Christianity’s validity for non-Jews. <sup>23</sup> 

 Recognizing the same God, through common religious propositions and faith articles, is a matter of theological choice. Were one to highlight all that is strange, foreign, and unacceptable about Christian faith, one would have to forego recognition of the same God. What leads to affirmation rather than rejection of the “same God” premise is more than the practical, social, and economic needs that mandate recognizing the two religions as serving the same God. Ultimately, it is the product of religious intuition, whereby religious affinity and commonality are recognized and by means of which the identity of God in both traditions is affirmed. <sup>24</sup> 

 The approach to Christianity that is born of the Tosafot continues to develop along the lines of the two arguments mentioned before, the “same God” argument and the permissibility of Shituf. The “same God” argument is noted time and again in later Ashkenazi literature, and its expressions are increasingly robust. While creation forms the basis for the “same God” argument, it does get expanded in later iterations. The famed passage from Rabbi Moshe Rivkes of the seventeenth century makes the point:

 What the talmudic rabbis state, in this matter, was only said with reference to the gentiles who were in their own times, who would worship stars and signs and who did not believe in the Exodus and in the creation of the world. But these non-Jews in whose shadow we, the Israelite nation, dwell and are spread amidst them, they believe in the creation of the world and the Exodus from Egypt and in the principles of faith, and their entire intention is to the maker of heaven and earth . . . We are obligated to pray for their welfare . . . David’s prayer for God to pour his wrath on the nations who do not know Him (ps. 79,6) refers to the non-Jews who do not believe in the creation of the world and the matter of signs and miracles that God did with us in Egypt and in the giving of the Torah. But the nations in whose shadow we live and dwell under their wings, they believe in all these, and we continually pray for the\[ir\] peace. <sup>25</sup> 

 Worshipping God rather than stars and astral bodies is related to other fundamental beliefs. These in turn function as indicators of the identity of the God 

*[p. 141]* ^pg147

in whom one believes. Creation remains the primary defining feature. Creation is posited in contrast with belief in the stars, presumably following a worldview according to which the world is primordial and noncreated. God’s creative power continues to define His relationship with creation. Accordingly, God can accomplish miracles. The Exodus and Sinai are the archetypal miracles. A different reading might be that the universal creation finds its counterpoint in those singular moments of Israel’s particularity in the Exodus and at Sinai. Christians recognize these moments through their biblical faith. This expanded formulation could yield several alternative definitions of how one recognizes the same God. God’s creative power is surely the clearest distinguishing mark of God’s identity. His power to perform miracles, His intervention in history, His revelation—these are all divine identity markers. It may be that belief in a common scripture itself points to the identity of the God in whom one believes. <sup>26</sup> Thus, not only God’s actions but the acceptance of the Scriptures in which His deeds are narrated provide ways of identifying God. 

 What we note is that the “same God” argument become denser. It is important to realize that it is not formulated in an attempt to define the minimal criteria for proper Jewish faith. Unlike a parallel discussion of the principles of faith, where we do indeed find a focused discussion of what are minimal requirements for proper faith, references to other religions and how we recognize commonality of faith with them are not characterized by an attempt to offer precise criteria. Were this the case, Rivkes would be applying stricter, hence more exclusionary, criteria, than the earlier Tosafot. Rather, the sense of theological affinity has deepened. Consequently, more arguments are brought in support of the sense of a common God, making the argument more robust. 

 It is clear there are no binding criteria on what amounts to proof that a member of another religion has faith in the same God. The choice of Creation, Exodus, or common scripture are all secondary moves that affirm a primary intuition, that recognizes theological affinity and commonality. While Creation remains the most powerful and most prevalent argument in the literature, other arguments could in theory be put forth. In theory, mystical experience, moral rectitude, the love of God, and many other features of the religious life could also be appealed to as spiritual and theological commonalities by means of which the identity of God in Judaism and in another religion could be established. Returning to Hinduism, creation remains a powerful point of appeal by means of which Jews and Hindus might support a realization that they worship the same God. But it is not necessarily the only one. Healing, purity, holiness, the spiritual life, miracles, and more can all be elicited in support of a religious commonality that is broad enough to justify the affirmation of a common God, provided the theological will for such an affirmation is there. Thus, a strong sense of approaching the same God leads one to highlight some aspect of the tradition as proof for that claim. 

 Affirmation of a common God does not come at the expense of recognition that Christianity also worships God in ways that are foreign to Judaism. The “same God” argument does not yet resolve the problem of Avoda Zara. A complementary strategy to the “same God” strategy is needed in order to justify and legitimate everything that is different and foreign in Christianity. This is where 

*[p. 142]* ^pg148

the Shituf argument complements the “same God” argument. Perhaps the original intention of the Tosafot was that Shituf did not refer to God, but to saints, while the “same God” argument accounted for the recognition of the common God. If so, Jesus and the Trinity were somehow overlooked, by highlighting intention. The intention is to God. Theological error does not invalidate the fundamental intention, hence Christians worship the same God as we do. The history of the Shituf idea, apparently, could not tolerate such overlooking. It did not seem possible to affirm the same God without making sense of all that is different in the Christian God. Accordingly, Shituf was made to address the Godhead itself. According to this understanding, non-Jews worship the same God as we do, but are permitted to conceive of Him in ways that are mistaken and that lead them to worship other beings either alongside Him or as part of what constitutes their definition of God. In some way, this makes the “same God” argument all the more powerful. Even though we recognize that someone or something else is worshipped alongside or as part of God, still the affirmation of the common God is powerful enough to override these differences, leading one to recognize the validity of Christianity. In other words, what unites us in our faith is more powerful than what divides us. 

 The halachic authorities that are willing to extend Shituf to Hinduism implicitly affirm a recognition of the same God. Such recognition is obviously partial, yet significant enough to afford Hinduism legitimacy for its worshippers and recognition as a valid and normative religious form. What are the grounds upon which such recognition of the same God is made? Given that Jews have spent far less time with Hindus and Hinduism, we cannot expect a paraphrase of the Tosafot, along the lines of “Concerning the Hindus who are among us, we know them to not be idolaters.” Yet some such recognition must underlie the declaration that Hinduism is permissible through the Shituf construct. How then would these decisors justify the implicit “same God” argument? It is likely that the initial application of Shituf to Hinduism, such as we find in the positions of Rabbis Herzog and Steinsaltz, is based on the recognition of a supreme God, beyond manifestation in form. God, thus understood, is close enough to the Jewish notion of God to suggest a same God, and therefore the applicability of the Shituf construct. Associating God the absolute with the acts of creation further strengthens the recognition of the same God. <sup>27</sup> Since there is no common story or common revelation, none of the more specific arguments that have been advanced in relation to Christianity would apply to Hinduism. However, inasmuch as recognition of the same God is established at the intuitive level, before advancing specific arguments, another important dimension may affect recognition of the same God in Judaism and Hinduism. 

 Daniel Sperber’s work is suggestive. <sup>28</sup> Unlike Rabbi Herzog who admits to having little knowledge of Hinduism, except by way of minimal familiarity with ideas, Sperber’s theological will to affirm the validity of Hinduism is formed on impressions created by encounters with Hindus. These have left the unequivocal impression that the religious life, moral character, and spiritual experience expressed in the lives of Hindus are not only valuable, but are model in ways that suggest complementarity and identity with a Jewish recognition of God. The 

*[p. 143]* ^pg149

present Jewish encounter with Hinduism is informed largely by direct contact with practitioners and their spiritual lives. The quest for spirituality and the thirst for religious experience almost privilege firsthand encounter with living individuals and their spiritual lives and experiences over the more theoretical theological statements found in books and systematized in philosophical schools. Thus, in contradistinction to Christianity, where spiritual life and experience play a minor role in affirmation of the same God, in the case of Hinduism here is a major source for such affirmation. <sup>29</sup> Implicit in this approach is a very different way of making the “same God” argument, not based on theological positions but on spirituality and the recognition of the fruit of the spiritual life in the lives of believers. This leads us to the third position of the Jewish Middle Ages, that of Meiri, and how it should be understood within the framework of a “same God” discussion. 

 Let me begin by suggesting how the “same God” question and Avoda Zara should be related to one another, from Meiri’s perspective. One way of presenting Meiri’s views of other religions is to describe him as having moved from framing the broader issues in terms of Avoda Zara to thinking of them in terms of the “same God” question. Systematic application of this perspective yields a systemic revolution in relation to contemporary religions. Meiri is the one and only halachist who resolves the question of Avoda Zara fully and exclusively by using a “same God” strategy. Having recognized that the God of Judaism and of other contemporary religions are one opens the way for him to declare all talmudic issues pertaining to Avoda Zara as no longer valid for contemporary religions. Application of Avoda Zara would seem impossible, once it is recognized that two religions worship the same God. Avoda Zara would thus be understood, following biblical foundations, as worship of another God, rather than as the entry into the domain of otherness, be it the otherness of other people, other religion, or other practices and ways of worship. For Meiri, therefore, there is no question that the God of Christianity and the God of Judaism are one. Details of theology are secondary to the approach to God. The same holds true for variations of ritual practice. 

 In this context it is important to recall that Meiri says nothing whatsoever about the form of worship that religions known to him practice. His sole criteria are the moral life that a religion preaches and its basic notion of God. Meiri’s criteria for a true and valid religion highlight the religious and spiritual life of its believers. Religion is about transforming the human person toward a higher spiritual vision, associated with God. Meiri’s structure drives home an important lesson for the “same God” discussion. There is no God of this and God of that religion. There is only *God* . Therefore, a religion, a lifestyle, or a set of practices either does bring one into relationship with God, or it does not. The core question is thus even more fundamental than the “same God” question—it is simply the “God question.” Once it is recognized that a given religion provides access to God, all other details are secondary. The “same God” question thus resolves the problem of Avoda Zara. 

 Applying the moral test to the question of the identity of the God worshipped in a religion assumes God can be known through the fruits of contact with Him 

*[p. 144]* ^pg150

and with the teachings communicated in His name. Increasingly, contemporary religious thought focuses less on appropriate understanding of God and more on the effects of the religious life upon the human person. Accordingly, we may consider the effects of contact with God upon the human person as indications of the identity of God. The basic moral indicator, posited by Meiri, can be extended. It can include those expressions of moral and spiritual excellence that constitute religious perfection: humility, service, loving-kindness, compassion, etc. It can further be extended to formative experiences of God, as these register within human awareness and as these shape the religious personality. <sup>30</sup> Recognizing God through the traces of contact left in His human relationship partners may serve as an alternative to theological formulations that seek to recognize the nature of God across religious boundaries. The serious implications of such a position are that true and false knowledge of God are no longer the demarcating lines distinguishing between my religion and the religion of the other. Instead, true and false knowledge, true worship and idolatrous appeal to religion, real contact with God and lack thereof, are qualities that are inherent within every religion, as indeed Meiri saw. Validity and value in religion are not simply inherent in the religion, in its doctrine, and in its practices. They are no less a function of the degree to which religion forms a true attachment to God. Jews are as susceptible to moral critique as are non-Jews, and by extension, the moral imperfections of Jews do not invalidate the Jewish religion, or suggest another god, any more than the common moral imperfections of another religion. 

 Seen in this light, there is little to argue against the recognition that Jews and Hindus do indeed worship the same God. As already discussed earlier, Hindus have a moral life, grounded in a religious worldview. Expanding Meiri’s criteria from morality to the traces of spiritual contact with the Divine as experienced in individuals’ lives is perhaps even more relevant to an appreciation of the Hindu sense of God. A major part of the contemporary draw of Hinduism is its message of spirituality and the example and testimony of its saints. If we pursue the line of thinking that grows out of Meiri, we would be entitled to consider these incontestable signs of the presence of God in Hinduism. Those who would argue against them, dismissing them as something else, have already prejudged the issue by applying other criteria and drawing their consequences. For them, if Hinduism is Avoda Zara, its spiritual fruit must be false, accidental, or in some other way divorced from its core practices. A line of reasoning informed by Meiri would argue the opposite. If we are able to find the traces of God’s presence in meaningful ways in the lives of believers and the broader religious culture of Hinduism, this suggests that ultimately it operates with a sense of God that we can recognize. If we can recognize the common fruits of the spiritual life in the lives of Jews and Hindus, the means and methods of worship used by Hindus to attain those ends become secondary to the higher spiritual quest shared by Jews and Hindus. Obviously, not all Hindus are saints and most of them do not necessarily exhibit the fruits of the higher spiritual life. But such is the spiritual life, a life that takes time, process, and patience, and wherein only few attain the goals of the religion, while the many practice their religion in varying shades of perfection. But if a religion can bring one to a real experience of God, one that 

*[p. 145]* ^pg151

can be somehow recognized by its fruits, the God at its center must be recognized as God, the same God known in our tradition. One might accordingly suggest a recasting of Meiri’s definition “who serve divinity *in some way* , even though their faith is far removed from our own faith.” From the perspective of spirituality, one may suggest that as long as God’s knowledge and presence are known in some way, through those special individuals who know Him, even if others have only partial knowledge, that religion too is recognized as turning to the same God. 

 There is an important attitudinal shift that must grow from adopting this position. Recognizing God’s presence and reality in the life and religion of the other constitutes a testimony to God who transcends differences in names of God and in forms of religious life. If the same God is known through another religion then our God is recognized through more than one religion. This requires a different attitude to believers of that religion, or at least to the finest among them. Testimony and listening would then replace derision and dismissal. 

 This lesson is obviously most applicable to a Meiri-based approach to other religions. However, the preceding discussion suggests that new horizons in our view of other religions open up once the concern for Avoda Zara is replaced by the “same-God” question. From this perspective, even the most uncompromising perspective on Avoda Zara in relation to Christianity, that of Maimonides, might still be able to maintain some aspect of positive appreciation and recognition. Meiri is the only authority who answers the “same God” question with a clear yes. All others may be presented as answering with a “yes and no.” Still, a “yes and no” is fundamentally different from the condemning and rejecting rhetoric that often accompanies the view of another religion as Avoda Zara. Thus, regardless of what position one takes on the Avoda Zara question with reference to a particular religion, reframing the discussion in light of the “same God” question amounts to an invitation to soften attitudes, listen in a more appreciative manner, and enter into a dialogue seeking a greater understanding of spiritual and theological common ground.

### 12 From Hindu God to Hindu g ods: Confronting the Particularity
of Hindu Deities

 The “same God” discussion takes as its starting point our own understanding of God and seeks to match it with the religious datum of another religion. 

Accordingly, it identifies aspects in the understanding of God in that tradition that allow us to proclaim God, as recognized in that tradition, as the same as our own God, in full or in part. A large part of the previous discussion hinges on recognizing the one God, known in Judaism, in the various configurations of Hindu religion. What this means is that in some important ways, Hinduism is declared to be, just like Judaism, a monotheism. As statements of contemporary Hindu leaders suggest, reading Hinduism as a monotheism is indeed an option. <sup>1</sup> But it is not so simple to apply a single label to Hinduism, certainly not the “monotheism” label. Some would distinguish between Jewish monotheism and Hindu monism, thereby drawing a distinction between the personal God of the Bible and the more philosophical view of the underlying unity of all reality in Hindu philosophical systems. Philosophically there is, of course, merit to the distinction. I fear, however, that it may not be as helpful to the present discussion as one might think at first sight. Underlying the distinction, as applied to two religions, is the attempt to capture “correctly” what one religion is versus another. Thus, a formula or term is applied to one religion, while another is applied to another. The problem is, as we know, that it is difficult to sum up an entire religion simply under one “ism.” Indeed, if we cannot speak of a single Hinduism or Judaism, how are we to correlate these to the two other “isms”—monotheism and monism. While it may be true that there are prevailing tendencies and characterizations that would support such generalizations, there are ample exceptions to each. Therefore, beyond being suggestive in a broad sense, I do not find the attempt to identify one religion with either of these labels to be all that helpful to the present discussion. 

 The same holds true for the label “polytheism.” One frequently encounters debates as to whether Hinduism is or is not polytheistic. Apologetics, self-understanding, philosophy, and the need to present oneself as similar to or different from others become so entangled in such a discussion as to render it not helpful. 

*[p. 148]* ^pg153

To simply label Judaism as one thing, while labeling Hinduism as another, can be as misleading as it is helpful, both in terms of obtaining a better grasp of the two religions and in terms of the halachic consequences of such labeling. I therefore would like to propose another means of tackling the issue that is usually approached by means of the labels “monotheism” and “polytheism.” 

 Following the work of Jan Assman, I would like to simply present the tension in terms of God and gods. <sup>2</sup> Assman explores the rise of monotheism and in so doing draws important historical distinctions between monotheisms of Ancient Near Eastern cultures—Egyptian, Babylonian, and Greek—and biblical monotheism. I would like to look at three formulations that Assman proposes as a way of introducing the following discussion. Monotheism, suggests Assman, can be summed up under one of three formulations—All gods are one; God is one; there is no god but God. <sup>3</sup> While the second formulation may be ambiguous, it is the third formulation that distinguishes biblical monotheism from religious insights that can be found in ancient cultures. <sup>4</sup> It is a qualitatively different monotheism that affirms there is no god but God and one that recognizes the existence of other gods while affirming their ultimate unity. 

 There are several implications to these varying formulae. For most of the present chapter I would like to consider the claim arising from Assman’s work that the type of monotheism of “All gods are one,” in relation to Hinduism, is incompatible with Judaism, in view of what he calls “the mosaic distinction,” expressed in the claim that there is no god but God. The resources of postbiblical Judaism do, in my view, allow us to revisit this categorical claim. But before doing so, I would like to touch upon another expression of Hindu religious thought that could serve as a bridge between the “same God” discussion and the following discussion of Hindu gods. I would like to turn for a moment to a Hindu theology of religions, seen in the context of a “same God” orientation, as well as from the angle of “all gods are one.” I would like to present here a short prayer of a contemporary swami, one that I believe completely encapsulates a conventional Hindu view of other religions and one that is relevant to our discussion. Chandra Swami practices mainly meditation, with little to no ritual in the life of his ashram. Before meditation a short prayer is recited and this is the only communal act of prayer or worship practiced in his community. The daily evening meditation opens with the following prayer, authored by Swamiji:

 O Lord: Thou art Absolute Existence, Absolute Awareness, Absolute Bliss, Absolute Power, Absolute Life, Absolute Light, Absolute Love. 

 People call thee by different names. 

 They call thee: Ishvara, Krishna, Christ, Hari, Rama, Alla, Shiva, Adonay, Indra, Mother Bhagwati, and so on and so forth. 

 A rose called by any other name still remains a rose. 

 Though called different names by different people, Thou art, O Lord I am sure, One and the same Supreme Being. 

 The one without a second. 

 This prayer encapsulates the most important insight of Hindu theology of religions: in all religions it is the same God who is worshipped, though He is 

*[p. 149]* ^pg154

known by different names. The prayer’s beginning and end offer classical philosophical affirmations concerning God, who is one without a second. As there is only one God, all names must refer to the same being. The list of names, including one of the names of God common in Judaism, suggests that all religions are one in terms of their ultimate referent. Thus, God worshipped in all religions is the same God. 

 Jews would have a hard time subscribing to the affirmation that it is the one and the same God, known as Christ, Krishna, Siva, and the God they worship. <sup>5</sup> Our “same God” discussion allowed us to recognize the ultimate same God as worshipped in Judaism and in Hinduism, either in part or in full, depending on the particular viewpoint adopted. But even this recognition does not allow us to consider the names of God, particularly as these are identified with incarnations or deities, to be one and the same. Differently put: Same God—yes; same gods—no. 

 There remains a meaningful gap between the vedantic perspective captured in this prayer and our efforts at constructing a “same God” argument for God, as known in Judaism and as known in other religions. The main reason why we would have a hard time assuming this view is because it is not God that is recognized as one in all religions, but incarnations, manifestations, and what we might readily relate to as gods, rather than God. Thus, the question is whether such a view is an affirmation of a “same God” position in relation to all religions, or whether it is really a latter-day statement that “all gods are one,” which, as Assman suggests, does not cohere with Jewish monotheistic sensibilities. 

 A comparison is helpful. The ancient world also struggled with the relationship between divine personalities and sought to establish their identity. We possess various texts from antiquity or late antiquity that strike a note similar to the contemporary Hindu prayer. Let me cite one:

 The sons of Ogyges call me Bacchus, Egyptians think me Osiris, Mysians name me Phanaces, Indians regard me as Dionysus, Roman rites make me Liber, The Arab race thinks me Adoneus Lucaniacus the Universal God. <sup>6</sup> 

 Here we find a quest for a sole and supreme divine principle behind the innumerable multitude of specific deities, as part of an earlier ecumenical moment, in late antiquity. As Assman states, “It is part of a general conviction in Late Antiquity regarding the universality of religious truth, the relativity of religious institutions and denominations and the conventionality of Divine names.” <sup>7</sup> Yet, Jews of the period never considered such unifying tendencies sufficient for identification of the various gods with their own God. Chandra Swami’s identification of all gods, Hindu and otherwise, as names of the one supreme being, therefore as one and the same, goes a step beyond where a Jewish “same God” theology can go. The same kind of hesitancy that ancient Jews would have shown toward global identification of all gods as one is likely to govern contemporary 

*[p. 150]* ^pg155

attempts to disregard all difference, in the name of an underlying unifying divine principle. Meiri, who holds the greatest promise for an expansive application of a “same God” theology, speaks of the religions, not of their gods. The argument would therefore have to be constructed and proven, rather than assumed and taken for granted. 

 But Chandra Swami’s prayer does more than assume. It also offers an argument. “A rose called by any other name still remains a rose.” This is an argument from experience. Through all these different religions and forms God is experienced, and it is the direct knowledge of God that allows us to recognize God as one and the same, precisely because there is only one God, without a second. Here “all gods are one” leads to the statement that “God is one,” possibly even to “there is no god but God,” inasmuch as all true religious experience points to the same God, and cannot point to anyone but God. If so, Chandra Swami may have articulated a theology that Meiri might be comfortable with, drawing on the experience of the living God as the ultimate criterion of recognition across religions. Seen in this light, a contemporary Hindu theology of religions allows us to understand more deeply one particular Jewish theology of religions and its possible experiential basis. The distinction between God and gods may, in this reading, be inconsequential, inasmuch as any true encounter with the Divine, whatever it is called, is an encounter with God. 

 Even if such a theology of religions challenges us to think through our criteria for viewing other gods, we cannot simply apply it globally. Neither the criterion nor its successful application in the lives of members of other faiths are sufficiently well-established as to override long-standing consideration of otherness in relation to other gods. We must therefore continue to wrestle with the tension between God and gods, and in particular with the affirmation that there is no god besides God. According to Assman’s reading, Judaism undermines the basis for Hindu religion. How can the commonalities identified in the “same God” discussion serve as a bridge between these religious cultures if the one culture assumes the existence of gods whose very existence Judaism denies? The preceding discussion of the “same God” played down this difference, highlighting only the ways in which Judaism and Hinduism could affirm, in full or in part, their recognition of a common God. It seems, however, we cannot content ourselves with reference to the common God. We must also ask how we are to deal, from a Jewish perspective, with the many gods of the Hindu pantheon. I would therefore like to now address this question, from the vantage point of the various theoretical and theological options surveyed before, as well as of several additional authors. In order to do so I would like to propose a threefold division of Hindu gods. <sup>8</sup> In relation to each I will consider what resources we have for viewing it as less strange, possibly making sense, and consequently beyond the charge of Avoda Zara, for its practitioners. While none of this amounts to Jewish recognition of the truth or validity of belief in any particular deity, it could suggest a way for recognizing the permissibility and legitimacy of such an approach for the Hindu believer. At the very least, it would allow us to consider the particular faith in something that might have been considered as Avoda Zara (were it not for the broader factors discussed in the “same God” section) as expressions of error, or 

*[p. 151]* ^pg156

at least theological difference, without carrying the full weight of condemnation typically associated with Avoda Zara. Hindu gods may thus be contained, “tolerated” if you will, within the range of acceptable theological difference, even if it is conceived as an error that is either permissible or may be overlooked. 

 Hindu gods may be considered as falling within one of three categories:

 A. Gods that can be identified with or associated with forces or aspects of nature. 

 B. Holy men and women, past or present, who are recognized and worshipped as god. 

 C. Gods and goddesses who are neither. While some of these are smaller, local gods, the most important Hindu deities fall under this category. 

 Let us now consider how our previous discussions allow us to look at each of these categories. 

 A. The first category in this discussion is forces of nature that are considered divine. One example are great rivers, such as the Ganges, that are worshipped as expressions of the Divine. Ancient Vedic religion, the earliest form of Hinduism, gave worship to various forces of nature, that were considered as gods—fire, wind, the sun, etc. This attitude remains part of present-day Hinduism. Judaism is often presented as the alternative to the worship of forces of nature. <sup>9</sup> What is ultimately under discussion is the nature of God, the cosmos, and religious action. According to this view, faith in Israel’s God assumes a break with the forces of nature and a stark division between creator and creation. While this understanding may be considered representative for most of the history of Jewish thought, understandings that collapse the distinction and that find God within creation do abound, especially in the mystical tradition. In terms of Hinduism, we have already considered the belief in God as pervading all of creation. There is, however, another way of considering the worship of forces of nature than through the understanding of the supposedly equal and undistinguished divine presence in all creation. 

 Natural forces are worshipped as deities, the Hindu term being *devas* . Already in the Middle Ages, Muslim thinkers who reflected on Hinduism translated devas, using the term “angels,” rather than “gods.” <sup>10</sup> The term assumes a populated heaven, in which created beings, who are empowered in relation to nature and to various aspects of life, dwell. These citizens of the heavenly realms are powerful and elicit worship, but they are still created beings, not to be confused with the most absolute power. In considering these many gods from a Jewish viewpoint we are better off considering them as the equivalents of angels. How would that impact our understanding of this class of divinities, from a Jewish perspective? 

 It seems that the question under discussion is not one of cosmology or metaphysics. A Hindu view of where gods fit in the broader cosmic view could be translated to Jewish categories. The fundamental question is whether such beings may be worshipped or approached in prayer. Here we can revisit our earlier discussions. At least two of the approaches we studied would allow us to accommodate such worship. We recall that Rabbi Herzog raised the possibility that 

*[p. 152]* ^pg157

Hindu worship, like Christian worship, may be legitimated as an expression of permissible Shituf. The terminological distinction between Brahman and *devas* , a distinction that is lost by appeal to the common term God or gods, suggests clear awareness of the metaphysical distinction between these categories, paralleling the emphasis placed in Jewish sources on the distinction between creator and creation. Worship of an aspect of creation, along with the creator, is precisely what Shituf achieves. If one further considers the “pantheistic” perspective, wherein the created being is not worshipped on its own, but rather as an expression of the absolute, the case for recognizing or legitimating such worship for the Hindu practitioner becomes even stronger. 

 The worship of forces of nature allows us to also revisit Nachmanides’ perspective and its relevance to our discussion. As discussed, Nachmanides grounds the permissibility of worshipping angels, stars, and other forces of nature in the cosmic structure, known to worshippers, in accordance with a divine governance and allotment. One recognizes that the application of Nachmanides to another religion, rather than to worshippers of yore, may go beyond his original intention. <sup>11</sup> But were one to go along with such an application of the structure of his thought, we would have a basis for accepting, or understanding, how various aspects of nature, both visible and invisible (angels), can be worshipped, within a philosophical or theological frame that recognizes the supremacy of God the creator. 

 Meiri does not provide us with any resources that would be particular to the worship of natural forces. His perspective is conditioned by Christianity and Islam and the issue never arises for him. In this instance, constructing an argument on the basis of Meiri involves the greater extension of authorial intention, yet it does not seem to be impossible to contain within his conceptual framework. If morality and some recognition of the true reality of the Divine are the fundamental requirements, details of a faith system may be dismissed as having only secondary importance. To argue that a pantheistic understanding of God falls within the range of acceptable error does not seem to violate Meiri’s thought structure. The same goes for the argument that God is worshipped along with or through parts of His creation. What would count for Meiri is that some correct notion of God is maintained, a requirement that Hinduism would surely meet. 

 Once we recognize that many Hindu gods can be approached or understood as angels, additional factors should be brought into the conversation. Judaism has been reluctant to address prayer to angels, considering that prayer and worship are due to God alone. Offering prayer or worship to any being besides God is tantamount to acknowledging the divinity of that being. <sup>12</sup> But this official line has many deviations. We find prayers to angels in all periods of Jewish thought. The more mystically minded groups, such as the Hechalot mystics and later kabbalists, are not as careful in maintaining this distinction as one might commonly think. When prayer is offered to angels, one must assume that this is justified by appeal to an understanding of the Divine that accommodates it, and by the awareness of the praying person that both distinguishes such prayer from prayer to “God,” properly speaking, and integrates this prayer act in the broader approach to God. It seems to me that a similar attunement of conscisousness characterizes the 

*[p. 153]* ^pg158

Hindu worshipper of natural forces. Certainly, for the educated Hindu, a worshipful approach to a natural force, being, or minister would be integrated within a broader spiritual understanding that does not compromise knowledge of the absolute God. The analogy from instances of Jewish prayer to angels would make legitimation of such practices possible even for those who consider that non-Jews must follow the same rules as Jews, when it comes to Avoda Zara. <sup>13</sup> 

 I would like to illustrate this latter claim with reference to one particular instance of worship of a natural force, the river Ganges, considered by Hindus to be divine. I have reflected on these issues while watching contemporary practitioners, including some well-known religious teachers, offer worship to the river Ganges. One particular teacher who comes to mind in this case is Swami Chidananda (Muniji) of Rishikesh. His ashram, located on the banks of the Ganges, offers a spectacular worship of Ganges that is shown on Indian television daily. It is an elaborate affair consisting of singing, chanting, children’s choirs, and the traditional waving of lights and sending light floats down the river. This teacher certainly follows the vedantic understanding. Visiting him and talking to him makes it hard to consider that one is in the presence of a simple-minded, pagan worshipper of nature. He offers the kind of sophisticated take on Hinduism that is often taught in the West. Indeed, he is much traveled and has a large Western following. Clearly, his is not a primitive view of nature and its forces. Precisely for this reason he has no problem worshipping the river or, more correctly stated: worshipping God as manifest through the river Ganges. Another swami with whom I am friendly, who lives on the banks of the Ganges, and who is one among the foremost exponent of the unitive system of Vedanta, enjoys a most intimate relationship with the river and its spiritual reality. It is a focus of devotion and source of spiritual nourishment, blessing, and personal identification. It almost seems as though Mother Ganges is his primary means of approaching the Divine. For this learned sage, appreciation, reverence, and worship of the river do not conflict with and are even expressions of a monistic worldview. Worship of the river and, by inference, of other forces of nature can thus be spiritually and metaphysically sophisticated, very different from the typical caricature of primitive nature worship that is often presented as part of Judaism’s view of other religions. 

 B. Let me move on to the second class of gods worshipped—humans and, primarily, spiritual teachers. The subject has already come up in the course of our discussion. Before revisiting some theoretical possibilities that arise out of the discussions of various rabbinic authorities, we should recall why this form of worship is particularly problematic and why it touches so deeply upon Jewish sensibilities. Historically, the Jewish confrontation with Christianity does put the worship of a human being at its center. This is certainly one factor by means of which to account for the deep animus against human worship. Going further back in time, we recall rabbinic objection to emperor worship, how identity-forming it was and what place it occupied in Judaism, and for that matter also in Christianity, during the early centuries of the common era. But the roots may be even deeper. Several biblical passages indicate the association of monarchy, in other words the political order, with idolatry or paganism, or however we may chose to describe that to which Jewish religion is opposed. Ezekiel’s prophecy 

*[p. 154]* ^pg159

against Pharaoh is a strong case in point. Ezekiel: “Thus said the Lord, I am going to deal with you, O Pharaoh king of Egypt, mighty monster, sprawling in your channels, who said, my Nile is my own; I made it for myself.” <sup>14</sup> The point to be mindful about is the association of power, political power, and idolatry or the recognition of another god. As Jan Assman notes, in both major cultures surrounding Israel, Babylon and Egypt, the monarchy is grounded in the cosmic order, itself the subject of the religious order of that culture. <sup>15</sup> Consequently, the rise of monotheism, that is the particular brand of monotheism characteristic of Israel’s faith, is nothing less than an act of liberation—a liberation from the forces of nature as well as from the oppression of the political order. <sup>16</sup> If so, the depth of opposition to worship of humans cannot be divorced from its political context. It is as much a political as a spiritual act. 

 Pagan philosophers have offered a defense of ancient paganism that could have in theory been accommodated by Jewish believers. While monotheisms are different, as Assman argues, it is not beyond the realm of the imaginable to consider that Jewish monotheism could have recognized the various forms of worship of gods as cohering within a broader pattern of faith that has strong resemblances to Israel’s faith. Yet, no attempt was made to understand those religions on their own terms. In today’s terms we might say there was no room for dialogue with them. While such a precedent is interesting, it does not control the history of Jewish approach to other religions, nor need it limit the range of theological options that we might consider in approaching a religion like Hinduism. Today’s attempt to understand is grounded in the broader social, political, and diplomatic circumstances that invite, almost mandate, revisiting attitudes to other religions. Not only did earlier periods not harbor under similar premises, the circumstances for interacting with other religions precluded such theoretical reflection or openness. If religion and power go hand in hand and if affirming the theoretical possibility of some degree of legitimacy in another religion has political implications, there is little leeway for such affirmation. <sup>17</sup> 

 Openness to another religion and affirming its legitimacy for its practitioners requires theological will. It is interesting to consider the gradual emergence of the permissibility of the worship of Jesus for Christians as an option in viewing Christianity, as it developed over the course of hundreds of years of contact with Christianity. To begin, it is worth recalling a fact already alluded to, namely that the earliest strands of rabbinic literature rarely if ever contain the charge of idolatry in relation to Christianity. This charge arose over the course of time. <sup>18</sup> One wonders, then, to what extent does the view of Christianity as idolatry represent gradual theological development within Christianity or the gradual awareness of Christian doctrine by Jews. But another possibility exists as well. It may be that only by association with empire does Christian faith become problematic in specific ways, reminiscent of pagan culture and following its examples, in outward worship of images, as well as in conceptualization of Christ in terms of empire and empowerment of the ruling order. 

 Despite the eventual setting in of the view of Christianity as Avoda Zara, some authorities found a way of accommodating it and declaring it legitimate for its worshippers. I submit that despite obvious reservations relating to the 

*[p. 155]* ^pg160

worship of a human person, it was possible to develop constructs that legitimated Christianity precisely because the figure of Jesus was not a political figure. It was neither a living figure nor an explicitly political figure, even if it provided authority and a comprehensive worldview that undergirded contemporary power structures. Thus, the affirmation of the possible acceptability of the worship of Jesus by Christians, or at the very least reducing the negative associations it produces through several halachic strategies, allows us to revisit attitudes to worshipping humans, once the political dimension has been neutralized or excluded. 

 Issues of power and theological will are particularly important when it comes to a contemporary appreciation of Hinduism. In terms of its religious structure, many of its customs and, above all, in terms of how the many gods are reconciled with the one God, there are striking similarities between Hinduism and Ancient Near Eastern religions. It is as though there were continuity between these religious cultures that occupied geographic regions not too far from one another. It is somewhat odd to be constructing theological arguments for the recognition of Hinduism as valid for its practitioners against the background of the total rejection of a similar religious worldview by Israel’s prophets and by earlier Jewish cultures that made no attempt to accommodate or recognize surrounding religious cultures. It is only by appeal to circumstances and power structures that one can entertain the present exercise. If yesteryear’s power dynamics necessitated rejection and segregation, today’s global dynamics invite a different response. If the religious cultures that were encountered in the past ruled Israel and competed with it, we are now in a situation of encountering a new religious culture, under radically different circumstances. Political and power concerns are cast aside in what is essentially a spiritual and metaphysical encounter, devoid of the range of political associations that shadowed earlier encounters. And, most important for the present discussion: if earlier cultures tackled the problem of the worship of humans in relation to power authorities and political figures, Hinduism places the same challenge before us, but in relation to spiritual figures and as part of a vision of the spiritual life. The completely different context therefore allows us to consider whether a different response might be possible to this challenge. Can a response that was good for its time make way for a different consideration, suitable for the particular circumstances of the present encounter? In what follows I shall revisit some of the earlier discussions, with an eye to applying them to the challenge of how to address the worship or worshipful attitude offered to great teachers and spiritual beings. <sup>19</sup> 

 As already discussed, there is a significant difference between worshipping a live person and the memory of a person who has ascended to heaven. While many Indian teachers are revered and worshipped in their lives, in the long run all important teachers are worshipped posthumously. Even the great incarnations of Rama and Krishna are usually understood as historical figures, thereby bringing them within the context of the present discussion. If so, whatever Jewish precedent we possess in relation to accepting Christianity as valid for Christians, despite or along with its worship of Jesus, could be extended to various Hindu teachers, incarnations and avataras. While recognizing that the internal Hindu logic that allows affirmation of their divinity is more profound than Shituf, 

*[p. 156]* ^pg161

appealing to an understanding of manifestation of divinity as or through a person, Shituf does afford us at a minimum a Jewish category by means of which to make sense of the phenomenon, without compromising Jewish faith. 

 Meiri’s affirmation of Christianity as a valid religion does not specifically refer to faith in Jesus and the incarnation. For present purposes, Meiri is both more and less helpful than the Tosafists. He is more helpful in the sense that details of belief do not seem to matter, once a baseline of proper faith and morals has been established. Whether non-Jews err one way or another, and whether we disagree with one belief or another, is really inconsequential. What counts is the quality of the moral and spiritual life and the possibility of reaching God. This is far-reaching in its potential application, but it is also less helpful to making sense of how exactly Meiri would view incarnations and the worship of humans. One supposes he would just prefer to ignore them. 

 By contrast, Nachmanides does provide an interesting resource for revisiting this question. Let us recall how he describes the second class of worshippers of stars and astral bodies. Nachmanides writes:

 Now, these are the people who began making the many forms of graven images *,* *asheirim* and sun images. They would make the forms of the astrological signs in the hours of their strength, according to their rank, and they would empower the people, so they understood, with force and success. . . . Closely related to this kind of Avoda Zara was the worship of human beings. When people of a country saw that a certain individual, such as Nebuchadnezar—had great power and that his star was very much in the ascendancy, they thought that by accepting his worship upon themselves and directing their intentionality towards him, their sign would rise with his. 

 As suggested in our discussion earlier, the acts here described are coherent and rely on a valid view of the power of astrological signs and on correct observation of the impact of the planetary powers on groups and individuals. The astrological theory that Nachmanides propounds is based on a correct observation of the governance of nations and individuals. However, the legitimacy of such worship can be disputed. In relation to the first category brought by Nachmanides, the worship of the Separate Intelligences, Nachmanides affirms that the worshippers did not forget the God on high, who is source of all power. He makes no similar assertion in relation to the second category, thereby suggesting that it may involve forgetting the full cosmic view, where God is the source of all ultimate power. This accords with his description, later in this passage, of Sennacherib and Pharaoh, who are worshipped as gods in light of this astrological understanding, as “wicked, but not absolute fools.” It would seem that the claim for their divinity, while based on correct astrological foundations, is nevertheless an expression of wickedness, since appropriating the title “god” leads to a forgetfulness of the one true God. <sup>20</sup> This leads one to question whether had such worship not led to claims of divinity and had it been undertaken with awareness of the ultimate power of God, perhaps they would have not been considered wicked. In that case, approaching them as divinities may have been no different than approaching higher beings in that manner, provided one maintained awareness of God. 

*[p. 157]* ^pg162

 From a Nachmanides-based perspective, how would we deal with the worship of individuals whose function is to lead one to knowledge of God, rather than to obscure it? How different would his attitude be if the cases under discussion were not political leaders but spiritual teachers? But more importantly, Nachmanides describes worship of humans as an attempt to connect to something else through them, their good fortune, obtained by association with an astrological force. It is clear to the worshipper, according to this scheme, that the person being worshipped is not God, and the worship is purely instrumental to the worshipper’s own purposes. How different this is from an approach that worships God within the human, based on complete faith that the Divine is manifesting through the person. The intentionality is so radically different, both based on the motivation of worship and on how the relationship between the worshipped person and God is conceived. <sup>21</sup> This twofold intentionality sets Hindu practices distinctly apart from the wicked precedent described by Nachmanides. If Nachmanides’ model could allow for acceptable worship of humans, within the framework of the religious life allotted for non-Jews, these distinctions could make all the difference. 

 One further note on Nachmanides in relation to Meiri. According to the suggested application of Nachmanides’ thought, worship of spiritual teachers may not be considered Avoda Zara. This thought matrix could be superimposed on Meiri’s thought. We were unable to account for how Meiri would view specifically the worship of Jesus and the worship of teachers in general. Reading Meiri in light of Nachmanides could provide an answer that would make sense for Meiri too. Because their intention is toward God, and does not eclipse awareness of the Divine, it is possible to accommodate the worship of human beings within the broader framework of the approach to God, recognized as legitimate for other religions. 

 A concluding thought may be offered in this context. One of the most underdeveloped theological resources for addressing this question is the notion of the image of God. I have suggested that the relatively minor role it plays in rabbinic thought is a means of limiting its application to non-Jews. <sup>22</sup> Yair Lorberbaum, in turn, ascribes to this notion a very central place in his view of rabbinic thought and legislation. <sup>23</sup> The notion of the image of God is developed not only in Jewish thought but in Christian thought as well. We have here an important site for potential enrichment through a theological exchange. For some Christian authors, the image of God contains the full potential of the human person and this potential goes to the extremes of the divinization of the human person. <sup>24</sup> Jewish reflection has largely ignored the universality of the concept and has, on the whole, not extended its reflection that far, given its own reticence to think in terms of divinization or related expressions of actualizing human potential. A fruitful dialogue could ensue in view of instances of the attainment of high degrees of spiritual life and how these could be understood in light of the notion of the image of God. <sup>25</sup> Seeing such actualization in terms of the image of God does not resolve the halachic difficulties associated with offering worship to a human person. <sup>26</sup> But it does offer a conceptual tool for making sense of it within a Jewish framework as well. Thus, rather than simply dealing with the challenge in terms of the permissible or the nonpermissible or attempting to otherwise 

*[p. 158]* ^pg163

justify it as we seek to make sense of other religions, the phenomenon of offering worship to great teachers and spiritual beings can open up a dialogue around what it means to be fully human and how far the divine potential, expressed through the notion of humanity’s creation in God’s image, may be extended. 

 C. On Hindu gods, great and minor. We are able to make sense of nature forces and their view as divine, because we share the same natural world, and can understand how natural forces can be viewed as divine. We can understand why holy men and women and great teachers are considered divine, because we too have holy men. There is commonality of experience and reality, and this commonality allows us to suggest ways of understanding and, consequently, of accommodating Hindu worship of such gods. Underlying the exercises above is a sense of common experience that aids in understanding. Let us briefly dwell upon such commonality and how it aids in understanding through what has been called “translatability.” 

 We noted above an emerging trend of talking of the “same God” in discussions that take place across religions. A parallel discussion has been taking place, in close proximity in time. This discussion speaks not of the “same God” but of the translatability of gods. Jan Assman may be credited with launching this discussion in his *Moses the Egyptian,* and the discussion has continued with various publications, notably Mark Smith’s *God in Translation.* <sup>27</sup> That these two academic conversations take place with no reference to one another is in part due to the different periods under discussion. The “same God” discussion is contemporary, while the translatability discussion refers to ancient religions, Egyptian, Babylonian, Greek, and the like. <sup>28</sup> But there is perhaps a more fundamental distinction between the two conversations. The “same God” discussion refers to religions that understand themselves as monotheistic and therefore should, on the face of it, subscribe to belief in one and the same God. That the issue is complicated in discussions, and not only with reference to Christian belief in the Trinity, may be a sign of how academics complicate discussions as much as of possible inherent complexities in the subject matter. <sup>29</sup> The “translatability” discussion, by contrast, is a discussion that relates to polytheistic religions, and in particular to gods associated with natural forces. <sup>30</sup> Ancient lists of gods suggest that with reference to natural phenomena one can equate the gods of one nation’s pantheon with those of another, as they address the same phenomenon. Reality is the same; it is known by different names. Thus, translatability may be recognized as a feature of reference to gods, in their variety and diversity. <sup>31</sup> I cite this discussion as a way of introducing what I find as the common denominator of the third class of Hindu gods, from the Jewish perspective—the difficulty in translating them to something immediately recognizable within Judaism. 

 In this third group of gods we find many gods, possibly the majority of known Hindu gods. Many of these are small local gods. With reference to these it is extremely hard to translate them to any category that has meaning. Their names, stories, images, and worship are all completely foreign and therefore resonate with the fundamental strangeness of Avoda Zara. A measure of interpretive generosity will lead to viewing them as a Hindu’s version or equivalent of an angel. If so, these deities may be dismissed as not really significant for a Jewish 

*[p. 159]* ^pg164

consideration of Hinduism, any more than they are for an internal Hindu view of gods great and small. Seen as angels, their worship could be tolerated in terms of Shituf or in light of Meiri’s approach to other religions. What would matter with reference to the worship of smaller local gods are not the answers to questions relating to the god and his or her biography as much as questions pertaining to how this tradition relates to the great tradition. Do followers of this tradition share in the broader philosophical understanding of the tradition? Is there a tradition of learning that balances the actual worship of local deities? Who speaks for this tradition and what kind of Hinduism does it represent, both in geographic terms and in terms of greater and lesser traditions? The more localized the tradition, the more detached it is from superstructures of metaphysical meaning, indeed from a linguistic tradition that characterizes learning, primarily the Sanskritic tradition, <sup>32</sup> the harder it is to relate to it in ways that distance it from Avoda Zara. <sup>33</sup> 

 Vedantins might offer a reading of all forms of religion in light of their understanding of the absolute in relation to particular manifestations of the Divine. But adopting this view as an internal Jewish view requires a lot of theological will and would require a more detailed analysis of the life of faith of a given group or practitioner. To the extent that a vedantic view of the smaller, local gods is superimposed on practices, and is not corroborated by self-understanding and a broader philosophical view, it is hard to adopt the vedantin’s perspective for a Jewish view of these practices. 

 The most significant challenge relates not to smaller localized deities, but to the great gods of the Hindu tradition, due to their popularity and the position they occupy in the overall economy of Hindu religious life. In all discussions of Hindu religion in terms of Avoda Zara, and of Hindu faith as presented to a Jewish audience, I have not found any attempt to account for or to explain the particularity of Hindu faith in those gods that are the most popular across India. The number is not huge: Siva and some of his offspring; some, though not all, the incarnations of Vishnu, primarily Krishna and Rama, as well as popular figures from the epic associated with the latter, like Hanuman, the monkey-god; and the mother, Devi, in various manifestations, independently or as spouse to the great gods. One particular deity that seems to enjoy almost universal popularity is Ganesha, with human body, elephant head, and a genealogy that sees him as Siva’s son and provides the story of how he got his elephant head. <sup>34</sup> That these great gods include gods who fit into the category discussed previously—humans who are worshipped—confirms that the present discussion and its taxonomy are carried out from a Jewish perspective, which does not correspond to internal Hindu categorization. <sup>35</sup> 

 With reference to all these gods one might apply the perspective that they are worshipped while at the same time recognizing and affirming a supreme being, Brahman, beyond. Their worship could therefore be dismissed as secondary and ultimately insignificant from Meiri’s perspective, or as yet a further expression of Shituf. Formally, this move may be legitimated, thereby allowing the great entities of Judaism and Hinduism to come to a better understanding, as demonstrated in such public settings as summits of religious leaders. Philosophically minded practitioners are mindful of the fact that all gods, including the great gods, are 

*[p. 160]* ^pg165

part of the created order and therefore relative, subject to eventual demise and reabsorption into the preexistent state, or even expressions of a cosmic illusion or dream, *maya.* Yet, the prominence of some of these gods in the lives of believers—with some gods playing a more important role in the lives of some communities, and others in the lives of others—gives pause for thought. Does the move that subsumes the better part, for many practitioners— *all* —of the religious life into a higher metaphysical perspective that usually eludes believers and is often unknown to them really do justice to Hindu religious reality? Can a presentation of Hinduism that ignores the concreteness of the personalities and biographies of deities, their worship, and the role they play in the daily lives of the faithful do justice to the religion? Is it really sufficient for purposes of dialogue and recognition to subsume all gods Hindu under the elevated umbrella of a philosophy that sees them as secondary to the metaphysical ground of being? To me, this move, characteristic especially of the vedantic philosophy, is formally sufficient in terms of diplomatic recognition, but ultimately unsatisfactory, in terms of accounting for the day-to-day faith in Hindu gods and goddesses. Differently put: we come here to a point where the question is not only *who* speaks for Hinduism, but also *what* speaks for Hinduism? How much do the great gods speak for and represent Hinduism? 

 The “same God” discussion appealed to the supreme being as what might provide a basis for the recognition of the same God. If we look to the gods of Hinduism we must identify another strategy for thinking of them within a Jewish consideration of Hinduism. The most obvious strategy is translation, but, as Assman has taught us, the uniqueness of Judaism and consequent monotheisms is the untranslatability of gods into the Jewish system. Moreover, Hindu gods are where otherness is most pronounced. The issue is more fundamental than images and their worship or the names that we do not recognize. The problem is that they are presented as personalities and stories are told of them. The entire domain of myth and popular stories associated with the gods would seem to lie beyond the range of translation, hence of recognition. One could, at best, affirm part of the faith of Hindus, faith in the absolute, as either identical or close to Jewish faith or as providing theological shelter for the other half—the belief in the actual gods. But the gods themselves would seem to be beyond any recognition. Unless, that is, we can identify a mechanism of translation. 

 Translation is not only a need for making sense of gods across religions. It is also an internal need. The stuff of the gods are stories and myths, some of which also translate into rituals. Making sense of these stories is an internal concern. As part of making sense of them, one often comes across techniques of abstraction. Abstraction is a form of translation. The story and indeed the very identity and function of a god is translated to a more abstract level. Thus, Brahma is creator, Vishnu is preserver, Siva is pure consciousness, and Ganesha is swiftness and agility in the divine realm. Of particular importance is the abstraction of the powers associated with the mother with reference to creative processes and their energetic counterpart. 

 Such techniques of abstraction and translation provide a means of interpretation that allows one to consider what these Hindu gods mean, or could mean, 

*[p. 161]* ^pg166

beyond their biographies, images, names, and rituals. The relationship between abstraction and the mythical and biographical is of necessity complex. The one does not necessarily replace the other but exists alongside it. Its importance varies, accordingly, from one interpreter to another. Considering the great gods as *principles* , rather than as *personalities* , certainly facilitates conversations across religions. It allows one to relate to aspects of the faith in specific gods, rather than others, as opposed to ignoring the gods altogether in favor of a higher spiritual principle. Just how successful such translation and recognition tactics could be remains a subject for future thought. Translating images of the great mother to a kabbalistic system seems intuitively to make more sense than translating the figure of Ganesha into something recognizable within Judaism. <sup>36</sup> 

 Abstraction is not the only possible way of dealing with the gods. The worship of the important gods does not conform to the simple hierarchical view of vedantic philosophy, wherein the great absolute is worshipped beyond and through the many deities, including the most important and celebrated ones. Within the religious life of Hinduism, the major gods have become the focus of sects and schools that structure the view of the spiritual life around them. Siva for important schools, like Saiva Siddhanta, is the God supreme, and not a created being, subordinate to a higher source of origin. For schools like Gaudiya Vaisnavism, Krishna is the supreme being, and not simply an avatar of another deity, Vishnu, himself only an aspect of the created order. For these schools, faith in God is identified with a specific form, a series of stories and myths and a related philosophical worldview that makes sense of all these. There is great variety between schools of thought, corresponding to choice of supreme deity. While all share in the recognition of an absolute reality that transcends this world and even the created order, how it is understood and imagined varies from school to school. With this variation we recognize the complexity of Hindu religious life and the challenges it presents to a Jewish view of Hinduism, challenges that cannot be glossed over with a simple blanket application of a single principle. Moreover, the strangeness or otherness of the great gods does not disappear even if we recognize in them expressions of an absolute reality, identify them with higher states of consciousness or even affirm the positive impact they have in the lives of believers, thereby allowing ourselves to ignore these gods. Hindu gods may be approached as part of broader philosophical systems; they may be explained through abstraction and translation mechanisms. But something of their concrete expression, in its otherness, continues to haunt our attempt to understand them. It may be here that ritual and image have the greatest impact. We cannot translate any Hindu god into complete philosophical abstraction because he or she is always associated not only with specific stories but also with concrete iconography, images, and pictorial and ritual representation. If philosophy leads us to an abstraction that facilitates recognition, ritual reminds us of the particularity and the otherness of gods. If philosophy takes us to the realm of the mind, story and ritual take us to the realm of the imagination. Thus, even if we are willing to go along with the theologically generous view of Hindu gods as expressions of metaphysical principles that we can recognize, we cannot escape the encounter with the human imagination and the central role it plays in approaching the Divine. It 

*[p. 162]* ^pg167

seems to me that ultimately we cannot escape tackling the imaginative aspects associated with Hindu gods. If we do not wish to look away from the reality and the concreteness of the most central aspect of the religious life of India, we must attempt a theory of religious imagination. 

 When religious discourse is governed by notions of truth and falsehood, it is very difficult to make room for a positive appreciation of the imagination within a view of another religion. <sup>37</sup> One cannot fully convert or capture the value and meaning of mythical and pictorial representation of gods into abstract and philosophical terms that can be translated across traditions. That which remains specific, the core of otherness of a religion and its god or gods, is therefore judged as false, not meeting the standards of truth, philosophical or otherwise, known in the religion conducting the view of the other. Accordingly, when we view an image of a god, hear the story of his or her biography and deeds, and more broadly encounter this dimension of the faith of the other, we have little choice but to dismiss it as a phantom of a people’s imagination, and as such phantasmagoric, ultimately false. Almost instinctively, we approach the images of another religion in terms of how faithfully they represent or correspond to a celestial or heavenly reality, and accordingly judge them to be false. But as Diana Eck writes: “The images of the gods are not ‘likenesses’ of any earthly form. They are fantastic forms, with multiple heads and arms, with blue, green, or vermilion coloring, or with part-animal bodies. They are not intended to ‘represent’ earthly realities, but rather to present Divine realities. They stretch the human imagination toward the Divine by juxtaposing earthly realities in an unearthly way.” <sup>38</sup> This, then, presents before us other challenges and invites us to develop a theory of religious imagination, as it applies to other religions. 

 In what follows, I shall suggest how such a line of reasoning may unfold, drawing on internal Jewish discussions of the role of the imagination and considering whether these might be applied to other religions. Clearly, such an extension requires theological will and generosity, and these in turn are motivated by the attempt to carry the recognition of the “same God,” based on the various strategies that make it possible, to a more particular consideration of how this “same God” relates to the otherness of the individual god and his or her personality. 

 There is no accepted Jewish theory of religious imagination. Neither the Bible nor rabbinic literature engages in such a discussion. Even when gods of other religions are judged as false, there is no appeal to the human imagination as part of that judgment. Judgment of the imagination as inferior, misleading, and opposed to truth is largely a product of the Jewish philosophical tradition, which is, in turn, indebted to the Greek philosophical tradition. If anything, the wealth of images of the Divine found in the foundational corpora challenges us to account for how richly God is imagined and how to relate the imaginative expressions of living faith and experience to a more philosophical view of God. Briefly put, the challenge is how to reconcile an official view of God as being beyond form and beyond imagination, let alone beyond the possibility of representation, with the rich data suggesting that religious experience, particularly prophetic and mystical experience, imaged and imagined God. <sup>39</sup> This tension could have led to a broader theory of religious imagination. However, efforts at neutralizing 

*[p. 163]* ^pg168

anthropomorphism were mainly linguistic and did not attempt to resolve the tension by constructing a theory of religious imagination. Attention to the imaginative power occurs mainly in reflection on prophecy and mystical experience. Resources for a broader theory of religious imagination are therefore to be found mainly in the realm of mystical experience, where vision and imagination are not only part of a literary heritage that must be accommodated, but part of ongoing experience. While recognizing the obvious divide between internal religious experience, at its most profound and intimate, and the view of other religions, what bridges these extremes could be a common theory of religious imagination. The appeal to texts from the mystical tradition will be undertaken in the interest of extracting from them such a theory. 

 Biblical and rabbinic tradition do not shy away from representing God in words. If other religions present a challenge, and are usually condemned, for representing God in physical objects—images, statues etc., Jewish sources present an analogous challenge in their liberal verbal representation of God. One of the earliest statements that addresses this challenge is a poem that has come to us from the mystical circle of German pietists, active in the twelfth century. The hymn called Song of Glory is still recited in synagogues to this very day, at the end of the Shabbat service. The hymn tackles the challenge of multiple representations of God, especially as portrayed in rabbinic literature. It states:

 I have not seen Thee, yet I tell Thy praise, Nor known Thee, yet I image forth Thy ways For by Thy seers’ and servants’ mystic speech, Thou dids Thy sov’ran splendor darkly teach And from the granduer of Thy work they drew the measure of Thy inner greatness, too. 

 They told of Thee, but not as Thou must be, Since from Thy work they tried to body Thee To countless visions did their picture run, Behold through all the visions Thou art one. 

 In Thee old age and youth at once were drawn, the grey of old, the flowing locks of dawn The ancient judge, the youthful warrior, The man of battles, terrible in war. <sup>40</sup> 

 The passage seeks to reconcile conflicting images of the Divine—a warring youth versus a compassionate old man. Words for image and imagination appear three times in the Hebrew, suggesting the importance of the imaginary power to this passage. The key statement, as it would read in a more prosaic translation is: “they likened you through many visions, yet you are one in all forms imagined.” This serves as a summary statement for an entire theory of religious imagination. Religious imagination produces many images and forms of God. God is one and his unity manifests through this variety of images. Imagination is thus the human tool for perceiving God. It commits a permissible error of imagining God, while God himself remains beyond the various images attributed to him, one and the same. The author of this hymn does not seek to account for why such 

*[p. 164]* ^pg169

imaginations are necessary and how they may be legitimated. His task is to reconcile conflicting images received within tradition. Were we to account for why such imaginations are permissible and constitute part of scriptural experience, a likely answer would be because they facilitate access to God, making His presence real, opening the believer up to the intensity of encounter with God. The multiplicity of religious images is instrumental to deep and authentic encounter with Him. Because these various images are found in Scripture, there is no question as to their propriety and legitimacy. But the theoretical grounds for such legitimation could perhaps be extended to other forms of the human religious imagination, and how it reaches out to God, making His presence real. 

 Remaining for still a moment with this text, it is worth noting that its theory of religious imagination seems to be that what is seen through the imagination occurs in the mind or the heart of the viewer and does not correspond to some external reality that is properly conveyed through the vision. <sup>41</sup> As Elliot Wolfson notes, we can distinguish between different understandings of what is envisioned and its relationship to God, or to what is really “out there.” Wolfson refers to these as veridical and docetic. <sup>42</sup> If for earlier members of the circle of German pietists, vision corresponds to something real, for the author of this hymn, all visions are equally irrelevant to a true depiction of reality, forcing us thereby to develop a theory of how religious imagination functions and how to validate it. <sup>43</sup> 

 The question occurs also, as Wolfson notes, with regard to the earliest circle of Jewish mystics, who ascend to visions of the heavenly throne. <sup>44</sup> What are we to make of the detailed descriptions of travels through celestial realms, culminating in the beautiful vision of the enthroned God? Do these correspond to a reality that is portrayed in these texts? Were we to say so, this would constitute a serious challenge for later generations, who may not recognize God in the form portrayed in these texts. If so, should these texts simply be dismissed as non-normative, ultimately mistaken, perceptions of God? There is an in-between path, based on recognition of the importance of the imagination in cultivating religious experience and in aiding the aspirant into the presence of God. <sup>45</sup> 

 For our purposes, it is most helpful to identify those moments where imagination is not only cultivated, but where its cultivation may be at odds with a higher view of God. Recognition of a higher truth that in some sense invalidates what is imagined, while upholding the inherent value of what one visions and imagines, could provide a precedent, a structure, that could be extended to instances beyond Judaism where we identify a conflict between a higher, philosophical, view of God that affirms His unity and alternative workings of the religious imagination that seem to be at odds with such recognition. Hassidic literature is the latest chapter in Jewish reflection that has brought to us rich resources with regard to religious imagination, including many exercises for cultivation of the imaginative faculty through guided imagery, meditation, etc. <sup>46</sup> I would like to point to several moments within this literature that are helpful to our discussion. The first concerns the legitimacy, even encouragement, of active imagining of God, even though it is known that these imaginings are false. It relies on the already cited controversy of Maimonides and Rabad concerning the status of someone who imagines God to be possessed of form, on account of how God 

*[p. 165]* ^pg170

is portrayed in Scripture. <sup>47</sup> Maimonides is a champion of the view that a correct understanding of God is essential for a proper relationship with Him and ultimately for attaining salvation. He can therefore not tolerate such error. Rabad, by contrast, is much more forgiving, citing Scripture and the precedent of many sages who have followed its plain sense and accepted an anthropomorphic view of God. 

 One master of Jewish spirituality, and meditation in particular, R. Kalonymous Kalman Schapiro, placed great value on the use of imagination in the religious life. So much so, that for him the cultivation of the imaginative faculty, as part of enhancing a sense of the reality of God, could come even at the cost of theological propriety. At one point, in response to the query of a student who struggles to reach God and to have a proper vision of him, the rabbi writes:

 My counsel is that a person . . . at the beginning of the growth and expanding of his mindfulness should depend upon the view of the Ravad, who responded to the Rambam: “Why does he call a person \[who attributes physicality to God\] a sectarian? A number of great and good people . . . have entertained this idea . . . ” ( *Hilchot Teshuvah* 3:7). It is difficult to understand the words of the Ravad. Does the verse not clearly state, “You saw no image” (besides other such statements)? But in line with what was stated above, we can understand this as follows. The Ravad is not saying that we should delude ourselves that there is some image in the upper realm, God forbid, let it not even be uttered. Rather, we must know in truth that God has no image, Heaven forbid. But we beings formed of physicality, corporeal people who possess form, visualize such a thing so that our mindfulness will be able to grasp, expand and broaden. Then, when God helps us and our mind grows stronger so that we can think of Him with a strong and clean mindfulness, and when an image of a spark of prophecy is revealed within us, this physical image will of itself cease to exist. When we pray, we will be able to picture that we are standing before God and His throne of glory—realizing that whatever we imagine is merely the way that our senses—our ears and mind—are capable of interpreting things. <sup>48</sup> 

 What is special in this text is the willingness to sustain a clearly wrong or false imaginative approach to God as a station on the way to a true visioning of God. The problem of theological correctness is dealt with by significantly stretching the ancient dispute between Maimonides and Rabad. What was a theoretical discussion concerning the status of someone who was misled into a wrong understanding of God by the plain sense of Scripture has become a principled approach to active imagining of God in prayer. Clearly, the primacy of religious experience for this author was such that he was willing to risk theological propriety on the road to God realization. <sup>49</sup> 

 I would like to point to two additional sources that could serve as resources for the present reflection. The first is to be found in the teachings of Rabbi Nachman of Breslav:

 Descriptions and praises of God <sup>50</sup> exist in the aspect of the imaginative faculty. This is because all of the descriptions and praises with which we think of God, be He blessed, are in the aspect of the imagination. This is because in the inner part of 

*[p. 166]* ^pg171

the intellect, God, be He blessed, is stripped entirely of all praises and descriptions. Thus, all of the praises and descriptions are necessarily in the aspect of the imagination. And so, when the imagination is refined and rectified, then it is possible to arrange praises and descriptions of God, be He blessed. But when the imagination is not rectified, then we have no knowledge whatsoever of how to describe God, be He blessed, as it were, with praises and descriptions, because all praises and descriptions of God are in the aspect of the imagination. <sup>51</sup> 

 In this passage we encounter explicit reference to the imaginative power. <sup>52</sup> The term, taken from earlier philosophical discussions of prophecy, is of great importance for R. Nachman, so much so that true faith is contingent on cultivation and purification of this force. It is thus a factor in the everyday faith of every person, and not only a concern of the prophet and a theory of prophecy. Purification of the imaginative power does not lead to transcending the imagination in favor of an abstract view of God. Rather, the appropriate and purified imagination allows us to praise God. Praise, especially as captured in Jewish liturgy, relates to God through a variety of anthropomorphic images. Far from being trivialized or rejected, these are viewed as necessary for the religious life. Thus, we cannot approach God without resort to the imaginative power. Approaching God without the integration of the imagination will be either cold or, even worse, impossible. R. Nachman recognizes that there is something ultimately false in all applications of the imaginary faculty. God Himself is beyond all praises and attributes. All that we say of Him can only be said as an expression of the imagination. Yet this does not invalidate the imagination; it only requires its purification so that God can be properly approached through it. <sup>53</sup> 

 Another text, coming from a very different hassidic milieu, struggles with religious experience, how it must resort to imagination and how imaginations are of necessity limiting and therefore in an ultimate sense wrong. The discussion, once again, concerns the internal faculty of the imagination and seeks to legitimate religious experience, even given the knowledge that the imagination is by definition lacking, imperfect, and ultimately even false. The discussion, by R. Aharon Halevy of Strashelye in his *Shaarei Avoda,* refers to the need to experience internally what are essentially metaphysical and spiritual cognitions of the absoluteness of God and of the fact that He alone exists. In this context he applies the recognition that all is God to justify even the workings of the imagination as being valid: 

 God Himself descends through the aspect of the imagination, as in the case of the prophets . . . concerning whom it says “I saw God” (Isa. 6,1), *literally* . . . . God revealed Himself literally, through whatever degree of surrender \[or: annihilation\] they practiced, which was \[still\] an aspect of the imagination. 

 And from this you can draw similar conclusions with reference to all levels below that of the prophets, all the more so in every Jewish soul, that each and every person, according to his imaginings \[attains something that\] is true. For this was the purpose of all creation, that God should be revealed specifically through the imagination. Because the purpose of all of creation is to reveal His divinity, blessed be He, even from the perspective of being \[reality\]. And from the perspective of being 

*[p. 167]* ^pg172

there are infinite levels, yet from the angle of Truth—all is true. Because imagination is also His power, blessed be He, descending in the aspect of the imagination to each and every one. <sup>54</sup> 

 The text contains the nucleus for a broader theory of the imagination that could be extended beyond the traditional bounds of Judaism and its authoritative texts. Grounding a theory of the imagination in creation itself suggests that it is a vision relevant to all creatures. If the purpose of all creation is for God to be known, it is through imagination that He is known to all His creatures. Note that reference to the soul of Israel appears as an *a forteriori* argument, suggesting that the legitimacy of approaching God through the imagination is not limited to an inherent capacity of the Jewish soul. Rather, it is something fundamental to the human person. <sup>55</sup> 

 Other hassidic teachers go as far as to associate the image of God itself with the imaginative faculty. Their teachings may have not intended to tie all humanity to the image of God, considering rather its application to Israel. Nevertheless, if we take the association of the imaginative faculty and the image of God seriously, important possibilities emerge. The various spiritual aspirations of humanity, and the imaginations through which it captures these aspirations, may be considered as expressions of a divinely given capacity, expressing what it means to be human, made in God’s image. 

 In this spirit let us consider the following interpretation of Gen.1,26 by a noted hassidic author, R. Simcha Bunem of Przysucha:

 The word ‘ *adam* (man) is derived from *’adammeh* \[i.e. I will imagine\]. <sup>56</sup> After God made everything beautiful and glorious He wanted to display His actions so that one would see everything. But those who exist, apart from human beings, do not comprehend anything but themselves. God created man and he is the power that is comprised of the upper and lower realities, which all can be imagined in the soul of a person. This is the essence of man that he sees, comprehends and imagines, like no one else. This is \[the import of\] “let us make man in our image, like our likeness, for only something somehow similar in kind can envision <sup>57</sup> that which it is like. <sup>58</sup> 

 Man’s power of comprehending and imagining is a divine quality endowed in the human person by means of which he can comprehend God’s works and God Himself. The imaginative faculty is an aspect of a commonality man possesses with God, by means of which he can come to know Him. <sup>59</sup> 

 In view of the preceding discussion, a theory of religious imagination that could account for the workings of the imagination in other religions would be constituted by three factors:

 A. A dissociation of the content of imagination from an “out-there” truth component, so that what is produced by the imagination need not be considered true in a descriptive sense, or correspond to spiritual reality as it is, even though some degree of abstraction and translation into philosophical terms seems unavoidable. 

*[p. 168]* ^pg173

 B. Recognition of the instrumentality of religious imagination in reaching God and making His presence real, such that it enlivens the spiritual life, producing beneficial fruits. 

 C. Appreciation of religious imagination as an expression of a divinely given capacity, grounded in the notion of the image of God. 

 When might we apply such an attitude to the religion of the other? If our starting point is the recognition of the same God and if we seek to deal with the portions of another religion’s view that strike us as other and strange, what might be conditions and criteria under which we could profitably apply this strategy? As a first stab at articulating a minimal theory for recognizing or accommodating expressions of religious imagination in religions’ view of God and gods, I would like to propose three criteria:

 A. The first criterion seems to be recognition of the Creator as the ultimate source of life and creation. As we noted in our “Same God” discussion, this is the single most central criterion by means of which the Christian God was recognized as the same as the God of Judaism. 

 B. Faith and recognition of God, especially vitalized and internalized with the aid of the imagination, produces transformative fruit in a life of faith. Thus, the workings of the religious imagination have a beneficial spiritual effect. Our discussion of recognizing the fruits of the spiritual life as an extension of Meiri’s strategy illustrate what kind of beneficial impact the spiritual life, enlivened by power of the imagination, would have. These fruits are to be distinguished from those that would come forth from the darker side of the imagination, which would lead to fear, superstition, magic, attempts at controlling and manipulating reality and the likes. Thus, in referring to the fruits of the imagination, I am referring to the ways in which religious imagination aids in making real fundamental spiritual teachings, deriving from and affirming God’s existence in the life of the believer. 

 C. Within the beneficial fruits, I would like to single out one in particular—love. A full religious life, lived with the power of imagination, is one in which God is real and therefore an object of love and devotion. If imagination not only makes God real, but allows us to cultivate a loving attitude, to God and to others, this is a positive fruit of the spiritual life as such and of incorporation of the power of imagination in it. 

 Applying these three criteria means that the imaginative power is judged by similar standards in Judaism and beyond. The discernment therefore shifts from correct or incorrect imaginings to beneficial or harmful fruits of working with religious imagination. Religious imagination, then, is universal, and we would seek to discern its workings and their possible fruits in all religions. 

 Let us apply all this to Hindu gods. The Shituf construct, especially as applied to a vedantic worldview, would recognize in Brahman the supreme creator. Specific gods and manifestations are ways of making him real to the human 

*[p. 169]* ^pg174

person and therefore resort to the imagination. The practice of having a personal god, an *ishta devata* , as a focus of the spiritual life and a means of approaching the absolute, would illustrate the importance of the religious imagination in making God real in the life of the person and its ultimate instrumentality. Through the individual deity—Rama, Krishna, Ganesha, the Mother etc.—one’s spiritual life becomes engaged, interiorized, and real. This is particularly so in the devotional *bhakti* movements that make love the focus of the approach to the individual deity. Thus, the three suggested criteria are played out in relation to different aspects of the Shituf construct, some in relation to the absolute, some in relation to the individual deity. Their totality makes for Shituf and it is precisely their totality and complementarity that make the religious life full and real. 

 Other configurations of these criteria are possible. In some of the religious movements, the God who is worshipped assumes all qualities to Himself. Thus, for some Krishna or for others Siva are not manifestations of a higher being, but the supreme being himself. Krishna or Siva *is* the creator. How they are imagined, envisioned, the stories told of them, how they are made real in the lives of believers—all these would be considered by the Jewish observer as expressions of the imaginative faculty. In the best of cases, such as in the lives of great masters, poets and saints, these imaginative expressions are pathways to a deeper spiritual life, making God real and generating the fruits of contact with Him in the lives of devotees. Rather than highlighting the unfamiliar and strange expressions of the religious imagination, we are called to consider their fruits, their impact in the lives of believers. <sup>60</sup> Of course, not all believers will benefit in the same way. This is as true in Judaism as it is in other religions. But it is enough for our purposes if spiritual advancement is made *possible* by means of the imagination. That some may never go beyond an external application of the imagination is analogous to a superficial and externalized observance of a Jewish way of life. Whatever core allows us to apply a “same God” strategy is sufficient for inviting us to consider a positive approach to another religion’s imaginative world, even if those who reap its full benefits are only few. 

 Considering the role of the imagination in accounting for the faith of other religions is related to points raised earlier in this study. One suggestion made earlier relates to Nachmanides’ view of the allotments of gods and angels to different nations. I suggested that the collective imaginative faculty of a people, expressed in how their god or gods are imagined, might be considered an application of Nachmanides’ principle. We may also recall Rabbi Herzog’s application of the principle of Shituf to Christianity. Rabbi Herzog speaks of Jesus as having been elevated to divine status through the workings of the frivolous human imagination. <sup>61</sup> Given that Rabbi Herzog upholds the legitimacy of Christianity, following the opinion that Shituf is permissible for non-Jews, what he is in effect endorsing is the way in which human imagination complements and expresses fundamental religious truths. 

 There is an even more principled way of accommodating religious imagination, by focusing on what is core to the notion of Avoda Zara. While what follows does not necessarily legitimate all workings of the religious imagination of other cultures, it does allow us to revisit the relationship of the real and the imaginary 

*[p. 170]* ^pg175

with reference to Avoda Zara by suggesting that application or recognition of the workings of religious imagination, even if not fully permissible, actually reduces the charge of Avoda Zara. In what follows I wish to present the halachic views of one notable twentieth-century halachic authority, Rabbi Abraham Isaiah Karelitz, known as the Chazon Ish. In his notes on the Shulchan Aruch he revisits issues of the definition of Avoda Zara, with particular reference to the foundational discussion of the Tosafot and their view of permissibility of Shituf. <sup>62</sup> While the Chazon Ish himself seems to rule against permissibility of Shituf, and reads the Tosafot in that light, he offers some important distinctions and resources for a consideration of other religions. The Chazon Ish considers the foundation of Avoda Zara to be the confusion of creator and creature. Thus, any act of worshipping a created being as God amounts to Avoda Zara. This is true whether the created being is present or deceased, and one continues worshipping him or her posthumously. One consequence of this understanding is that if someone does not approach a created being as “God,” but rather considers God, here understood in the fullness of divinity, as manifesting through a particular being, this is not to be considered as forbidden worship. <sup>63</sup> A further consequence of this clear definition of the core of Avoda Zara as confusing creator and creature is, in a startling statement, that the worship of imaginary beings is not to be considered as Avoda Zara. <sup>64</sup> If one worships a being that has no reality, one is, in fact, engaging in a mistaken view of God. <sup>65</sup> This is not Avoda Zara, but rather a lesser category of *minut* , wrong view of God. The Chazon Ish goes as far as considering that while a non-Jew is forbidden to worship through Shituf, he is not forbidden to entertain mistaken theological notions, *minut* , through Shituf. 

 Clearly, the Chazon Ish does not endorse all products of religious imagination in other religious cultures as legitimate. But he does remove them from the realm of Avoda Zara, thereby facilitating a discussion of their potential value and how they might be accommodated within a more positive appreciation of other religions. According to this understanding, then, we would have to consider the imagination a distinct category, as we view the gods of other religions. The imagination may give expression to mistaken notions of the Divine, but these are not ipso facto Avoda Zara. Moreover, even when these images are translated into artifacts of worship, these artifacts are not, in and of themselves, Avoda Zara, but only an inappropriate approach, corresponding to a lower level of error committed in relation to imaginary beings. 

 Perhaps we can revisit Meiri in this light. We never really understood how Meiri could live with theological error and with misrepresentation of God in other religious cultures, ignoring all that was “wrong” in them and highlighting only the common core of a knowledge of God and a moral life that affords those religions legitimacy. One way of making sense of Meiri is through a theory of religious imagination. Alongside true knowledge of God there is also the operation of the human imaginative faculty. God is not known or understood as abstract philosophical truth. Rather, the reality of God is expressed through a series of correct cognitions, in the field of metaphysics as well as of ethics. Along with these we also find ways of portraying God that are adaptations and accommodations of the human imagination. How inventive the human imagination 

*[p. 171]* ^pg176

is can be seen in the creation of mythologies and art and more fundamentally in how it pictures God and the gods. When Krishna or Siva are approached as “God,” rather than as in some way derivative expressions of absolute being, correct spiritual cognitions are intertwined with imaginary ways of relating to the Divine. Application of the imagination does not invalidate the commonality and validation that Meiri recognizes. 

 A more positive appreciation of the imagination, such as we find in certain streams of Jewish thought described above, <sup>66</sup> can be further applied to Meiri’s views, in recognizing the instrumentality of the imagination and how it aids in the spiritual life. The fruits of the religious life, bringing together true cognition and an imaginative approach, are not in correct knowledge, but in a full spiritual life. The imaginative life is necessary for cultivating the fullness of the religious life, in both moral and spiritual terms. Seen in this way, the imagination is a necessary component of the religious life. It should be appreciated by its fruits, rather than by its truth content. The high spiritual life that has been made possible for some followers of Hindu gods could serve as testimony to a Meiri-based view of religions that judges religions by their impact and fruit in the lives of believers. Engaging the imagination would accordingly be considered a value, not a hindrance. 

 Seen in this light, we might go as far as to consider the mixture of the cognitive and the imaginative a fundamental and necessary part of any spiritual life, including the religious life of Jews. Shituf might accordingly refer to the bringing together of these faculties in any religious life. Accordingly, there is some dimension of Shituf and, consequently, some mistaken or idolatrous understanding that is a necessity in any religious life. As some of the hassidic masters suggest, with reference to Jews, this concerns how they view and imagine God. As there is no other object of worship, imaginative errors and limitations are contained within the permissible, even if ultimately erroneous, approach to God. Their justification lies in the spiritual life they lead to. For non-Jews, the range of flexibility is larger, permitting the incorporation and application of the imaginative faculty in the religious life even as it imagines other beings. Whether it is in wrong or imperfect imagining of God, or viewing God as manifesting in other beings—real and imaginary—in both cases the absolute purity of the knowledge of God eludes us. God remains in important ways beyond, for Jews and non-Jews.<sup>67</sup> Acknowledging this fact of the spiritual life allows us to recognize greater commonality in the human condition, facing the Divine, and to cultivate a greater openness and tolerance toward those expressions of imagining God that do not cohere with our own tradition.

### 13 Revisiting the Worship of Images: Glimpses of a Future Dialogue

 We have noted that Avoda Zara refers both to the otherness of the deity worshipped and to the strangeness of method of worship, primarily the 

strangeness and otherness of worshipping God through images and forms. In the relation between theology and worship, we have noted the theoretical possibility that Avoda Zara would be defined by acts of worship. This may be the viewpoint of the formative rabbinic period, where we find little express discussion of what constitutes Avoda Zara and why it is problematic. Such attitudes were perpetuated emotionally in later periods and, as we have noted in various places, some later authorities appeal to the problematic ritual, particularly to image worship, as markers of Avoda Zara in their own right. However, the various voices we have analyzed, coming from the different literary genres of the Middle Ages, all seem to privilege theology over and against worship. The various strategies by means of which the same God is affirmed all rely on theology, rather than worship. For such views we would have to consider that worship is in and of itself meaningless. It requires a theoretical structure to interpret it and a religious framework of attachment to and relationship with a deity to endow it with meaning. We also recall that prioritizing philosophy over worship is an internal Hindu option, and not only a way for the outsider to make sense of Hindu practice. But even if the philosophical or theological perspective allows us to recognize the same God as worshipped in both religions, the otherness of worship does not disappear. Just as a “same God” position invited ref lection on the relationship between God and gods, so too further thought must be given to the concrete means by which God is worshipped, and in particular to the use of images. Is the only option available to us to disregard or excuse such practices, in view of a higher understanding of God, or is further engagement with these practices possible? We recall the concluding words of Rabbi Steinsaltz’s seminal essay on tolerance, where he ref lects on the merits of his Shituf-based position. Regarding it, Steinsaltz says: “It does not require most religions to give up, or modify the meaning of, such words as *true* and *truth* . It provides a basis for conversation among religions without the expectation of compromise between or reconciliation of claims.” <sup>1</sup> Given the foundations of recognition, afforded by 

*[p. 174]* ^pg178

Shituf, or other “same God” strategies, the present section seeks to advance this very conversation. In ref lecting on issues such as the worship of images, one seeks not to condone practices involving image worship but to engage them. What are the challenges, lessons, and opportunities that a thoughtful engagement of these practices might yield? Differently put: What might a future Jewish-Hindu dialogue, based on the premises of some degree of recognition and affirmation of the same God, look like? 

 I would like to raise several points in the present discussion, without any claim that these are the most important points to emerge in a future dialogue. As they have come up as points for reflection in my own thinking process, it is likely they will be of significance to others as well. The first concerns the very understanding of the meaning and function of the idol in worship and how it is conceived in relation to divinity. Our rejection of the worship of forms is so fundamental that we take little time to consider what the form means to the worshipper, in what way it mediates divinity, and how divinity is perceived through it. We implicitly assume that any use of image in worship implies an identification of the worshipped object with divinity, often identifying the two in such a way as to limit the scope of divinity and contain it fully within the worshipped object. It is easy to ridicule a “primitive” or simplistic view of the use of images. The Hindu view of images, however, is usually quite sophisticated. Divinity is never fully identified, nor fully contained, in an image. It would be fair to summarize the relationship between divinity and the image as that between soul and body. The analogy is fairly exhaustive. Soul enters body at a certain point in time. Prior to that, there is only dead matter. What makes life significant is the soul, with the body serving as its vehicle. At the other end of the process we encounter the departure of soul from body. A lifeless body requires disposal and does not elicit great respect, except by memory and association with the living person. 

 Important Hindu images are understood along similar lines. They are vehicles for something, rather than objects bearing religious meaning in their own right. What is worshipped is the spirit of the Divine, dwelling in them, rather than the physical object. <sup>2</sup> They are consecrated as ritual objects following an elaborate ceremony, whose purpose is to draw the divine spirit to the object. <sup>3</sup> Significantly, the life span of most images is limited. <sup>4</sup> The end of the life of the image is its drowning in water, where it is dissolved, returning to its primordial elements. Jewish travelers have observed the latter ceremony, but have not offered any reflections on its significance. <sup>5</sup> 

 I recall a conversation with two prominent Hindu leaders, Sri Sri Ravi Shankar and Sugunendra Theerta Swami, one of the leaders of the Madhva school of philosophy. The two represent different philosophical schools, the former closer to a monistic view of reality, wherein the soul, God, and all reality are one, whereas the latter belongs to the dualistic school that insists on the ontological difference and distinction between God and creation. Notwithstanding significant theological differences, when approached by me in discussion concerning the meaning of the worship of idols both replied, jointly, along the same lines. The argument from the perishability of the idol and its mandated ritual destruction affirms the fact that it is not the idol, but the divinity that manifests through it, that is 

*[p. 175]* ^pg179

worshipped. The eventual immersion of the idol in water was almost an immediate response to the presumed charge that they simply worship wood, stone, or other crafted materials. 

 On another occasion I queried Sugunendra Swami regarding the need for worshipping through images. As a spiritual figure who has spent all of his life in spiritual practice, since being identified at a young age, based on astrological indications, as a future religious figurehead, and who has since spent thousands of hours in worship and meditation, what need did he have for worshipping through images? His answer was suggestive. Rather than dismiss the use of idols as something external, needed for the sake of the masses, or some such similar argument, Swamiji insisted on the limitations of his own consciousness and his own need for an object for focus in worship. The idol was an instrument designed to raise his consciousness toward a higher reality, even as the real presence of the idol was affirmed. Instrumentality coupled with presence thus provides the logic that accounts for the use of idols in the framework of a developmental worldview that seeks to raise the consciousness of the believer to a higher spiritual realm. 

 That images have a cognitive function and are taken as an aid to concentration and spiritual development is already an important complement to a simplistic view that simply sees in them the form of the god that is being worshipped. But perhaps even more important is the possible analogy to the Temple and its function in Jewish tradition. Theological reflection on the Temple wrestles with the problem of the relationship between God as present in the Temple and His presence either in the entire world or in the heavens. The Temple is ultimately instrumental. It is a means of realizing divine presence and making it available to people. <sup>6</sup> It may also be an object of meditation. <sup>7</sup> Through the physical medium of wood, gold, etc., divine presence comes to dwell. Its dwelling is initiated by an act or a process of consecration. And while the departing of the divine presence is not ritualized, rabbinic sources speak of it precisely in such terms, as departing from the Temple, leaving it destroyed spiritually, leading to its physical destruction. It may be then that our thinking, conditioned as it has been by derogatory rhetoric, has been misdirected. If we compare idol with Temple, rather than with God, a more appropriate appreciation for the purpose and function of images may emerge. <sup>8</sup> 

 There is an even more fundamental issue that arises from encounter with Hindu religious practice. The issue relates to images, but it relates to a much more fundamental aspect of how the Divine is understood and as such is relevant both to the question of God and gods and to the question of representation in and through material objects. If the idol is understood in terms of embodiment and its equivalent is the Temple, we are led to more fundamental considerations of divine embodiment and what it means to talk of God’s body. Popular as well as halachic attitudes to other religions may be driven by a sense of otherness, but they are also nurtured by implicit assumptions about the possibility, or rather the impossibility, of divine embodiment. Thus, Judaism is often presented in light of its philosophical iteration as rejecting the notion of a divine body, while other religions are reduced to a caricatural understanding of divine embodiment, one that not only provides no challenge, but also leaves room for little response but derision. Theological facts, however, are more complicated.

 The following thoughts owe a great debt to Ben Sommer’s *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* . <sup>9</sup> A scholar of the Hebrew Bible and the Ancient Near East, Sommer revisits biblical monotheism and how God is understood in biblical sources. Sommer undercuts the conventional distinction between monotheism and polytheism and suggests instead a typology of different understandings of God and His body. Specifically, he points to understandings that present a fluid body, contrasted with understandings that oppose such a view. Fluidity of divine body means that the body of God is unlike the human body. It can appear in multiple locations simultaneously, it can enter and be identified with various objects, and it can be manifested as other beings coming as part of God and representing him to a greater or lesser extent, though never fully. Sommer claims that while all of biblical (and I add: rabbinic) Judaism is anthropomorphic, important strands in biblical thought understood the divine body specifically as a fluid divine body. Sommer sees this faith as analogous to the religious understanding of some of Israel’s ancient neighbors. What distinguishes Israel from other cultures was its monotheistic faith, not its claim for lack of body or its understanding of the divine body as a fixed, nonfluid, body. Sommer further sees fluidity as a theological strand that finds expression in various postbiblical Judaisms. Finally, by focusing on this problem he stands on its head much anti-Christian polemic, by suggesting that Christian faith in the Trinity and in the incarnation conforms to an ancient Jewish model and is therefore not to be considered at odds with Jewish faith. 

 What Sommer does not spell out for us are the implications of the recovery of this theological strand to a Jewish view of Hinduism. He does, however, come close to it. As he gropes for an appropriate language by means of which to describe the religious understanding he presents, he applies several times the Hindu term “avatar.” The Jewish God can have avatars, manifest in various persons and beings and dwell in various objects. The already acknowledged similarity between Hindu practices and Ancient Near Eastern practices thus brings us back to the question of how to view Hindu practice and belief against the formative biblical background. <sup>10</sup> The question takes on very particular coloring when this biblical background is a theology that is commensurate with Hindu practices rather than the mockery of these very practices in prophetic rhetoric. The Hindu understanding of divinity and its manifestation is particularly close to this biblical strand of thought, which captures it much better than the later construct of Shituf. For the Hindu, God *comes as* . It may be the great teacher, the force of nature, or a particular avatar, deity, or lower being, all of which are expressions of God. We could try to make sense of these while keeping on our hierarchical spectacles, through a Shituf view, but it is much truer to the nature of the phenomenon under discussion to appreciate Hindu God, gods, and their concrete manifestations, embodiment, and worship through a model that is closer to Hindu self-understanding. The biblical model uncovered by Sommer invites us to consider whether what we might consider as so strange as to be beyond the pale of theological possibility is not in fact an important and much neglected way of viewing the Divine, which is part and parcel of our most ancient roots and their many expressions in the course of Jewish history. Thus, encounter with 

*[p. 177]* ^pg181

this particular religious other is an occasion to encounter and rediscover a part of our own self. 

 Fluidity, manifestation, and embodiment of the Divine raise anew the question of the awareness and intentionality of the believer. The biblical believer seems well aware of the complexity of manifesting persons as he relates to God and to His manifestations interchangeably. <sup>11</sup> The Hindu believer who considers that God *comes as* is also likely aware of this complexity. But the Hindu believer who is not aware of higher philosophical teaching is not. Does this alter how we view divine embodiment? Is it the case that awareness is a condition for embodiment to work as a theological strategy, or can embodiment as a fundamental mode of viewing the Divine also be validated when only some believers are cognizant of it? If we entertained the possibility that Shituf might function because teachers and sages are aware of the higher aspect of God, could the same be maintained if the core theological understanding requires maintaining awareness of the relationship between the Divine and its manifestation/embodiment? This is the kind of question that invites further reflection. 

 Divine embodiment in a Hindu context may be in a better position for recognition by a Jewish view of God than the Ancient Near Eastern gods. Ancient Near Eastern gods are all, in some way, limited in power, subservient to a higher order that is nonpersonal. There is a realm of power that is independent of and greater than the realm of divinity. <sup>12</sup> Whether it is chaos or fate, the gods are limited and do not have ultimate control. While astrology is ubiquitous in Hindu religious life, <sup>13</sup> the structure of Hindu religion and the understanding of Hindu divinity is not circumscribed in the same way as polytheisms of the religions of Greece, Egypt, and Mesopotamia. If the message of Hebrew monotheism was one of liberation from nature and its servitude, <sup>14</sup> the Hindu gods are themselves instruments for such liberation. Moreover, the relationship between Hindu gods and Brahman would seem to be the exact opposite of that of Mesopotamian gods and the controlling forces beyond. Hindu gods manifest this absolute, rather than being helpless in face of it. This is a very different religious structure and one that is much closer to a Jewish view of God, especially the kind recovered and presented by Sommer. 

 What, then, makes the Jewish God unique? For theologies that reject divine embodiment, let alone the notion of a divine body, the answer is obvious, ultimately leading us to a dichotomy between correct and incorrect views of God. For the kind of theology presented by Sommer the question is harder to address. Especially if we recognize that ultimately it is the same God that is known in the two religions, what is the heart of the theological divide? Is it only the particularity of names and forms of manifestation, unknown and therefore not recognized by Judaism? But if deep theological structures can be affirmed and differences are in name only, this significantly reduces the gap, making theological difference extremely fine. Perhaps the difference would be educational. Judaism’s uniqueness may lie not in its faith but in its educational strategy, as it seeks to make a certain faith broadly known and understood by all believers. <sup>15</sup> In the same way that we suggested a dialogue between Jews who do not worship by means of Shituf and non-Jews who do still lies ahead, so too a dialogue between Jewish 

*[p. 178]* ^pg182

and Hindu theologies of divine fluidity is a conversation of the future, an exercise not only for our theological imagination but also for spiritual inspiration. 

 Let me move on to the next point in this series of reflections on the shape of a future dialogue. What I shall present here is not only a pointer for a future dialogue but an actual reflection on idolatry, inspired by the Hindu concept of the Divine and how it is related to the idol, as developed by a contemporary Orthodox rabbi. These reflections were recently published in the journal *Tradition* , a mouthpiece of the Rabbinical Council of America, the umbrella organization of American Orthodox rabbis. In other words, these reflections have taken place within mainstream Orthodoxy, a sign of the potential for serious engagement, under appropriate circumstances. The article by Rabbi Herzl Hefter is titled “Idolatry: A Prohibition for Our Time.” <sup>16</sup> Hefter begins by juxtaposing the actual practice of worshipping idols and the various forms such worship takes in the East with its absence in the West. Does this mean that idolatry is simply part of Judaism’s immune system, without serving any practical purpose? Hefter seeks to construct a notion of idolatry that would have meaning for our time and that is not simply identified with the worship of images. Following a hassidic author as well as Tillich, Hefter argues that monotheism is not automatically free from idolatry. The point is not how many gods one believes in, but *how* one believes. By claiming that our religious understanding is direct and complete, we are both making God finite (in our own image) and limiting the potentiality of human growth and development. The graven image is but an expression of this problem. Turning to Heinrich Zimmer’s work on Hinduism, Hefter finds an echo for these concerns within the actual practices and understandings associated with Hindu images. According to Zimmer, the static nature of idols is a major concern for Hinduism. Hindu statues, at least of a certain period, seek to convey dynamism and movement. The view expressed through art is that “there is nothing static, nothing abiding, but only the flow of relentless process, with everything originating, growing, decaying and vanishing—this wholly dynamic view of life . . . is one of the fundamental concepts of later Hinduism.” <sup>17</sup> Zimmer similarly speaks of the possibility of the image conveying a sense that everything “dissolves into undifferentiated, transcendent, subtle essence.” This suggests that idols seek to capture something that is beyond, while attempting to overcome any finality in how they capture the Divine. Hefter contrasts this with how one approaches the concepts of the Torah and how one may take the ideals of religion and turn them into idols, seeking security and happiness in making them fixed and final forms of a revelation, rather than gateways that are forever open to a mystery beyond. In arguing against the ways that idolatry can manifest even in an image-free religion, Hefter writes: “Appearances, then, may be deceiving. Hinduism, with its plethora of idols and graven images, may not at the end of the day be idolatrous, while the extreme literalist elements of the monotheistic faiths may ‘err’ in that direction.” <sup>18</sup> 

 Hefter has unknowingly adopted a stance often taken by Hindu apologists, who reject the charges of idolatry by offering an alternative definition of what constitutes idolatry. Idolatry is not a piece of stone or wood. It is an attitude. And that attitude can be found in any religion. Mistaking images for the essence of 

*[p. 179]* ^pg183

idolatry runs the risk of inculcating a spiritual blindness that will, at the end of the day, lead to idolatry within Judaism itself. By contrast, the self-awareness of at least some Hindus functions as a buttress against slipping into the kind of fixity that blocks the needed openness toward the most high. 

 Hefter’s position is striking not only for his willingness to examine Hinduism in terms other than those based on outward appearance. Hefter has entered a meaningful spiritual conversation with Hinduism, on the core concerns of Avoda Zara and what they really mean to a religious life. He has pointed out the danger of facile identification of idolatry with the worship of forms and the immanent danger of idolatry, the limitations placed by us on God, regardless of the religion we practice. This is a true spiritual conversation. It is based on a genuine attempt to hear the deep message, the wisdom, of the Jewish tradition, through the authorities he cites, as well as of the Hindu tradition, through the literature on it. This is the kind of wisdom-based conversation I have in mind when I talk of listening to the spiritual testimony of those who practice the worship of idols. Such listening is a means of gaining a deeper understanding not only of themselves but of the unique charge and mission of Judaism, as well as of the spiritual traps it might unwittingly fall into. 

 Idolatry, as it emerges from Hefter’s presentation, is more about attitude than about object. Idolatry does not require an object of worship to flourish. It is an attitude of mind. Scripture and dogma may be subjects of an idolatrous attitude, as much as the worship offered to a statue. Now, the similarity between this position and words I have heard numerous times from Hindu leaders, experts in Vedanta, who worship through idols, maintaining a sense of distance and self-awareness, is uncanny. Swami Yogaswaroopananda, with whom I have spent dozens of hours working through my own concerns with idolatry as practiced by Hindus, affirmed to me time and again that the issue is not the statues. The issue is the limitations of the mind. Any form of limitation of mind is a kind of idolatry, hence the need to free the mind and to take it beyond all limitations. That such an attitude can flourish in the midst of the actual worship of God by means of physical objects suggests that the object of worship is not necessarily the most burning concern for a spiritual understanding of idolatry. 

 This leads me to the next point for dialogue and one that arises directly out of conversations on idolatry. The difficulty for the outsider in attempting to grasp Hinduism is the sheer diversity of perspectives that seem to exist alongside one another, without a need to force one view upon others, or to reconcile them in standardized ways. This is as true of the different sects, with their preferred deities, as it is of the different levels of religious life and understanding within any given system. Our discussion of the worship of idols has explored the relations between the physical acts of worship, aimed at specific objects, and the broader philosophical worldview that informs them. One of our theses has been that worship is subservient to religious recognition, and that therefore it is the spiritual understanding that has the final say on whether a form of worship is Avoda Zara or not. However, we also noted that there is a problem with regard to who speaks for Hinduism, and that not all those whom we classify as Hindus share this higher philosophical perspective. There ensues a tension between religious recognition 

*[p. 180]* ^pg184

and religious practice. This is a problem for the outside observer, especially when attempting to tackle a question as complex as that of Avoda Zara. But it also poses a challenge for the insider and has been a point that I have queried time and again: What is the religious status of those who do not have the “higher” perspective of Vedanta? Doesn’t the worship of idols run the risk of inculcating error? The answers I received time and again can be cast either in terms of a variation on Meiri or in terms of the just stated concern about limitation of the mind. 

 The answer that seems to represent a widespread attitude of Hindu teachers is that there is time, that one must be patient, and that eventually all will reach a fuller understanding. <sup>19</sup> Now, patience has, in a sense, become Judaism’s trademark, as it patiently awaits the day when the entire world will come to know the one God. However, when it comes to its own educational efforts, these seem geared at ensuring a modicum of proper religious understanding. It would not be conceivable for a program of Jewish education to postpone the teaching of fundamental religious truth to some indefinite future time. But this is exactly what Hindu philosophers seem content to do. 

 There are many contributing factors that lead to the development of such an attitude, and it would be foolish to try to reduce complex religious tendencies to a single cause. Living with a sense of reincarnation, where evolution follows the slow process of one life after another, certainly contributes to this attitude. So does the spiritual recognition that what really determines the value of things is how God views them, and not the importance we attach to things. This could be one important lesson, on this issue, drawn from the scriptural classic, the Bhagavad Gita. <sup>20</sup> But an important ingredient in the mix is an attitude that is marshalled as typically Hindu and as one of the virtues of the Hindu religious outlook. The attitude is tolerance. Hindu apologists will not tire in their presentation of Hindu religion and Hindu culture as tolerant. It is tolerant toward other religions and it is similarly tolerant toward the lower stages of religious formation, affirming that these too have their place, and that in time they will be outgrown in favor of fuller spiritual knowledge. Thus, in the gap between ritual and philosophy emerges an attitude that is really a moral attitude, and one that challenges us. 

 Increasingly, we have come to struggle with one of the consequences of the religious worldview associated with monotheism, the heritage of violence and intolerance. <sup>21</sup> The struggles have been the result of being confronted by the political consequences of the kind of exclusivist religious worldview that insists on “getting it right” religiously, whatever that may mean, and of the violent repercussions of such a worldview. The first decade of the twenty-first century has seen a spate of scholarship on the issue of religion and violence and much concern about the fundamentals of religious epistemology and thought, and the way these contribute to violence, subtle and overt. What has been driving much of the contact between religions in recent decades has been the need to establish a global ethos of acceptance and coexistence. It is this same drive that has made violence and tolerance important factors in the assessment of religion and its virtues. Against such a background we may consider whether the epistemological, as well as psychological, orientation of a more patient and tolerant approach to religious awareness and education might not be worthy of cultivation. If so, 

*[p. 181]* ^pg185

Hinduism has what to teach us, and that teaching seems related to its acceptance of the worship of idols, even while affirming a religious worldview that transcends such worship. We must consider the merits of the Hindu critique of limitation of the mind. If another religion provokes us to violence and anger, then in some way these attitudes point to limitation and intolerance in our attitudes, a limitation that could be critiqued as idolatrous. Viewed differently, if we expand upon Meiri’s notion of the moral dimension of religion as the ultimate test of its value, would it be appropriate or possible to include within such a broader view of morality also the virtues of tolerance, acceptance, and maybe even nonviolence (at least ideological nonviolence)? 

 Proverbial opposition to idolatry claims that it is tolerant because not much is at stake. There are no absolute truth claims to defend, hence its tolerant nature.<sup>22</sup> I think it would be a mistake to reduce Hinduism to an indifferent idolatry. Much is at stake in Hindu religious life and Hinduism’s history has known great debates as well as religious competition and even struggles. But its overall ethos is, for the greater part, one of tolerance in the midst of affirmation of the most important truths and their urgency. It may be that this cannot be easily transported into other religious cultures, inasmuch as it is also a function of the social structure of Indian society, where stability and identity are provided by a stable social order, organized through a caste system, rather than by means of religious identity. Still, we have here an important testimony to the values and attitudes that can accompany the religious life. These attitudes must challenge us, not by way of choosing this over that, affirming the superiority of one culture over another. Rather, associated with the worship of idols is the challenge of an alternative view of what constitutes true idolatry. Is the worship of physical idols the core of idolatry, or is idolatry an attitude, a limitation of mind, a constriction of divine life and love that ultimately generates violence? 

 These are constructive and generative questions that can enrich our religious reflection. They grow out of an encounter with another culture and provide the positive and growthful edge to what would otherwise be construed as a hopeless battle between our own view that rejects idols and the Hindu view that tolerates them. How far this dialogue could go and what its mutual fruits can be must be left to the future dialogue. For present purposes, I hope to have at least suggested some fruitful ways in which the conversation on the worship of idols and images can go beyond the superficial level at which it has mostly been carried out to date. This conversation can open up new avenues through which we might be spiritually challenged and through which Judaism’s own understanding of self and other might grow.
