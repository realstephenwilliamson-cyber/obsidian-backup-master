## The Purpose of Education in Relation to Students and Their Expectations

*12 July 1957*

Ladies and gentlemen,

\[…\]<sup>1</sup> these two moments, the purpose of education and the need for education, point in different directions. The traditional concept of education and culture with which we have all grown up is no longer really honoured, if I can put it so rather crudely. Of course, there is much talk of the importance of ‘general education’, much talk of the need for people to have a broader horizon, and so on, but this all still suggests something like trimmings on the side, as it were, and is not taken that seriously by people outside our own sphere, the academic sphere. It is simply taken to signify a kind of extra quality that might be tacked on to really useful or practical knowledge, the knowledge of things that are required in the context of work in so-called real life, whatever that is supposed to mean. We might say that, for us, the meaning and purpose of education is no longer ‘substantial’ in the sense of presupposing an unspoken and unproblematic relationship between the university, society, among the teachers and our students, from which the process that we call education would arise. To illustrate what I mean here, I often point out a minor terminological difference that strikes me as rather symptomatic. In *Faust* – and forgive me if I start right away by quoting *Faust* at you – you will recall the famous line ‘a good man in all his obscure striving is always conscious of the right path.’<sup>2</sup> Now if you consider the inflated contemporary use of the word *Leitbild* – ‘model’ or ‘guiding image’ – this will make the difference that I wish to emphasize drastically clear to you. In other words, the good man in his obscure striving<sup>3</sup> is 

*[p. 64]* ^pg71

no longer conscious of the proper path, and we can no longer assume that relationship between the obscure and less illuminated realm and the rational realm of articulated consciousness which, at least so we like to believe, prevailed during the forty to sixty years of so-called cultural and intellectual classicism, of so-called German Classicism. That is why the question of identifying the proper model is now being raised all the time, the question as to what we are supposed to be educating people for in the first place. And there is a kind of sorcery in play as soon as we have to ask what our model or guiding ideal for education should be, one which is no longer immanent to the process of education itself but needs to be located outside before the process in question can be oriented in that direction. Once we put it that way, things can hardly turn out well.

In concrete terms, it looks as though we are confronted here by two unmediated and to a certain extent mutually antagonistic moments: on the one hand, the ideas about education and its purpose that we ourselves bring with us or have acquired by wrestling with these questions, ideas that we may have already modified and in a critical manner; on the other hand, what the students themselves want, or at least what they need, drawing on what they have to learn from us in order to make their way in the world. Now I would say that both these sides are equally problematic. The idea of the end or purpose of education is problematic, as I just said, on account of that remarkably abstract and arbitrary character which readily allows it to become nothing but ideology – another speech for the Kaiser’s birthday to assure his Highness that everything depends on the preservation of the True, the Good and the Beautiful, or, in even more hideous language, on the preservation of ‘cultural values’, or, as Troeltsch once put in a weaker moment, on the requisite ‘cultural synthesis’.<sup>4</sup> On the other hand, however, there is no doubt that the idea that we can think about the crisis of the universities without looking very closely at the actual consciousness of the students is equally problematic. And no less problematic is the attitude expressed by questions such as ‘What is it we need?’ and ‘What is it we don’t need?’, the attitude that is perhaps best captured by that truly wretched word ‘ballast’, the hostility to things of the mind and the spirit, the tendency to treat these things as a dispensable luxury – attitudes and tendencies that we often come across in extreme cases and will no doubt continue to come across in future. A blind appeal to the traditional ideal of culture and education is no help here, for that is certainly not going to encourage anyone to change their ways. Nor, on the other hand, does it help simply to adapt to that ever more perceptible tendency that ends up robbing the mind of any transcendence whatsoever once it 

*[p. 65]* ^pg72

has become nothing but a means of reproducing what exists anyway, what is the case anyway. There are no neat solutions that could be applied once and for all, for the problem that I have briefly outlined for you is not a merely internal academic matter but a problem that involves society as a whole. But precisely because it is one that we do constantly encounter *intra muros*, we are also easily tempted to believe that it is simply connected with the specific conditions of our university life, with the numerous academic conditions that are certainly involved. But it is extremely difficult to separate these specifically academic aspects of the problem from those that are connected with society as a whole. For, as I should perhaps point out here, there is actually a by no means minor but very significant broader problem of sociology involved here – namely the extent to which the phenomena we are observing, whatever they may be, are to be ascribed to the specific things in the context of which these symptoms or manifestations reveal themselves, and the extent to which these particular relationships are in truth the bearers or expressions of much more far-reaching tendencies that are rooted in the distinctive character of contemporary society. Here I might just draw your attention to certain features that are particularly relevant in terms of society as a whole. The first of these, of course, is that the humanistic ideal of culture and education has become problematic in the context of late industrial society because it has long since ceased to be directly connected with the needs and imperatives of this society. Voltaire’s observation that ‘Où il n’y a pas le vrai besoin, il n’y a pas le vrai plaisir’<sup>5</sup> holds here too, namely in the sense that, once a substantial need for particular cultural and intellectual goods, or indeed for any specific goods, no longer prevails in the context of social life, these goods can no longer simply continue to exist unchallenged but are seized upon by that life and exhibit a tendency to degenerate into little more than decoration. Thus you need only to recall how a concept like that of the secure, harmonious, autonomous, resilient and unwavering personality – which lay at the heart of the classical German doctrine of education and culture as expressed by Wilhelm von Humboldt – has essentially lost its substance today simply because the imperatives directed at human beings in the administered society of today are of a kind that render this ideal irrelevant or even directly negate it. In other words, what the administered world tends to require is accommodation, adaptability, the ability to fit in, and other qualities of this kind, rather than the capacity to persevere and preserve one’s own personality. I do not think it is too outrageously materialist to argue that the idea of such independent individuality is essentially connected with the idea of the free entrepreneurial spirit 

*[p. 66]* ^pg73

in which the self-reliant human being can achieve something in the world, whereas in our contemporary society this type of person can certainly no longer be described as an archetype. Now I would not want you to misunderstand me here, for I am perfectly well aware that in earlier social phases the number of free and independent individuals engaged in trade and commerce was extremely limited in relation to other members of society. Yet we can say that this idea of the liberal self-determining and self-reliant individual, who would serve the harmonious interest of the whole even as he pursues his own interests and intentions, undeniably left a significant mark on the high liberal era at least. And I should add that the traditional structure of society and the culture that was bound up with the latter, and there was no other substantial culture to talk about, formed part of these same conditions of social life as a whole. Thus one only has to think – if I may take an example from a realm that is particularly close to me – just how few individuals today, relative to the population at large, even among the so-called educated and cultured classes of society, still learn to play the piano and become acquainted in an independent way with the great tradition of music. It will then be immediately clear to you just how little what we call culture in the sense of cultural goods – namely the great works of art, of philosophy, of science, of learning – is genuinely present today for people who are not already experts or specialists of one kind or another. And the problematic thing that makes all our efforts with regard to the crisis of education and culture so extraordinarily difficult and important, what makes these things so urgent, is the fact that, while this high bourgeois liberal culture – which was once intrinsically formative and relatively homogeneous for all of its limitations – has indeed vanished, nothing has actually taken its place but the mass culture that is administered by the culture industry, a mass culture that is now falsely and misleadingly characterized as late bourgeois ‘middle-class society’.<sup>6</sup> For what we are really talking about here is in large part a fundamentally ideological phenomenon and one that is extraordinarily problematic as far as its cultural and intellectual substance is concerned.

And perhaps finally we must bear in mind that the human spirit itself, in the period we have recently lived through, has been so unutterably compromised, has proved so powerless to resist the unspeakable disaster that has afflicted the world under the totalitarian regimes of both stripes, that the world of the spirit – the cultural and intellectual things that all of us here today are inclined to assume are undeniably valuable and unproblematic – has lost much of the authority it once enjoyed, and not without its own 

*[p. 67]* ^pg74

complicity it has to be said. We might add that the whole modern development of philosophy also expresses this insofar as the doctrine of the primacy of spirit over reality, which in German idealism also formed the intellectual core of the classical conception of culture that I mentioned earlier, is also irretrievably lost, not however because human beings have become so bad, so evil, so shallow, so bereft of spirit, but for the far deeper reason that the very theory that affirmed the primacy of spirit over everything that is, or even the identity of spirit with everything that is, has proved untrue and is no longer present. If I add that mass society has now brought an enormous number of human beings into relation to the world of spirit, although they are far less qualified in terms of what was traditionally required for this than the relatively very small proportion of the population that formerly participated in it, then in an admittedly rather unsystematic way I have already outlined for you in certain crucial respects why I actually believe that this crisis of culture and education is one that concerns society as a whole; and that it would be a very serious mistake merely to ascribe this crisis to the universities, or simply to content ourselves with lamenting the intellectual and cultural level of a considerable number of the students that we have in our charge. For sentimentality of that sort is not appropriate, and I believe that the first condition for improving things is that we shed such sentimentality entirely. But that means nothing less – if something better is to present itself in all our obscure striving, and I expressly invoke here our ‘obscure striving’ and not some external ‘model’ – than this: we must look to the consciousness of the young people entrusted to us in the academic context; we must take pains to understand what our current prospects are rather than setting out to develop models of university reform that start from considerations on the overall purpose of education or proceed from above by ignoring the actual situation in a completely undialectical way. Do not misunderstand me here, for in saying this – though I hardly imagine that I, of all people, am likely to be misunderstood in this regard – I certainly do not mean to encourage the suggestion that, for fear of losing our customers, our students, to technical schools or of seeing them go elsewhere, to America perhaps, we simply have to accommodate ourselves to the views and attitudes of the students, to agree with whatever they say, to convince them that they will make better salesmen if they know how to interpret an ode by Hölderlin. That is obviously not what I mean. On the contrary, a genuine acknowledgement of the current situation among the students will unquestionably give rise to an element of fruitful tension and very fruitful resistance. In what follows I hope to be much more specific in this regard. But if we do 

*[p. 68]* ^pg75

not know the precise situation among the students – not *the* students<sup>7</sup> exactly, but at least among a very large number of them – and if we fail to acknowledge this situation and take it really seriously, then everything else will prove merely academic in the problematic sense of the word, namely quite powerless to affect anything. And if we are serious about university reform, we must make sure that it actually leads to something, rather than merely affording us the narcissistic satisfaction of beholding the abstract reflection of the ideals that we basically already share. At the same time, however, and this is something I particularly want to emphasize in relation to the facts I am about to present to you, it is also very important to consider more closely the actual state of consciousness on the part of the students, for some of the claims that we are tempted to make on the basis of direct and personal observation, but even more on the basis of hearsay, are over-simplified. For it can easily happen, especially when we encounter a negative state of things, that we fail to consider or analyse it with sufficient care, and this can lead us to exaggerate the worst side of the situation and thus to form an unrealistic judgement about the latter. I would say, therefore, that, when we look more closely at this problem regarding the general state of consciousness on the part of students, we also recognize the possibility of correcting certain judgements and exploring other avenues in ways that may all too easily be overlooked when we consider these things solely from the perspective of the cultural critic – something about which I have made certain critical observations before.<sup>8</sup>

Now the data from the survey that we have conducted in relation to students<sup>9</sup> – and specifically in relation to students in Frankfurt – are a little ‘cold’, as we say in the field of social research, for some of this research dates from several years ago now. Whether the research data would fall out differently today, and in which specific respects, is a question that could only really be answered empirically. But I would think that they indicate at least a certain tendency, and unless the *communis opinio* at this point is entirely remote from the facts, or has no connection with them whatsoever, we have to say that the ‘economic miracle’, considered specifically as an intensification of a certain vulgar materialist consciousness, has also contributed something else. I offer this relative qualification in order to protect myself from the possible charge that, while I claim certain things are true today and here, we actually cannot precisely know this for sure; but I still believe that we have very good reason to assume that what I am saying is not that far wide of the mark.

Now in Hegel’s *Phenomenology* we already come across a problem, which is indeed one of the central problems of dialectical thought, 

*[p. 69]* ^pg76

namely that there is a central contradiction in bourgeois society between what Fichte called *The Vocation of Man*,<sup>10</sup> between the inclinations, talents, needs and potentialities of human beings, and what confronts them in civil society itself – which is based on the division of labour and makes very specific and often alienating demands upon them. I believe that this contradiction has developed and unfolded ever more strongly within the historical development of society as a whole. And if I am not completely mistaken, the most distinctive thing about our contemporary situation is precisely that the quantitative growth of this contradiction has now produced something that is qualitatively new. And I would like to say a little more to you about this before I go on to present a range of interpreted data, though more for purposes of illustration than with any pretension to provide strictly reliable results.

\[…\]<sup>11</sup>

Ladies and gentlemen, by way of conclusion I would just like to say a few words about where we should go from here. First of all, I should like to make it clear that a so-called synthesis in the sense that we strive on the one hand to preserve and promote culture and education from above, while trying to relate them directly to practical demands on the other, seems to me to represent a far too external and superficial view of the situation. In this I fully endorse the opinion of Schoenberg, who claimed that the only road that doesn’t lead to Rome is the middle road.<sup>12</sup> But I would like to emphasize at least three aspects here – so that we can escape the sphere of more or less either edifying or pessimistic generalities right from the start – aspects that I think can all be related to one another in a meaningful way. On the one hand – this is the first point – you will actually find the ideology of culture and education, albeit in the watered-down form of a general ‘overview’ and things of that sort, among a relatively large proportion of students. This concept of a general culture and education is certainly upheld in an overly pragmatic form, if only in the sense of the line from the operetta that declares ‘If I only have your love, I do not need fidelity’.<sup>13</sup> In other words, once human beings have tasted blood, once they have at least come into contact with that sphere of mind or spirit that transcends the practical realm of utility, this sphere immediately takes on a certain sense of allure, of radiance, of the beautiful. And human beings may well develop a genuine awareness, especially if we help them in this, that their hard-nosed realism actually denies them something that in truth they actually desire for themselves. And perhaps something could really be accomplished on this basis – for I believe that we should not make things too easy for ourselves with the critical and damning judgement 

*[p. 70]* ^pg77

of so-called ideologies, because the latter must also harbour certain truth moments if they are to seem convincing in the first place, and because we can try and change things precisely by concentrating on these truth moments. If a person exhibits a very bad character, yet attempts to rationalize the latter by announcing all kinds of good and noble motives for his behaviour, this shows at least that such motives somehow already appear in the spiritual economy of the person in question. And the more seriously we take these things, the more we encourage that person to draw the relevant conclusions, the sooner it looks possible to try and change the original situation.

In this connection I would also like to emphasize, especially after what I have said about the reification of consciousness among so-called experts and professionals, that wherever we encounter some particular professional interest,<sup>14</sup> wherever groups of people share a passionate interest in some specific field or object, we inevitably discover that some intellectual and cultural element is also involved. We find this element with any human being, with any worker, with any craftsman who is passionately interested in what they do or make – in the sense that they care about the matter in hand as more than simply a means to an end, as something with which they are engaged and to which they are committed, as something in terms of which they also determine themselves precisely by working on it and with it as they do.<sup>15</sup> And my own view, if I may be allowed to make this suggestion while standing on one foot, as it were,<sup>16</sup> is just this: that in thinking about the ‘specialists’, as Ibsen calls them,<sup>17</sup> we should not actually try and dissolve this particular specialist interest within the broader context of more general educational and cultural concerns but focus far more intensively upon it – in such a way that immersion in the matter at hand, in the thing itself, comes to shed its own light, and we learn that the shortest path to genuine universality is to be found precisely when we abandon the quest for the merely general. We must never imagine that the relationship of the mind and spirit to what appears alien to it is the same as the relationship between the abstract universal and the merely concrete, for the path of potential sublimation, of potential ‘spiritualization’, can only be pursued through concretion.<sup>18</sup>

In conclusion, if the observations that I was able to present only in brief here today are correct, we must say in regard to the pseudo-concrete and utilitarian outlook which characterizes the second type of student, as we have found,<sup>19</sup> that a certain resistance also plays a very significant role, inasmuch as the students forcibly deny themselves what they wish for all the same. And if we are genuine pedagogues – and while we are not all equally gifted in this respect, 

*[p. 71]* ^pg78

and I am the last person to claim this ability for myself, we should acknowledge this as a desideratum – it would certainly represent an advance if we could help this incipient human resistance to become more conscious of itself, and thus rouse it into the self-consciousness of its own condition and situation. And I would perhaps go further and say that the path that leads through these problems that I have only briefly touched upon today can only be the path of self-reflection, of concrete self-reflection in terms of the matter itself, rather than any merely general preaching on behalf of some ideal that is held up in contrast to the current situation. If we could help human beings themselves to become conscious that what they say is not actually what they mean or think, then I believe that things would already change as a result. For I would like to say, once again, that the situation here is not unequivocally clear but is in truth an antagonistic situation and a contradictory one; and a contradictory situation has two sides to it. And we should not forget, simply through fear of one of these sides, that the other side can perhaps be allowed or encouraged to speak as well. That is all that I really wished to say to you here today.
