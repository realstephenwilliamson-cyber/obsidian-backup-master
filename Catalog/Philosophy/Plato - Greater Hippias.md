## About

A dialogue attributed to Plato, also called the *Greater Hippias*. Socrates questions the visiting sophist Hippias of Elis on what the fine or beautiful (*to kalon*) is; seven attempted definitions fail, and the talk ends without an answer.

- **People and setting:** Socrates and Hippias, who has been away from Athens for a long time on diplomatic missions for Elis, notably to Sparta, and is about to lecture in Phidostratus' schoolroom. Socrates invents an unnamed acquaintance who keeps challenging him, a third voice used for comedy. Wikipedia gives no dramatic date.
- **Composition:** uncertain. Wikipedia reports Woodruff's suggestion of about 390 BC. The IEP places it in the early group, written after Socrates' death and before Plato's first journey to Sicily in 387 BC.
- **Authenticity:** disputed. The IEP gives the strongest point against it: no ancient source mentions the work. Dorothy Tarrant (1928) argued it was spurious, and Wikipedia calls C. H. Kahn (1985) the lone modern holder of that view. Grube (1926–27), Guthrie (1975), Woodruff (1982) and Ledger's computer study (1989) favour genuineness. Wikipedia says recent research finds it more likely authentic than not; the IEP says most scholars find it fits Plato's style and content.
- **Definitions tried:** a beautiful girl; gold; wealth, health and honour with a fine burial; the appropriate; the useful; the beneficial; pleasure through sight and hearing. Socrates closes with the proverb that fine things are difficult.
- **Pages and name:** Stephanus 281a–304e. "Major" separates it from the *Hippias Minor*, which has the same chief character.
- **Place among the dialogues:** like the *Charmides*, *Lysis* and *Euthyphro*, it is anatreptic: it refutes common opinions without necessarily offering a solution (Wikipedia). Wikipedia adds that the idea of something good in itself first appears here, at least obliquely.
