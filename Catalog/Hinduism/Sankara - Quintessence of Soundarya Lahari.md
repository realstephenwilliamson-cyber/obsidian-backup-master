## About

A Sanskrit hymn to the goddess Parvati as Tripura Sundari, traditionally ascribed to Adi Shankara. The title means "the waves of beauty"; W. Norman Brown rendered it *Flood of Beauty*. Wikipedia describes it both as a hymn collection and as a Tantra textbook.

- **Attribution:** Wikipedia gives it to Pushpadanta as well as Shankara. One legend has Gaudapada, the teacher of Shankara's teacher, memorize Pushpadanta's verses and pass them down; another has Shiva give Shankara a 100-verse manuscript, which Nandi tears, so that Shankara keeps 41 verses and writes 59. Wikipedia's statement that scholars place its composition in Kashmir cites Raghunāthācārya (2002) but carries a "who?" tag, because the scholars are not named. The title of Brown's edition calls the work only traditionally ascribed to Shankara (HathiTrust).
- **Date:** neither source opened gives a date of composition.
- **Parts:** Wikipedia's opening gives 103 verses, but its account of the parts uses 100: verses 1–41, the *Ananda Lahari*, on inner worship, kundalini, the Sri Chakra and mantras; verses 42–100, mainly on the goddess's appearance.
- **Contents:** a Shakta Tantra text on puja and the Sri Yantra, pairing about 100 hymns with roughly 100 yantras and the practice and results that go with them. It treats reality as non-dual, with Shiva as the holder of power and Shakti as power.
- **Commentaries and translations:** more than 36 Sanskrit commentaries, including Lakshmidhara's and Kameshvarasuri's *Arunamodini*. Virai Kaviraja Pandithar made a Tamil version in the 12th century. In English: Brown (Harvard Oriental Series 43, Harvard University Press, 1958); Subrahmanya Sastri and Srinivasa Ayyangar (1937); Swami Tapasyananda (1987); Mani Rao (2022).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it a famous Sanskrit literary work, and stresses that it is a Tantra textbook as well as a collection of hymns. It has also reached concert music: John Tavener arranged 100 of its verses as the orchestral work *Flood of Beauty*.
