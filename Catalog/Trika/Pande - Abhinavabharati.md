## About

Abhinavagupta's Sanskrit commentary on the *Nāṭyaśāstra*, Bharata's treatise on drama, and the oldest surviving commentary on it. Its best-known contribution is to the theory of rasa, the aesthetic savour that a performance yields.

- **Author:** Abhinavagupta, Kashmiri Śaiva teacher of the Pratyabhijñā (recognition) school, who wrote more than 35 works, the largest being the *Tantrāloka*. Dates differ: the Wikipedia articles on the commentary and on the *Nāṭyaśāstra* give c. 950–1020; the article on Abhinavagupta gives c. 950–1016; Britannica says he flourished in 1014.
- **The text commented on:** Wikipedia describes the *Nāṭyaśāstra* as 36 chapters and about 6,000 verses, first compiled between 200 BCE and 200 CE; Britannica dates Bharata to the 1st century BCE–3rd century CE. Its music theory fills chapters 28–34; chapter 28 sets out the scale, the śruti and the seven svaras.
- **Rasa theory:** Abhinavagupta reads Bharata's rasa-sūtra through Ānandavardhana's theory of expression (abhivyakti) in the *Dhvanyāloka* and through the Pratyabhijñā philosophy. Aesthetic experience is the self manifesting its innate dispositions, such as love and sorrow; the spectator contemplates the self's bliss, and the characters are universalised, which makes the experience akin to spiritual experience.
- **Predecessors:** the commentary cites older commentators on the *Nāṭyaśāstra*, among them Kīrtidhara, Bhāskara and Lollaṭa, whose works are lost or not yet found (Wikipedia, "Natya Shastra").
- **Edition:** Wikipedia cites a three-volume Delhi edition of the *Nāṭyaśāstra* with the commentary, edited by Pushpendra Kumar with M. M. Ghosh's translation (New Bharatiya Book Corporation, 2006).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it the most studied commentary on the *Nāṭyaśāstra* and one of the main reasons for Abhinavagupta's fame.
