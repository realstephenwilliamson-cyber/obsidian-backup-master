## About

The oldest of the four Vedas: a collection of hymns (sūktas) in Vedic Sanskrit, addressed chiefly to Indra, Agni and Soma. It is the oldest known Vedic Sanskrit text and belongs to the class of scripture called śruti.

- **Size:** 10 books (maṇḍalas) and 1,028 hymns (Wikipedia and Britannica). Wikipedia's opening gives about 10,600 verses; its infobox gives 10,552, from Aufrecht's 1877 edition. Books 2–7, the family books, are the oldest; books 1 and 10, with 191 hymns each, the youngest. Book 9 is wholly devoted to Soma.
- **Date:** Britannica gives about 1500 BCE. Wikipedia gives c. 1500–1000 BCE for most of it, notes a wider estimate of c. 1900–1200 BCE, and lists others: Witzel and Flood c. 1500–1200, Anthony c. 1500–1300, Oberlies 1700–1100. Jamison and Brereton expect the question to stay contested.
- **Place:** the north-west of the subcontinent; Britannica says the Punjab region of India and Pakistan.
- **Transmission:** handed down orally. The word-by-word recitation (padapāṭha) is ascribed to Śākalya, and only the Śākala recension survives in full. Britannica says it was written down about 300 BCE; Wikipedia reports estimates from the 3rd century BCE to the end of the first millennium BCE. The oldest surviving manuscripts date to about 1040 CE (Nepal). Thirty manuscripts in Pune, the oldest of 1464, entered UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2007.
- **Commentary and editions:** Sāyaṇa's 14th-century commentary covers the whole text. Rosen's Latin version of 121 hymns (1830) was the first in a European language. English translations include H. H. Wilson (1850–88), Griffith (1889–92) and Jamison and Brereton (three volumes, 2014). Van Nooten and Holland (1994) made the first attempt to restore the whole text to its metrical form; Tulsi Ram's translation appeared in Delhi in 2013.
- **Why it matters:** its early layers are among the oldest Indo-European texts and matter for reconstructing Proto-Indo-European. Some verses are still recited at rites such as weddings.
