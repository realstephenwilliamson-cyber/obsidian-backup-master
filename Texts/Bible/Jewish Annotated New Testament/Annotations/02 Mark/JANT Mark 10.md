### 10.1–16: Marriage, divorce, and children

*[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-1|Text]], p. 90* · ([[Matthew 19#^19-1|Mt 19.1–15]]; [[Luke 18#^18-15|Lk 18.15–17]]). ^10-1

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-1|1]]:** Jesus travels into Judea, where Jerusalem is located. ^10-1-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-2|2–9]]:** As in 2.25–26, after a challenge from the Pharisees (v 2), the original clever 48) are lacking in the best ancient authorities d Other ancient authorities either add or substitute *and every sacrifice will be salted with salt* e Or *how can you restore its saltiness?* f Other ancient authorities lack *and* retort (v 9) is supplemented by a scriptural and legal argument (vv 3–8). Here a rigor greater than that in Torah is commended (cf. [[Matthew 05#^5-20|Mt 5.20]],[[Matthew 05#^5-31|31–32]]; [[Matthew 23#^23-1|23.1–3]]): the *commandment* allowing divorce ([[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^24-1|Deut 24.1–4]]) was given because of *hardness of heart.* Mark insists that the prohibition of divorce goes back to creation ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-27|Gen 1.27]]; [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^2-24|2.24]]), a legal move not unlike Paul’s argument of which scriptural passages take priority ([[Galatians 03#^3-17|Gal 3.17]]). The prohibition of divorce appears in other NT texts ([[Matthew 05#^5-32|Mt 5.32]]; [[1 Corinthians 07#^7-10|1 Cor 7.10–11]]) and likely derives from Jesus. Claims that it was introduced to protect women from being abandoned are not supported by any of these texts. The Qumran sect also prohibited divorce with the same scriptural argument: marriage was ordained at creation (CD 4.19–5.2). Among Jesus’ followers the prohibition of divorce might have addressed those who were separating for celibacy ([[Matthew 19#^19-10|Mt 19.10–12]]; [[Luke 18#^18-29|Lk 18.29–30]]; [[1 Corinthians 07#^7-5|1 Cor 7.5]]; see also [[LXX Wisdom of Solomon 04#^4-1|Wis 4.1–9]]). ^10-2

> [!note] Jesus Dying as a Ransom *(p. 90)*
> Verse 45 is the climax of the middle section of Mark’s Gospel, and it expresses a great irony. James and John expect the Son of Man to come in glory, but Jesus insists that “the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many.” The suffering servant of Isa 52–53 likely underlies this pronouncement, but “ransom” (Gk *lytron*) is unclear. Though there has been lengthy discussion among Christian theologians about how this ransom works—what it accomplishes and how—there is no agreement about the meaning of “give his life a ransom for many” in its Gospel setting. Theories include the idea that Jesus’ death pays off the
>
> “debt” of human’s sins, either to Satan or to God, to avert divine retribution (see above on “cup,” v 38n.), or as a sacrifice (like the Temple sacrifices) to cleanse the followers of Jesus from their sins. In the first century, among Jews and Jesus’ followers, a notion of sins as debts and remission of sins as paying off those debts was common (cf. [[Matthew 06#^6-12|Mt 6.12]]). In the previous passage, giving alms was acquiring “treasure in heaven” (10.21), and the ransom Jesus pays may be analogous: by dying he pays off the debt of sins, but to Satan or God? The phrase “for many” can indicate a sacrifice in payment for humans’ sins (see 14.24n.; [[Isaiah 53#^53-12|Isa 53.12]]), but only in the eleventh century did this notion come to dominate western Christian theology.

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-11|11–12]]:** Biblical law allowed only men to initiate divorce ([[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^24-1|Deut 24.1–4]]), but in this period Jewish women, in accordance with Roman law, also initiated divorces, as Mark and Paul indicate. ^10-11

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-13|13–16]]:** *Receive the kingdom of God as a little child*, without regard to one’s position in it. ^10-13

### 10.17–31: Riches and entering the kingdom of God

*[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-17|Text]], p. 91* · ([[Matthew 19#^19-16|Mt 19.16–30]]; [[Luke 18#^18-18|Lk 18.18–30]]). ^10-17

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-18|18]]:** *Why do you call me good?* Jesus’ reply is based on the Torah that the young man has known all his life; he does not need a new teaching. ^10-18

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-19|19–20]]:** The *commandments* here are from the second half of the Decalogue ([[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-1|Ex 20.1–17]]), those concerning interpersonal relations. ^10-19

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-21|21]]:** Many biblical passages mandate giving *to the poor* ([[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^24-13|Deut 24.13–22]]; Am 2.6; [[Psalms 085#^85-12|Ps 85.12]]; [[Psalms 089#^89-15|89.15]]; [[Proverbs 10#^10-2|Prov 10.2]]; [[Proverbs 19#^19-17|19.17]]; [[LXX Tobit 04#^4-5|Tob 4.5–7]]; [[LXX Sirach 07#^7-29|Sir 7.29–36]]), as did the rabbis (*t. Peʾah* 4.19; *Lev. Rab.* 3.1), but giving all of one’s possessions is not commended in rabbinic tradition, since that would put one’s family at risk. Some NT texts promote a higher level of giving ([[Acts 02#^2-43|Acts 2.43–47]]; [[Acts 04#^4-32|4.32–5.11]]; 2 Cor 8–9; [[James 02#^2-1|Jas 2.1–7]]). ^10-21

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-25|25]]:** Contrary to a commonly cited medieval legend, there is no narrow “Eye of the Needle” gate in Jerusalem. The Talmud ([[Berakhot 55#55b|*b. Ber.* 55b]]) uses a needle’s eye and an *wife* b Gk lacks *the money* c Other ancient authorities add *for those who trust in riches* elephant to make the same point. ^10-25

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-28|28–31]]:** This dialogue between Jesus and Peter lies right before the last of the three Passion predictions (see Introduction and 8.22–10.52n.) and is thus near the end of a long transitional arc between the Galilean ministry (1–8.26) and the Passion (11.1–16.8). ^10-28

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-29|29–30]]:** *House or brothers or sisters*, in new religious movements, followers often leave their homes and deny their familial relationships, but they are recompensed with fictive kin and new households. Here followers of Jesus comprise the new family. The new father will be God (see 3.31–35n.; 11.25n.; 14.36n.). ^10-29

### 10.32–45: Third Passion prediction

*[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-32|Text]], p. 92* · ([[Matthew 20#^20-17|Mt 20.17–28]]; [[Luke 18#^18-31|Lk 18.31–34]]; [[Luke 22#^22-24|22.24–27]]). ^10-32

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-37|37]]:** *Sit* at *right hand* and *left, in your glory,* James and John foresee Jesus enthroned with God, perhaps as judge of the coming age, and want to be seated on each side. [[Psalms 110#^110-1|Ps 110.1]] provides the background for this, but *1 En.* 45.3 and Rev 4–5 also show an application of this to an apocalyptic time frame. ^10-37

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-38|38]]:** *Cup*, a metaphor for consequences, usually suffering for sin, that must be accepted ([[Psalms 075#^75-9|Ps 75.8]]; cf. [[Isaiah 51#^51-17|Isa 51.17]],[[Isaiah 51#^51-22|22]]); Jesus’ metaphor indicates that he is accepting the cup in place of others; see 14.36n. *Baptism*, here Jesus’ special destiny. ^10-38

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-40|40]]:** *For whom it has been prepared*, perhaps the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, on the basis of [[Matthew 08#^8-11|Mt 8.11]]; [[Luke 13#^13-28|Lk 13.28]] (see [[Mark 12#^12-24|Mk 12.24–27]]). ^10-40

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-42|42–45]]:** A concise description of the Roman political system. As the previous two chapters indicate, Jesus’ followers are to create an alternative community. ^10-42

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-42|42]]:** *Great ones*, 9.33–37. ^10-42-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-45|45]]:** *Serve*, see [[Isaiah 52#^52-13|Isa 52.13]]. *Ransom*, see “Jesus Dying as a Ransom,” p. 90. ^10-45

### 10.46–52: Blind Bartimaeus

*[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-46|Text]], p. 92* · ([[Matthew 20#^20-29|Mt 20.29–34]]; [[Luke 18#^18-35|Lk 18.35–43]]). ^10-46

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-46|46]]:** *Bartimaeus*, means *son of Timaeus*. ^10-46-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 10#^10-47|47]]:** *Son of David, have mercy on me*, some contemporary Jewish healers healed in the name of Solomon, who was literally the son of David (Josephus, *Ant.* 8.42–49). The Son of David healing formula may have influenced the followers of Jesus. In *Pss. Sol.* 17.21 the coming king will be the son of David: “See, O Lord, and raise up for them their king, the son of David / at the time which you choose, O God, to rule over Israel your servant.” In both Matthew (1.6–7) and Luke (1.27; 3.31), Joseph is David’s descendant, but Mark lacks a genealogy. ^10-47
