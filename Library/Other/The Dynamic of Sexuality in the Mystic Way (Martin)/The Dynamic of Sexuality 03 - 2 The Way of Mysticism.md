## 2 The Way of Mysticism

### Views of Mysticism

It is a problematic undertaking to define mysticism, for there has been a range of reported experiences. Apart from the variations that occur within particular traditions, there are also manifold differences across religions.[^1] William Inge, writing in 1899, listed twenty-six definitions of mysticism by various, mainly European writers.[^2] A landmark study by William James in 1902 proposed a set of criteria by which mystical experiences may be characterized; namely, ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, and passivity.[^3] He used this so-called ‘mystical group’ to underpin his analysis of a range of mystical experiences, which were culled from anecdotal and written evidence of the period, as well as from various religious traditions, with a plain emphasis upon Christianity. James viewed mystical experiences as being authoritative, if only for the recipient; and he suggested that there existed a weak case for a transcultural commonality of religious mystical experience. James’ criteria can be shown to be flawed in a number of ways; however, it is beyond the scope of this thesis to give a detailed critique.[^4] 

Evelyn Underhill, in her exposition of the mystic way, presented the normative goal of mysticism as being that of a unified consciousness with Ultimate Reality, which could be conceptualized in a personal or non-personal way.[^5] She rejected James’ criteria, suggesting instead that mysticism can be characterized as being active and practical; as being wholly transcendental and spiritual; as being fixated upon the all-encompassing One, who is a personal object of love; and as having the soteriological goal of ecstatic union with this One Being. She paid marginal attention to non-Christian traditions. Rudolf Otto in a seminal work,[^6] argued that all religions are based on the *mysterium, tremendum et fascinans*, or a fascinated attention to the awful mystery of God.[^7] Indeed, the numinous experience of God formed the very basis for understanding mystical endeavour. Robert Zaehner, in his examination of western and eastern mysticisms, and building upon the work of Otto, distinguished three types of mysticism: panenhenic, monistic, and theistic (the last being, he thought, the highest form).[^9] By panenhenic is meant experiences of unity or rapport with nature, which engender a sense of unbounded consciousness; they are typified within the Indian context by the Upaniṣads. In monistic mysticism there is an experience of undifferentiated unity, which is achieved by centering within oneself; this mode finds its supreme expression in the school of thought known as Advaita Vedānta. In theistic mysticism there is a sense of unified consciousness with a transcendent God; such an outlook is exemplified in Christian mysticism, Indian Sāṁkhya, and the Vaiṣṇavite philosophy of emotional *bhakti*. These varieties of mystical experience illustrate, for Zaehner, that there cannot be a common platform. Walter Stace likewise followed Otto in his typology of mystical experience, referring to the ‘introvertive’ and ‘extrovertive’ type. He argued furthermore that a clear distinction could and should be made between the actual mystical experience and the subsequent interpretation placed upon that experience.[^10] 

In terms of the psychological assessment of mysticism the work of Abraham Maslow has been notable. He uses the term ‘peak experience’ to refer to an awareness of a transcendent quality in existence, which leads to a sense of completeness and personal fulfilment. Such ‘peak experiences’, he believes, are open to almost all people, and are attainable during sexual or spiritual events. From a tantric point of view, to the extent that a sexual experience is a ‘peak’ experience it is just a spiritual experience. Maslow sees peak experiences as being based in the natural world, and in that sense they can be researched and investigated. He further believes that the use of hallucinogenic drugs enables some control over these experiences.[^11] Such a proposal has been taken up more than once by investigators. For example, Walter Pahnke performed a controlled experiment in which the drug psilocybin was administered to graduate student volunteers.[^12] A nine-category typology of the mystical state of consciousness was derived, based on the work of Stace. It was found that the experimental group scored higher on these characteristics than did the control group. Pahnke concluded that the ingestion of psilocybin acted as the ‘facilitating agent for the difference in the phenomena experienced’.[^13] Moreover, the biochemical basis to mystical experiences may be stronger than previously supposed, with ‘\[s\]ome of the indolic substances in the body \[not differing greatly\] from the psychedelic drugs’.[^14]

That said, Brainard then approaches the issue of defining ‘mystical experience’ through an examination of nominal and real definitions. A nominal definition is a statement about the meaning of a word or phrase within its ‘community of discourse’; that is, as a lexical definition or stipulative definition in a particular application. A real definition is a statement about the objects, events, or other phenomena to which the word or phrase refers. So, a nominal definition of ‘mystical experience’ would place the term in its discursive context and explicate the interest or intention lying behind its continued existence and use, while a real definition would purport to give traits that exist in the experiences themselves, independent of the observer. He then asks, ‘\[d\]o the traits that characterize “mystical experience” actually exist as part of experiential phenomena independent of the mind that identifies an experience, or are the defining characteristics strictly categories of thought for the observer?’[^16] Philosophically, this question turns on the debate over the reality of ‘universals’.[^17] For a nominalist (nonrealist), ‘universals’ are dependent on the mind of the observer, and arise from ‘shared, persisting interests and intentions embedded in words and language’.[^18] For a realist, universals preexist outside the mind and we discover them. Brainard concludes that the way we choose to read, and approach, mystical texts may or may not lead us to take account of the experiential content. 

Of passing importance then is what definition of mysticism, if any, this thesis will employ. ### Women and Mysticism in the Christian Milieu

Bernard McGinn, in his monumental work on *The Presence of God: A History of Western Christian Mysticism*,[^22] charts the growth and practice of mystical endeavour in a sustained informative manner. For the first millennium of Christianity mystical experience was conceived of within the context of reading and praying the biblical text, particularly within a community or monastic setting.[^23] Subsequently however by the twelfth century mystical understanding took on a more experiential flavour, of a personal encounter with God beyond the text. This was so especially for women, who were not generally privileged to have access to Scripture, and were prohibited from teaching, except by example. Whereas male monastics and scholastics were invested with authority by virtue of their positions, women’s claims to authority were naturally circumscribed.[^24] They were allowed to teach in a private way if that teaching derived, *ex beneficio*, from divine favour granted by the Holy Spirit, and of course as long as they used sound doctrine. The use of Latin was predominant in scholastic writings, but after 1200 there was a noticeable tendency to use vernacular languages; equally, poetry in the vernacular became a significant means of expression of mystical consciousness. Such is the revolutionary nature entailed by the use of vernacular languages to convey mystical experiences, and the rise of gender and denominational issues, that for McGinn it constitutes a new stage in the history of Western mysticism.[^25]

The idea of mysticism as comprising intense, personal, and ineffable experiences is critiqued by Grace Jantzen.[^29] She argues that this modern construction is not in accord with the mediaeval exemplars of Christian mysticism; such as, Hildegard of Bingen, Bernard of Clairvaux, Mechthild of Magdeburg, Meister Eckhart, or Julian of Norwich. Certainly, the modern philosophical construction of mysticism as a private and subjective affair is quite different from the classical Christian notions of mysticism, with its emphasis on reading the text in a mystical light. There is an issue of power here in that access to scripture was bound up with ecclesiastical – and by extension worldly – authority. Since women were denied access to the text of the Bible through their lack of education, and the ecclesiastical bars, they could not assert their own forms of mystical behaviour, and as a consequence posed no threat to male defined notions of mysticism. However, as they eventually began to develop novel (sensible, or sense-directed) forms of mysticism, which were not dependent on intimate knowledge of the texts and theological learning, they started to pose a threat to Church sanctioned ideas of mysticism, and were consequently traduced and silenced. Later again, with the separation of Church and State, when religious experience was no longer seen as a source of knowledge and power, it then became acceptable for women to have mystical experiences. In short, the definition of mysticism, especially as it involves ‘ineffability’, has altered over time in such a way as to preserve (male) power bases.

Amy Hollywood explores the relationship between the texts and theological understandings of Mechthild of Magdeburg, Marguerite Porete, and Meister Eckhart.[^41] She questions the emotionalizing and somatizing ascriptions placed on female mystics, and the undue emphasis on suffering. For male clerics the wounded body represented the visible activity of God; and women religious, in inscribing pain upon themselves, or having it inscribed upon them by their hagiographers, derived or gained the teaching authority that was otherwise denied to women. However, by locating sinfulness, temptations and detachment within the will, Mechthild and Marguerite sought to undermine this association of women with the fleshly. Redemption is found in the union of the soul with God, and the encumbering will has to be overcome to achieve that goal. Hollywood argues that Eckhart, in his metaphysic of detachment, followed the idea of union without distinction espoused by Mechthild and Porete. 

Ulrike Wiethaus investigates the ecstatic experiences of Mechthild by taking as her point of departure the humanistic psychologies of Abraham Maslow and Roberto Assagioli.[^42] Maslow proposed the value of the self-actualizing experience, in which there is an innate urge by human beings to find a better and more complete sense of self. Assagioli promoted a ‘psychosynthesis’, in which realization of a ‘higher self’ is attained by unifying and enhancing otherwise disjointed and underdeveloped aspects of the psyche. The ecstatic experiences of Mechthild, along with those of the numerous other women mystics of the late Middle Ages, can be seen as equivalent to modern day altered states of consciousness, argues Wiethaus. She concludes that the similarities between Mechthild’s mediaeval mystical approach and modern humanistic psychology support the thesis that transpersonal experiences ‘extend beyond culture-bound theoretical superstructures’.

### Hindu Mediaeval Mysticism

The primary texts of Hindu thought are contained within a body of literature compiled by the sage Vyāsa, from the revelations received by the ancient seers (*ṛṣi*), and communicated orally over a long period of time. These Vedas (‘knowledge’, ‘sacred teaching’) are the foundation for most of the later developments in what is known as Hinduism.[^43] The priestly class of the Vedic Aryans, the Brahmans, continues to preserve and safeguard its heritage. The later Upaniṣads (‘secret scripture’) are a series of glosses based around the Vedas, and are generally believed to have been composed from about 600 to 300 BCE, although texts with the title *upaniṣad* continued to be composed through the mediaeval and modern periods.[^44] They expound on the requirement of wisdom in order to gain transcendence and liberation; and the explicit goal is ultimate identification with *brahman*. Upaniṣadic poetry is expressly designed to impart notions of Truth, unlike Western poetry, which is for the most part not framed to impart such ideas. While both traditions treat of beauty and aesthetic values, Hindu poetry absolutizes beauty as the ultimate expression of *brahman*.[^45] 

In the *Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad*, composed around the fifth or fourth centuries BCE, there is a distinction between the Lord Rudra as Supreme Being (Éśa, who is later Śiva) and the individual soul, which is without gender, journeying from body to body according to its karma until liberated through the efforts of yoga and the grace of the Lord (*prasāda*). This text represents a transition from the simpler monism of the Upaniṣads to the theism of the later Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava traditions.[^46] Although both the Vedas and Upaniṣads seek the apprehension of truth as the highest goal, they do so through different means; the former does so through outer worship, and the latter does so through inner worship. The most influential school of Indian theology has been the Vedānta (a compound of *Veda* and ‘anta’, end), which is recognized as the central philosophical paradigm of Hinduism. Vedānta refers to the Upaniṣads and their teachings, together with the subsequent traditions that they inspired. The pithy form of the Sūtra (‘thread’) is used to convey and summarize the teachings of the Veda and Upaniṣads. The foundation text is Bādarāyaṇa’s *Brahma Sūtra*, also called the *Vedānta Sūtra* and *Uttara Mīmāṁsā Sūtra*, composed around 200 BCE; this text concerns itself with investigating the realm of *brahman*. There are three important traditions of Vedānta: Advaita (‘Non-Dualist’), Viśiṣṭādvaita (‘Qualified Non-Dualist’), and Dvaita (‘Dualist’).[^47] 

The great exponent of Advaita was Śaṅkara, or Śaṅkarācārya, born in Kerala at an uncertain date, possibly 788 CE. He became a renouncer, composed commentaries, and established a monastic order, the Daśanāmis, which has centres at Śṛṅgerī, Dwarka, Badrinath and Puri. It is generally accepted that he wrote commentaries on *Bṛhadāranyaka* and *Taittirīya Upaniṣads*, and the independent work, the ‘Thousand Teachings’ (*Upadeśasaharī*). He propounded a philosophy in which the self (*ātman*) is ontologically identical with the absolute (*brahman*), and in order to realize that truth it is necessary to cultivate discrimination: between the self and the not-self; and between knowledge (*vidyā*, *jñāna*) and ignorance (*avidyā*). Consequently, Śaṅkara extols those sections of sacred scripture that deal with knowledge (*jñānakāṇḍa*), rather than with action, which, taken alone, is incapable of giving liberation. Although both the Advaita Vedānta and Tantric systems are non–dualistic in outlook there are still great differences.[^50] In the Vedānta the Absolute is the only Real, while the phenomenal world, which is made up of the many things that we experience, is just an illusion; in the same way, a rope seen in the gloaming may appear as a snake. So, for the Śākta, creation is real, as is the consequent striving for liberation. 

The word *tantra* derives from the verbal root *tan-*, ‘to stretch, expound’ and *tra-*, ‘to save’. It is applied in some Indian literature to a technical ‘exposé’ or ‘handbook’, which tries to deal with a certain subject in a more or less extensive way. It can thus mean simply ‘tradition’, or ‘specialism’, as for example in the word *Bhūtatantra* ‘the lore of exorcism of spirits’, or *Mātṛtantra* ‘the set of rules for the worship of Mother goddesses’.[^51] It gradually came to denote individual religious texts of Śaiva, and especially Śākta, denomination. Those who follow the way of *tantras* are called *tāntrikas*. Although ‘Tantra’ is often indistinguishable from ‘Āgama’, with the literature of some older Tantric sources being referred to as such, there are none the less characteristic differences between Śaiva Āgama or Vaiṣṇava Saṁhitā texts and typically Tantric texts. speculations on the (usually bipolar) nature of the Absolute, cosmogony, the creative nature of sound and word, micro-macrocosmic equivalence, the powers of speech, communication and handling of mantras (very important and frequent), symbolic interpretations of words and names, construction of and initiation into *maṇḍalas* and worship of deities therein (this ritual aspect of Tantric lore is also very important); many pages are devoted to prediction, the obtaining of magical proficiency, varieties of Kuṇḍalinīyoga, etc. [^53]

### Comparative Mysticism

The notion that diverse religions share a common background, that they are equally valid epistemologically, and that it is only in the culturally specific interpretations where differences arise, has its proponents,<sup>92</sup> but it is not for the most part favourably reviewed in scholarly circles. Similarly, a common core has been allowed for the ostensibly differing mystical experiences within Western and Eastern traditions, making due allowance for the possibility of sub–categorizations within that range.<sup>93</sup> Alternatively, a plurality of types of experiences has also been proposed.<sup>94</sup> The actual experience of a mystical event, or events, and the interpretative framework upon which subsequent articulation is built, raises problematic issues. In particular, it has been suggested that mystical experiences are basically informed by one’s cultural and social background. This view is championed by the ‘Katzian’ school.<sup>95</sup> For his part, Katz argued that there are no pure, that is unmediated experiences, and one’s beliefs, concepts and expectations actually shape the kind of mystical experience that one has.<sup>96</sup> This has two main implications, he asserts: firstly, no clear distinction can be made between the experience and the interpretation of that experience, as Stace would otherwise seek to have it; and secondly, given the diversity of cultural experiences, there are bound to be diverse mystical experiences. This ‘constructivist’ position has been criticized in turn by Forman,<sup>97</sup> and Shear,<sup>98</sup> who contend that the so-called Pure Consciousness Event (PCE), or introvertive mystical experience, can hardly be the outcome of one’s past experiences and concepts, since by definition these PCEs involve no empirical content. There have been a number of comparative studies of Christian and Hindu mysticisms.[^99] Pertinently, some of these studies have focussed on affective kinds and devotional kinds (*bhakti*), for which obvious similarities may be adduced. Sandra Eveland compares the Kṛṣṇa yearnings of Mīrābāī with those of Mechthild of Magdeburg;[^100] Deirdre Green charts the intersection of *bhakti* poetry and Carmelite mysticism;[^101] while June McDaniel contrasts the notions of divine love in Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism and Catholic mysticism.[^102] Most recently, Denise Henusek explores the mystical experiences of the Vaiṣṇava saint Rāmānuja (ca. \[illegible\] in relation to the apparently similar theistic mysticism of St John of the Cross.[^103] There have been no major comparative studies in English that focus on Christian mysticisms and Tantric systems. Moreover, I am not aware of any research that considers the particular focus of the present study, which will compare the mystical endeavours reported by the beguine Mechthild of Magdeburg, with the descriptive theology of the Śrīvidyā tradition of Śākta Tantrism. 

A final point must be made about the limitations imposed upon this study due to the lack of access to original sources. As the nuances of language can be occluded in translations, there is a danger of drawing unsubstantiated interpretations. The original sources derive from thirteenth century Germany, in the case of Mechthild, and mediaeval India, in the case of Tantrism. Since I am not conversant with either mediaeval German or Sanksrit I shall have to rely on translations into English. The original manuscript of Mechthild’s book, written in a dialect that is derived from Low and Central German, is now lost and only Latin and Middle High German versions survive. Much scholarly work has been done in Germany on Mechthild’s text, culminating in a recent two volume critical edition of the Middle High German manuscripts, with separate notes, by Hans Neumann.[^104] Since these are not available to me I shall rely on a recent translation into English by the noted Germanist Frank Tobin, who utilized Neumann’s work.
