## Chapter 10

GUATTARI AND STIEGLER 

ON THE THERAPEUTIC OBJECT: 

OBJET RE-*PETIT*-IVE A-B-C 

Here I wish to pursue an analysis of the potential link between the thinkers Félix Guattari and Bernard Stiegler (Bradley, 2021a, 2022a) as I see in both thinkers a profound rumination on the question of therapeutic care and curation at the institutional level. My concern is with the institutional object and its deadly repetitions (‘repetition automatism’ linked to the death drive as the institution itself suffers from forms of traumatic stress disorder). We can take the institutional object to mean something like the therapeutic patient club (*club thérapeutique*; Guattari, 2009a; Rolnik & Guattari, 1986); the grid (*la grille*),<sup>34</sup> or collective equipment (Guattari, 2015b), that is to say, a vacuole of possible resistance within institutions. By and through agitating the coefficient of transversality my argument is that this might problematize the dyadic and sometimes dysfunctional transindividual relationships between doctor and patient, teacher and pupil (Stiegler et al., 2020). My concern is with the passage from the machinic objet petit ‘a’ in Lacan <sup>35</sup> to Winnicott’s transitional object (objet petit ‘b’), <sup>36</sup> and then beyond their functions to the institutional object itself which Guattari idiosyncratically calls the objet petit ‘c’. The rationale behind this move is to consider how the mediating third object (Bradley, 2012) needs to be recast in light of the poisonous and addictive nature of transitional objects such as smartphone technology (Fitzpatrick, 2020; Serres, 2015). What I mean by this is that while there is extant educational research on the use of technologies such as the printing press, or the school journal in Celestin Freinet’s case – technologies which work to open up the classroom productively and creatively to that which is immediately outside in the local community and beyond – my point is that we must rethink the pharmacological possibility of the incorporation of the transitional object at the institutional level, which 

*[p. 196]* ^pg211

is to say, to understand the relationship between institutional objects such as the therapeutic club?” and the transitional object (digital technologies) in order to appreciate the toxic effects of the latter critically. Yet we also must understand the pharmacological or therapeutic aspects of the transitional object in itself, which is to say to understand its precise curative usage at the institutional level (Cole & Bradley, 2018). 

I am treating the institution (such as the hospital or school) as an environment or milieu subject to semiotic a-signifying analysis in the Guattarian sense (Genosko, 2008) and in which all of those working and living there are ‘prisoners of the same knot of problems’ (Guattari, 2015a, p. 88). However, I am treating the school as a ‘psychic environment’ (Stiegler et al., 2020, p. 121) in which the student develops or comes into being. Subjectivation is what brings the student into being. As such, the school is a pharmacological site of therapeutic care or illness, emancipation or disempowerment (Fitzpatrick, 2020). Stiegler insists that positive pharmacology is the ‘primary task of the university of tomorrow and of the new academic context of scholarly life’ (Stiegler, 2015a, p. 162). And from a social ecological perspective at once schizoanalytic and social anarchist (Guattari, 2005; Bookchin, 1996), I treat the school as an institution interpretable through social and mental ecologies, operating in and through the ecological milieu proper. 

While Guattari at times adopted a technophilic perspective in his post-media prospectus<sup>38</sup>, seeing in new micro technologies such as the French Minitel system and pirate radio the potential for the opening up of new incorporeal universes of value and the possibility of dissensual modes of articulation (Bradley et al., 2023; Ueno, 2012), Stiegler places technicity at the heart of an emergent therapeutic community for our digital epoch. But for him it behooves us to actively create therapeutic tools to counter the shock or disruption of digitization. One must simply experiment with the therapeutic adoption of technology as one understands the *pharmakon* of the digital. Stiegler considers that technology and technics are integral to the (de)formation of the human and equally the non-inhuman becoming of the species. As such he critiques and questions hitherto forms of psychoanalysis for neglecting the importance of technology and technics, especially in terms of the (de)formation of memory through reticular, platform capitalism and algorithmic governmentality (or what Guattari calls 

*[p. 197]* ^pg212

Integrated World Capitalism). What is at stake for Stiegler is the possibility of becoming or the reinvention of the non-inhuman human through technics.

I am interested in how the school qua institutional object participates in the production of subjectivity and how it promotes or fails to promote collective therapeutic practices of care. Again, this is a question of subjectivation. I explore this through a dissensual methodology or parological metamodelization (Genosko, 1996, p. 272) which understands the nature of objet petit ‘a’ (Lacan names this the object-cause of desire or the unattainable object of desire — Lacan et al., 2018), the objet petit “b’ or transitional object in Winnicott (1991) and what Guattari names the objet petit ‘c’ (1984, p. 115).\*?

My concern with this series is with the cuts, interruptions, breakdowns or deadly cycles of repetition in their machinic interplay. For me, this is to understand the toxic effects of the transitional object and the therapeutic potentiality of the institution object to counter those effects. It is to begin to coagulate a different machinic object. It is to grasp how a new understanding of the place of the institutional object might help us to use digital technologies therapeutically and pharmacologically (to use the poison differently and curatively — to concoct new addictions). Yet, because of space and as my concern is principally with education and the school, the analysis of psychotherapy in France (Freinet movement, Jean Oury and Guattari at the La Borde clinic), the institutional psychotherapy and the institutional analysis movement in the 1950s (Francois Tosquelles, Frantz Fanon at the Saint-Alban hospital), mental illness (R. D. Laing at Kingsley Hall and the Philadelphia Association) will not be treated in depth here. However, I claim that mental illness ought not to be read solely from a psychiatric or psychological perspective but must be grasped creatively and constructively through schizoanalysis (Deleuze & Guattari, 1983), the ecosophy of Guattari, and pharmacology (Stiegler, 2013; Bradley & Kennedy, 2020).

It seems fitting that we must look elsewhere for more critical metamodelizing perspectives to fully appreciate the complexity of the toxic societal effects of the transitional object (Berardi, 2021; Han, 2017a). This is so because the established forms of addressing the crisis of the institutional object seem unable to get to the heart of the crisis in psycho-and neuro-pharmacological therapeutics. We must therefore metamodel a

*[p. 198]* ^pg213

new appreciation of the plasticity of not only our brains but the institutions and technical devices which shape our mental lives. 

Both Guattari and Stiegler stress that the objects of our attention and addiction must be situated in the institutional context, which itself must be treated through social, psychic and environmental ecologies, but also equally in the family and community context. This is why ecosophy and pharmacological critique go hand in hand, which is to say that therapeutic care must be situated in diverse social and ecological milieux. The question in the time of disruption (Stiegler, 2019), precarity and ecological devastation is how therapeutic, curative and pharmacological practices at the institutional and psychiatric level transform everyday life in the school and its environs. What is of principal importance is to understand the current disastrous and destructive mediation of the digital object in the dyadic relationships between teacher and student, parent and child in the institutional context. How are social relations affected? How can one restore transindividuation circuits between the generations (Bradley, 2021b)? How can they be rebuilt? How can one build an effective coefficient of transversality (Eng, 2012; Cole & Bradley, 2018)? <sup>40</sup> To answer these questions, it is necessary to understand the curative and therapeutic potential of the institutional object, as well as its toxic and addictive consequences.<sup>41</sup> For Stiegler, the mental ecology and the disturbance of psychic apparatuses (our noetic brains) are in a perilous state because, as he consistently argues, the programming industries (both audiovisual and algorithmic – TV, radio, internet, advertising) compete with academic institutions for the attention of its consumers and users to such a disastrous and commanding degree that “children no longer have the necessary attention span to be ready to listen” (Stiegler et al., 2020, p. 127). Consequently, teachers and parents are ‘massively affected by this competition’ (p. 127). Stiegler goes so far as to argue that mother and child are caught in a deadly repetition of attention-distraction. Following Winnicott, he observes that the increasingly ‘uncontrollable’ child now competes with the smartphone for the attention of the parent. At times, the parent is at a loss as to what to do in this predicament. Perilously, when the child is intimate with its objet petit ‘b’ there is a risk of a ‘quasi-autism’ developing in children exposed to smartphones from an early age.<sup>42</sup> Faced with this ‘ecology of addiction’ parents and teachers must learn how to 

*[p. 199]* ^pg214

‘detox’ their child or student and in light of the global epidemic of addiction with the objet petit ‘b’ of the smartphone, Stiegler argues that educators, have “an absolutely incommensurable responsibility” (Stiegler et al., 2020, p. 128). 

Critics like Stiegler and Guattari thus claim that the school qua institution can and must be the object of treatment and cure. In particular, Stiegler encourages us to explore new ways to use exosomatic technologies, that is, the technologies which are external to our bodies and enhance our memory, mind and body. But the school like the hospital is “ill” (Oury, 2007) and made all the more so by the intrusion and presence of the digital object. Again, the question is to find different, more therapeutic addictions. The task is to undertake an institutional analysis to appreciate this dynamic. The digital objet petit ‘a’, the object of desire as such acts as a transitional object (objet petit ‘b’) and this must be grasped in terms of its insinuation in institutional and social dynamics (objet petit ‘c’). Objet petit ‘c’ might be the therapeutic structure, the transversal coefficient structure in the institution (Cole & Bradley, 2018). To understand the objet petit ‘c’ we must understand how it may “mediate the relation between the institution and the individuals within it” (Eng, 2012, p. 339). Going forward, this will mean probing the question of the subject-group for the Sartre-influenced Guattari and the pharmakon for the Derrida-influenced Stiegler.<sup>43</sup> 

The Therapeutic Club as Objet petit ‘c’ in the Education 

Context 

In the glossary in *Molecular Revolution*, Guattari’s understanding of the ‘a’, ‘b’, ‘c’ of the objet petit is explained by Rolnik as follows: 

Objet petit ‘a’: A term proposed by Lacan in the framework of a generalized theory of partial objects in psychoanalysis. Objet petit ‘a’ is a function implying equally the oral object, the anal object, the penis, the gaze, the voice, etc. To this objet petit ‘a’ Felix Guattari proposed, some fifteen years ago, to add petit ‘b’ objects corresponding to the transitional objects of Winnicott, and petit ‘c’ objects, institutional objects. (Guattari, 1984, p. 290) <sup>44</sup> Contra Lacan, Guattari considers the objet petit ‘a’ as a disruption vis-[illegible]-vis structuration and subjectivation.<sup>45</sup> The detachment of objet petit ‘a’ affects 

*[p. 200]* ^pg215

the subject’s constitution by a process of splitting what is both conscious and unconscious. As objet petit ‘a’ becomes further entangled with machinic forces redefining the relationship between machine and structure, it destabilizes the relationship between machine and structure and releases forces from the structure. The objet petit ‘a’ becomes the *objet machine ‘a’* (Genosko, 2009, p. 6), an ‘infernal machine’. Here objet petit ‘a’ is a Lacanian term found in his psychoanalytic theory of partial objects. Objet petit ‘a’ is usually defined as the oral object, the anal object, the penis, the gaze, the voice, and so on. But Guattari reads objet petit ‘a’ in terms of its molecular motion, its destabilizing influence at the level of the institution. Such a treatment of the objet petit ‘a’ addresses desire from where it flows marginally and successfully or becomes further entrapped and neuroticized. In *Molecular Revolution*, Guattari writes of the role of the signifier in the institution: 

Only if we get back to the molecular order of desire machines, in other words, something more basic than the group and the individual (towards what Lacan calls the objet petit ‘a’) shall we succeed in breaking apart the mass produced monoliths of our institutional structures so as to free those in marginal positions of desire from the neurotic dead ends in which they are at present. (Guattari, 1984, p. 79) 

But added to this series beginning with objet petit ‘a’ is the objet petit ‘b’ corresponding to the transitional objects of Winnicott, and then, innovatively, the objet petit ‘c’ or institutional objects (Guattari, 1984, p. 290). In order to more fully appreciate the vacuoles of possibility in the hospital, clinic or even the school, Guattari writes: 

That objective might be a therapeutic club. We may say that that club is the institutional objective (Lacan’s objet petit ‘a’, at the institutional level) that makes it possible to start up an analytic process. Clearly the analytical structure, the analyzer, is not the therapeutic club itself, but something dependent upon that institutional objective, which I have defined elsewhere as an institutional vacuole. It might, for example, be a group of nurses, psychiatrists or patients that forms that analytical, hollow structure where unconscious phenomena can be deciphered, and which for a time brings a subject group into being within the massive structure of the psychiatric hospital. (Guattari, 1984, p. 37) 

*[p. 201]* ^pg216

Following his mentor Jean Oury, Guattari, it appears is trying to account for institutional vacuoles, that is, those sites – therapeutic clubs etc. – that assume a transversal ‘institutional objective’ in a given psychiatric setting. The therapeutic club read as objet petit ‘c’ in the education context can be seen as empowering students to take charge of their everyday activities (Cálo, 2016). Printing and publishing a collaborative journal work would be an example of this (Freinet, 1990). This could act as machinic mediating third (Bradley, 2012) or what I tentatively term the machinic objet petit ‘c’. The logic behind this is to allow students to fully participate in their own education, to contribute to that education and contribute to the care of fellow learners and to those outside the confines of the school. Philosophically, this is to combine the three ecologies of Guattari with the pharmacological principles of Stiegler. Yet, the mutual transversal operations at work must be understood in the wider social context just as the root and branch transformation of the school must be seen in the context of mental illness and socio-political alienation (Guattari, 1992). In terms of pathoplasticity, there is no point in treating students without treating the school because the school as such is ill, which is to say, psychopathological phenomena such as addiction to mobile phones and excessive internet use are influenced by ecosophical patterns which span the wider socius in which we find a link between psychopathology and crisis-riven interpersonal relationships. An analysis of objet petit ‘c’ can thus be undertaken to show that mental disorders are the effect of the atmosphere of the school, wherein the freedom or autonomy of the student, the effects of routines and repetitive tasks, the sense of responsibility can prevent the improvement of communication. This again is a question of the coefficient of transversality. Nowadays, young people are ever more captured by media, by trends, memes, fads, shrink-wrapped tastes, preformatted models, algorithmically-concocted suggestions, to the extent that a mental implosion occurs leaving them passive and in thrall to the marketing industries. Faced with the grave prospects and reality of his day, Guattari demanded pragmatic, therapeutic interventions in the mental life of students to escape those very noetic repetitions which engender depression, paranoia and anxiety (see Carstens, 2017). Indeed, in The *Three Ecologies*, Guattari stresses – in terms of his schizoanalysis and aesthetic paradigm – that one must avoid at all costs the trap of deathly repetition: 

*[p. 202]* ^pg217

I have stressed these aesthetic paradigms because I want to emphasize that everything, particularly in the field of practical psychiatry, has to be continually reinvented, started again from scratch, otherwise the processes become trapped in a cycle of deathly repetition (*répétition mortifère*). The precondition for any revival of analysis – through schizoanalysis, for example consists in accepting that as a general rule, and however little one works on them, individual and collective subjective assemblages are capable, potentially, of developing and proliferating well beyond their ordinary equilibrium. (2005, pp. 39-40) 

Schizoanalysis tries to find ways to escape stagnant unconscious formations, ‘petrified systems of modelization’ (Guattari, 1992, p. 68). And at the institutional level, psychotherapy ought to be focused on the body, groups, signs – ‘grafts of transference’ (Guattari, 1992, p. 7) – that is the recomposition and resingularization of desire, the rearrangement of biological, psychical and social territories. To understand the prevalent addictions in our example we must understand how the objet petit ‘a’ and ‘b’ work in tandem with the institutional objet petit ‘c’. Here Stiegler can be read alongside Guattari to understand the disruption digital technologies cause to transindividual circuits (between the parent and child, teacher and student, doctor and patient) (Stiegler, 2010, 2019; Bradley, 2020). The challenge is to look at the institutional object to assess the crisis and breakdowns, the flows and the cuts, and then to address the transversal disruption in the hierarchical structure between teacher and student but precisely in a curative and therapeutic manner, that is, to search for and curate negentropic forms of knowledge (Bradley, 2022a, 2022b). Again, this can be understood as the coefficient of transversality. The assessment of the structure of the *objet repetitive a-b-c* (*objets répétitifs a-b-c*) as I am naming it would consider the therapeutic space of the school qua institutional object to address the often-passive processes of subjection on the one hand and the processual acts of subjectivation on the other. I think an argument can be made for aligning the pharmakon of technology with the schizo-analytic process of chaosmosis because, as Guattari says, the question is always pragmatic. It is a question of how to put things together or to metamodel them in such a way as to put them into operation with what Guattari calls ‘mutant Universes of value’. He writes: ‘How to produce it, collect it, enrich it, reinvent it permanently in order to make it compatible 

*[p. 203]* ^pg218

with mutant Universes of value’ (1992, p. 135). We thus must look at technology pharmacologically and schizoanalytically, to work out how technology adds to or subtracts from thinking as such. Again Guattari writes ‘computers, expert systems and artificial intelligence add as much to thought as they subtract from thinking’ (1992, p. 36). Guattari says that we must look at the dynamic aspect of chaosmosis to address not the individual qua ‘terminal’ point per se but to see the cybernetic terminal status of a subject in processual becoming. In other words, technology does not begin or end with the subject but passes through it in terms of a multiplicity. One can read the refrain as a milieu of subjectivation and therefore it is important to account for how the objet petit ‘a’ functions. This is not grasped in terms of a hermetic object of desire, or a tiny part in a broader milieu, but to understand the milieu as itself an arrangement or *agencement* of desire or multiplicity in processual chaosmotic becoming (Guattari, 2005, p. 36). One way to do this is to provide a multiplicity of practices allowing students to discover new spaces and ways to inhabit the school and, further, to connect new technological practices with the outside. The question then is one of how to expand the therapeutic care of educational spaces in any number of multifarious social and environmental dimensions. 

Existential Therapy 

I continue to look for a form of existential therapy which can utilize technology in a therapeutic fashion within the institutional and educational context in order to promote a sense of care and materialist ecosophical spirit. In future research, I want to understand how social psychotherapy and psychoanalysis can work in tandem with the principles of institutional analysis, especially regarding the proletarianization of knowledge, that is the loss of the *savoirs*. In our unprecedented and disruptive digital epoch, how can the school maintain its historic role as a therapeutic community, and what resources do we have at hand to work towards the pharmacological constitution of spirit (Stiegler, 2014)? In light of this, what is demanded is a pharmacology of the spirit and a new form of existential, materialist ecosophical therapy (objet petit a-b-c) which is incorporative of digital technologies (objet petit ‘a’ and ‘b’) but set in an institutional milieu (objet petit ‘c’) and functioning all the while to ward off the deadly repetitions of 

*[p. 204]* ^pg219

stupidity, intoxication, stimming, addiction, burnout, depression and exhaustion which define the contemporary era of planetary semiocapitalism (Eng, 2012; Featherstone, 2016; Han, 2017a, 2017b, 2022). Such a pharmacology of the spirit and existential therapy would incorporate digital devices as therapeutic, machinic repetitions, driving repetitions – consistencies as a ‘production of difference’ (Stiegler, 2015b, p. 19). And this is where objet petit ‘a’, ‘b’ and ‘c’ must function transversally, that is, function as a coefficient of transversality. As Bazzano writes: ‘Rebuilding the anamnesic circuit means to some extent recreating on a transindividual/cultural scale the equivalent of Winnicott’s transitional space, a space where independent thinking can be fostered through experimentation and playful, committed adherence to development and maturity’ (Bazzano, 2021, p. 6). As the transitional object mediates transitional space, conditioning psychic encounters with the world, it is fundamental to the fostering of care and societal trust. For Stiegler, the transitional object is the first pharmakon constitutive of *philia.* However, consumer capitalism and the cultural industry work to destroy the potential of the transitional object, resulting in infidelity or what he calls disindividuation. In other words, the transitional object is essential for the possibility of sublimation as such but the cultural industry nulls desire, spurs on the drives, and agitates the compulsion to repeat madly for any absurd purpose. While for Guattari, the educational principles of transversality, reciprocity and the potential which he found in incorporeal universes of value are vital, for Stiegler we must build a therapeutic economy of care and contribution. What I find essential is to understand how to think *jouissance* in terms of the three-fold field of objet petit a-b-c. This is a question of the machinic repetition of the institutional object – both fruitful and potentially deadly. 

Conclusion 

We can say that both Stiegler and Guattari in the last instance are keen to escape the repetitive deadly ipseity of the drives (Sass et al., 2018). At the risk of outbursts of violence in the acting out of the drives, we need less of the mad jouissance that drives us towards unreachable objects of desire and more towards circuits of therapeutic care and ecology of spirit and value 

*[p. 205]* ^pg220

(following Paul Valéry).\*6 We need less of the paranoid-pole of the drives and more of the sublimation of the drives towards our non-inhuman becoming (O'Gorman, 2010), toward desire as such. Why so? For Stiegler, if the drives are led by the insatiable desire to desire all objects through consumption, we are left only with objects of the drives rather than objects of desire and attention. This is the end of sublimation as such, the end of desire as such. 

All in all we need a little less paranoid-fascism and a little more schizoid breakthrough. We could have less of the paranoid fatalism that haunted anti-psychiatrist David Cooper who proclaimed that a psychologically damaged humanity should be ‘extinguished’ at the end of the 20th century if revolution proved impossible. Why? Because as he says ‘it will no longer be the human species’ (Cooper, 1980, p. 148). So a little less of that and a little more of what Guattari points to as ‘communities of resistance’ and ‘the recomposition of subjectivity’ (Guattari, 1994; Melitopoulos, 2016, p. 33). This I think would resist the elusive ‘objet-machine petit ‘a’’ (Guattari, 1984, p. 115) and, we could say, transform the machinic objet petit ‘c’ into a site of becoming and sublimation. 

Stiegler and the Question of Bifurcation 

For what Stiegler invokes is the question of bifurcation precisely at the limit or the extinguishing of possibility. According to him, Deleuze and Guattari’s line of flight concerns the question of bifurcation. His antidote to cynicism is a pharmakon of shared knowledge, new therapies and therapeutics allowing “exosomatization to be put at the service of care” (Stiegler, 2021, p. 69). He argues for therapeutic prescriptions against and for disruptive technologies but rails against short-term therapeutic addictions. Here we need new stratagems to deal with the toxic environment we inhabit and inhere in and the toxic time we *exist* within. More than ever, we need a new system of extimacy. Stiegler writes: 

\[F\]aced with the Anthropocene event, a new ecology of educational subjectivity can be formed, taking account of its technical conditions and related evolutions, that is, of the development of exosomatization, by fighting against its toxicity and by cultivating its curative potential for new psychic and collective individuation. (Stiegler, 2021, p. 120) 

*[p. 206]* ^pg221

What we need to find in the time of the Anthropocene, Stiegler argues, are lines of flight or points of escape, that is ‘a reasoned practice’ (Hesselberth & de Bloois, 2020, p. 169), a withdrawal from addictive media, a ‘desire to create exit points… ways out of entrapment in intoxication and seduction’ (p. 169). For him this is to experiment with negentropic models. The line of flight would be a point of bifurcation, and one which resists the entropy of closed systems: 

For the greatest of all lines of flight is the Anthropocene! How to transform an anxious and paralyzing Anthropocene that produces denial and regression into a line of flight? We have to build the Neganthropocene, one that is immensely exciting, stimulating. The Neganthropocene is not at all about rejecting pharmaka and intoxication. We have to rethink these questions of hyper-synchronization of behaviors by television and the internet very precisely. (p. 179) 

This again I think can be understood as necessitating a re-evaluation of the tools of transversality we have at our disposal – what I propose as the machinic objet petit a-b-c. This indeed demands a mutual co-creation of therapeutic tools, a new community of resistance and a ‘new ecology of educational subjectivity’ (Stiegler, 2021, p. 120). To appreciate the loss of sublimation pervading the institution one needs an institutional analysis of the objects of desire that function there. More than this though, we need proposals of institutional therapy which understand the object at the level of the assemblage. The objet petit a-b-c is part of this rearrangement of desire because it is part of the desire manifest precisely in the assemblage. Objet petit a-b-c is thus its own object of institutional analysis, it is part of institutional therapy, part answer to the question of the relation to the outside, that is, how practices within institutions can take on a life of their own and connect with the outside. Objet petit a-b-c would be the line of flight connecting to the outside. Objet petit a-b-c would be a tool of transversality offering a new coefficient pathway. At root is a question of desire, a question of arrangement, a question of the assemblage, a question of consistencies (Stiegler, 2013), of flow, health, memory and of habit. How a practice is productive is answered by the refrain of the objet petit a-b-c, by the coefficient of transversality – how it produces and pertains to territory, how it pertains to everyday practices and repetitions, and how it can sustain 

*[p. 207]* ^pg222

something like health or wellbeing, something like productivity and prosperity. We can even say, how it pertains to making teaching operate as a ‘work of art’, as Guattari says in *Chaosmosis* (1992, p. 133). Institutional analysis is therefore militant to the extent that in attending to institutional arrangements one intervenes directly in the social field and one connects with the outside of the school. This means not only to find one’s own line of flight, experimentation and becoming but also to transform, modify and mutate that which is under analysis. It is to embrace a new ethico-aesthetic perspective and paradigm and new mode of becoming. We cannot undertake anything more pressing today than to attack institutions which operate as closed systems. We must perform a militant analysis of these desireless and undesirable closed institutions. We need a new form of openness, an open system (Axelos, 1984), a glasnost of thought.
