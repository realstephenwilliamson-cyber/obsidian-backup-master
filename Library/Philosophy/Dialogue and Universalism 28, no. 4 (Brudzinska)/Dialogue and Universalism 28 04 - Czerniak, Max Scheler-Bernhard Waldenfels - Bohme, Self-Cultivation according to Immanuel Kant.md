## Czerniak Suffering PO KOREKCIE3

*Stanisław Czerniak* 

MAX SCHELER—BERNHARD WALDENFELS. 

TWO PHENOMENOLOGICAL CONCEPTIONS OF SUFFERING 

*ABSTRACT* 

This comparative study of Max Scheler’s and Bernhard Waldenfels’ conceptions shows how they differ in their philosophical assumptions. Whereas Scheler’s strove to define the essence of suffering, which he saw in the objective situation of being a victim (sacrificing the inferior for the superior good), Waldenfels emphasized the intentional aspect of suffering and its connections to activity (suffering was to be the necessary and passive “other side” of activity). In this context Waldenfels introduced the distinction between suffering as a) that what happens to us, and b) that what we subjectively feel as “brutally” imposed upon us, ignoring all eidetic questions related to suffering as well as the metaphysical threads which Scheler addressed. The author runs a detailed and critical analysis of Scheler’s position, to which he voices multiple objections, and concludes that it coincides conceptually with the axiological conception of tragedy he propounded in his work *On the Tragic*. In the section on Waldenfels the author reviews the polemical arguments against his views voiced by several contemporary German philosophers. 

Keywords: suffering, self-sacrifice, part and whole, pain, tragic, intentionality, experience of suffering, relief. 

1\. MAX SCHELER’S CONCEPT OF SUFFERING. 

GENERAL ASSUMPTIONS, POINTS OF DISPUTE 

In his article “The Meaning of Suffering,” Max Scheler seeks to define the essence of suffering. As we shall see, however, in his quest he focuses not so much on individual experience and the ways in which—with appropriate phenomenological reduction—the fundamental meaning of suffering can be revealed by its experiencing, as on a set of metaphysical conditions of the possibility of suffering, in a sense ignoring the issue of its cognitive reception through direct experience. Indeed, Scheler lists no examples of suffering 

*[p. 54]* ^pg56

subjects following the path of phenomenological reduction to find knowledge about what he calls the metaphysical essence of suffering at its end. His metaphysical definition of suffering is more an axiom, an *a priori* starting-point for his further argumentation: 

“I assert that each kind of pain and suffering, regardless of how the person who suffers behaves toward any of them, is the subjective reflection in the psyche of sacrifice. Each suffering is correlated to objective events of sacrifice in which a goal of a lower order is surrendered for a goal of a higher order.”[^1] 

Here, Scheler ties the possibility of suffering to a specific relation between beings. Suffering is a sacrifice only where there exists a “fundamental ontological relationship \[…\] between part und whole.”<sup>2</sup> The sacrifice involves an act (condition) performed (experienced) by the part on behalf of the whole, with which the part is “related in solidarity.”[^3] Thus, Scheler observes, “in a purely mechanical world there could be no possibility of pain and suffering.”[^4] A rather obvious conclusion here is that suffering is a condition that only affects the life processes of organic units—living beings suffer, minerals do not. The parts of wholes are relatively independent components of the wholes, with specific functions in the overall structure. They can, however, resist the *telos* of the whole, i.e. break out from their roles, disrupting the harmony that ensures the “good” of the whole (i.e. its biological survival). The clash between two kinds of resistance: the resistance of the parts to the whole and of the whole to the parts—as well as the sacrifice made by the parts (the whole’s resistance is stronger than that of the parts, which means that the whole is the dominant side of the conflict and “forces” the parts into subsidiary roles)—are what creates the ontic space in which pain and suffering appear. 

However, the nature of the relation between the experience of suffering and the objective condition of sacrifice is an open issue here. Because one can easily imagine situations, where suffering accompanies the part’s resistance to the whole, but for reasons external to the entire system, e.g. connected with rivalry between wholes, the whole in question does not pursue its prime interest at the cost of its parts, hence there is no objective moment of sacrifice, which involves the execution of a certain good on the ontic level of the whole. In such cases one could, perhaps, speak about a “sacrifice in vain,” but Scheler’s definition does not cover this category of sacrifice. In defining suffering by the functions it

Here, suffering is equally futile as in the earlier case, but in a different way—the parts not only “sacrifice” themselves to something than cannot come into being for reasons unconnected with them, but they suffer although the whole has no “need” for it, nor is its real addressee. Scheler’s theory would be defendable against such accusations only on the grounds of a very strong metaphysical assumption—which Scheler fails to make—that all wholes are monads, i.e. systems, whose internal states have exclusively internal causes. Such states would by nature base on, and be reducible to, part-whole relations. In other words, the suffering Scheler writes about would then be the internal suffering of monads and its objective functions could not be hindered or blocked by any outside causes, nor any cause-and-effect “logic” other than that which regulates its functions within the monad. 

By identifying pain and suffering with the ontic level of a part of a given whole, Scheler also automatically excludes the possibility of suffering being caused by the whole itself. Moreover, one can assume that the source of such suffering could lie in the resistance offered to the whole by its parts. Are, therefore, the two kinds of resistance present here—of the part to the whole and the whole to the part—not accompanied by two different kinds of suffering, which, in addition, are able to penetrate each other, and would therefore be very difficult to separate in the experience of suffering? This would be the case, for example, if we assumed that the parts and the whole are too “loyal” to each other not to co-experience their respective suffering. In view of the possible disjunction that only one of the two—the part or the whole—would actually suffer, this would make the true carrier of the experienced suffering variant difficult to identify. Thus, in focusing on the ontic problem of the function of suffering, Scheler bypasses the experience of suffering, which is able to “radiate to” or “taint” the entire system on whose peripheries it appears. 

 Before we take a closer look at some of the above-mentioned doubts and cite some concrete examples to illustrate them, it may be worthwhile to quote another conceptual version of Scheler’s definition of suffering: “We call ‘pain’ that experience of subservience, limitation and constraint a life unit (cell, tissue, organ, organ system) must undergo for the organisation and integrity of the whole.”<sup>5</sup> Thus, some of the questions we asked before may, for instance, apply to the following: If I am in pain because of a sick liver, is this pain really an expression of the “resistance” of the organism as a whole against the ailing organ and the organ’s “sacrifice” for the benefit of the whole? And what does the

sick liver “sacrifice” to the whole in this case if the whole cannot function well without an organically functional liver? Does this mean Aristotle, whom Scheler criticised and in opposition to whom he constructed his philosophy of suffering,[^6] was right to call pain a symptom of processes that endanger life and its biological goals? The individual components of Scheler’s definition of suffering—the parts, the whole, the resistance of the part, the resistance of the whole, the part’s sacrifice on behalf of the whole—are very hard to distinguish, especially in the case of physical suffering. In our example, the organic whole does not “resist” the needs of its part, the liver, but reacts to the signals which come from it. Thus, it is my liver that is in pain, but I as a “whole” suffer as the carrier of its ailment (for instance physical pain may cause spiritual suffering, e.g. in face of the necessities it may force me into as I—as an organic whole—undertake measures to still it and eliminate its causes). 

 Of course, certain kinds of pain are as if by nature related to sacrifice on behalf of something organically superior or equal (birth pains, menstruation pains, growing pains, the pain of a healing wound), but this rather specific paradigm is inapplicable to very many kinds of physical suffering. 

 It is easy to see, however, that Scheler’s theory aptly defines the nature of the suffering which appears in the sphere of the individual’s relation to its species. Individuals suffer and die, but their demise and the suffering that heralds it are a necessary (and, in this stoical sense, voluntary) sacrifice for the further endurance of the species (in today’s socio-biological terms, we would say the genotype, or the genetic pool typical for a certain group, family or clan[^7]). Indeed, Scheler realises that it is not the abstract “physical pain” category but the concept of “individual death” and the suffering that accompanies it that best express his general definition of suffering: “Death demands a natural self—sacrifice of the individual for the procreation and advancement of the species. This is the „sacrifice” that surrenders one life for more and automatically higher life \[…\] Pain viewed in a similar way is like a death in miniature, a sign of death, a sacrifice of part of the organism for the preservation of the whole.”[^8] Whereas elsewhere he states that, “the idea of sacrifice closely unities death and pain”[^9]. Still, a conception that commands me to recognise the condition of my ailing inner organ as a source of suffering mainly because it is a natural “reminder” about my impeding death and the individual sacrifice I will thereby make for the interests of my species seems to narrow down the ways in which suffering can manifest itself as physical pain.

It appears, however, that most of the suffering experienced by humans does not serve life in this sense, but simply happens (road accidents, natural disasters, epidemics, unless we cynically assume—and without any proof to support the claim—that such suffering “enriches” the biological substance of the species). This in a sense precludes the definition of any pain or suffering by the functions it performs, because suffering that just happens to affect us usually cannot be ascribed to any axiological hierarchy. On the contrary, it is frequently a kind of *par excellence* anti-good, an experience that does not stem from acts of rational will and upsets the rational order that governs such acts, and whose injurious contingency does not belong to the realm of our aims and ambitions. Such suffering is different from that which we “take upon ourselves” as carriers of existential meanings, thus diminishing the accompanying pain (suffering that means something to the sufferer “hurts” less). Therefore, Scheler appears to be wrong in this point as well, as he fails to distinguish suffering from pain and completely ignores the fact that suffering can be defined by the specific meaning given it by humans (*vide* Viktor E. More still, one can safely assume that the very awareness of suffering being nothing but a sacrifice on behalf of the whole could itself become a source of irremovable spiritual suffering, proof that the world is governed by blind fate. Then, any encountered suffering would have a pre-determined “functional” meaning, with the suffering subject’s sole task being to unravel the *fatum*: discover the interest of the whole for which it happened to be suffering at the given time and in the given place. This, of course, limits the freedom of individual suffering, i.e. the suffering subject’s ability to “confront” its pain by not so much by discovering its sense, as constituting it in a certain axiological space. Also, here it would be a far-going simplification to say—as someone probably might in a polemic with the present reflections—that all undertakings aimed at giving suffering meaning must resort to the category of sacrifice in Scheler’s understanding, i.e. that by imbuing suffering with meaning we make it a means towards something “higher” than the suffering subject and its needs. Because imbuement with sense does not come down to referring the part to the whole. Suffering can have meaning (“value”) for me also in cases where I do not recognise the value involved as any sort of service to the whole, and when it is not axiologically superior to the values that annihilate suffering itself. 

 Scheler aims to build a phenomenological, “essential” substitute for Aristotle’s inductive definition of suffering, but he underestimates the strong rooting in experience of this definition’s empirical content. Indeed, one must agree with Aristotle, that suffering accompanies disturbances in the equilibrium that in living organisms bases on their experience of the “natural pleasantness of existence,” or, in more psychological terms, contentment flowing from the fulfillment of specific needs. Suffering comes when this nature- and culture-conditioned

*[p. 58]* ^pg60

homeostasis between needs and the means by which they can be fulfilled is upset (whereby it is evident that the conditions of the homeostasis change over history—tamed animals suffer in different situations than their wild-living counterparts, and contemporary Europeans suffer for different reasons than the ancient Greeks did). Suffering is an experience, and its definition should embrace the entirety of the necessary and sufficient conditions of the appearance of experiences which people know as pain. However, most such experiences a) do not involve the suffering carrier’s awareness of his/her sacrifice, and b) are unrelated to the objective situation of being a victim of the interests of a whole (to what “whole” does the sacrifice brought by, say, the suffering inflicted on children by sexual deviants contribute?). Organic beings suffer in certain situations. Describing these situations may give ground for inductive generalisations about suffering, and even provide “essential” knowledge about it (to someone who believes such knowledge is possible), but only provided that the cognitive accuracy of such endeavours bases on their non-transgression of the sphere of experienced suffering. As we have seen, Scheler crosses these boundaries by identifying suffering with death. In the empirical dimension, such identification is groundless—as already the Epicureans knew—because humans may experience pain when they are dying, but they do not experience death itself, as death is for them the end of all experience, and in this sense does not belong to them. Thus, a philosophy of suffering as confined exclusively to the sphere of experience and its interpretations can hardly be reduced to a philosophy of death as an offering made by the individual to its biological community. I am only able to experience someone else’s death, and any reflection about death-related suffering in the social sense is only possible in this context. But Scheler did not mean this aspect of death when he called it “the continuation of the individual’s pain.” 

 Questionable here, however, is not only the connection of the phenomenon of suffering to the ontic status of the victim, but also the indirect inclusion of good—or a hierarchy of “goods”—among its formative components. According to Scheler, suffering appears where there is a collision between different kinds of good. Then, the good that stands higher in the hierarchy in a sense reaffirms its existence through the suffering that accompanies the lesser good’s sacrifice for its sake. But does suffering always denote a conflict between hierarchically ordered “goods”? What about physical or moral suffering in cases where the inflicting side holds itself for a “tool of evil”? Also, suffering often accompanies situations in which it is very difficult to define the hierarchic order of the good involved. Scheler’s definition excludes suffering in cases where the kinds of good are of equal rank (e.g. in the sphere of vital or spiritual goods, to use Scheler’s own classification) from the whole debate. Does it recognise someone’s encounter with conflicting and equal-ranking vital goods and ultimate choice of one of them as suffering? It is precisely this relativisation of suffering down to a conflict between hierarchically unequal kinds of good that in Scheler

*[p. 59]* ^pg61

’s case brings suffering (as we observed earlier) out of the realm of individual, subjective perception. By nature subjective, here suffering becomes an objective phenomenon, a necessary correlate of a certain *status quo* in the sphere of objective values; this *status quo* is determined by the hierarchic dependencies between certain kinds of good, e.g. individual and collective interests. Scheler’s objectivised definition of suffering practically deprives humans of the privilege to identify their suffering themselves. According to Scheler I suffer not because I feel and know that I am suffering, but because I have found myself in a certain objective situation (in the ontic and axiological sense) which by its nature generates all suffering. 

 This brings us to a question which easily suggests itself in this context—about the difference between “authentic” and “inauthentic” suffering. Is suffering that is only experienced and not rooted in the above-described metaphysical/axiological conflict of “goods” especially susceptible to mystification and therefore labelled a “lesser value”? This is a question that should be put especially to Scheler, the author of fundamental distinctions and analyses in the field of cognitive error and axiological illusion.[^10] If, as Scheler maintains, axiological illusion appears when, in my act of subjective interpretation, I wrongly perceive the objective hierarchy of “goods” (e.g. by putting vital over religious values), then does the suffering caused by this misinterpretation not take some of its properties from the very cognitive acts that accompany it? Is it not, therefore, illusory suffering? And would not the related objective correlates of value-embracing acts be equally illusory? In other words, if the cause of my suffering is my sacrifice of a higher value for a lower one, then am I really suffering? Can we speak about sacrifice here—however understood? And if there is no sacrifice, then does suffering appear at all? 

 In his text Scheler clearly distances himself from Arthur Schopenhauer’s philosophy and its theories about the sources of suffering and the spiritual means to overcome it[^11]. Yet Schopenhauer’s metaphysics of will appears to offer a much more adequate description of the nature of suffering than Scheler’s eidetic phenomenology. Individuals suffer not because they serve a whole, Schopenhauer says, but because the whole is not “concerned” about them, being a blindly rushing entity chiefly “occupied with itself” and its expansion, which in turn takes place on a cosmic scale, hence in a sense above living beings and their suffering. Human suffering is not an independent variable of cosmic expansion, has no direct bearing on the fate of the whole, and does not decide about any “success” of the whole that could be described in metaphysical terms. It is born from the accidental intersection of different kinds of forces and causal relations. The unique course of someone’s life “happens” to cross the temporal

Of course, Schopenhauer argues, there would be no suffering if there were no individuals endowed with the ability to feel. If the primal whole had not individualised and diversified itself, there would have not only been no individuals, but also none of the suffering they feel. However, Schopenhauer’s definition of the sources of individual suffering seems to be more accurate than Scheler’s: it does not arise from conflicts between the objective values in which individuals participate, but from the absence of a superior value at the source of suffering and the individual’s constant exposure to collisions with the rivalling interests and egoism of other individuals, and individuals moving in the opposite direction. Here, suffering is not a service to the laws of nature, but emerges from the chaos that exists on their peripheries. 

 One could also say that for Schopenhauer the condition of the appearance of suffering does not lie in any lasting ontic or axiological bond between the parts and the whole. Much rather, it is precisely the lack of such a bond that makes suffering what it is—the testimony of the risk every individual being takes by existing. 

 This approach to the conditions of the possibility of suffering allows Schopenhauer’s theory to escape the problems encountered by all conceptions which on one hand rationally accept the existence of suffering in the world, but simultaneously maintain that the whole is something like a heaven-sent supreme authority over the parts, a higher value which justifies the evil the parts experience when they suffer. Theodicean philosophers before and after Leibniz always came up with new—more or less sophisticated—argumentation in defence of the goodness of God, the creator of the world and the suffering and evil experienced by His creatures.[^12] Scheler seems to continue this approach—suffering is part of the world’s order. However, while Leibniz generally contents himself with the “best of all possible worlds” conception, Scheler offers more detailed

There is, perhaps, no God here, but there is a whole, a superior good for which all sacrifice is worthy of metaphysical and ethical approval. The assumption is that the whole is by nature good, and that the evil that appears within its boundaries has axiological import because of this fact. The theodicean question *si deus, unde malum*? is asked in a general sense (its object is not God but all wholes), and at once annulled: the whole is good, but suffering is not essentially bad, because in serving (sacrificing itself for) the whole, it is by definition a carrier of good. There is no need to defend God against Himself—all suffering serves a higher purpose and can be theologically reconciled with the idea of divine goodness and perfection as an attribute of supreme being. As can be easily seen, Schopenhauer’s conception invalidates theodicy far more radically: the absolute is not good, it is blind to values, and evil is born spontaneously in its emanations in face of the gradual individualisation of life and the rising risk of everything colliding with everything as the world becomes more and more complex. Therefore, suffering has certain natural and universal causes but no ethical substantiation, so there is nothing to hinder suffering individuals from seeking the means to effectively still their suffering, or even liquidate its source (Schopenhauer’s nirvana conception). Because individuals who experience suffering unburdened by the knowledge that their condition is a necessary sacrifice on behalf of a higher good possess some extra existential “leeway” in their conscious and practical encounters with suffering. 

 At this point I would like to make a brief historical and philosophical commentary. Scheler’s philosophy of suffering bears a striking resemblance to the axiological conception of tragedy he propounds in *On the Tragic.*<sup>13</sup> In a general sense, Scheler sees tragedy as the annihilation of a positive value in an axiological conflict with another positive value. Tragic here is the destruction of one good by another, the fact that it is not humanly possible to avoid situations in which goods of importance and value are “written off” as lost, deprecated in a clash with other (rivalling) values. Also, tragedy by nature does not involve guilt, because it is not the will of its subject that causes it, but the objective character of the axiological conflict and its effects. 

 Common to both theories—of suffering and tragedy—are the following motifs: a) both suffering and tragedy belong to the sphere of relations between values and their carriers; b) in these relations, one side “wins” (attains dominance, moves into the foreground) at the other’s cost; c) suffering and tragedy are conceptions relating to the condition of the loser in this axiological conflict (the sacrifice of the part, the tragic defeat of positive values); d) this defeat is necessary as both tragedy and suffering are part of the objective order, the metaphysical structure of the world, and not the subjective order of human situations and experience. 

<sup>13</sup> Scheler, M. 1954. “On the Tragic.” *Cross Currents*, 4, 178–191.

 These similarities suggest that Scheler at least partly identifies suffering with tragedy, i.e. that he sees all suffering as tragic. When I suffer, I know that some values die “for the good” of others. My being a victim of suffering is accompanied by my awareness of this axiological conflict and its inner logic, according to which certain values are sacrificed in favour of other values. The only difference between situations that generate suffering and those that generate tragedy would be that in suffering the sacrifice is an inferior value, while tragedy may arise from a conflict between equal-ranking ones. 

 Thus, we can summarise our doubts about Scheler’s conception of suffering as follows: not all suffering is tragic, because not all suffering is accompanied by the demise of one value in favour of another. Tragic suffering is a rather narrow class of suffering, mainly experienced in the social and cultural sphere of human existence (e.g. the tragic figure of Antigone). Physical pain can be tragic in the Schelerian sense only if it involves the sacrifice of the individual and its personal goods for the benefit of other persons or certain communal values, like in offering someone one’s own organs for transplantation purposes (at the risk of one’s health), or accepting inevitable suffering to save values of importance to the community. My suffering is not tragic when: a) I suffer though no value would be impaired in any way if I did not (i.e. the world would be at least as valuable without my suffering as with it); b) the demise of values is not caused by axiological rivalry but the causal order in which individuals participate (illness as a contingent physical indisposition disenables me from pursuing spiritual values whose condition is health). In this sense, much of the suffering caused by disease is devoid of tragedy—of course if we assume that the suffering itself does not multiply values, i.e. is not a necessary condition of the appearance of values unattainable to healthy individuals. 

 Pessimistic conceptions of artistic genius which measure an artist’s greatness by the extent of his/her suffering would, of course, fall under the Schelerian definition of tragedy. These, however, are exceptional experiences in the sea of sufferings, which from this perspective appear trivial and devoid of a “higher sense”—although at all times rationally “tamable” by suffering beings. 

2\. BERNHARD WALDENFELS ON SUFFERING 

 Where Scheler’s phenomenological conception of suffering lays emphasis on its meaning—or essential knowledge about suffering—Waldenfels, the leading representative of contemporary German phenomenology, focuses on the intentional character of the experience of suffering. As with their other experiences, humans tend to “objectivise” suffering, to “grasp it, experience it as precisely that what it is, treat it in a specific way, endow it with meaning.”[^14]

 In Husserl’s terms, one could say that suffering is never given to us directly, but through its “silhouettes”—similarly as the perceived image of, say, a jug includes the “assumedly perceived” image of its rear part. Also suffering is experienced in the context of earlier and current experiences which add to its meaning. Humans confront the world through the meanings they give to its various fragments, hence in Waldenfels’ view also religions not so much offer substitute remedies for suffering (as Scheler maintained on the examples of Buddhism and Christianity),[^15] as refer to a sphere that is fundamental to suffering and institute themselves upon their interpretations of its nature and meaning. 

 An important conclusion here would be that there is no “one” essence of suffering. Suffering is what the subject experiences as such, what is suffering to it on the grounds of its own, unique inner and outer perception, which the subject identifies as suffering according to its own, subjective system of meaning. To supplement Waldenfels, one could add that the thesis about the multi-dimensional nature of suffering appears to be confirmed by language itself, where the term “suffering” appears in phraseological contexts that are difficult to assign to any “single” fundamental and supreme meaning. Indeed, suffering physical pain is different from, say, suffering hunger or poverty, experiencing painful loss in the sphere of higher goods (e.g. losing one’s reputation), or suffering under negative outside stimuli or surroundings (e.g. not being able to stand someone). Waldenfels himself makes a clear division between suffering understood as that what happens to us (*Widerfahrnis—*experience) and the suffering that we subjectively feel as “brutally” imposed upon us (*Überkommnis—* coming-over), and which in extreme cases can paralyse us (*lähmt*) physically and mentally up to the point where our activity ceases completely.[^16] In the first case, Waldenfels notes, suffering is, in a sense, a natural ingredient of all activity. Activity unaccompanied by thus-understood suffering is extremely rare, and in a broader context at least carries the possibility of total or partial loss of control over its particular elements, phases or effects. By acting, I take control of and subjugate something, but at the same time I open new areas of confrontation with the world, over which I am losing my control. Activity is indeed a consequence of a series of conscious thoughts, but what happens to me during this activity takes place against my conscious will and my plans. Thus, suffering of the *Widerfahrnis* type is the suffering an active individual is exposed to in moments of helplessness and passivity, which are an inherent part of all activity as its hidden, darker countenance—its Janus face. “You cannot weave a carpet without destroying its pattern,”[^17] Waldenfels says in this context, meaning the sphere of light and shade that makes up human life, which consists both “of

“Suffering is woven into all the forms of our lives, action and passive endurance complement each other like breathing in and breathing out; the sensitivity to injury (*Empfӓnglichkeit*) that characterises our existence is the reverse side of our readiness to absorb stimuli \[…\] To combat suffering, which lies so close to the source of life, would be to drain the sphere of experience that stretches between us, the world, and other people.”[^19] Here, Waldenfels introduces the term “over-overcoming” (*Über-bewӓltigung*), where the prefix “over-” functions similarly as in “overdoing,” to describe a defensive strategy whereby the subject, in its striving to eliminate suffering at all costs, eventually loses touch with the reality of life, into which suffering is ontically inscribed. He also remarks that the over-overcoming category applies only to suffering of the *Widerfahrnis* type, as it would hardly be possible to accuse a doctor of exaggeration in easing the pain of a suffering patient if he/she had the means at hand. However, he also asks: “Who would have had the boldness to treat van Gogh at all costs?”[^20] For Waldenfels, the demarcation line lies between suffering that enhances life and suffering that leads to its destruction or decay. Thus, the “over-overcoming” charge is justified not when suffering is eliminated to save life or make it bearable, but when its life-generating qualities are ignored. 

 Waldenfels lists the following defensive strategies used to overcome suffering: A) Vertical forms of relief. “One’s own weakness is compensated by external strength.”[^21] Humans seek relief from suffering in the transcendental sphere, they implore help from gods, to whom they ascribe causative and preventive powers over their lives, hence also their suffering. 

 B) Lateral forms of relief. Empathy as a form of participating in someone’s suffering can not only reduce its effects, but also the intensity with which it is experienced by the suffering subject (“a sorrow shared is a sorrow halved—*geteiltes Leid ist halbes Leid*<sup>22</sup>). 

 C) Defensive-offensive strategies. The suffering subject may retreat to positions in which it is as least partly secure from suffering, or may even decide to withdraw from life, in keeping with the maxim: “Let life suffer without me” (*Auszug aus einem Leben, das man seinen Leiden überlӓßt*).”[^23] More frequent, however, is an active response, where the suffering subject consciously interacts

Here, however, Waldenfels spies a risk: “The distance to the world which is being deepened here also becomes the distance I have to myself and my close ones, so the question that may arise is, if this depreciation (*Herabstufung*) of suffering does not also mean the weakening of empathy.”<sup>24</sup> Another consequence of offensive strategies is the moralisation of suffering, 

where suffering is not only separated into deliberately administered and circumstantial, i.e. that which is inflicted by an identifiable human agent and that which occurs without willful human participation, but humans are predominantly seen as its cause in many so-called “life situations.” Here, the experience of suffering goes in pair with the desire to bring its real or imagined perpetrator to account, and most suffering functions within a system consisting of one’s own or someone’s guilt and the experienced baseness, injustice or injury. Waldenfels rightly connects this strategy of taming suffering to the rationalisation processes that laid the ground for Western civilisation. When suffering becomes a moral issue, suffering that was heretofore not seen as intentional, as someone’s liability, begins to be perceived as the effect of rational activity (in the sense of activity undertaken by a subject on the ground of its convictions). Indeed, also in our day the police increasingly step out against clairvoyants who have erred in their verdicts concerning, say, missing persons, thus adding to their families’ suffering. In other words, clairvoyance, something that is *par excellence* irrational (provided it exists at all), is reduced to a regular rational activity, whose subject commands a pool of means and methods with which to pursue it, and whose ineffectiveness is solely attributable to the subject’s errors. 

 Thus, one can say that one of “over-overcoming” in human civilisation is the changing mode in which life was experienced in the complexity of all its active and passive aspects. The true power of technology and the triumphs of science find reflection in the subjective feeling of power their users experience. If at all noticed, the passive aspects of human existence, hence also the experience of suffering for which no one is responsible, are pushed into the background or quite to the sidelines of existential activity. 

3\. WALDENFELS’ THEORY OF SUFFERING. SUFFERING 

AND THE LINGUISTIC MEDIUM 

 Presented by its author at a conference, Waldenfels’ conception of suffering raised a strong response from the gathering, including some criticism. Participating in the ensuing debate were among others the philosophers Rainer Piepmeier, Norbert Bolz, Ludger Oeing-Hanhoff, Hans-Michael Baumgartner, Hermann Krings and Jürgen Ebach. For the purposes of our present reflections, 

*[p. 66]* ^pg68

I wish to make brief reference to one of its threads, namely the expression of suffering by language. 

 In the debate, Piepmeier points to the character of the relations between suffering and language,[^25] a theme Waldenfels had ignored. Does the circumstance, Piepmeier asked, that all human suffering is somehow linguistically articulated and imbued with some kind of meaning signify that language is by nature a remedy for suffering? As we know, this was the direction taken by Arnold Gehlen[^26] in his anthropological reflections. For Gehlen, it was language that “relieved” humans from the natural failings of their biological apparatus, and he believed it was primarily language that enabled humans to cope with the overload on stimuli that reached their perceptive system without, as in the case of animals, being sieved by instinct. When I name something, recall something, cast my thoughts into the future, command someone to do something, etc., language in its various functions takes over the role of the instincts I lack: it narrows the field of perception down to what is named, protects the subject from the “invasion” of incoming stimuli by a curtain of imagination focused on the past or future, enables humans, whose social role obliges them to respond to commands, to concentrate only on what ensures their efficient fulfillment. Thus, language “frees” us from suffering caused by chaos, a form of suffering we have been in a sense “elevated” to by nature, which over millions of years of evolution has deprived us of the instinctive corset that guides the perception of animals, allowing them to see only what serves their existential needs. 

 Waldenfels himself took a more reserved stand in this debate and, probably to avoid entanglement in the naturalistic assumptions of Gehlen’s anthropology, limited himself, in true phenomenological spirit, to postulating the description of linguistic articulations of suffering, He believed shouting to be an important threshold in such articulation, so what he thought was needed were descriptions of its various forms by the criterion of their distance from this basically prelinguistic form. Another important thing for him was the status of the subject of articulated suffering, and the accompanying issue of the distance from suffering articulated in various subjective situations (the complaints of a patient, the questions asked by a doctor when interviewing a patient, this doctor’s lecture on a certain syndrome of suffering related to the given ailment, the suffering experienced by patients as a subject of private conversations between doctors, etc.).[^27] 

 However, from the point of view of the intentions behind Piepmeier’s question, the choice of this line of inquiry into the linguistic mediation of suffering was rather evasive. Because the question was not only about “how” people tamed their suffering by language, but also about what such language-mitigated

 A). The above confrontation raises questions about the presented phenomenological suffering conceptions’ cognitive horizons. As we have seen, Scheler’s eidetic suffering theory can be accused of ignoring experience as a source of suffering, in other words, it can be looked upon as not “source-based,” and even “unphenomenological.”

Waldenfels, on the other hand, who is far removed from any eidetic pursuits and makes direct reference to experienced suffering, does it in such a way that his opponents react by accusing his theories of being based on incomplete research. More still, some of these accusations suggest his claims would have been secure from charges of inconsistency if he had at least tried to define the essence of suffering.[^31] 

 Would, thus, the main conclusion be that the phenomenology of suffering must contain both moments—phenomenal and eidetic—and that the lack of either not only deprives this philosophy of its phenomenological character (in the classical, Husserlian meaning of the term), but also makes the construction of a philosophically coherent theory of suffering difficult or even impossible? This someone would have to rely on the linguistic articulations of suffering, e.g the suffering subject’s own opinions, and simultaneously on the philosophical generalisations that have been built over them. In other words, a philosopher of suffering whose field of interest includes his/her own suffering would not select it, the experience of “this present” pain, as the starting-point of his/her explorations, but would rather begin with certain philosophical assumptions as to the way in which human subjects experienced their living body (*vide* Hermann Schmitz’s conclusions).[^34]

THE PHILOSOPHICAL ANTHROPOLOGY 

OF ARNOLD GEHLEN AS A CRITIQUE OF THE AGE 

OF TECHNOLOGY 

*ABSTRACT* 

The author distinguishes three main interpretations of the concept, as well as the developmental trends in philosophical anthropology, and reflects on their relationship with critical social philosophy. Consequently, he follows up with an explication of the main assumptions of Arnold Gehlen’s philosophical anthropology and seeks to find out how they influenced the categorical particularity of his critique of postmodern society, labeled as “the crisis of institutions.” The author provides more detailed reflection in references to Gehlen’s *Die Seele im technischen Zeitalter* (published in English as *Man in the Age of Technology*)*,* and its analysis of the so-called new subjectivism. The article ends with a critical conclusion, in which the author makes note of certain ideological incongruities in Gehlen’s philosophical standpoint. 

Keywords: philosophy of man, philosophical anthropology, impulse flooding, drive surplus, language, social institutions, action, new subjectivism. 

1\. INTRODUCTORY REMARKS 

The concept of philosophical anthropology has many meanings, and certainly not all of its interpretations have served as a starting point for social critique. This was also not the case where each single stand within the framework of a given anthropological model had a *par excellence* critical overtone. It is worth asking whether we are dealing here with more than just coincidence or a specific philosopher’s preferences. 

In my opinion, it is reasonable to distinguish three paradigmatic ways of understanding the term: 

1\) Philosophical anthropology as a synonym of “philosophy of man,” which is a sphere of philosophical reflection which takes man (the human being) as its 

*[p. 76]* ^pg78

main object of interest. Such human-oriented philosophies (“anthropologies”) have been contained in broader philosophical doctrines (e.g. Sartre’s existentialism), or coherent parts of doctrines (for example, thomism contains a defined philosophy of man). Not always, however, do we encounter clear demarcation lines. Martin Heidegger in his *Letter on Humanism* criticized philosophical anthropology, but before the so-called *Kehre,* in the part of his reflections he called existential analytics, he searched in the *Sein und Zeit* (*Being and Time*) for human “existentials” such as “anxiety” or “care,” and built upon them an unquestionably original, philosophical tectonics of the human being (*Dasein*). 

2\) Philosophical anthropology as a collective term for philosophical reflection focused on the specific “dimensions” or “aspects” of human existence, such as work, love, death, physicality, suffering, otherness (the so-called regional anthropologies). Hence, philosophical anthropology would be constituted by a philosophy of suffering, or love, which would not have to simultaneously pretend (as in the previous example) to be a complete philosophy of the human being, or a singled-out element of a larger whole. 

3\) Philosophical anthropology as a classical philosophical anthropology of the twentieth century. The shared assumption of its creators (Max Scheler, Helmuth Plessner, Arnold Gehlen) took the form of a certain “biologisation” of reflection about man, which involved attempts to define—in the language of philosophy, although simultaneously on the grounds of biological knowledge about the characteristics of the various levels of life (plants, animals, homo sapiens)—the “monopolies” of the human species in the sphere of living beings. In other words, man’s special identity is defined here through confrontation with what a philosopher referring to the heritage of the biological sciences recognizes as the ontic/biological status of a plant or animal. One assumes that man stands in many ways above that sphere (a fundamental qualitative difference), but simultaneously is deeply rooted in it. Although these concepts differ in the categories which define the biological particularities of plants, animals and humans (the above-mentioned monopolies), their common assumption remains that these cross-special confrontations are a key criterion for anthropological definitions. 

Already a brief look into subject literature shows that the above-listed philosophical anthropology models belong to the critical paradigm of social philosophy to a varying degree. Probably least predestined in this respect are regional anthropologies, which oftentimes display a descriptive character (as in e.g. “patterns of love in Western culture”), and due to their limited theoretical ambitions do not supply the instruments necessary for undertaking socio-cultural critique within the dimension of universal philosophical reflection. As for the two remaining paradigms, although they have served as a doctrinal source/starting point of critical, social philosophy, it is difficult to speak about any obvious, one-directional ideological dependencies. Let us look at some examples: 

*[p. 77]* ^pg79

Within the paradigm of “philosophy of man” the anthropological segments of the larger, doctrinal wholes quite often fail to breach descriptive, sub-disciplinary analysis. However, such “comprehensive” philosophies of man as Jean–Paul Sartre’s existentialism, explained in the *Being and Nothingness*, are also difficult to see as a beginning of a social philosophy in whatever understanding of the term. Heidegger’s case is different only at first sight. It might appear that his category of *das Man* is nothing else than an anthropological criterion of critique aimed at an incapacitated, common, individual consciousness in the social dimension. However, the argumentation contained in the *Sein und Zeit* does not go beyond the ontology of the existentials, because the doctrinal meaning of *das Man* reveals itself against the semantic background of the concept of *Eigentlichkeit*, and there is no room here left for speaking about some broader project pertaining to historical critique of any given social entity. On the other hand, however, there exists an entire thought tradition, such as Critical Theory, where anthropological premises lead to critical conclusions in the field of social philosophy. Here the sense-creating relationship between anthropological philosophy and social critique is a basic, doctrinal assumption. It seems that only these additional, doctrinal assumptions lead, in the case of the philosophy of man, to a categorial feedback between these two categories. Wherever they do not appear (*vide* Jean-Paul Sartre’s approach in the *Being and Nothingness*), philosophy exhausts its ambitions in the ontological, or epistemological, field. 

The situation within the framework of the second paradigm seems to be even more mysterious. While both Plessner, and Gehlen developed complex models of social and cultural critique on the grounds of their philosophical anthropology, Scheler lacks such inclinations. Why is this so, even though all these thinkers approached philosophical anthropology as an area of claims superior to the claims of disciplines like philosophy of history, or culture? It is worthwhile to point our attention to certain hidden premises that are internal to the doctrine, and which also in this case fulfil the role of a necessary condition of building argumentative bridges between anthropology and social critique. Scheler defines man as a being endowed with a spirit (*Geist*), treating that predicate as a timeless attribute equal in ahistorical status with the category of substance (*ens a se*). From this perspective, man “participates” in the spirit in a metaphysical dimension, and is not treated as a being, transforming through evolution in its biological, or historical manifestations. Scheler does not grade human spiritual competences, and does not look at them as goods endangered by an empirical threat of degeneration, or even loss. Only that motive seems to turn on a social, critical train in Plessner’s, or Gehlen’s reflections—they registered the historical symptoms of unfulfilment through the historical, social entities of the criteria they recognized as the “conditions of possibility” for humanness. Scheler, on the other hand, employed the ahistorical criterion of “essence of man,” which for him ruled out meticulous historical 

*[p. 78]* ^pg80

inquiry into the conditions for “realizing” that which is human, including its critique. 

This brief introduction already allows us to name this paper’s main pursuit, which is the anthropological reconstruction of the premises of social critique on the example of the views of one of the three great classics of twentieth-century philosophical anthropology—Gehlen (1904–1976). 

2\. KEY ASSUMPTIONS IN ARNOLD GEHLEN’S PHILOSOPHICAL 

ANTHROPOLOGY. BETWEEN GEHLEN AND SCHELER 

According to Gehlen, man—compared to beings from the world of fauna—appears as a “deficient being” (*Mängelwesen*), and to a much lesser extent physically equipped to live in nature and face the typical dangers it presents than animals.[^1-p78] Without “claws and fangs,” as Gehlen writes, and without instincts channeling impulses from the outside world, being born “too early” from the point of view of physical maturity norms typical for many mammals, man is forced to search for compensation of that innate mark of lack of adaptation. Otherwise, as a species, man would be sentenced to extinction. 

That inalienable compensatory function is fulfilled, according to Gehlen, by language and social institutions. Language reduces the flood of stimulations reaching human receptors from the outside (*Reizüberflutung*), which have not been preselected by instincts. By naming objects and events in his surroundings, man “tames” reality, making it into an ordered structure of objects and processes, which stop surprising the human observer, and appear as elements of particular classes of objects and phenomena identified in terms of meaning. The conceptual function of language grants a teleological structure to the chaos of impulses—man conceptualizes, for example, future states of objects, in which the observed objects participate. They are no longer “raw data” in character, and become included into imagined, or conceptualized, projections of the future. Imagination, of course, can refer to the past as well. Someone who “lives by his memories” as well as someone who “looks to the future” will relieve his or her perceptual apparatus, reduce its “overload” combined with a surplus of actual impulses. The communicative function of language, on the other hand, allows its user to focus on information important for the scenarios of different types of social situations, as well as the expectations of the participants in the interaction. That which allows those interactions to preserve “correctness” particular to the acts of speech—as researched by John Searle—becomes important within the field of sensations and observations. This excess of drives is reduced, according to Gehlen, by social institutions (family, property, tribal identity with its hierarchy and diversification of social roles, the state), which channel human drives as, unlike animal drives, they lack fixed objects of desire clearly defined by an instinct. The final word of Gehlen’s philosophical anthropology is the concept of *ratio*, to which he grants a linguistic and social form simultaneously: humanness is fulfilled where there is an opportunity for spontaneous, rational behavior as compensation for the human-typical inaptitude for complex, instinctive behavior. Rationality of action demands concentration on the goals of a given activity and the tools that enable their attainment, which is impossible outside of language or the social sphere that guarantees the stability of the subjective and objective conditions of activity.

Gehlen speaks about the social channeling of the drives that constitute humanness. Scheler, on the other hand, assumes that the spirit/spiritual value system is a system of lock chambers channeling drives and implementing them into spiritual activities. 3\. THE MAIN THREADS OF GEHLEN’S ANTHROPOLOGICAL CRITIQUE 

OF CONTEMPORARY SOCIETY AND CULTURE 

The doctrinal starting point for this critique is the contention that the above listed social institutions are in crisis in the second half of the twentieth century. As one could suspect, the illustrative importance of this crisis factor, as well as the depth of the anthropological risk connected with it are, in Gehlen’s mind, related to the very nature of social institutions. On the basis of contractualism (a tradition in social philosophy started by Thomas Hobbes) one could argue that the historical “crisis of institutions” does not constitute an anthropological catastrophe, because a hypothetical situation in which their “modified” historical role is “negotiated” is admissible. However, according to Gehlen, social institutions do not stem from conscious/rational decisions made by social actors, do not constitute the realization of any “social contract,” but rather their historical source could be found in anthropogenesis itself: the process of becoming a human being, and the loss by instincts of their behavior-steering functions. Gradually, instincts are replaced by social and institutional steering mechanisms. Individuals are not aware of these phenomena, and do not control their course—they are of a quasi-natural character. As a result, Gehlen’s institutions are creations which do not succumb to the will of individuals. Man cannot “rebel against institutions” freely and without consequences—they do not constitute an object of democratic legitimization. No public vote can, for example, have the democratic power to remove the family as an anthropological fundamental institution. 

Gehlen discusses that “pre-political” (in the sense of political discourse within the boundaries of the Greek polis) weight of institutions in an interesting way, basing on the example of the genesis of archaic magic and religion. Primitive peoples, he assumed, displayed an atavistic desire for a directed reaction to rare impulses, which would evoke—in the case of animals (e.g. the mating plumage of birds)—a particular sequences of instinctive behaviors. When perceiving a nugget of gold, a rainbow, or a full moon, primitive man experienced

In a sense, traditionalists rationalize their longings, granting objects of the said longings the status of an absolute, fundamental value.

4\. GEHLEN AS A CRITIC OF CONTEMPORARY 

“LOSS OF EXPERIENCE” AND “NEW SUBJECTIVISM” 

Gehlen introduces interesting concretizations of those general anthropological statements in his work entitled *Die Seele im technischen Zeitalter,* and published in 1957.[^11-p87] A comprehensive reconstruction of its author’s views would go beyond the scope of this text, therefore in the following passage I would like to focus solely on that train in Gehlen’s reflections that is guided by the aforementioned category of action, which is of key importance for his philosophical anthropology. 

Most likely under the influence of cybernetics (he quotes Norbert Wiener in his work), Gehlen highlights the element of self-guidance in action, and an element of looped feedback embedded in it. He writes: “The circle of action (*Handlungskreis*) is a modifiable, directed motion capable of correction on the basis of its outcome, and which in the end may become automatized and wholly habitual.”[^12-p87] Three elements are of particular importance to Gehlen: a) taking action upon inner motivation (in expectation of subjectively defined success); b) the subject’s empirical contact with reality, which enables distinguishing success from failure; c) the habitual character of actions, which is directly dependent on the degree to which individual actions are rooted within social, institutional structures.

In that context, Gehlen also considered the phenomenon of the so-called “new subjectivism.” He writes: 

“That modern subjectivism is rooted in the wider cultural situation; in the presence of a flood of stimuli that overtax our capacity for emotional response, an emphasis on inner elaboration and ‘psychologization’ represents an attempt to keep things under control; external determinants are vital, though unacknowledged. Emotional reactions can no longer be invested in an external world which has become so reified and deprived of symbolic undertones; raw nature no longer opposes us with a felt resistance, the exertion of bodily effort has become largely dispensable. How could this not have as a consequence a continuous flow of internal experiences, which must be monitored through unceasing awareness and reflection? It is here that art, law, even religion become subjectivized and weakened. ‘Ideas’ bud forth everywhere, and one can only deal with them by discussion, this being the appropriate form of external elaboration. This intellectualization and subjectivization of a culture screened away from action is a novelty of our own historical era.”<sup>24</sup>

Gehlen’s stand contains two important and mentionsworthy doctrinal ambivalencies. The first is of a methodological character, while the second sends us back to a particular inconsistency between the assumptions of Gehlen’s philosophical anthropology and the pessimistic social-psychological diagnosis presented in *Die Seele im technischen Zeitalter*.

In other words, one could ask whether Gehlen, as the author of *Die Seele* …, is primarily a strictly realistic diagnostician of contemporary society, or rather its critic who refers back to certain anthropological, normative standards. Much appears to suggest that he sees his doctrinal solutions in *Man, His Nature and the Place in the World* as a kind of ideal model in the Weberian understanding, a blueprint, with which he juxtaposes contemporary industrial societies. If man is infinitely flexible, one could assume that his contemporary “version” is simply a self-legitimizing “historical norm” which does not undergo any secondary, ahistorical anthropological assessment. In order to stabilize their existence after “growing out of” the animal stage, hominids needed strong institutions. How does an anthropologist-philosopher know, however, that “all” men are dependent on that kind of “strong,” institutional stabilization of existence? How can it be proved that someone who is institutionally entirely “relieved” from the flood of impressions is more “valuable” (in the sense of being more “anthropologically legitimized”) than someone who has to cope with emotional “overload”? It seems that Gehlen has failed to pay sufficient thought to the question of the historical argumentative strength of his paradigmatic anthropological models. He attempts to measure “late culture” (*Spätkultur*) with the prehistoric measure of *Urmensch* (prehistoric man).<sup>30</sup> Is such a practice justified in the light of the deep-going assumptions of that entire anthropological tradition, which was most fully expressed by Plessner, who claimed that man was “unfathomable,”<sup>31</sup> hence indefinable, and continuously sought himself by stepping outside of that very self? SELF-CULTIVATION ACCORDING TO IMMANUEL KANT 

*ABSTRACT* 

The author reflects on the anthropological role of the “self-cultivation” category in the philosophical system of Immanuel Kant, for whom self-cultivation stood as the central idea of the Enlightenment. Kant believed that it was man alone who created himself to a rational being, that his rationality was not a granted good but something he had to mature to by way of multiple disciplinary (the reduction of animality in the *humanum* sphere), civilizing and moralizing (the latter patroned by the Kantian categorical imperative) measures. An interesting avenue in Gernot Böhme’s approach is his assumption that this conceptual perspective applied to all three Kantian Critiques, e.g., that *Critique of Pure Reason* propounds the disciplining of human cognition under the banner of subordinating the sphere of intuition (*Anschauung*) to the categories of intellect (*Verstand)*. These categories are not inborn in the human mind, but are built by the willful disciplining of the perceptual elements of cognition anchored in the animal fundaments of the *humanum*. Towards the close of his essay Böhme attempts a critique of Kant’s philosophy, accusing it of reductionism and depreciating many anthropological powers. 

Keywords: The Enlightenment, education, self-cultivation, *animal rationabile*, disciplining, civilisation, moralization. 

1\. THE ENLIGHTENMENT AND SELF-CULTIVATION 

Europe has known several self-cultivation programmes—the Socratean focus on self-care, the Epicurean endorsement of pleasure, or the Stoic emphasis on self-control. The self-cultivation variant of the Enlightenment was one more such programme, and related to the others. However, it differed strongly from its predecessors in that it did not propound a specific philosophical mode of life nor the lifestyle of any specific social group. On the contrary, it was not elitist but addressed to all. In effect, it was more modest in some respects, but on the whole much more demanding: the self-cultivation programme of the Enlightenment

*[p. 96]* ^pg98

professed to finally make humans truly human. This was because the bourgeoisie was the first social class in European history to lay claim on representing humanity as such. The essence of this programme was to *humanise—*in equal measure other social classes, like the poor and the working class, as the aristocracy—by propagating bourgeois norms and lifestyles.[^1-p96] 

The bourgeois self-cultivation programme was formulated already in the early phase of the Enlightenment with the aim of teaching humans to be human. The fourth part of Comenius’ seven-volume *General Consultation on an Improvement of All Things Human* (1647–1670), the *Pampaedia*, professed universal education. In it, Comenius set forth an educational programme that was not only to embrace all people, but was to form them from the cradle until death. Upbringing and education were a permanent element of European culture since ancient times. However, they were not addressed to everyone but reserved for the wealthy and the ruling classes, who were taught higher skills and refined conduct. Also, education almost exclusively embraced the male part of society. It was not until Comenius that it began to be accessible to all, to humanity as such. 

In the closing years of the Enlightenment era, Kant summarised his theses regarding universal education in his lectures on pedagogy with the words, “Man can become man through education only.”[^2] This, therefore, means man is not yet actually human in the sense of his development under whatever circumstances, that man as an empirical presence is not really man, because he has not yet attained his designation—which is to be a rational being. Thus, man is his own development project. Kant states this clearly in his reformulation of the classical definition of man as *animal rationale* into the formula of man as *animal rationabile*:[^3-p96] reason is a part of human nature, but only as a possibility. Man in his empirical form is not rational, but rather *should* become so. Kant explicitly described this project of educating man to humanity as self-cultivation.[^4-p96] Alongside nature, history and science, education was one of the four main dimensions of the Enlightenment.[^5-p96] Therefore, it should not be perceived only as a critical project. Much rather, it was a broad-scale educational movement. In

The quest for a different self-cultivation model calls for the revision of the Enlightenment project together with its project of educating man to humanity. This is best done where its far-reaching implications are most evident—in the writings of Kant. In effect, the unity of Kant’s philosophy and the common elements that bind his works together will appear in a new light.

3\. DISCIPLINING 

It may be somewhat surprising to see the *Critique of Pure Reason* described as the disciplining phase in Kant’s self-cultivation project*.* The reasons for this are twofold: First, Kant’s critique of reason has proven so successful, that everyone seems to have forgotten about his actual aim, which was to discipline reason, curb its unbridled imagination and put an end to its fantasies. We have overlooked or forgotten the fact that Kant developed his critical philosophy in connection with his analyses of Emanuel Swedenborg’s speculations, and that the basic premises of the *Critique of Pure Reason* can be already found in the *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer Illustrated by Dreams of Metaphysics*,[^14-p100] which came out fifteen years earlier. The *Critique of Pure Reason* is usually read as a substantiation of objective cognition and readers often do not even reach the part where Kant outlines his findings—the section entitled *Transcendental Doctrine of Method* which in one half consists of a chapter called “The Discipline of Pure Reason.” Here we can find formulations which very much resemble those in the *Lecture-Notes on Pedagogy*. Reason must be tamed where it transgresses the boundaries of pure cognition and abandons its natural function of broadening perception for fantasy: 

“But where neither empirical nor pure intuition keeps reason in a visible track, namely in its transcendental use in accordance with mere concepts, there it so badly needs a discipline to constrain its propensity to expansion beyond the narrow boundaries of possible experience and to preserve it from straying and error that the entire philosophy of pure reason is concerned merely with this negative use.”[^15-p100] 

The second reason why the *Critique of Pure Reason* is usually not perceived as a disciplining project is connected to the rather vulgar way in which the Kantian cognition theory is interpreted. This interpretation runs as follows: There is a cognitive subject to which we relate all our representations, and tied to this cognitive subject are certain categories and perception forms, through

He tolerates it only insofar as it serves intellect, and thereby ultimately becomes a function of intellect.[^19-p102] 

This disciplining of the imagination in the Kantian cognition theory is only the tip of the iceberg when it comes to Kant’s dispute with the imagination. He saw the imagination as an outright threat to humanity if humanity was to remain in rational control of itself. Quite in keeping with what we find in the *Lecture-* *Notes*, his chief aim was to subjugate the animal in the human being, and the imagination represented the animal element in the human emotional makeup. The imagination is a vital force, which is best evidenced by its function as a producer of dreams. Kant held dreams for necessary as they were what continuously stimulated our vital forces while we were asleep. If we did not dream in our sleep we would die.[^20-p102] Kant sees danger already at this point, because dreams could “spill” over into our conscious life. Therefore, Kant’s anthropology recommended the rules of dietetics—to go to bed early and rise quickly and energetically early in the morning.[^21-p102] 

 The *Anthropology* devotes many a paragraph to imagination, which Kant holds responsible of all the world’s suffering, diseases, abnormalities and derailments. From hypochondria (which he himself had to battle for while), through drunkenness, exaltation and enthusiasm to mental ailments, and especially insanity and delusion—everywhere there, he sees the unbridled force of the imagination at work in the background. Recognisable in this litany are some typical Enlightenment enemies. Always characteristic here is what Kant describes as a symptom of drunkenness: that the affected person is in “the unnatural condition of inability to order one's sense representations according to laws of experience.”[^22] The imagination is a threat to the sober and pragmatic approach to cognition, and at an even earlier stage obscures the important boundary between dreams and reality. However, much more is at stake—the autonomy of the human person itself. As Kant concludes, “the borderline of self-control can be so easily overlooked and *overstepped*.”[^23-p102]

Not out of modesty but because he shared the common belief that “genius is mixed with a certain dose of madness.”[^25-p103] 

Kant’s verdict is especially severe on the kind of imagination that underlies feelings of sympathy like compassion, infatuation, or emulation. Thus, it is not advisable for hypochondriacs to visit lunatic asylums out of curiosity—establishments which, owing to their forbidding reputation, were often referred to as a disciplining measure in the Enlightenment era’s upbringing model—because they have to “fear for their sanity.”[^26-p103] As for love, Kant notes, “This sickness, the effect of an inventive power of imagination, is incurable: except through *marriage*.”[^27-p103] 

With the disciplining of the mind on one hand, and of the imagination on the other, most crucial in man’s self-cultivation to a pragmatically judging subject is the enforcement of the rule of intellect. In order to be able to contribute to the pool of objective experiences we share with others, and thereby also to elevate ourselves, it is necessary to style one’s own cognitive abilities according to the pure rules of intellect. 

4\. CIVILISATION 

Self-cultivation understood as civilisation mainly addresses the development of taste. Taste is, as Kant put it in the heading of paragraph 40 of the *Critique of Judgment*, “a kind of *sensus communis.*” Of course, taste must first evolve socially and the human individual must adjust its preferences accordingly, otherwise society will become barbaric[^28-p103] and the individual an aesthetical egoist. Already at this stage it is clear that the development of taste involves the individual’s integration into an ordered life environment. 

In the *Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View* Kant offers this definition of taste: “Taste is the faculty of the aesthetic power of judgment to choose with universal validity.”[^29-p103] This is true in equal measure for the choice of clothes

This also gives the development of taste the status of a preparatory course for morality. As Kant observes in the here-quoted paragraph of the *Critique of Judgment*, “our ability to judge \[provides\] a transition from sense enjoyment to moral feeling.”[^33-p104] Kant considered this transition especially important, because he believed that in a civilised phase in which the human individual was still enabled to pursue its own interests, it was also learning conduct that corresponded with moral demands (even if its roots did not lie in morality) through its adjustment to taste and accepted customs. This is the content of the paragraph “On Permissilbe Moral Illusion” \[Von dem erlaubten moralischen Schein\].[^34-p104] Taste as a conduct guideline does lead to respectability, but not yet to morality.

5\. MORALISATION 

In his work *On Education* Kant writes: “We live in an age of discipline, culture, and refinement, but we are still a long way off from the age of moral training.”[^35-p105] Perhaps this is why Kant’s reflections on moralisation are considerably more tenuous than on disciplining and civilisation. The goal is clear—an envisioned kingdom of rational beings in which all behave according to the categorical imperative, i.e. in such a way that the maxims that guide their conduct can simultaneously function as a common laws. But how does one become such a human being? From the point of view of self-cultivation it is not enough to construct a moral system, the question is much rather what abilities man should possess to be able to comply with it. Unlike contemporary ethics, Kant posed this question, concluding at the close of his 1785 *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals* that the issue perplexed him: 

“But now *how* pure reason can for itself be practical, without any other incentive that might be taken from anywhere else, i.e., how the mere *principle of the universal validity of all its maxims as laws* (which obviously would be the form of a pure practical reason), without any material (object) of the will in which one might previously take any interest, should for itself yield an incentive and effect an interest that would be called purely *moral—*or in other words, *how pure reason could be practical—*all human reason is entirely incapable of explaining that, and all the effort and labor spent in seeking an explanation are lost.”[^36] At this point, however, it should be noted that Kant had hardly any opportunity to undertake steps towards his own moralisation. In the stolid bourgeois environment in which he lived, orderly conduct, i.e. the outer appearances of morality, were quite sufficient to get by. In fact, it is difficult to say when in his life Kant began to see morals as a serious issue.[^37] Nonetheless, it soon became clear to him that theory would not suffice in the sphere of practical philosophy, so he posed the question again in the chapter on motivation in the 1788 *Critique of Practical Reason*. There, finally, he found the force he sought to enable the transition from moral judgment to moral action, and this in observance of binding laws. 

One could say that alone the conformity of the subjective maxim with the law was enough to call an activity moral. Kant, however, insisted that in order to be moral it was not enough for an activity to be legal, also its motives had to be rooted in lawfulness. Moral activity had to be motivated by moral laws, and

This exclusion has a negative effect on the emotional sphere as it gives rise to a feeling of *humiliation*. However, this feeling evolves into a positive one—of respect for the law. If we compare such individual moralisation through humiliation and awakening respect for the law to the processes involved in disciplining and civilisation, we will find that a social element is still missing. Because it is there that the disciplining of subjective perception corresponds with the development of an intersubjective experience pool and the evolution of the common-sense taste with regard to the beautiful and appropriate that is typical for civilised societies. In the moral sphere we could compare this to the kingdom of rational beings idea. However, this conception still fails to embed the individual moral subject in an intersubjective context—which we find in Kant’s political philosophy: for him the embodiment of practical reason is the republic. Kant visualises the republic as a form of statehood in which individual freedom is reality because obedience is required only to those laws that can be accepted by all. Because this freedom, which Kant also calls *external freedom*, is “the right through which I am required not to obey any external laws except those to which I could have given my consent.”[^39] This indicates that Kant at the time did not yet consider his era ready for moralisation, although the French Revolution had given him hope for humanity’s evolution in that direction.[^40] 

6\. A DIFFERENT EUROPEAN SELF-CULTIVATION CONCEPTION? 

If, at the close of this revision of the European self-cultivation project in its Enlightenment version, we asked ourselves about the conclusions to be drawn from it, we could at first find ourselves at a loss to find an answer. There is no doubt about the magnificence of this project and what it brought into being: objective science, the civilised bourgeois lifestyle, and constitutional democracy. However, when we ask about the type of human being that inhabited those times, then, in light of Adorno’s verdict, we have to think about it. Were the

But one may also ask if this should at all be expected for such a self-cultivation programme, which from the very outset based on and propounded harmony between the community and the individual. In this system alternative forms of knowledge, multiculturality or civil disobedience were unimaginable. 

Let us once again cast a critical eye at the areas of self-cultivation expounded by Kant: Already the sober style in which the disciplining of the subjectto a carrier of objective knowledge is described suggests considerable rigourism. For the subject, disciplining means the discreditation of all its personal experience and creativity. The subject that this produces proves to be highly unstable as it is constantly plagued by what it is supposed to be avoided: fantasy, bodily sensations, temptation. Only what conforms to the unity of the common experience pool is accepted as knowledge, which rules out subjective experience and alternative knowledge. 

The education of the human being to a *fine* human being implies renouncement of uncivilised wildness, of man as a natural being. What is shining through here is the arrogance of the Enlightenment, which placed the European human on the pinnacle of all human evolution on the ground of his civilisation. Kant’s text is also full of disdainful and derogatory remarks about savages, Caribbeans, and, interestingly, women. Moreover, it is not even suggested that other cultures and ethnic groups are capable of development (not much later Hegel will explicitly deny this ability to the *black continent*). And as for women, the Kantian project of educating man to a rational being does not include them because, as Rousseau advocated, women should remain as *natural* as possible as their immaturity is an integral part of their gender. Of course, in these circumstances there is no room even for the thought that the evolution of taste and civilisation might actually lead to particularisation. The accompanying discrimination is necessary element of this ideology. 

Finally: the moralisation of man as a psychological mechanism whereby the individual is brought to respect the law through the humiliation of its seconsciousness. This is doubtless authoritarian, and teaches to attain authority by obedience and this way to learn how to command. It is not surprising, therefore, that Kant always refers to citizens as subordinates. Such moralisation can only be possible in the utopian harmony between moral subject and the state which Kant envisioned as the *republic*. Where this harmony is disrupted or completely absent, the individual subject becomes a carrier or paralysed object of circumstance. The identification of good with the common good does not allow something like situational ethics or strict commitment to *this particular person here* to even appear. 

The above gives us *ex negativo* at least a rough idea of a new conception of self-cultivation. Its aim is to revive the natural in man, i.e. the ability to “be body” (which Kant calls animality), to rehabilitate subjectivity and admit 

*[p. 108]* ^pg110

subjective knowledge, install an ethic of the pathic, legitimise contingency and raise human sensitiveness to the ethic relevance of situations. It also strives to teach man not to act on the pre-assumption that the only truth lies in the whole, the state, the market or the media. In other words, it seeks to develop personal resistance, the strength to civil courage and the spirit to deviate from the trodden path. Most concisely speaking, the autonomous object of cognition can no longer function as the ideal of self-cultivation. This ideal is much more the sovereign human being, whom Sigmund Freud described by his demands: to be able to love, work and cope with frustrations.<sup>41</sup>
