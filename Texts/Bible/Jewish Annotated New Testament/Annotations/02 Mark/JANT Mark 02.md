### 2.1–12: Faith and the forgiveness of sin

*[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-1|Text]], p. 74* · ([[Matthew 09#^9-1|Mt 9.1–8]]; [[Luke 05#^5-17|Lk 5.17–26]]). ^2-1-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-4|4]]:** Typical roofs were constructed of wooden beams placed across the walls, covered with reeds and clay. It would be possible to dig out the clay to lower someone between the beams, but it is difficult to imagine how that process could proceed while people were meeting below. In the story, the extraordinary measures become a sign of faith that deserves Jesus’ attention. ^2-4

**[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-5|5]]:** Repentance of sins is not mentioned here (contrast 1.4,15; 6.12); *faith* (in Jesus’ healing power) is the defining marker of those who become Jesus’ followers (see 1.15n.). *Forgiven*, some Christian tradition has seen in this story an opposition between Judaism with restricted forgiveness and Christianity that offers forgiveness before it is requested. Neither view is accurate. Judaism has various understandings of forgiveness and the route back to acceptance for sinners. The NT elsewhere affirms exclusion, shunning, cursing, and even the death of sinners ([[Matthew 18#^18-17|Mt 18.17]]; [[Acts 05#^5-1|Acts 5.1–11]]; [[1 Corinthians 05#^5-1|1 Cor 5.1–5]],[[1 Corinthians 05#^5-9|9–11]]; [[2 Corinthians 06#^6-14|2 Cor 6.14–7.1]]). ^2-5

**[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-6|6–7]]:** In a largely illiterate society, *scribes* were literate people who handled texts and accounts, and were also interpreters of the Law. They existed both at the local level in the villages of Galilee and at the highest levels of Jerusalem society. Often associated in the Gospels with the Pharisees, they were functionaries for other groups as well. They appear here and in what immediately follows as rule-bound and unable to grasp the significance of Jesus’ ministry. Since it is God who forgives, they view his statement as *blasphemy*, but Jesus’ followers claimed the power of forgiveness (cf. [[Matthew 16#^16-19|Mt 16.19]]; [[Matthew 18#^18-18|18.18]]; [[John 20#^20-22|Jn 20.22–23]]). An earlier Jewish text, the *Prayer of Nabonidus* (4Q242), describes a Jewish exorcist pardoning the sins of the Babylonian king Nabonidus and curing him. In the Hebrew Bible, humans may intercede with God to forgive, but they—even Moses—never forgive directly (e.g., [[Torah 37 - Sh'lach#^14-19|Num 14.19]]; Am 7.2). ^2-6

**[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-10|10]]:** *Son of Man*, see “Son of Man,” p. 74. ^2-10

### 2.13–17: Tax collectors and sinners

> [!note] Son of Man *(p. 74)*
> The title for Jesus that Mark uses most often is Son of Man or “son of humanity” (Gk *ho huios tou anthrōpou*). Although originally simply an evocative way to say “human being” ([[Ezekiel 02#^2-1|Ezek 2.1]]), it underwent a significant transformation in the pre-Christian era, as reflected in [[Daniel 07#^7-13|Dan 7.13]], which refers to the angel of judgment (probably Michael) as “one like a son of man” (KJV), that is, one who looked like a human being. In apocalyptic Jewish texts such as *1 En.* 37–71, “Son of Man”
>
> became a term for God’s eschatological heavenly judge. Jesus may have spoken of the Son of Man as a figure other than himself, the coming judge, but Mark’s narrative identifies this figure with Jesus. Mark introduces the important idea, retained by Matthew and Luke, that the Son of Man also suffers and is killed. Mark thus identifies Isaiah’s suffering servant ([[Isaiah 52#^52-13|Isa 52.13–53.12]]) and the suffering righteous person (Wis 2–5) with the Messiah and Son of Man (see 8.27**–**33n.). As far as we know, this identification is original to Mark.

### 2.18–22: Fasting

*[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-18|Text]], p. 75* · ([[Matthew 09#^9-14|Mt 9.14–17]]; [[Luke 05#^5-33|Lk 5.33–39]]). The followers of Jesus and John the Baptist never fully merged ([[Matthew 11#^11-18|Mt 11.18–19]]). The Mandaeans, a small group, many of whom live today on the border of Iran and Iraq, trace their origins to John the Baptist. The Hebrew Bible mentions fasting in relation to repentance, during mourning (including mourning the destruction of the Temple, [[Zechariah 07#^7-3|Zech 7.3]]; the prophet suggests that this practice will be rescinded in the future in 8.19), and for supplications ([[Joel 1#^1-14|Joel 1.14]]; [[Esther 04#^4-16|Esth 4.16]]; [[Ezra 08#^8-21|Ezra 8.21]]). Fixed days for fasting begin to appear in the Second Temple period (see [[Zechariah 08#^8-19|Zech. 8.19]]; cf. 7.3). Some Jews such as the Pharisees, and John the Baptist and his disciples took up a discipline of fasting, as did some followers of Jesus ([[Matthew 06#^6-16|Mt 6.16–18]]; *Did.* 8.1), but here and in [[Luke 07#^7-33|Lk 7.33–34]], fasting while the *bridegroom*, the eschatological Son of Man, is present, is rejected. ^2-18

### 2.23–28: Plucking grain on the Sabbath

*[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-23|Text]], p. 75* · ([[Matthew 12#^12-1|Mt 12.1–8]]; [[Luke 06#^6-1|Lk 6.1–5]]). This controversy story, like many of the others, involves a legal challenge to Jesus, characterized as petty and mean-spirited, and a pithy rejoinder that addresses more pressing human needs (v 27). The argument form, familiar in both Greek *wine into fresh wineskins* philosophical and rabbinic legal material, is from the lesser to the greater: if David could supersede law to meet human needs ([[I Samuel 21#^21-2|1 Sam 21.1–6]]), so could Jesus’ disciples (vv 25–26;see also 7.6–13; 10.3–8; 12.26–27). Mark does not accurately follow the biblical text, a fact that makes the question—*Have you never read?*, accidentally ironic. The Samuel story is modified in various ways here: in 1 Sam 21, *David* is not explicitly depicted as acting from hunger, and he does not enter the *house of God* to eat *the bread of the Presence*; the priest is Ahimelech, not *Abiathar*. This story, like the Gospels’ other conflict stories, was likely created to define the identities of Jesus’ followers and their opponents. The depiction of Pharisees in the field on the Sabbath to observe behavior is also likely not historical and was created as part of this boundary definition. ^2-23

> [!note] Tax Collectors and Pharisees *(p. 75)*
> Tax collectors enjoy a special, even a favored status in Matthew, Mark, and Luke. Yet they are totally absent in Paul, John, and most early Christian texts. Not collectors of Roman direct taxes or of the Jewish Temple tax, and neither slaves nor poor, the Gospels’ tax collectors were minor officials responsible for local duties and tolls; in some cases they controlled local monopolies, such as the sale of salt. Since these low-level en-trepreneurs kept whatever was in excess of the amount they had agreed to supply to the Roman administration, they were easily portrayed (justly or not) in both rabbinic and Christian texts as ex-tortionists, morally questionable, and the least likely to repent ([[Matthew 05#^5-46|Mt 5.46]]; [[Matthew 21#^21-31|21.31]]; [[Luke 03#^3-12|Lk 3.12–13]]; [[Luke 07#^7-34|7.34]]; *m. Hag.* 3.6; *m. Ned.* 3.4; *m. B. Kamma* 10.1–2). For Mark, the tax collectors (who would have been seen as traitorous, unfaithful, and greedy) are positively contrasted with the Pharisees (who would have been seen as righteous, faithful, and eschewing of wealth). See “Pharisees,” p. 619.

**[[NRSVue Mark 02#^2-27|27–28]]:** In rabbinic law, Sabbath restrictions could be set aside if a life was in danger. Jesus advocates a similar Sabbath exception (*t. Shabb.* 16.12; and cf. [[Mark 03#^3-1|Mk 3.1–6]]). Since *Son of Man* could simply mean a person (see “Son of Man,” p. 74), it is possible that this saying originally meant that everyone is *lord even of the sabbath* in the sense of enjoying the day rather than feeling burdened by Sabbath laws (*b. Yoma* 85b reads: “The Sabbath is given for you, not you to the Sabbath”). ^2-27
