## Chapter 6

CEREBRA:

““ALL-HUMAN”, “ALL-TOO-HUMAN”,

“ALL-TOO-TRANSHUMAN”

In thinking the passage from the “all-human cerebrum” (H.G. Wells) to what one might call the contemporary “all-too-human” cerebrum in neo-liberal societies and beyond to the “all-too-transhuman” cerebrum'® in the cybernetic society (Kurzweil, 2016), and contrasting Wells’s idea of a new world order with the dystopia of the disordering un-world (Nancy, 2002/2007), while considering the prospects of a “world brain” faced with the realities of the “global mnemotechnical system” (Stiegler), in highlighting the differences between the global and authoritarian instrument of “control” in Wells and the descriptions of the control society by Deleuze, and finally, in critiquing the “unifying of the general intelligence services of the world” in Wells’s work (1938, pp. 3-4) and the capturing of the “general intellect” !? (Wark, 2017), this chapter maps the contemporary prospects of the “world brain” against the backdrop of the worldweariness of the present.

This chapter will compare Wells’s writings of the 1930s and 1940s with Stiegler’s work in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, and apply their respective utopian and dystopian philosophies to the contemporary crisis in education and the fate of the university as an institution. This is undertaken to ask how the promise of the “brain of mankind” (Wells, 1938, p. 92) — the cerebrum of which is the most superior and anterior of the brain’s major regions — has been devastated by the “global mnemotechnical system” (Stiegler, 2012/2015). This is undertaken to construct a philosophy of education which may extract from the viewpoints of these two very different thinkers new problems and possible solutions regarding the fate of education. Instead of detailing the belief systems underpinning the world brain, I will, through a reapplication of the concept’s use, critique not so

*[p. 115]* ^pg130

much global information systems or emergent intelligence but more precisely the “global mnemotechnical system” as Stiegler designates it —seeing it as a vital complement, update and critique to Deleuze’s societies of control thesis.”°

History of the Concept of the “World Brain” A proliferation of names and concepts come to mind when we think about the notion of the “world brain”. Chief among them, of course, is Diderot and D’Alembert of the Encyclopedist movement in France in the mid-eighteenth century. In the 19th century, Vladimir Vernadsky (1863-1945) adopted the concept of “noosphere” or global consciousness and later Teilhard de Chardin (1881-1955), one of the originators of transhumanism, embraced an idealist philosophy which predicted the evolution of the human mind into the “noosphere”; this, he believed, would eventually signal the emergence of the “spirit of the Earth”. The growing interest in the idea of a human mind in the 19th century by such writers comes at a time when the machine age and technological revolution had yet to be fully understood. This optimism vis-a-vis the possibilities of technology, as we shall see, is tempered by the pessimism of Stiegler whose view is that technology did not appear as a concept of philosophical value as such before the 18th century and that symbolic misery or proletarianization has increased since the beginning of the machine age. The “world brain” also has other contemporary rivals, for example, the global brain (Bloom, 2000), as well as the global mind and social brain. There is Pierre Lévy’s notion of collective intelligence and virtualization (1994/1999), Metaman (Stock, 1993), Kevin Kelly’s hive mind (2003) and ongoing discussions regarding the melding of human and machine by Elon Musk and the long-standing critique of such a reality by Paul Virilio (1999).\*! Yet, in this chapter, Wells’s conception of the “world brain” is focused upon because its promise and vision seem to have been eclipsed and conspicuously overturned in the current era of the “global mnemotechnical system” and this for me is worthy of thought.

*[p. 116]* ^pg131

What is the Wellsian “World Brain”? 

To probe this change, we shall examine Wells’s World Brain (1938) in which he conceives of a new world encyclopaedia, organized in terms of social repression and control. Wells thinks the “world brain” may act as an antidote to nationalism and the threat of war. As such he aims to create a world order to counter the threat of violence and the outbreak of planetary violence. The “world brain” he believes is the basis for an actual “world mind”. For Wells, this is a real possibility, as he says: “the creation of a greater mental superstructure to reorient the mind of the world is an entirely practicable proposal” (Wells, 1942, p. 158; Rayward, 1999). 

All-human cerebrum 

In the wreckage of Europe after the First World War, Wells continued to construct new hopes and emergent, kinetic utopias (Wells, 1905; Partington, 2002; Feather-stone, 2010). Yet the optimistic belief that modern utopias would not be static but kinetic, and would “shape not as a permanent state but as a hopeful stage”, leading to “a long ascent of stages” (Wells, 1905) seems to no longer apply to our time. For some, the hope in both static and kinetic utopias seems at an end. Indeed, Featherstone (2010, p. 7) is one such scholar who finds that in the late 20th and early 21st century, the sense of kinetic utopia has taken on a new, “parasitical” meaning, contrary and antithetical to its originary impulse. Postmodern kinetic utopias are now tied to neo-liberal ideology, extending into every space and time, creating a new form of existential claustrophobia. With the prospects of world peace, a new world order, and new forms of humanism perceived as belonging to a less realistic age, have we moderns created an “all-too-human” cerebrum conditioned by neo-liberal societies and are we on our way to creating its dystopian “all-too-transhuman” cybernetic variant? In our time, even as the grand projects or narratives have seemingly fallen by the wayside, we use the “world brain” every day in its google mode (Japanese young people refer to google as google-sensei – google teacher) (see Hillis et al., 2012). But for some writers, this reality is one that is perturbed, psychotic, schizophrenic even. The vessel is definitely there to hold information efficiently and infinitely but knowledge is elsewhere. It is not in our heads. 

*[p. 117]* ^pg132

The loss of kinetic utopias might be conceived as a loss of the desire for becoming other; the loss of the desire for the Overman, the abandonment of any free vision of the future; this signals the reign of nihilism, the last man and the leveling away of difference and bifurcation. As we know with Nietzsche in his *Genealogy of Morals*, memory is the invention emerging from the internalization of pain. The retention of memories is the device of the man of *ressentiment*, the man who must remember. If we all-too-human moderns toil from the hypertrophy of a schizoid inner life, this suggests that a global memory would serve nihilistic, slavish ends. The knowledge of mankind is the knowledge of the last man. In the exteriorization of global memory, in its Wellsian global reach, memory and knowledge serve sickly, nihilistic and ignoble ends. 

We shall concern ourselves with the capturing and storage of memory and the corresponding loss of attention. In the 1930s, Wells (1938, pp. 13– 14) called for the “unifying of the general intelligence services of the world” in a “gigantic and many-sided educational renascence” – the end goal of which, for Wells, was a new world order. In *The World Brain* (1938), Wells writes enthusiastically of the limitless possibilities of the planetary cerebrum: 

This new all-human cerebrum… need not be concentrated in any one single place. It need not be vulnerable as a human head or a human heart is vulnerable. It can be reproduced exactly and fully, in Peru, China, Iceland, Central Africa, or wherever else seems to afford an insurance against danger and interruption. It can have at once, the concentration of a craniate animal and the diffused vitality of an amoeba. 

There is no practical obstacle whatever now to the creation of an efficient index to all human knowledge, ideas and achievements. To the creation that is of a complete planetary memory for all mankind. 

His new world order combines human progress with universal education. This world community of around two billion educated individuals is overseen by a central encyclopaedic organization run by experts – whose remit is to inform, suggest, direct and unify. Experts would sift through avalanches of information to extract knowledge for the betterment of mankind. For Wells, this new encyclopaedism is the fundamental problem and indeed a fundamental solution in world education matters. 

*[p. 118]* ^pg133

Writing in 1938, Wells claims that the construction of a “world encyclopaedia” with its “planetary memory for all mankind” was not only a feasible but a vital necessity lest mankind suffer its own demise. There is no other choice for humanity: It is either knowledge or extinction, a new world or nothing; we are in the last chance saloon, says Wells: “We have to make a new world for ourselves or we shall suffer and perish amidst the downfall of the decaying old” (Wells, 1938, p. 47). For Wells, the encyclopaedia is a “scheme for the reorganization and reorientation of education and information throughout the world” (Wells, 1938, p. 17). Overseeing the world encyclopaedia would be “one single world university” which would administer, research and publish the encyclopaedia (this is the modern day goal of Wikipedia). A year later in The Fate of Homo Sapiens (1939), Wells goes on to claim that the world encyclopaedia would be “a permanent institution, a mighty super-university, holding together, utilising and dominating all the teaching and research organizations at present in existence.” In defence of his somewhat authoritarian idea, Wells claims this vision is democratic in principle as it would not signal another form of elitism led by the intellectual class for the intellectual class but rather a means to make contemporary knowledge and thought universally accessible to all.

In his novella The Camford Visitation (1937), and writing on the question of world memory, Wells details the technology behind the encyclopaedia: “Every book, pamphlet, paper, statement, letter, photograph, chart in existence, every fact and all discussion could be filmed upon an ultra-microscopic scale, stored away, indexed \[...\] and any part or item in it reproduced upon a suitable screen whenever required. This collection would constitute a sort of world memory”. He anticipates the now everyday occurrence of access to the internet for many people across the planet: “any student, in any part of the world, will be able to sit with his projector in his own study at his or her convenience to examine any book, any document, in an exact replica.”

He imagines a cerebrum for humanity, a cerebral cortex, a memory and a perception of reality for the entire human race (Wells, 1938, p. 91). A world mind is formed within the cortex of a “world brain” by the emerging technologies at hand. In his day, it was micro-film,” now, it is the internet. In what for some appears a nightmare vision of unpalatable consequences,

*[p. 119]* ^pg134

Wells predicted the organization would spread “like a nervous network, a system of mental control about the globe, knitting all of the intellectual workers of the world through a common interest and common medium of expression into a more and more conscious co-operating unity and a growing sense of their own dignity, informing without pressure or propaganda, directing without tyranny” (Wells, 1938, p. 33). 

Given the time and context of his writing, this optimism did not last long. In the aftermath of Nazism and World War II, and finding no new vision of society apparent on the horizon, no new Overman or utopia in sight, no new kinetic utopias, Wells declares that the “pattern of things to come” has faded away. In his despondent *Mind at the end of its Tether* (1945), he finds no way out of a “jaded world devoid of recuperative power”. As he says the end of everything in life is “close at hand” and “cannot be evaded”. He writes: 

So that in the present vast confusion of our world, there was always the assumption of an ultimate restoration of rationality, an adaptation and a resumption. It was merely a question, the fascinating question, of what forms the new rational phase would assume, what Overman, Erewhon or what not, would break through the transitory clouds and turmoil. 

All-too-human cerebrum 

Although I hold a certain critical viewpoint on Stiegler’s work (Bradley, 2015) I do believe there is much to be gleaned in terms of the philosophy of education. This chapter tries to articulate this view through a comparison with Wells’s science fiction because both Wells and Stiegler see education as central to the task of re-enchanting society. While Wells sees education and the university in particular as a critical, natural organ of the “world brain”, Stiegler decries how the universities are failing to provide a space of invention and reflection from the command and control of the “global mnemotechnical system” (Stiegler, 2012/2015, p. 167) – the network of computers which externalizes human memory in databases. Stiegler analyzes the way in which we use objects to exteriorize memory and how technology affects, directs and controls our attention span.<sup>23</sup> In both Wells and Stiegler, this all-human cerebrum is also an all-memory cerebrum. We must remember lest we recommit the errors of the past. In its Stieglerian 

*[p. 120]* ^pg135

iteration, in the hyper-industrial society, the all-memory cerebrum in its current form is an endangerment to society because it leads to a loss of attention, to a loss of wonder, to a loss of concern for the pain of others, to a loss of knowledge transmitted across the generations (transindividuation circuits).

Even if there is much that is hyperbolic or bombastic in Stiegler’s work this can be set aside as there is an unswerving commitment in his writing regarding contemporary youth culture that suffers through a kind of hyper-attention disorder, overseen by an automatic superego of the global media. Youth suffer from myriad woes — cognitive overflow, stimulation addiction, depression (symbolic misery), and a death drive which can spill out into violence (Stieger, 2008/2010b). Clearly, this is a concern for educationalists around the world as it impacts on the “subjectification” of students, that is to say, the coming into presence of the individual in society (Biesta, 2016). Whether this is an unintended consequence of the vision of a “world brain” is to be determined below.

For the moment let us briefly turn to Michel Serres’ book Thumbelina (2012/2015) to see how knowledge has left our heads and fallen into our palms. Reading Thumbelina one quickly gets the impression that the ghost of Wells is speaking through the French philosopher. I say this because Serres raises the question of the role of the teacher as that person who is good at sieving through vast amounts of information, who is expert at understanding information and so is a master of creating knowledge. And in Wells’s work, running the “world brain” is a group of educated administrators who index and sort and clarify vast sways of information in a similar manner. However, from Serres’s perspective, this role is no longer the preserve of the teacher as he or she no longer is able to offer this vocation. He writes (2012/2015, p. 30):

Why is Thumbelina less and less interested in what the Teacher-Voice has to say? Because, given the increasing supply of knowledge in an immense depository — everywhere and always accessible — a one-time and singular supply becomes ridiculous. Why is this so? Because overabundant knowledge is always within reach, in the small device carried by Thumbelina.

*[p. 121]* ^pg136

There is a phase change underway in how young people learn because, for Serres, a new paradigm has taken root with knowledge no longer bequeathed solely by the teacher as repository or paragon of learning and pedagogy. Rather, the whereabouts of knowledge is “all in computers”. Having outsourced much of her retentional memory to mobile devices, Thumbelina – as Serres designates the user – has knowledge “in her hands” (p. 28). As a result this radically transforms teaching and knowledge. Although aware that the mass media has long held the role of the real teaching function, and that teachers are less and less heard, Serres insists optimistically that young people are learning afresh, using data differently, no longer dependent on rote learning compared to former generations. He suggests that such a situation demands that teachers respond in due fashion. 

As the technological revolution of the 20th century has achieved the philosophical goal of education – that is to say, to make knowledge accessible to the masses, much like the “world brain” imagined by Wells – young people now are learning and creating and inventing independently, all of the time, always on the go. One expects this is in keeping with Wells’s earlier kinetic, utopian vision. However, Serres is critical of leaving knowledge production in the hands of search engines and digital content producers. This view is shared by Stiegler who is concerned with the prospect of the “obsolescence of the human” because what is at stake in this age, he says, is a “combat for the politics of memory and, more precisely, for the constitution of sustainable hypomnesic milieus” (Mitchell & Hansen, 2010, pp. 68–69). If our age is on the way to inexorably becoming transhuman, he advocates continued vigilance against the culture industry and control societies, and rails against the “society of consumption” because consumer capitalism (marketing) via interconnected digital platforms strengthens the machinery that exploits desire. Thumbelina is holding, outside of herself, a “cognition that used to be inside her” (p. 18), Serres writes, adding: “In our hands, the computer-box contains and manages what we used to call our ‘faculties’: a memory thousands of times more powerful than our own; an imagination stocked with millions of icons; and a faculty of reason as well, since software programs can solve hundreds of problems that we could never solve on our own” (p. 19). Knowledge is provided to all. Faced with the historic reality Serres asks if Thumbelina is presiding over “the end of the era of knowledge” (p. 20). Put another way, as 

*[p. 122]* ^pg137

Thumbelina has the knowledge and equipment hitherto promised to her by the university and her professors, Serres writes that because everything is in her hands (pp. 28–29): “It is accessible, from any portal, via the web, from Wikipedia. It is explained, documented, and illustrated, with no more errors than those found in the best encyclopaedias.” 

For Serres, then, it appears we have entered into the era of transhuman post-knowledge in which all that matters is invention for the sake of invention. At the end of knowledge, invention springs. Serres argues: “The voice of yesteryear is no longer needed – unless someone, original and rare, invents. It is the end of the era of knowledge” (p. 29). And in remarks on the democratization of knowledge that Wells one imagines would welcome, Serres claims that as knowledge – long held captive in libraries and by elite intellectuals – is accessible to all and made ready-on-demand by electronic media, particularly online search engines, students are at liberty to frolic in “the incandescent joy of invention” (p. 19). From Stiegler’s point of view, however one may react to this brave new world, what must be acknowledged is a new relation between primary, secondary and tertiary memory (retentions in the language of phenomenology), and therefore a new understanding of the pharmakon (poison and cure) of memory and control and the very condition of possibility of invention itself. In this respect, it is this new mnemotechnics, or act of memory, which this chapter is seeking to account for. 

World Brain and global mnemotechnics 

Stiegler is concerned with how “technologies of spirit” redirect attention, desire and memory. We might put it better by saying that as communication technologies control or affect the relations between individuals and communities, through audio-visual consumption, the libido is increasingly destroyed. Moreover, in the new era of human technological development, computation and the production of symbols affect the role of memory. Stiegler insists we are currently witnessing the fusion between technical and mnemotechnical systems and globalization as such. For example, writing has undergone a transformation to form a global mnemotechnical system. In Stiegler’s language, the global technical system is a mnemotechnical system for the industrial production of tertiary retentions – that is to say, 

*[p. 123]* ^pg138

those memories outsourced to data banks, etc. The flux of consciousness is captured by this production. The result is that the global mnemotechnical order creates endemic stupidity or bétise of unparalleled magnitude —through what Stiegler designates generalized proletarianization, that is, the general loss of knowledge. There is a passage from absolute knowledge to non-knowledge, and ultimately this leads to the destruction of knowledge. In For a New Critique of Political Economy (2009/2010a) Stiegler refers to a global mnemotechnical system which disseminates “systemic stupidity” and which structurally prevents the reconstitution of long-term transindividual circuits — that is to say relationships between parent-child, teacher-student (Stiegler 2009/2010a, p. 5).

What is produced by such a “global mnemotechnical system” is a disindividuated self (2006/2012), a self which suffers like the hikikomori or social recluse in Japan in terms of Weltschmerz. In other words, it is the loss of a relation to the outside, to the world, to authentic relations. Here we find concrete expression of the movement from “world brain” to worldweariness. Crucially, pure cognitive labor power is essentially “evacuated of knowledge” — leading to a loss of wonder, a kind of brain damaged, autistic subject unable to relate ethically to the world of others (Malabou & Miller, 2012). Or as Stiegler puts it, we produce more and more “information without knowledge” (p. 129). One can surmise that this is clearly at odds with the Wellsian vision of the “world brain” as Stiegler is arguing for taking care of and cultivating youth, so as to inspire belief that there is something better than the present order of things. Stiegler writes:

To take care, to cultivate, is to dedicate oneself to a cult, to believe there is something better: the non-inhuman par excellence, both in its projection to the level of ideas (consistencies) and in that this ‘better’ must come. This is exactly the ethos for which techniques of the self are required \[...\]. (Stiegler, 2009/2010a, pp. 178-179)

This stance is opposed to the perceived sense of systemic stupidity and calls for a pharmacological critique that may engender a new form of therapy, a therapeutics of the pharmakon — a kind of “philosophy of care” (Stiegler, 2013, p. 24). Why? Taking care is to cultivate, transform and improve. It is concerned with the therapeutic and curative pole of the pharmakon. The search is on to find a way of paying attention through the invention of

*[p. 124]* ^pg139

therapeutics. Stiegler thinks of the pharmakon as a “weapon”, a way to invent new tertiary retentions and prosthetics adoptable as therapeutic pharmaka (2012/2015, p. 74). Accordingly, in order to re-enchant the world, philosophy must lead a new fight: its remit is “to find the new philosophical arms needed for this political combat” (2006/2012).

The struggle against proletarianization is the fight against the destruction of cognitive functions and the short-circuiting of reason (2012/2015, p. 171), and for Stiegler, this is the task of philosophy in its current articulation: it is to forge a critique of impure, technological and instrumental reason. We can see that the “global mnemotechnical system” as described by Stiegler is distinct from Wells’s world brain idea as the latter saw the world brain not as a technological network, but as an institution and bureaucracy. Whereas our time sees technologies of information dominating the creation of knowledge, Wells envisioned the world brain as a “permanent institution”; it was viewed as an organ interconnected with the world network of universities, with the remit to co-ordinate them for the betterment of humanity. The purpose was to disseminate the “general intelligence of the world”. The world brain would be a kind of “mental clearing house for the mind” (Wells, 1938, p. 69), a site where knowledge and ideas were “received, sorted, summarized, digested and clarified” (Wells, 1938, p. 69). Staffed by experts and indexers like any good library it would be aided by a vast army of workers (17 million is the figure quoted in 1993 book The Shape of Things to Come), who would undertake the information sifting. Wells writes:

Nor was there any conception of the need of a permanent system of ordered knowledge, continually revised until the twentieth century was nearing its end. To the people of the Age of Frustration our interlocking research, digest, discussion, verification, notification and informative organizations, our Fundamental Knowledge System, that is, with its special stations everywhere, its regional bureaus, its central city at Barcelona, its seventeen million active workers and its five million correspondents and reserve enquirers, would have seemed incredibly vast... We are still enlarging this Brain of Mankind, still increasing its cells, extending its records and making its interactions more rapid and effective. A vast independent literature flourishes beside it.

*[p. 125]* ^pg140

Here it seems fair to question whether the modern university in its current organization could perform such a role of educating for the purpose of democratic enrichment. Such a task is made more difficult by the fact that teachers and professors have to compete with the technological capturing of memory as Serres argues above. So the project and positive prospects of the “world brain” seem to have been replaced by the “global mnemotechnical system” which dominates the sensorial world of its global usership or spectatorship. While Wells clearly envisaged something very different from the chaosmosis of the internet, since the world brain was designed in a nonrhizomatic fashion, the concept remains central to considerations regarding the constitution of knowledge in the current milieu.

To return to Stiegler for a moment, he has called for a root and branch return to “the base of knowledge” (which I think is akin to Wells’s “educational renascence’’). This “base of knowledge” would be, I believe, to return to universities the historical role of the bastion of knowledge. From his reading of Simondon, Stiegler’s demand for a return to the “base of knowledge” is to contest the destruction of the “subject” or what he designates as a process of disindividuation. Indeed, we may think of this as a new dream of encyclopaedism. This encyclopaedism, the central problem of world education, for Wells, is also the concern of contemporary philosophy in the sense that philosophy is tasked with a kind of Wittgensteinian mission, a kind of tidying up of the mess of information. Both Wells and Stiegler have a concern with fashioning a new kind of humanism, seen respectively as positive and nihilistic, which I shall consider in the light of the “all-too-transhuman cerebrum” in the next section.

All-too-transhuman cerebrum

Mankind is at the end of its collective tether, but the global mind is at the beginning of its collective tethering.

— Joff P.N. Bradley

For Pierre Lévy (1994/1999), thinking the contemporary crisis through the concepts of information machines and collective intelligence grants us access to a new kind of object which is the evolution of the human. This brings us to the question of the transhuman. Pierre Lévy is talking about a new form of humanism, “universality without totality”, not a postmodern,

*[p. 126]* ^pg141

posthuman reality but one directed by Enlightenment goals. This form of transhumanism is differentiated from the work of Teilhard de Chardin (1955/1961). In this work the goal of the planetary brain (noosphere or world-brain) displaces man (anthropos) and foregrounds ecosystems. This noosphere is therefore a primary component of the “planetary” consciousness. Moreover, the desire for the becoming cyborg of mankind is another such transhuman expression. Yet for Stiegler, this transhuman dream is but an extension of the logic of contemporary capitalism (Livingstone, 2015). Furthermore, Deleuze and Guattari distinguish between the spiritual dimension of the noosphere in Teilhard de Chardin and their cybernetic vision of society. They write (1980/1987): “There is no biosphere or noosphere, but everywhere the same mechanosphere.” Perhaps it is this concept which can help us to reconceptualize the Deleuzian notion of social control (Deleuze, 1992).

Moreover, we can think of this transhuman nightmare and its connection with memory, the world brain and social control if we briefly consider Dennis Potter’s Co/d Lazarus (1996), a British science fiction TV drama set in the 24th century where society is dominated by the virtual world. In this society controlled by large corporations, memories from deceased human brains, some of which lived in the 20th century, are retrieved through cryogenic techniques for the vicarious pleasures of citizens. The problem arises when one of those brains, Feeld’s, is aware of its resurrection, is witness to the memories brought back to consciousness, and suffers excruciatingly as a consequence. The horror of this experience suggests some ways in which the world brain and the retention of other forms of knowledge can become a matter of control and trauma as Virilio suggests.

As a great deal of focus is given over to educational matters in Stiegler’s oeuvre, for example the nature of the “ailing university” (Stiegler, 2008), the fall-off in attention and care, the breakdown of the school classroom and the corresponding destruction of the bond between teacher and student (loss of transindividuation circuits),”\* philosophers of education are beginning to read him more and more. There is already a growing body of work on Stiegler in English in the field of the philosophy of education (Adams, 2017; Bojesen, 2016; Bradley, 2015; Kouppanou, 2015; Lewin, 2016; Peters & Jandric, 2015; Reveley, 2014; Reveley & Peters, 2016; Vlieghe, 2014). Moreover, Reveley and Peters (2016) recognize Stiegler as a thinker and

*[p. 127]* ^pg142

fellow traveller in the philosophy of education. They argue that from Stiegler’s point of view various forms of “social innovation in education” (or invention) – that is, “open education’s implicative sharing and democratization of knowledge” (Reveley & Peters 2016, p. 1459) – may act as a counterweight to the prevalent “global mnemotechnical system”. Critically, Stiegler, they contend, needs to rethink this aspect of “social innovation in education”. Let us look at a few relevant observations made by Stiegler regarding the nature of the digital and its impact on the university. 

In *The Digital Future of the University* (2013), Stiegler sets out his views on the significance of the digital. The digital constitutes a new episteme, which is one affecting the nature of knowledge in all its forms. For Stiegler the transformation of how knowledge is created, stored, interpreted, and transmitted calls for a new episteme. He argues it is inconceivable that universities “do not have at the core of their concerns and the top of their priorities the digital transformation of knowledge” (2013). The digital, he says, functions in our epoch the same way that writing did in antiquity. We are in the midst of a cultural revolution which is radically transforming the nature of intelligence and memory and therefore it is imperative that philosophers of education understand this *fundamentally*. Concepts in Stiegler’s work which shine light on this view are principally the “destruction of knowledge”, the psychical effects on youth (“frustration”, anxiety, “disruption” or “shock”) and drive-based addictions. Moreover, Stiegler is steadfast in his critique of both the proletarianization process under capitalism (following the exteriorization of knowledge there is the spread of symbolic misery) and what the corresponding excrescent growth of alienation entails for education. Stiegler’s criticism of the proletarianization of workers and consumers extends to students in higher education institutions. He frames this perspective vis-[illegible]-vis the ongoing paradigm shift in thought regarding the anthropocene. With respect to a pervading sense of trauma, Stiegler speaks profoundly as a philosopher of education and has specifically stated the necessity for producing new forms of knowledge in the wake of the global ecological catastrophe. He explains (Stiegler & Sloterdijk 2016): 

I would say that the question is what is melancholy in the anthropocene and the status of melancholy and that is of denial. Because the real question of anthropocene for me is madness. The real question is madness. The increase 

*[p. 128]* ^pg143

of madness. It is statistically testified today in many countries for example in China there is an increasing of depression etc., etc. The question is that in the situation in which everybody anticipates the end of the anthropocene, as the possibility of the end of life as such. This is repressed and denied by people and this produces madness. This produces symptoms and sometimes those symptoms are Jihadism, suicide, auto-intoxication, fascism etc., etc. So the question is not can we have something but rather we must produce this possibility and the problem is that today we are overcome by such a disruption that makes us incapable to produce it and I think it is possible to produce it but we have to revisit completely the base of knowledge. Writing against what he calls cognitive and affective saturation, Stiegler, in critiquing the loss of consciousness and affect, claims disaffected people and human wastelands are as conspicuous as “disused factories” and “industrial wastelands” (2006/2012, p. 90). Thus, he is not calling unthinkingly for a return to some pristine form of humanity but for a noesis or thinking which makes thought capable of producing a future which can bifurcate into becoming otherwise — which I read as a kind of utopia, a return of wonder, the provision of belief in the future through forms of collective intelligence. He is distinguishing between the proletarianization of reason (destruction of knowledge) and where the understanding has yet to become a process of mere automatic computation. And while not a straightforward Luddite, he is critical of the exteriorization of knowledge in machines and apparatuses (in tertiary retentions) when and where this leads to a deficit of knowledge.

From my point of view, Stiegler’s work remains timely because it updates and extends some of the themes found in Deleuze’s speculative, short essay on control societies — a concept which continues to be applied in the philosophy of education (Cole, 2017). It also situates the philosophy of education centrally in the world and age of digital media. Importantly, central to Stiegler’s work is the loss of creativity in modern societies, a thesis which Deleuze and Guattari share in What is Philosophy? (1991/1994). It can be argued that Stiegler is a thinker who compels the philosophy of education to confront the nihilism of the age, to question the ‘all-too-human’ goals, as Nietzsche might put it. This is to approach pedagogical questions from a philosophical point of view and, conversely, philosophical questions from a pedagogical point of view (Peters & Jandric,

*[p. 129]* ^pg144

2015). Here, I want to follow the point made by Peters and Jandric and name Stiegler as a pedagogical philosopher, a “provocateur”, who can be used to develop “philosophy as pedagogy”. In other words, he is a thinker who proffers a “pedagogical philosophy” (Peters & Jandric, 2015, p. 169). This is to evoke the conception of philosophy as a form of therapeutics, or invention in itself, a view one imagines is shared by Stiegler. For Stiegler, his is agreeable but only if the emphasis and critique pertain to digital or ‘cybernetic capitalism” and this compels philosophy to examine the new ‘ forms of power and control in the epoch of digital reason. Here philosophy is considered a weapon to contest disindividuation, deskilling and proletarianization.

Philosophy is posited as a form of invention, a warding off of the sad affects and prevalent models of stupidity in higher education — the loss of noesis (intellectual apprehension), and the loss of the savoirs (loss of knowledge of how to do, be, live, think, etc.). Stiegler insists, following Gilbert Simondon, that proletarianization consists in the loss of knowledge in relation to techniques of production, or a loss of savoir-faire (know-how or job skills) (Stiegler, 2006/2012). There is also a loss of knowledge of the techniques of living, which Stiegler refers to as the loss of savoir-vivre (social or life skills) (Stiegler, 2006/2012).

This is the agon against the becoming-wild (devenir mécréance) in education. Stiegler argues that the battle against stupidity produces a pharmacological critique that may itself engender a new therapy. Indeed, both Deleuze and Stiegler call for action against the intolerable, the wastefulness of youth, and decry the mindlessness or stupidity of youth turned to pulp (/a loque). In the case of Deleuze, he says he was always against his students turning to pulp. Stiegler continues to rail against a range of maladies — the fall off in deep learning and retention, proletarianization, uncontrollable or unliveable societies, the loss of ethos or sense of place. The current formation of the world brain in its digital mode is accelerating the trend towards the loss of the world. We moderns are left with a brain without a world. In a tone full of anger, frustration and resolution against the loss of knowledge, and loss of world building, Stiegler writes (2006/2012, p. 62):

\[T\]he slow decomposition of the motives for living, in the ruined milieu of what had previously contained possibilities of existing, signifies that the

*[p. 130]* ^pg145

reign of despair, that is, the destruction of every reason to hope, has now become immense and terrifying.

So Stiegler is considering the possibility of a pharmacological university (Featherstone, 2017a) which can be the primary site of struggle and escape, the primary site for the creation of the world, a key site of struggle between intelligence and stupidity, a possible antidote or safe haven for those “perfectly indifferent to the world” — those without wonder (see Abbinnett, 2018). The task of philosophy here is of constructing such futures and hopes (Featherstone, 2017a, 2017b). Stiegler is also defending deep attention or more simply the capacity to read in a sustained manner. He is writing against the capturing of available brain time by marketing industries — those industries that Deleuze and Guattari (1991/1994) mock as the fake inventors of concepts. As Stiegler says, young people suffer from a “colossal” attention deficit disorder of “unprecedented scale and magnitude”. Proletarianization, he argues, as the destruction of knowledge, as the destruction of the possibility of re-interiorization, is the destruction of the time required to undertake reading, and of the attentive capacities that have to be cultivated to make reading a possible and desirable act. This is important because when Wells talks of the army of researchers necessary to sustain the “world brain”, in his optimistic vision he could not have foreseen how the capturing of attention has been exploited by the marketing industries.

While Deleuze exhorts us to search for new weapons, for Stiegler these will be technological inventions. Stiegler’s point is that the modern forms of knowledge which have emerged since the beginning of the industrial revolution (Stiegler claims the idea of technology did not appear as such before the 18th century) are based on technological innovation. In this context, the world brain is one such modern form of knowledge and one based on technical breakthrough. For Stiegler, the task is to use new digital technologies like the internet in a dis-automatizing, de-proletarianizing way, which is to say, to re-interiorize knowledge or memorize knowledge rather than exteriorizing it in vast archives of knowledge like the world brain. He is arguing for an economy of contribution to counter the control of users by big data and to restore fundamental forms of knowledge in the world’s cerebrum.

Conclusion

A key notion in Stiegler’s work is that the “symbolic misery” of being human is what engenders “societies of control” (Stiegler, 2004/2014). This is a very interesting extension to Deleuze’s societies of control thesis. And it also fills a lacuna in Wells’s utopian oeuvre regarding the notion of “symbolic misery”. Both Deleuze and then Stiegler argue that individuals become hyper-synchronized “dividuals” or big data sets (reduced to sampling data, marketing data, archive data, banks of information) and are subsumed and massified. Deleuze says that societies of control operate through and by computers, where technological evolution involves a mutation of capitalism, and where marketing becomes the center of corporations of power (and their corruption). The operation of the markets then becomes “an instrument of social control”. This is consistent with Guattari (1977/1984, p. 249), who argues that control works in subtle ways: “Certainly social control has never been achieved with as little violence as it is today.” So again to return to the promise of the “world brain”, if it is left to the market, the masses will be quickly socially engineered and controlled by purveyors of occupied attention, and the exercise of the democratic access to information databases is thereby imperiled. The end to be served would not be for human betterment — a human cerebrum (world peace) but the maximization of profit — an all-too-human cerebrum satisfying the drives. Moreover, for Stiegler, it is necessary for philosophers to examine new forms of power and control in the epoch of digital reason, as well as to understand the nature of our becoming, encompassing both our all-too-human and all-too-transhuman cerebrum
