## Chapter 3

## Transcendence, Poetics, and the Formation of the I Can

In chapter 2 we saw that Ricoeur’s early eidetic phenomenology of the will brackets two realities that are contingent rather than essential features of the will: fault and Transcendence. Ricoeur studies fault indirectly, following a hermeneutical detour through the symbols and myths of sin and evil. He recapitulates the diverse symbols of evil in one central concept: the bound will. In this chapter we will examine the other reality that was bracketed: *Transcendence*. This theme is also of vital importance to Ricoeur’s Philosophy of the Will, since Transcendence liberates freedom from its bondage (FN 29). This chapter will show how this liberation takes place through poetic discourse, working through imagination and desire to disclose transformative possibilities for the *I can*. We will also see that this poetic liberation is not merely an intellectual process, but engages and mobilizes the self in the fullness of its embodied being. By setting forth the essential features of Ricoeur’s poetics, we will be setting forth some of the basic principles that underlie Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, which we will discuss in chapter 4.

### Transcendence

Central to the problem of fault is the issue of the subject setting itself up as absolute, as self-positing, as the origin of its own acts. Transcendence brings about the radical decentering of this self-centered subject. Near the end of *Freedom and Nature*, Ricoeur describes a second Copernican revolution, which would reorient the self around Transcendence (FN 472, 476). Ricoeur had planned to examine the liberating power of Transcendence in Volume Three of his Philosophy of the Will, under the title of *Poetics of the Will*. Ricoeur never completed this work, and regretted his youthful ambition in 

*[p. 58]* ^pg75

laying out such a massive research program, but he does give some idea of what the work was meant to examine and some of the thinkers he would engage (e.g., Blondel, Pascal, Dostoyevsky, Bergson, Marcel) (FN 31). He does this in the introduction to *Freedom and Nature* and in his subsequent work, which continues to work out the sort of insights and arguments one would expect from the unpublished *Poetics of the Will*.

In *Freud and Philosophy*, Ricoeur writes that the *Poetics of the Will* “concerns the radical origin of the *I will*, that is, the source of effectiveness of the act of willing.” He describes this “new dimension as a call, a kerygma, a word addressed to me” (FP 524–525). What is given in this call is the transcendent source of deliverance, redemption, salvation, and “rediscovered integrity” (FN 29). Ricoeur is cautious about objectifying Transcendence, since this liberation is still something that happens “to a freedom” (FN 30). We can detect Barth’s influence here, with Ricoeur insisting that Transcendence is a contingent event, and as such cannot be examined under the usual modes of cause and effect, nor be treated as a universal, necessary, or essential structure of the will. Transcendence is “Wholly Other,” and thus it initially “repels me” in my desire to be a self-positing consciousness. This repulsion reveals “the most fundamental choice of philosophy: either God or I” (FN 477).

As other as this liberating Transcendence might be, it does not deliver the will in a purely extrinsic way. Freedom is a gift in which “I receive all,” yet “I am complete freedom in the very receiving of that gift.” Transcendence therefore “becomes immanent to the extent to which the will purifies itself in becoming actively associated with its own liberation” (FN 31). In short, it requires my consent. I can say *Yes.* “To say *yes* remains my act” (FN 479). This requires a transformation of the self, which becomes botha passive recipient and an active participant in the liberation of its freedom. This will be an enduring theme in Ricoeur’s work—namely, the conviction that liberation requires both *alterity* and *ipseity*, both other and self.

Ricoeur also notes that the *Poetics of the Will* should include the notion of love and the other person (FN 31). The theme of the other does not figure into the eidetic analysis of the will, because the other can properly be considered not in terms of motive and obstacle for my decisions, but instead as the “thou” who “begets me in my very decision, inspires me in the heart of my freedom, and exercises on me an effect which is in some way seminal, akin to creative action” (FN 32). This is the sense in which Ricoeur writes that Transcendence “hides within it the ultimate origin of subjectivity” (FN 3). Thus Ricoeur promises that the encounter with the other will be central to the poetics of freedom.

According to Ricoeur, the relation between freedom and Transcendence cannot be thought systematically, any more than the relation of freedom and nature (FN 33). But the full sense of Transcendence is “more than a limit 

*[p. 59]* ^pg76

concept: it is a *presence* which brings about a true revolution in the theory of subjectivity. It introduces into it a radically new dimension, the *poetic* dimension” (FN 486). Reflection on Transcendence means grappling with the “a-logic of paradox,” and the task of poetics is to discern the paradox and mystery of freedom and Transcendence (FN 33).

### Poetics

According to Ricoeur, “Conversion of the imaginary is the central aim of poetics.”<sup>1</sup> Ricoeur’s concept of poetics is rooted in his work on imagination. “Poetics” comes from the Greek word *poiesis*, which refers to making, creating, producing. For Ricoeur, imagination is a poetic faculty because it is creative: “Poetry is the art of conjuring up the world as created. It is in effect the order of creation which description holds in suspension” (FN 30). The imagination creates a “free play of possibilities” in which we are able to explore new ways of seeing, being, and acting. Imagination effects a metamorphosis; it redescribes reality according to new and unforeseen possibilities.<sup>2</sup>

With this account Ricoeur undertakes a rehabilitation of imagination, which has had a dubious reputation throughout the history of philosophy. In contrast to the empiricist view of imagination as derivative of perception, such that it cannot give anything truly *new*,<sup>3</sup> Ricoeur argues that imagination is genuinely productive. His champion in this campaign is Kant, who discovered the role of imagination in mediating between intuition and understanding. The schematism of the imagination gives an image to a concept; it “is a rule for producing images” (TA 173). Ricoeur also goes beyond Kant, however, by identifying the link between imagination and language, observing that imagination is operative in discourse before perception. “Before being a fading perception, the image is an emerging meaning” (TA 173). Imagination transforms our way of saying, and that is what transforms our way of seeing, and our way of being and acting in the world. 

In *The Rule of Metaphor* Ricoeur credits Gaston Bachelard with showing “that the image is not a residue of impression, but an aura surrounding speech” (RM 214–15).<sup>4</sup> Bachelard is also important for showing the genuinely productive, creative potency of the image: it has the power to transform us, to initiate the “becoming of our being.” The image reverberates and brings about a “poetic creation.” In reading, the poetic image “becomes a new being in our language, expressing us by making us what it expresses: in other words, it is at once a becoming of expression, and a becoming of our being. Here expression creates being. . . . One could not meditate in a zone that preceded language.”<sup>5</sup> Further: “The poetic image is an emergence from language, it is always a little above the language of signification.” Bachelard 

*[p. 60]* ^pg77

rejects the instrumental view of language insofar as it neglects the ways in which language allows new realities to emerge: “By living the poems we read, we have then the salutary experience of emerging . . . poetry puts language in a state of emergence, in which life becomes manifest through its vivacity.”<sup>6</sup> In short, the poetic image is productive insofar as it brings about ontological novelty—something new in being.

This view of language is essential to Ricoeur’s account of the referential function of poetic discourse. Ordinary descriptive discourse refers to perceptible objects, familiar things, as well as the objects of scientific inquiry, verification, and measurement. Ricoeur calls this first-order reference. But poetic discourse does not refer to objects; it concerns our participation in and belonging to being, which is ontologically more originary than our objective relation to things. Poetic discourse involves a second-order reference, suspending the first-order descriptive function and referring to our being in the world.

Ricoeur first develops this insight in his work on metaphor, which he treats extensively in *The Rule of Metaphor (La métaphore vive—*“the living metaphor”). Metaphorical discourse is a work of the productive imagination occurring at the level of the sentence, in the form of a creative predication. Ricoeur cites an example from Baudelaire: “Nature is a temple where living columns . . .” Although nature is not literally a temple, this impertinent attribution produces a “semantic shock” and with it a semantic innovation—that is, it creates a new meaning, since the image of living columns “forces one to *see* life *as* architecture and architecture *as* life” (H 15). The *saying*-as of metaphor facilitates a new mode of *seeing*-as. Metaphor thus suspends a first-order reference to present actuality and initiates a second-order reference to the world as possibility.

Metaphorical innovation is not simply a matter of making up stuff. Metaphor has a “‘realistic’ intention” (RM 247), as it discloses new truth and previously unnoticed aspects of being. Metaphorical discourse invents new meaning, in the etymological sense of the word *invent*: to find, come upon, encounter. In the poetic function of metaphor, creation coincides with discovery, projection with finding. Metaphor breaks with our assumption that being is merely the sum total of things, a world populated by manipulable objects, and restores a more fundamental contact with being that is prior to the dualistic opposition of subjects and objects. 

Ricoeur also cites a poem by Hölderlin describing “the joyous undulation of the waves.” The positivist will insist that there is no objective reality in this image, that it is merely subjective value imposed onto objective fact, an inner feeling or emotion projected onto the neutral outward world. This purportedly “objective” view is phenomenologically inadequate, however, because it neglects the “‘poetic textures’ of the world,” which are ontologically 

*[p. 61]* ^pg78

prior to the split between subjective and objective, value and fact, inner and outer, projecting and finding (RM 246). Poetic discourse can therefore be restorative:

Poetic discourse brings to language a pre-objective world in which we find ourselves already rooted, but in which we also project our innermost possibilities. We must thus dismantle the reign of objects in order to let be, and to allow to be uttered, our primordial belonging to a world which we inhabit, that is to say, which at once precedes us and receives the imprint of our works. (RM 306)

Reflection still has a vital role to play here, but it is not one of transcending being to a realm of pure consciousness. Instead, reflection must begin in the ontological fullness of poetic discourse. Yet reflection is still able “to place itself at a distance and to consider itself . . . as related to the totality of what is.” Through the encounter with metaphor, the cogito once again sees that reflection does not begin with the self-positing I. Through reflection, the cogito sees that it, like language, “is installed in being” (RM 304).

Ricoeur is not advocating a retreat into fuzzy thinking. Reflection, like philosophical thinking in general, does not reach an end in metaphor, but is rather expanded and enriched by the encounter with metaphor—the *living* metaphor, that is. “Metaphor is living not only to the extent that it vivifies a constituted language. Metaphor is living by virtue of the fact that it introduces the spark of imagination into a ‘thinking more’ at the conceptual level. This struggle to ‘think more,’ guided by the ‘vivifying principle,’ is the soul of interpretation” (RM 303). The living metaphor gives rise to thought.

The same is true of narrative, which Ricoeur discusses at even greater length than metaphor in his three-volume *Time and Narrative*. Like metaphor, narrative is the work of the productive imagination and involves semantic innovation, that is, the creation of new meaning. The difference is that metaphor achieves this at the level of the sentence while narrative achieves this at the level of the plot. The productive imagination performs the work of *emplotment*, which brings different elements like characters, motives, intentions, causes, and chance events into a coherent story—“the temporal unity of a whole and complete action” (TN1 ix).

The narrative imagination is genuinely productive, not merely reproductive. Ricoeur makes this point by appealing to Aristotle’s notion of *mythos,* or plot, which is a kind of *mimesis*. Its mimetic function is not, however, merely imitative—a reproduction or copy of an action or event. Instead, the mimetic function of the plot (*mythos*) is “one of a reorganization at a higher level of significance and efficacity” (H 16).

Ricoeur outlines three stages of this mimetic activity. The first—mimesis<sup>1</sup>—is the narrative *prefiguration* of human experience, which is populated 

*[p. 62]* ^pg79

by characters, intentions, actions, and outcomes that have intrinsic narrative potentiality. They invite narrative *configuration* (Mimesis<sup>2</sup>), in which the narrator draws together these diverse elements into an intelligible whole (TN1 65). Narration also brings the flux of experience into a temporal unity, allowing us to understand and appropriate the past, and likewise to form a cohesive personal identity—something Ricoeur discusses at length in *Oneself as Another*. This narrative *con*figuration ultimately leads toward a third moment (Mimesis<sup>3</sup>), which is the *re*figuration of our understanding of ourselves and our world (TN1 70–71). Through the power of narrative to refigure reality, we encounter a world brought to life with new possibilities of meaning and action (TA 174). This refiguration is what Ricoeur calls the movement from *text* to *action*.

This movement occurs in different types of narrative, from historical to autobiographical to fictional. Fictional narrative projects possibilities, which initiate a “confrontation between the world of the text and the world of the reader.” Ricoeur therefore writes of the “heuristic force” of fiction: Through the fictional image, the reader can explore possible courses of action, anticipate consequences, and evaluate desires and ethical obligations (TN1 177). In his words: “Literature is a vast laboratory in which we experiment with estimations, evaluations, and judgments of approval and condemnation,” thereby allowing fiction to serve “as a propaedeutic to ethics” (OA 115). Thus we can see that narrative, like metaphor, involves a second-order reference. It discloses the world as possibility.

\*

Ricoeur summarizes poetic language with three key points: (1) Poetic language “breaks with everyday language” and “is constituted in the crucible of semantic innovation.” (2) Poetic language, “far from celebrating language for itself, opens up a new world, which is the issue of the text, the world of the poem.” (3) “The world of the text is what incites the reader, or the listener, to understand him or herself in the face of the text and to develop, in imagination and sympathy, the *self* capable of inhabiting this world by deploying his or her ownmost possibilities there” (FS 232).

Poetics is about semantic innovation—the emergence of new meaning—in which a new world is proposed, and which initiates a new self-understanding. But notice the presence of the word “capable” in Ricoeur’s summary. Ricoeur’s work on the poetics of the will is not only about changing our self-understanding on an intellectual level. More fundamentally, it is about the poetic transformation that makes us *capable* of inhabiting the world in front of the text. But how exactly does that happen? To answer that question, we need to consider Ricoeur’s work on the relation between imagination, will, and conatus.

### Poetics and the Conatus

Poetic discourse is transformative because it reveals new possibilities of being. Ricoeur’s notion of possibility needs comment here, since it is easy to misunderstand what he means if we think of possibility only in terms of our images or ideas of what could be. While that is part of it, for Ricoeur, possibility also connotes *dynamis*, *potentia*, potency, power. For Ricoeur, *I imagine* is not simply a variation on the *I think*, but on the *I can*. This allows us to understand what Ricoeur means when he writes: “Fiction and poetry intend being, not under the modality of being-given, but under the modality of power-to-be” (TA 86). “Power” is a key word here, indicating the capability that is poetically disclosed.

Ricoeur develops this idea of power from Aristotle’s notion of *dynamis*, as well as Spinoza’s idea of the *conatus*, which is a basic metaphysical principle of all beings: “Each thing, in so far as it is in itself, endeavors to persist in its own being.”<sup>7</sup> According to Ricoeur, “all Spinozist themes can be centered on the notion of life.”<sup>8</sup> In Spinoza’s *Ethics*, life is power—not in the sense of potentiality as distinguished from actuality, but in terms of active productivity. There is a dynamism in all living things, and this dynamic life is closely connected to “the power of the intelligence, which governs the passage from inadequate to adequate ideas.” This is why Spinoza’s *Ethics* is an ethics: it is all about the progression from inadequate to adequate ideas, so the mind can be free and powerful (OA 316).

Like Spinoza, Ricoeur sees a vital connection between the *conatus*—the productive, dynamic power of life—and the mind. This is why he writes that “we are powerful when we understand adequately our, as it were, horizontal and external dependence with respect to all things, and our vertical and immanent dependence with respect to the primordial power that Spinoza continues to name ‘God’” (OA 316). This phrase is crucial to Ricoeur’s account of the transformative potency of poetics: *We are powerful when we understand.* Ricoeur is not an intellectualist arguing that if we just have the right ideas we will become good. Remember that Ricoeur, following Nabert, has expanded the scope of reflexive philosophy. The understanding in question is not narrowly intellectual, not a matter of mere ideas, but rather a re-appropriation of the desire to exist and the effort to be. It is a discovery and recovery of the ultimate origin of subjectivity, which is not a self-positing ego but a transcendent source.

Because the ambition of the reflective method is to get hold once again of pure acts, through signs in which they reveal their signification, it never aims at a simple description but at a reappropriation of the primary affirmation by a consciousness which discovers itself dispossessed of that affirmation.<sup>9</sup> 

*[p. 64]* ^pg81

This account of the *conatus* is pivotal to understanding how the poetics of language—the word, the symbol, the metaphor, the narrative, the text—can transform the self. Reflexive philosophy “seeks to have understanding of self, based on ‘the text which its actions have constituted’ .  .  . coincide with ‘a regeneration of its being.’”<sup>10</sup> This regeneration is possible because there is “an underlying connection between word and the active core of our existence”:

Word reaches us on the level of the symbolic structures of our existence, the dynamic schemes that express the way in which we understand our situation and the way in which we project ourselves into this situation. Consequently, there is something which precedes the will and the principle of obligation, which, according to Kant, is the *a priori* structure of this will. This something else is our existence insofar as it is capable of being modified by word. (CI 454)

Our existence is “capable of being modified by word.” The something that precedes the will is, in Ricoeur’s view, the imagination. What moves us, however, is not simply the images alone but the way those images arouse the *conatus*, stirring our desire to be and effort to exist.

This is why the address of the word, the symbol, the metaphor, the narrative, is necessary to liberate the bound will. The conatus is not a self-existent, self-moving principle. It is moved by a Transcendence that is prior to it. Thus Ricoeur writes in *Freedom and Nature* that Transcendence is “an absolute positing of a presence which constantly precedes my own power of self-affirmation . . .” (FN 32–33). One of the fundamental features of fault is its incurvature—that is, the way subjectivity tries to set itself up as the absolute, self-positing origin of its own acts. The true encounter with Transcendence brings about the radical decentering of the self-centered subject. This decentering is radical because it requires an uprooting and recreation of the self. If poetics refers to an order of creating the world anew, this creation “can appear to us concretely only as a death and a resurrection. It means for us *the death of Self*, as the illusion of positing of the self by the self, and the gift of being which heals the rents of freedom” (FN 30).

Nabert is also an important influence on Ricoeur’s reflections on the movement of this self-transcendence. In his *Elements for an Ethic,* Nabert discusses the way goals—particularly, the goals given by culture—introduce schemata of inner regulation by which the self renounces its most immediate impulses and inclinations for the sake of “something other than self.” Nabert describes this as an “ascesis of inclination,” and it is through this “turning away from itself that \[consciousness\] has some chance of discovering the secret of its own being.” This ascesis awakens “a possibility of existence for the self,” freeing in it “a desire to be, a desire to be worthwhile,” so that the self begins to “identify its possibility of existence with the intentions and 

*[p. 65]* ^pg82

movements directed toward these goals. They serve and arouse a desire to be which would not have gained self-consciousness without them. When these goals have awakened self-consciousness, they unceasingly stimulate it . . .” At this point Nabert’s language is less radical than Ricoeur’s: “When these goals appear to contradict tendencies, when they redirect the energies of tendencies to other objects, when they force tendencies to delay their satisfactions, it is not so much to destroy them radically as to make them permeable to an action of a desire which transcends them.” This self-denial is a condition of possibility for “the highest forms of dedication of which man is capable” (EE 106–7).

Nabert’s concern here is with ethical rather than religious transformation, but these passages nevertheless contain several insights that inform Ricoeur’s account of the poetic transformation of the self. First, we only discover the source of subjectivity in self-transcendence. Second, we need schematism that enables self-regulation as well as the mediation of higher goals, or values—Ricoeur’s term in *Freedom and Nature*. This schematism allows the self to envision existential possibilities that would not otherwise be manifest. For Ricoeur this schematism is the productive imagination deduced by Kant. Third, these goals or values do not register on the intellectual level alone, but work on—or “unceasingly stimulate”—the conatus. Fourth, for Nabert the goal of ethics is the realization of freedom (EE 179), which is central to Ricoeur’s whole project in the Philosophy of the Will and beyond.

This is where the theme of capability fits into poetics. Figurative discourse—symbols, metaphors, narratives—allow me to perform imaginative variations on the *I can*. As Ricoeur writes,

it is in the realm of the imaginary that I try out my power to act, that I measure the scope of the “I can.” I impute my own power to myself, as the agent of my own action, only by depicting it to myself in the form of imaginative variations on the theme “I could,” even “I could have done otherwise, if I had wanted to.” . . . What is essential from a phenomenological point of view is that I take possession of the immediate certainty of my power only through the imaginative variations that mediate this certainty. There is thus a progression starting from the simple schematization of my projects, leading through the figurability of my desires, and ending in the imaginative variations of “I can.” (TA 178)

Ricoeur borrows this notion of “imaginative variations” from Husserl. From Hans-Georg Gadamer he borrows the idea of “play,” in the sense of a suspension of moral seriousness and commitment in a way that “opens possibilities of *metamorphosis*” in the self. Imagination allows us to intuit new possibilities of being, and this imaginative intuition is prior to any act of choosing or willing. Thus Ricoeur writes: “It is in the *imagination* that this new being 

*[p. 66]* ^pg83

is first formed in me.” Imagination responds “to the text as a *poem*,” and “the text first speaks to my imagination, proposing to it the ‘figures’ of my liberation” (TA 100–101). By “understanding oneself in front of the text” one receives “from it an enlarged self.” “As a reader, I find myself by losing myself. Reading introduces me into the imaginative variations of the ego. The metamorphosis of the world in play is also the playful metamorphosis of the ego” (TA 88). This is what allows the *moi* to become the *soi*.

Ricoeur published these claims in the 1970s, but they are a clear development of what he writes decades earlier in *Freedom and Nature*, where he argues that “\[t\]he will is fundamentally the ability to receive and approve values” (FN 21). The reception and approval of values comes through the imagination and arouses the *conatus*. Ricoeur makes a similar point in *Fallible Man*, describing how images “direct desire upon the world; I take pleasure in them; in them I am out of myself. . . . The image informs desire, lays it open, and illuminates it. Through the image, desire enters into the field of motivation. From the standpoint of value, desire may be compared to other motives and thereby sacrificed or privileged, approved or reproved” (FM 54—cf. Nabert’s EE 107). 

This imaginative desire is, of course, deeply fallible. It is subject to confusion and deception; we are finite beings, and so there is a basic “affective opacity” in the desired and anticipated thing (FM 54). And because of fault, our imaginative desires are also susceptible to a more corrupt *self*-deception, in which we suppress the truth and add an affective *distortion* to our already opaque desire. There is no guarantee that the imagination will work toward liberation; we also know the essential role it plays in the phenomenology of temptation, in anticipating possibilities of sin. What this means is that imagination and desire are essential ontological structures of the human being and, as such, are the site of conflict between good and evil. They are the locus of fallibility as well as fault, but they are also the target at which the transcendent, liberating word aims. And as we have seen, imagination and desire are both active and passive, so that they are at once passive recipients and active participants in the actualization of new possibilities of being.

### Ricoeur’s Askēsis: Forming the Capable Self

Most of Ricoeur’s account of poetic transformation revolves around the regenerative power of *discourse*, which addresses us through the imagination, redirects our desire to be and our effort to exist, and thereby liberates the will from its bondage to radical evil. In good hermeneutical fashion, Ricoeur focuses on the way texts mediate this liberating power, but we might also wonder whether this focus on textuality overlooks other aspects of religious 

*[p. 67]* ^pg84

formation, such as ritual, liturgy, and spiritual exercises or disciplines.<sup>11</sup> Given Ricoeur’s Reformed background, it is not entirely surprising that he focuses on texts, but he does provide resources for making sense of this essential component of religious life. These resources are especially rich in *Freedom and Nature*, with its phenomenology of embodied agency and the role of exercise in the formation of habit. This aspect of Ricoeur’s thought is particularly relevant to current discussions in philosophy and religious studies about the importance of practices in the formation of subjectivity, as well as various efforts to recover the ancient model of philosophy as an art of living (*technē tou biou*). 

For ancient Greek and Roman philosophers, this philosophical art of living involved spiritual exercise—*askēsis*—as a means of transforming the self and its way of being in the world. Recent years have seen a widespread interest in the ancient model of philosophy as a way of life, owing to the work of Pierre Hadot, Michel Foucault, and several others. On this account, ancient philosophers aimed to transform their way of seeing and being in the world through spiritual exercises. This is a slightly different sense from the way Ricoeur uses the concept of *askēsis*. As we saw in Chapter 1, Ricoeur typically uses the term *ascèse* in reference to a kind of self-denial. He sees the phenomenological *epochē* as a “spiritual discipline (*ascèse*)” (HAP 15) meant to transform the subject and make it capable of the truth. On the other hand, Ricoeur also sees psychoanalysis as a kind of ascetic purification of psychoanalysis, which can liberate the reflective self from its illusion of being the origin of meaning. These disciplines are primarily forms of methodological self-renunciation for practicing philosophy. We might wonder, then, if Ricoeur has anything to say about *askēsis* in the sense of ethical and religious practices of self-formation.

For Ricoeur, the primary types of spiritual exercise are reading and writing. A prime example of this ascetic reading appears in Ricoeur’s discussion of the work of mourning and preparation for death. Philosophy is training for death, as Socrates says in the *Phaedo* and many others have said since. Ricoeur takes up this tradition in his later works, where he reflects on the role of reading and writing as ascetic meditations in the face of death. Reading is one way of undertaking the work of mourning. Literary narratives aid in the development of our being-toward-death. They provide a sort of consolation “in the face of the unknown, or nothingness” by giving examples to the imagination so the self can imagine and anticipate something of what dying might be like. For this reason, Ricoeur writes: “Fiction has a role to play in the apprenticeship of dying.” Similarly, Ricoeur notes the way that “meditation on the Passion of Christ has accompanied .  .  . more than one believer to the last threshold” (OA 162). In this way, reading can facilitate a certain “mourning for oneself” (OA 162).

*[p. 68]* ^pg85

Likewise, writing can also facilitate this process, something Ricoeur demonstrates in his writings collected in *Living up to Death*. These are Ricoeur’s intensely personal reflections on learning how to live joyfully in the face of death—both his own eventual death and the recent death of his wife Simone. The reason these fragments are so personal is that they are not detached, scholarly reflections on the life and death of the subject, but instead Ricoeur’s own attempts at the work of mourning in written form. For Ricoeur, mourning requires a renunciation of oneself, one’s attachment to one’s own life, even one’s individual *ipseity*.<sup>12</sup> Ricoeur quotes Claude-Edmonde Magny to this effect: “‘No one can write unless his heart is pure, unless he has sufficiently cast off his own personality.’ ‘Writing, if it claims to be more than a game, or a gamble, is but a long, endless *labor of ascesis*, a way of casting off by keeping a firm hold on oneself through recognizing and bringing into the world the *other* one always is’” (LD 39). Writing is an ascetic discipline of self-denial, and it can play a vital role in the work of mourning and preparing for one’s own death.

While Ricoeur’s account of ethical and religious transformation tends to revolve around texts, these are not purely disembodied activities. They engage the self as lived body, and show the centrality of corporeality in the ethical and spiritual formation of the self. Spiritual exercises engage the body. Ricoeur shows this in *Freedom and Nature* when he discusses the discipline of the passions. This is a long-standing tradition of philosophy as *therapeia*, or the rational cure of the soul’s maladies. For Ricoeur, the passions are not simply emotions, which are “by nature good.” Emotion belongs to the fundamental nature or possibilities of the human being, whereas passion is a distortion of these possibilities. Further, unlike emotion, passion does not arise out of the body but from the soul, that is, from consciousness—most specifically, from the will. Passion is not, however, purely voluntary. It is an “intimate involuntary” in which the will binds itself. When passion is stirred, it faces possibilities from the involuntary—the body, the emotions—“but the spell comes from the soul” (FN 21). Passion only gains its hold through the power of the will. In Ricoeur’s words, passion is “an entirely spiritual power” (FN 277, 276). The will thereby binds itself. The voluntary reduces itself to the involuntary. Such is the paradox of passion.

Emotion is not, however, a simple or straightforward phenomenon. Like passion, it is fundamentally ambiguous. On the one hand, emotion is a “corporeal form of the involuntary” (FN 279)—a bodily involuntary that provides us with an excuse or “alibi.” Our emotions are not up to us, contra Stoicism. The Stoics were, however, correct to recognize that emotion is not merely a reflex. Emotions can surprise us, to be sure, but they emerge in relation to consciousness and, as such, they are “partly subject to wisdom.” The soul can 

*[p. 69]* ^pg86

recognize emotions as the embryonic form of passions, and by grasping them “from above” it becomes possible to work on curing them (FN 278).

One way of curing the passions is to work on the thoughts and evaluations that underlie them. For instance, regarding anger Ricoeur writes: “I do not cure myself of anger without curing myself of excessive self-esteem and of the susceptibility to injury deriving from it; these are the bad imaginations, oppressors of the will which constitute the combustible matter of emotion” (FN 278). Thoughts, evaluations, and “bad imaginations” give the emotions their power. As Ricoeur puts it: “If in emotion the body deprives me of my control, it is because first some thoughts—and almost always passions—have preceded this revolt” (FN 279). This revolt takes place through the imagination. Emotion projects its beliefs, thoughts, and judgments onto the imagination, which gives these representations “a vitality, flesh, and a quasi-presence.” I imagine myself as the wronged, mistreated, offended one. I therefore need to correct these representations in order to neutralize my “bad imaginations,” and I can do this if I “turn my attention to values higher than my threatened values or my insulted reputation,” or by “forcefully imagining the reasons opposed to anger or fear.” This process of alteration “engenders a revolution in the imagination” (FN 313–14). I begin to see the world in a different way. New meanings and possibilities show themselves.

This revolution also alters my response at the corporeal level. In Ricoeur’s words, “this action on the level of representation has repercussions right down to the visceral level,” insofar as it “engenders a contrary emotion.” Ricoeur credits Descartes with recognizing this: “Descartes showed admirably that the art of living lies in part in playing one ‘passion’ against another” (FN 314).

In order for these strategies to be consistently effective, though, the self needs the mediating role of *habit* (FN 314). The art of living requires habituation. The formation of habit is a way of changing ourselves through our acts (FM 57), so that we acquire new “capabilities for willing” (FN 292). Habit provides new structures, new schemata of meaning and movement. Habit changes what we are responsive to, what possibilities suggest themselves, and how we are disposed to respond. These new schemata are adaptable, and can be “transposed” to different situations (FN 288), allowing us to “launch an act with a minimum of effort” (FN 302). Habit inclines us to some possibilities and disinclines us to others (FM 57). In other words, habit gives the self a definite form.

This self-formation takes place not only in the soul but in the body as well. Virtuous habits are a matter of taming emotion and taking “possession of my body” (FN 315). Virtue consists in part in appropriating my own body, coming to know it as my own, and gaining a proper kind of bodily control. Ricoeur outlines three ways in which habit does this. Most basically, habit renders consciousness “less affective” (FN 314), so that the self 

*[p. 70]* ^pg87

gains emotional equilibrium, becoming more durable and less vulnerable to every emotional impulse. Second, habits also involve the “regularity and order” of discipline, as found in family life, school, and work. Discipline provides rhythm, which attenuates the convulsive power of emotion. Third, habit also develops through the “active exercise of the body.” “Gymnastics,” Ricoeur writes, “is related to ethics.” That intriguing claim deserves further consideration.

### Bodily Exercise and Spiritual Formation

How does gymnastics—that is, bodily exercise—have an impact on ethics? According to Ricoeur, emotion is “convulsive.” By contrast, exercise involves measured control of the body. It makes me more attentive and deliberate in my movements. Ricoeur describes this as a kind of “muscular introspection,” making me more acquainted with my body and its affective dispositions (FN 314). Gymnastics helps me to recognize my body as my own, and helps establish the self-possession of the *I can*. In this way gymnastics is—or at least can be—ethically formative. The ancients understood this. For instance, Diogenes the Cynic argued that we need two types of *askēsis* in order to live virtuously: exercise of the soul to form the right kind of representations, and exercise of the body for good health and strength. They are both essential. They are also interconnected. According to Diogenes, gymnastic training can help us to arrive at virtue. The same applies to “manual crafts and other arts”: the only way to develop these skills is through “strenuous practice” and “incessant toil.” These techniques require effort, and the self becomes accustomed to exerting effort in a disciplined way. This sort of training can prepare the way for ethics, as these habits can be transferred to the training of the soul.<sup>13</sup>

We find a similar point in Plato’s *Republic*. Socrates is discussing the best pedagogy for the guardians, and argues that it will require both music and gymnastic. At first it seems that music (which includes poetry and speeches) will form the soul and gymnastic will form the body (376e). As the conversation unfolds, however, Socrates argues that gymnastic also plays an important role in the formation of the soul. Music, poetry, and good speech form the right habits and affections in the soul, giving it good harmony, grace, rhythm, and disposition (400e). Music alone, however, leaves the soul soft, temperamental, and irritable. The problem is that the spirited element of the soul—the *thumos*—has not been properly formed. Without gymnastic, *thumos* starts to melt, and is eventually liquefied (411b).

The right formation of *thumos* is central to this Platonic pedagogy because *thumos* is the part of the soul that lies between reason and sensuous desire. Or as Ricoeur argues in *Fallible Man*, *thumos* is “the median function par 

*[p. 71]* ^pg88

excellence in the human soul” (FM 81). It is the intermediary between the vital and the spiritual (FM 126), the transition from *bios* to *logos* (FM 81–82). But it is not only an intermediary; it is a more ambiguous “mixture” of the vital and the spiritual (FM 127). As such, it is attracted to both, and can ally itself with either (FM 129). If *thumos* sides with reason, it takes the form of courage, righteous indignation, or endurance; if it sides with desire, it takes the form of irritation or fury (FM 8). Given this ambiguity, it is important that *thumos* is formed in the best way possible.

Consequently, in the *Republic* the goal of gymnastic is not merely to increase physical strength or force. People who pursue strength to the neglect of music end up with savage, brutal souls, without rhythm or grace. Instead, the goal is to arouse the *thumos* so that it becomes an ally of reason rather than the appetites and desires (410b-d). The result is the virtue of courage rather than the vice of rage. For Plato, gymnastic is a spiritual exercise insofar as it both arouses and directs *thumos*, the spirited element of the soul. The soul thereby gains the resilience and vigor it needs in order to exhibit the virtues of courage, moderation, and justice.

Plato’s point here intersects with Ricoeur’s discussion of effort. One reason exercise is formative is that it requires effort. Effort is an exertion against resistance, whether that resistance is in the world, in one’s body, or in one’s own spirit. By exerting effort against resistance, the will becomes stronger. Likewise, *thumos* is nourished and brought over to the side of reason. I do not get frustrated or angry, lashing out at whatever resists me; instead, I develop persistence, the spirit to struggle against opposition. It is entirely appropriate that Ricoeur translates *thumos* with the word *heart*. Gymnastic helps to form the heart. And as Diogenes points out, the results of this kind of gymnastic training can be transferred to ethical, spiritual formation.

### The Active Cultivation of Receptivity

With all of that said, we also need to note Ricoeur’s caution regarding the limits of ethical and religious habituation. He makes this point right at the beginning of *Freedom and Nature*, cautioning against drawing any “premature ethical conclusions” from its eidetic analysis. Ethical or religious wholeness—the reconciliation of body and soul, the harmonization of that which passion distorts—requires “a long detour.” In fact, it lies beyond our grasp (FN 22). New disciplines and new habits are in themselves unable to transform the self. The Greeks gave us an inspiring vision of the harmonious, virtuous soul, but they did not have a deep enough grasp of sin, or *fault*. They also did not have as high a sense of Transcendence, which alone can liberate the will. 

*[p. 72]* ^pg89

Ricoeur’s work in this area focuses on the poetic power of ethical and religious discourse to transform—or *refigure*—the self and its world. We might ask, then, how *askēsis* and habituation fit into Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion. Can refiguration become a habit? Strictly speaking, the answer is no. Refiguration depends on the transformative power of a word given prior to my own activity. The self is liberated and its world is refigured by a word it cannot initially speak to itself. But if refiguration cannot become a habit, we can still become more receptive to refiguration. Ricoeur also provides a cue for this in his discussion of consent in *Freedom and Nature*, where he describes the self being transformed in receptivity and patience (FN 479). This transformation is not only a change of our intellectual posture but also that of our bodily posture, such that certain bodily habits can make us more *receptive* to refiguration. These are active steps of ipseity making itself more receptive to alterity. Ricoeur also provides a cue for this in *Fallible Man*:

The body is a node of powers, of motor and affective structures, of interchangeable methods whose spontaneity is at the disposal of the will. It is enough to watch our familiar gestures in action to see how the body leads the way, tries out and invents, answers our expectations or eludes us.

He continues: 

The practical mediation of the body extends beyond *motor* habits in the strict sense. Our skills are also a kind of body, a psychical body, as it were: through rules of grammar and arithmetic, through social sophistication and moral knowledge we learn and form new skills. In all ways of learning, bodily and intellectual, there is this relation of an action to an enacted body, of a will to a power set in motion. (FM 56)

Intellectual skills become what Ricoeur calls an “enacted body.” This would include our hermeneutical skills, habits, or virtues: They become an enacted body, which is more receptive to new possibilities of meaning. These hermeneutical virtues would include habits of attention, of contemplation, patience, responsiveness, courage, and endurance.

These habits belong to the “enacted body” of the self. But what about the exercise of the physical body? If that seems like a bit of a stretch, consider the role of bodily comportment in spiritual formation. Liturgy is an embodied practice, and there are both Eastern and Western examples to illustrate this point: Certain postures, gestures, and movements are crucial for making us more attentive and receptive. When we kneel, when we bow our heads, when we raise our hands, we are adopting bodily modes of heightened receptivity. The same is true for the voice, which is an enacted body of its own. Certain acts of speech, singing, and chanting are also embodied practices that make 

*[p. 73]* ^pg90

us more available to Transcendence. In these ways, bodily exercise does indeed have significance for hermeneutics. It helps prepare the way for the refiguration of the self and the world.

### Reading as Embodied Practice

This brings us full circle, back to Ricoeur’s focus on texts as the means of transformation of the self. Ricoeur’s early phenomenology of the body can illuminate the role of embodied habits in the transformative practice of reading. Reading can be a spiritual exercise, but it engages the body in doing so. The text reveals a world I might inhabit—a world charged with meaning and possibility. These possibilities are possibilities for me *as body*, and it is because I am an embodied self—both agent and patient, with all my particular desires and aversions rooted in my flesh—that these possibilities matter to me. Moreover, my response to the text is also embodied, whether in surges of interest, arousal, distress, or flagging attention. 

In the ancient world there was a much clearer recognition that reading is an embodied practice. As Ivan Illich points out: “Throughout antiquity reading was considered a strenuous exercise. Hellenistic physicians prescribed reading as an alternative to ball playing or a walk. Reading presupposed that you be in good physical form; the frail or infirm were not supposed to read with their own tongue.”<sup>14</sup> The reason is that reading was done for the ears of others. Reading was not something one did alone, silently. People read aloud, and that is a practice that “engages the whole body.”<sup>15</sup> Throughout the first millennium, the practice of reading—and often *singing*—the text was understood as a thoroughly bodily affair. Memorization of the text was facilitated by “psychomotor nerve impulses,” in which the text is enacted through one’s bodily posture and motion. Illich notes:

Even today, pupils in Koranic and Jewish schools sit on the floor with the book open on their knees. Each one chants his lines in a singsong, often a dozen pupils simultaneously, each a different line. While they read, their bodies sway from the hips up or their trunks rock gently back and forth. The swinging and the recitation continue as if the student is in a trance, even when he closes his eyes or looks down the aisle of the mosque. The body movements re-evoke those of the speech organs that have been associated with them. In a ritual manner these students use their whole bodies to embody the lines.<sup>16</sup>

The pedagogical function of these practices is one of “fixing the spoken sequence in the flesh,” since memory often “means the triggering of a well-established sequence of muscular patterns to which the utterances are tied.”<sup>17</sup> 

*[p. 74]* ^pg91

This link between memory and patterns of movement can also arise in relation to music, as dance so nicely illustrates.

The bodily aspect of reading is easier to identify when we are reading aloud. The body is engaged more fully because oral reading involves posture, breathing, projection, volume, tone of voice, rhythm, pacing. All of these bodily aspects are significant for how I enact the text. The bodily aspect of *silent* reading, by contrast, is more obscure, since our speech and thought seem to move inward. Yet, as Drew Leder notes in his book *The Absent Body,* even these seemingly disembodied, “inner” acts of language and thought are accompanied by minute movements of the vocal apparatus.<sup>18</sup> We also tend to forget that our eyes are active—at least until they get too tired or dry and become uncomfortable. And what do our eyes see? Language embodied in markings on the page, or on the screen. This, too, is easy to overlook, since the written word typically becomes transparent, as we see through the physical markings to their intended meaning. 

The embodied aspects of silent reading are also evident in our physical engagement with the text: my relation to the object, whether in bound paper or on a screen; my bodily posture; the way I inscribe myself into the text and its margins by underlining and adding marginalia. Each of these points indicates the importance of the body to an aesthetics of reading. But the body is also relevant to an *ethics* of reading, insofar as the bodily dimension of reading is bound up in the ethical transformation of the self. The practice of reading transforms our bodily comportment in and to the world, and transforms the embodied *I can*.

As I read, what Ricoeur calls “the world in front of the text” begins to take shape. The text presents a world of possible meaning and action—a world I might inhabit. As we have seen, Ricoeur argues that the body mediates between the self and the world. This is true for the *actual* world around us, but it is also true for the *possible* world revealed in front of the text. As I begin to read, I encounter possibilities that present themselves for me: projects, courses of action, capabilities. These are possibilities for me as body. But the body needs to be made capable of recognizing these possibilities. At the beginning, Ricoeur writes, “corporeal existence is a principle of disorder and of indetermination.” I do not—I *cannot*—discern a determined project or self. My reasons are obscured, and I am “submerged in the essential passivity of existence which proceeds from the body.” Consequently, my projects are “confused” and my self is “unformed” (FN 143). I cannot grasp the totality of needs, desires, and ideas that are circulating in me at the present moment, nor is there an evident hierarchy that would allow me to prioritize them as good (FN 143). I cannot perform a coherent act of willing, because I do not really know what I want, what I need, or what I should do. My motives and values are inarticulate.

*[p. 75]* ^pg92

Reading is one of the ways by which we articulate motive and value. The most basic source of motive is the body (FN 85), which is the affective medium of all motives. These motives “issuing from the body” are given “common form” and “conventional value judgments” by the imagination. Imagination helps to give flesh, even a certain fullness, to motives like desire and fear (FN 101). These images are like an “advance emissary” for presence. Imagination is an “affective anticipation”—the anticipation of some desired pleasure, some feared pain (FN 107). And in anticipating some affect, imagination also anticipates some value, whether good or bad. This valuation is largely implicit rather than an explicit or abstract judgment, but the image nevertheless gives a “virtual knowledge of value” (FN 103–4).

This is how reading engages us as bodies. Reading gives a vast array of possibilities to the imagination, allowing us to anticipate things that we need, want, fear, and dislike. By presenting these possibilities in the mode of the *as if*, I undergo this affective experience in my imagination. This experience affects me not just in my thoughts, but in my body as well. The text can motivate, inspire, provoke, arouse, lighten, and sadden. I feel these things in a quickened pulse, a surge of indignation, and in the chills that pass through my body at something frightening. And by allowing me to undergo these affective images, the text organizes and focuses my motives. My body is no longer a mess of confused motives; instead, my motives become more familiar, more intelligible. Through the imagination, reading gives greater articulacy to my motives. I understand them better. This alone is ethically beneficial. 

Beyond this, the imagination also prepares me to form more explicit value judgments about my motives. I can better understand what is to be desired, what temptations I must resist, what is to be feared, what I am willing to undergo in service to my commitments, and how I might respond to the consequences of a course of action. Hence Ricoeur’s claim that fiction can be an ethical laboratory.

By helping us to focus our motives, reading thereby helps precipitate decision. Imagination anticipates these affective possibilities, so that when I decide, I recognize that *I can* carry out this project proposed to my body: “I feel capable, as an incarnate being situated in the world, of the action which I intend in general” (FN 203).

In addition to initiating the action, I must also be capable of maintaining control of my body. Consequently, the body must be tamed, domesticated, and directed toward its intended goal. The body is only able to motivate the will if the will has control of the body (FN 204). This bodily control requires the capacity to initiate and move the body, to stop moving and keep the body still. The body is subject to wild, undisciplined and uncoordinated movements. One of the ethical benefits of gymnastic is that it increases control over the body and its movements, rendering them more smooth and graceful and 

*[p. 76]* ^pg93

making me better acquainted with my body, so that I am less susceptible to emotional upheaval. Similarly, one of the benefits of reading is in the formation of bodily habits that bring the body under the control of the will. It takes discipline to sit still and pay attention, so that I overcome the restlessness and discomfort that can arise if I am not used to reading. Reading is a kind of immobile action, with its own feeling of capability. “Even the most immobile project . . . presents the world to me as horizon, as theater, and as matter of my actions” (FN 212). But in order for the world in front of the text to take shape, I must pay attention. This world depends on my attention, and this is rooted in corporeal habits. It takes physical discipline to read—to sit still, stay awake, stay focused. As Ricoeur writes, there is also a “muscular component” in attending to an idea. Attention too requires a certain muscular effort. “Even the most abstract thought is also physical” (FN 237, 255).

As we saw above, emotion acquires the destructive, binding power of *passion* through our thoughts, evaluations, and “bad imaginations.” What we need, according to Ricoeur, is a “revolution in the imagination” in which new possibilities are given and disordered emotions can be reordered. This requires the mediating role of habit, which reorders the self at the level of soul as well as body. This is true for gymnastic, and it is true for reading. Reading requires a whole ensemble of bodily habits that make me better acquainted not only with my thoughts but also with my body and its affective dimension. 

In sum, reading serves as an exercise, or *askēsis*, in affectivity. Reading allows the imagination to organize and focus my desire, fear, hope, and indignation by allowing me to undergo them in the mode of the *as if*. This gain in articulacy aids in the transition to motive, and increases my capacity for discernment and evaluation of my motives. The imagination then charges up my capacity to decide and stirs the conatus, so that I feel myself as capable of acting in the world. And the body is engaged at each step of the way, as I move from text to action.

The goal of this chapter has been to get a clear sense of the poetic transformation of the self, the embodied *I can*. We have seen how poetic discourse addresses the imagination and reveals new possibilities of being, a world I might inhabit. This address reaches down into the active core of the self—the conatus as desire to be and effort to exist, and this desire is also vital to the formation of the self. Finally, we saw that this formation is not a strictly intellectual affair, but involves the full embodied scope of the self as an embodied *I can*. This poetics becomes the basis for Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion. Indeed, his hermeneutics of religion carries on the project that was originally intended for the unfinished and unpublished *Poetics of the Will*. This hermeneutics of religion is the topic of our next chapter.
