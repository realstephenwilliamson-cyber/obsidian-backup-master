### 4.1–34: Parables and the kingdom of God

*[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-1|Text]], p. 78* · (Mt 13; [[Luke 08#^8-4|Lk 8.4–18]]; [[Luke 13#^13-18|13.18–19]]). The parables chapter is the central teaching section of Mark’s Gospel (see “Midrash and Parables,” p. 707). ^4-1

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-1|1]]:** For the later rabbinic idea of prophecy happening near water, see Mekilta de Rabbi Ishmael, *Pisha* 1.42–76. ^4-1-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-10|10–12]]:** *Secret of the kingdom of God*, communicated through parables that are explained to the insiders. Thus Mark suggests that these parables, without an explanation, are incomprehensible—to be understood they require a key. Jesus speaks in parables *in order that* those outside may not understand and seek forgiveness, in fulfillment of the difficult [[Isaiah 06#^6-9|Isa 6.9–10]], and then provides a key for those present. (This hard line is softened in [[Matthew 13#^13-13|Mt 13.13]]; see Mk 3.5n.) Ambiguous is whether the kingdom is present or lies in the future—or both. ^4-10

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-26|26–29]]:** This parable is similar to the previous parable of the sower, but simpler in that there is only one result of *seed* being scattered, and the point seems to be the surprise at the growth. ^4-26

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-27|27]]:** *He does not know how,* many parables involve miraculous appearance and inexplicable growth as a metaphor for the kingdom. ^4-27

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-31|31–32]]:** Great trees, typically cedars, were symbols of empires ([[Ezekiel 17#^17-22|Ezek 17.22–23]]; [[Daniel 04#^4-17|Dan 4.20–22]]), but *mustard* plants grow only a few feet high. Like many parables, this one is humorously satirical: the kingdom is like a scrubby, invasive bush! The parable suggests that the kingdom arises from an inconspicuous beginning but grows miraculously. ^4-31

### 4.35–41: Stilling of the storm

*[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-35|Text]], p. 79* · ([[Matthew 08#^8-23|Mt 8.23–27]]; [[Luke 08#^8-22|Lk 8.22–25]]). Like Jonah, Jesus sleeps in the midst of a storm; unlike Jonah (Jon 1.4–6), Jesus calms the storm. ^4-35

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-35|35]]:** *Across to the other side*, Jesus leaves a predominantly Jewish section of Galilee for the first time to go to the eastern, predominantly Gentile coast. ^4-35-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-38|38–40]]:** Mark here and elsewhere depicts the disciples as uncomprehending, weak-willed, or cowardly (4.13,38,40; 5.31; 6.52; 7.18; 8.17). Mark may be indicating that they do not deserve the authority they held in the early communities of Jesus’ followers, but the audience may also be able to identify with the disciples’ weakness and so be encouraged to be faithful followers. This passage evokes the recurrent motif in the wilderness accounts in Exodus and Numbers of the people’s mur-murings. The boat is likely intended as a metaphor for the tiny, buffeted community; this is how the boat was interpreted in later Christian art. ^4-38

**[[NRSVue Mark 04#^4-39|39]]:** Jesus *rebuked the wind* and *sea*, which follows the ancient near eastern and Israelite image of the god who conquers the sea (e.g., [[Psalms 065#^65-8|Ps 65.7]]; [[Psalms 089#^89-10|89.9]]; 107.29). ^4-39
