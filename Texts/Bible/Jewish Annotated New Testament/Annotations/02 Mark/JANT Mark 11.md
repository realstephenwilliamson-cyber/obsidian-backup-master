### 11.1–11: Entry into Jerusalem

*[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-1|Text]], p. 93* · ([[Matthew 21#^21-1|Mt 21.1–9]]; [[Luke 19#^19-28|Lk 19.28–38]]; [[John 12#^12-12|Jn 12.12–19]]). ^11-1-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-1|1]]:** *Mount of Olives*, or Olivet, the site of the coming eschatological battle ([[Zechariah 14#^14-4|Zech 14.4]]). A contemporary messianic figure, the so-called Egyptian prophet, also announced that God would appear on the Mount of Olives (Josephus, *Ant.* 20.168–72). ^11-1-3

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-2|2]]:** The apocalyptic vision of [[Zechariah 09#^9-9|Zech 9.9]] prophesies the need for a colt, an image in Abraham’s blessing of Judah ([[Torah 12 - Vayechi#^49-11|Gen 49.11]]); spreading garments (v 8) was associated with the coronation of a king in Israel ([[II Kings 09#^9-13|2 Kings 9.13]]). ^11-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-3|3]]:** *The Lord*, could conceivably refer to any honored person, but from the point of view of the audience, the title identifies Jesus as *Lord.* ^11-3

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-8|8]]:** *Leafy branches*, see [[LXX 1 Maccabees 13#^13-51|1 Macc 13.51]]. ^11-8

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-9|9]]:** *Hosanna*, “save, we pray!” in Aramaic. The quotation is from [[Psalms 118#^118-25|Ps 118.25–26]], incorporated into the Hallel, sung at the pilgrimage festivals of Passover, Shavuot (Weeks), and Sukkot (Booths) and other festive occasions. ^11-9

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-10|10]]:** Not a quotation, but an addition to make the connection with the *coming* Davidic *kingdom*. ^11-10

### 11.12–25: Cursing of the fig tree and the prophetic judgment against the Temple

*[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-12|Text]], p. 93* · ([[Matthew 21#^21-12|Mt 21.12–13]],[[Matthew 21#^21-18|18–22]]; [[Luke 19#^19-45|Lk 19.45–48]]). ^11-12

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-15|15–18]]:** John’s Gospel places the Temple action very early (2.13–22), but Mark, followed by Matthew and Luke, depicts it at the beginning of the Passion narrative. For Mark, the Temple scene provokes Jesus’ execution by the Roman authorities. The Temple compound was the largest in the ancient world in terms of area. In the outer court (sometimes called the Court of the Gentiles) moneychangers and animal merchants conducted the business necessary for pilgrims to provide sacrifices. ^11-15

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-14|14]]:** The text does not give the reason for this curse. Destruction of a fig tree is an image of God’s judgment ([[Isaiah 34#^34-4|Isa 34.4]]; [[Jeremiah 05#^5-17|Jer 5.17]]). ^11-14

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-15|15–17]]:** Why Jesus objects is not clear. Suggestions include: (1) He finds a violation of the sanctity of the Temple (cf. *m. Ber.* 9.5, not relating to money but to general behavior in the Temple precincts); (2) *for all nations* means an end to the exclusion of Gentiles; or (3) he condemns the purported economic injustice of the Roman-appointed Temple establishment in requiring money to be changed at the Temple. But the text mentions none of these motivations. The fall of the first Temple as a result of sin is mourned in some Prophetic texts, e.g., [[Isaiah 64#^64-10|Isa 64.11]]. ^11-15-2

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-17|17]]:** Mark combines [[Isaiah 56#^56-7|Isa 56.7]] and [[Jeremiah 07#^7-11|Jer 7.11]], while John’s Temple scene (2.16–17) quotes [[Psalms 069#^69-10|Ps 69.9]] and alludes to [[Zechariah 14#^14-2|Zech 14.2]]. Mark’s quotations are often interpreted as concerning the broad inclusion of Gentiles in the new age that is commencing, but the Isaiah quotation concerns the acceptance of both eunuchs and foreigners; the former are included and the latter observe God’s laws without becoming Jews. The allusion to [[Jeremiah 07#^7-11|Jer 7.11]], *den of robbers*, may suggest that Mark is opposed to abuses by Temple authorities or the selling of sacrificial animals in the outer court of the Temple, as v 16 indicates. The context of [[Jeremiah 07#^7-1|Jer 7.1–15]] is a condemnation of abuses by the people and the false sense of security that they may take in the Temple; it is not clear what particular abuses Mark has in mind. ^11-17

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-20|20–25]]:** In Mark’s treatment, the fig tree is now associated with a prayer that the Temple mount, the place of orderly worship and acknowledgment of God’s rule, be *taken up and thrown into the sea*, the place of primal chaos that God subdues in creation ([[Isaiah 27#^27-1|Isa 27.1]]; [[Psalms 089#^89-10|Ps 89.9]]; [[Job 26#^26-12|Job 26.12]]). Continuing the fig symbolism from the prophets ([[Hosea 09#^9-10|Hos 9.10]]; [[Micah 7#^7-1|Mic 7.1]]; cf. [[Luke 13#^13-6|Lk 13.6–9]]), Mark speaks of the coming destruction of the Temple and its leaders. ^11-20

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-25|25]]:** *Forgive . . . your Father in heaven may also forgive*, Mark does not include the “Our Father” prayer ([[Matthew 06#^6-9|Mt 6.9–13]]; [[Luke 11#^11-2|Lk 11.2–4]]), but this language may indicate knowledge of it. *Father*, see 14.36n. ^11-25

### 11.27–33: Question on authority

*[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-27|Text]], p. 94* · ([[Matthew 21#^21-23|Mt 21.23–27]]; [[Luke 20#^20-1|Lk 20.1–8]]). ^11-27

**[[NRSVue Mark 11#^11-29|29–30]]:** The leaders try to force Jesus to make a dangerous statement, but he turns their challenge back against them. *do not forgive, neither will your Father in heaven forgive your trespasses.”* ^11-29
