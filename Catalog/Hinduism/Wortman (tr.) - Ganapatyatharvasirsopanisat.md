## About

A short, late Sanskrit Upaniṣad in praise of Gaṇeśa, also called the Gaṇapati Upaniṣad. It identifies Gaṇeśa with brahman, the self (ātman) and the syllable Om, and explains his seed mantra *gaṃ*.

- **Attribution:** it is attached to the Atharvaveda, and its text includes the phrase "thus says Atharvaṇa". Chinmayananda, quoted by Wikipedia, says the attribution may or may not be so.
- **Date:** Courtright (1985) and Thapan (1997) date it to the 16th or 17th century; Ghurye calls it very late. It is no. 89 in the Muktikā list of 108 Upaniṣads, which Deussen dates to the mid-17th century. Wikipedia notes that scholars take its mention of the mūlādhāra cakra as a sign of late origin.
- **Structure:** 14 verses framed by opening and closing peace chants; verse 8 contains a Gaṇeśa Gāyatrī. The sanskritdocuments.org text (encoded by Sunder Hattangadi and checked by Kirk Wortman, updated 2004) also has 14 numbered sections, of which 11–14 describe the benefits of reciting it. Chinmayananda's edition numbers the verses differently.
- **Teaching:** Gaṇeśa is the supreme reality, one with Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Śiva and the other gods, beyond the three guṇas, and the source and dissolution of the universe.
- **Translations:** Vans Kennedy (1831, heavily abridged); Gudrun Bühnemann (critical edition, 1984); Paul Courtright (1985); Chinmayananda (1987); John Grimes (1995).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia, following Grimes, calls it the most important surviving Sanskrit text of the Gāṇapatya tradition, and notes that its full text is inscribed over the entrance to the hall of the Ashtavinayak temple at Ranjangaon. Britannica, which does not mention the text, says the Gāṇapatya sect regarded Gaṇeśa as the supreme deity and was at its height about the 10th century.
