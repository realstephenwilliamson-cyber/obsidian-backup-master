## Half-title

JESUS AND ISRAEL’S TRADITIONS OF

JUDGEMENT AND RESTORATION

*Jesus and Israel’s Traditions of Judgement and Restoration* examines the eschatology of Jesus by evaluating his appropriation of sacred traditions related to Israel’s restoration. It addresses the way in which Jesus’ future expectations impinged upon his understanding of key features of Jewish society.

Scholars have long debated the degree to which Jesus’ eschatology can be said to have been realized. This book breaks new ground by considering Jesus’ expectations regarding key constitutional features of the eschaton: the shape of the people of God, purity, Land and Temple. Steven M. Bryan shows that Jesus’ anticipation of coming national judgement led him to use Israel’s sacred traditions in ways that differed significantly from their use by his contemporaries. This did not lead Jesus to the conviction that Israel’s restoration had been delayed. Instead he employed Israel’s traditions to support a different understanding of restoration and a belief that the time of restoration had arrived.

STEVEN M . BRYAN is Dean of Studies and Instructor of New Testament at the Ethiopian Graduate School of Theology. He has been involved in theological education in Ethiopia since 1992.

## ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

This study began as a PhD thesis submitted to the Faculty of Divinity at Cambridge University in August 1999. I came to the study as a follower of Jesus. I remain so at its end, but with immeasurably increased sympathy for Jesus’ first followers, for whom he held a peculiar fascination even as much about him and his message remained a puzzle. Despite nearly three years of concentrated study, I am more aware than ever of how much about Jesus I do not understand. Still the fascination with him is with me all the more.

For cultivating that fascination, I am grateful to Dr Andrew Chester and Dr James Carleton Paget who supervised this work and whose thinking about Jesus so often challenged and stimulated my own. With unstinting encouragement, they have managed somehow to serve as guides on a path they let me find. I am especially indebted to Dr Carleton Paget for the enormous amount of time and thought he invested in this work during the final year of my studies in the midst of his own great hardship.

I wish to thank Professor Graham Stanton and Dr Ron Piper who served as gracious examiners of the work in its initial form and made a number of helpful suggestions for the present revision. I am also grateful to Professor Richard Bauckham not only for accepting this study as part of the SNTS monograph series but also for challenging my thinking at a number of points in a way that led to many improvements.

For the financial assistance offered by the Patteson Fund of Selwyn College and the yearly scholarships provided by both the Ethiopia and US offices of the Society of International Ministries, I am deeply thankful. A number of individuals and several churches supported us financially while we were serving as missionaries in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia from 1992 to 1996. They continued to do so during our three years in Cambridge in the hope that this time of study would bear fruit in further ministry in Ethiopia. We express our gratitude to them by reaffirming that this remains our hope as well.
