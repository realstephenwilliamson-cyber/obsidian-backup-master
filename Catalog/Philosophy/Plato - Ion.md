## About

One of Plato's shortest dialogues. Socrates questions Ion, a reciter of Homer, and concludes that his gift comes from divine inspiration, not from skill or knowledge.

- **People and setting:** Socrates and Ion of Ephesus, a rhapsode. Ion has just won first prize at the festival of Asclepius at Epidaurus, and Socrates hopes he will do the same at the Panathenaea in Athens.
- **Dramatic date:** Franco Trivigno (*Plato's Ion*, Cambridge) reports that scholars generally agree on a date before 412 BC.
- **Composition:** the IEP places it in Plato's early group (c. 399–387 BC); Jowett put it among the earlier dialogues. Trivigno notes that an early date is usually assumed, but that some, including Rijksbaron (2007), place it later.
- **Authenticity:** Goethe and Schleiermacher first doubted it; Trivigno adds Ast, Zeller and Wilamowitz. The present consensus accepts it as Plato's, with Thesleff (2009) a notable exception; stylometry has been inconclusive. Jowett noted that no early external testimony supports it.
- **Argument (Stephanus 530a–542b):** Ion claims special expertise in Homer, but Homer speaks of generalship, medicine and chariot-making, which Ion does not know, so his talent cannot be an art. Socrates proposes that inspiration passes from the Muse to the poet, the rhapsode and the audience like magnetism through a chain of rings (535e–536b). Offered the label "divine," Ion accepts it as the lovelier choice.
- **Place among the dialogues:** Wikipedia says Plato's critique of poetry appears more fully in the *Phaedrus*, *Symposium*, *Republic* and *Laws*. The SEP notes that *Phaedrus* 245a echoes its account of inspiration and that *Republic* X names Ion among Homer's praisers.
- **Why it matters:** the SEP reads the audience's tears and laughter (535b–d) as the root of Plato's claim that poetry shapes the soul. Gregory Vlastos read it as a critique of unjustified belief rather than of poetry as such.
