## Chapter 7: Narrative folklore of Khyāḥ from Tantra to popular beliefs: Supernatural experiences at the margins among Newar communities in the Kathmandu Valley

*Fabio Armand*

### 7.1 Introduction

Newar communities of Nepal have been widely studied, and a rich ethnographic literature is now available.<sup>1</sup> In this chapter, I will explore the folklore of the *Khyāḥ* or *Khyāk*, a type of supernatural being that haunts the narrative imaginaries of the Newar *ādivāsi* (Nepali, ‘indigenous’) group of the Kathmandu Valley. Through this exploration, I will present the specific ritual context of the Newars, which is characterized by the co-existence of two religious traditions—Hinduism and Buddhism, intertwined into a Tantric perspective—related to a twin-headed caste system organization and integrated into a complex socio-ritualistic cosmology. At the top of the system, the Newar Hindu Brahmans are *opposed* to a high Buddhist priest caste, comprising two categories of ritual agents—namely, the Bajrāchāryas and the Śākyas. Below them are two other castes pertaining to the noble (*kṣatriya*) category, the Śreṣṭhās and the Urāys, predominantly Hindu and Buddhist, respectively. A fourth group, that of the Jyāpu-farmers, reflects a more fluid territory standing in between what is specifically Hindu or Buddhist (Gellner 1986).

In what follows, I will present two different dimensions of Newar culture through which both etic and emic observers can comprehend the nature of *Khyāḥ*s: the dimension of Tantra, where these beings are direct emanations of the energy produced by the female goddesses known as *Aṣṭamātṛkā*s, and the dimension of popular beliefs, which view these beings as born from the divine union between Nāsaḥdyaḥ, the god of music and dance, and a human girl. Among the many motifs of this rich folklore, I will specifically address an initiation ritual of Newar girls, that of the *bāṁā tayegu* (Newari, ‘to place a barrier’, i.e. a seclusion rite), and the role of a particular kind of *Khyāḥ*. When this ritual goes wrong, a dramatic borderline situation gives us access to a specific belief: the *ātman* (Nepali, soul as inner self, carrying within it the spark of the creative principle, *brahman*, and characterizing each individual as an exclusive being) of a young candidate, who unexpectedly dies during *bāṁā tayegu*, is transformed into a *bāṁā Khyāḥ*, a female evil being that haunts the places where she used to live before passing away. These experiential narratives will provide us with the basis for developing a comprehensive analysis of the figure of the *Khyāḥ*, a supernatural being standing at the 

*[p. 138]* ^pg151

intersection of the three fundamental domains of beliefs, narrative, and ritual practices, and for better grasping the richness of the folklore of Newar communities.

### 7.2 Morphologies of the Khyāḥ from a taxonomic perspective

In order to introduce the figure of the *Khyāḥ*, we have to address a taxonomic issue that affects the classification of supernatural beings in indigenous religions. Indeed, any taxonomic organization concerning such imaginary beings always seems to sink into a kind of vague categorical fog. This is also the case of the *Khyāḥ*, for narratives about these beings undergo several processes of individual variation and changes, which shape the definitional boundaries that allow us to precisely identify their nature. Moreover, the complex question of the role of indigenous traditions in the development of Tantric perspectives in both Hinduism and Buddhism in the Himalayan context arises here. In order to comprehend this multifaceted supernatural being, we argue that it is necessary to consider the interaction, characterized by processes of negotiation and mutual transformation, involving both these more or less institutionalized religions and local indigenous traditions, where shamanic elements have been assimilated into Tantric perspectives. Most scholars<sup>2</sup> agree that, etymologically, the term *Khyāḥ* derives from the Sanskrit *yakṣa*, ‘demon, goblin’ (Malla 2000), but it is not sure if the beliefs about these beings belong to, or originated in, non-Indo-Aryan milieus.<sup>3</sup> According to my main informant, the *tāntrika* (Tantric practitioner) *gurubā* (Nepali, a Nepali honorific title for a spiritual teacher) Umesh Kumar Rajbhandari, who welcomed me several times in his house in Ḍallu (Kathmandu), we could individuate at least three different types of *Khyāḥ*, mainly by distinguishing them by their colour<sup>4</sup>: a white one, *seto Khyāḥ* (Newari, *tuyugu*), a black one, *kālo Khyāḥ* (Newari, *hāku*), and the *jāte Khyāḥ*, whose name is not transparent but usually associated with brownish colours (Newari, *siyu*) as *khairo Khyāḥ*.

While the last type of *Khyāḥ* is not well-known, the *kālo* and *seto Khyāḥ* are by far the most widespread in Newar folklore. The *kālo Khyāḥ* is often portrayed as a negative and frightening being, invisible but capable of causing a deep feeling of fear (Nepali, *jiu phurbu*, ‘to make the whole body throb with fear’); it can also cause pain in the heart (Nepali, *muṭu duknu*) or head (Nepali, *ṭāuko duknu*), causing dizziness (Nepali, *chhakkar lagnu*). The *seto Khyāḥ* represents the most significant figure in this Newar ghostly trinity: it is considered the *Khyāḥ ko rāja*, the king of the *Khyāḥ*s. Intimately linked to the goddess Mahālakṣmī, of whom it is the *pāsāḥ*<sup>5</sup> (Newari, ‘companion’), it receives orders from her and, through this privilege, it controls its black counterpart. Thus, during the *Mahālakṣmī pūjā*, on the third day of the Tihār, the great festival of lights, the group of Chitrakārs, a subcaste of Newar artisans, prepares painted images that depict the *seto Khyāḥ* offering a *pūjā* to the goddess of well-being and wealth.

In the light of the association with previously mentioned female deities, one may suggest a Tantric interpretation of the nature of these two-coloured entities. 

*[p. 139]* ^pg152

*Khyāḥ*s would be a direct emanation of the energy (Nepali, *kālī* <sup>6</sup>) generated by the *Aṣṭa Mātṛkā*s, the eight fundamental feminine principles. Being created in two forms, the *seto Khyāḥ* would represent a kind of reservoir of energy that the creative *Mātṛkā*s could access, activate, and use for their own purposes. The *seto Khyāḥ* would allow the transformation of the energy used for creating its counterpart, the *kālo* form: the latter becomes the protector of the *Mātṛkā*s, and it merely consumes the energy that produced it. In this Tantric framework, one could regard the *Khyāḥ*s as different forms of *śakti*, the dynamic energetic power(s) that can operate in our perceptible reality assuming a coloured manifestation according to the role they take.

Newar popular beliefs have constructed a rather different vision of the nature of this supernatural being. An elder informant from Sāno Khokana (Lalitpur, 1 March 2013) stated that every Newar house, *chhen*, has to possess one of the three main forms of *Khyāḥ* as its inhabitant: *seto*, *kālo*, or the one he calls *kailo*, grayish. Their presence would be fundamental to Newar dwellings, which otherwise could not be stable:<sup>7</sup> belonging to the army of Śiva Mahādeva (Nepali, *Mahādeva ko senā*), *Khyāḥ*s protect the houses and their inhabitants but also rice fields and animals. They would not be like evil *bhut* (Nepali, ‘ghost’), although they are still able to play nasty tricks on humans. In this function, they would rather cover the role of protective and trickster-like spirits, homologues of the familiar sprits of European folklore. My informant related that, in his childhood, he was bringing straw to the goats when suddenly he saw this type of being staring at him: afraid, he ran away, but his father blamed him because one should not be afraid of the *Khyāḥ*. His sister had even touched one once, reporting that it was very soft. On another occasion, a *seto Khyāḥ* was stealing soybeans from the mill, filling his large hat. When it realized that it had been discovered, and finding himself trapped in the room—a *Khyāḥ* cannot disappear of its own will if it does not wear its hat on its head—it was obliged to spill the seeds on the floor so that it could finally escape magically by putting its hat on its head. It is common to hear such stories that give us a more precise portrait of this supernatural being.

#### 7.2.1 Multiple types of Khyāḥ s

During my intensive fieldwork among different Newar communities, I could individuate other typologies of *Khyāḥ* that enrich even further the already complex nature of this supernatural being. First, the *misā Khyāḥ* (Newari, *misā*, ‘woman’) represents a feminine form of the *Khyāḥ*—the only one that I have been able to accurately identify—who is believed to be a kind of queen of *Khyāḥ*s.

The *lã pā Khyāḥ* (Newari, *lã*, ‘road’; *pā*, ‘to block, to guard’), a road-blocking entity, usually appears as a dark shadow that crosses the alleys or the outer courtyards of houses, preventing people who linger in the night from passing. The action of this *Khyāḥ* is often benevolent, intending to protect humans from the harmful entities that lurk in the surroundings. Ramesh Man Shrestha (Jyāṭā, Kathmandu: 5 November 2015) reported that during a *pancha dhān pūjā* (Nepali), an offering of five different types of rice seeds, he encountered a *lã pa Khyāḥ* 

*[p. 140]* ^pg153

blocking the main entrance door while walking home late at night. The *jhyāpe Khyāḥ* is a hairy *Khyāḥ* that could pair with the *gwayadu misa* (Lall 2007: 195), a *Khyāḥ* who wears thick whiskers. A more interesting figure is the *gwārā Khyāḥ*<sup>8</sup> (Newari, ‘round shape, ball’), which appears as a ball of rolled cloth: this round spirit can fall from the top of the ceiling and roll around the room where it appeared, hitting the legs of those present (Nepali, *kuṭṭa ma hirkāunu*) and, finally, disappearing. It can also be seen in the roads.<sup>9</sup>

In Newar folklore, the *Khyāḥ* may also play an important role as a bogeyman. Thus, the *dhāpālam Khyāḥ* is a kind of bogeyman supposed to carry away with him the children who are acting up and misbehaving (Nepali, *jiskāunu*). Our informant, Dipendra Bajracharya (Kuponḍole, Lalitpur: April 2014) told us that parents used to tell children stories about it to frighten them and prevent them from going out of their homes, especially after sunset. The term *dhāpālan* is an onomatopoeia indicating the noise produced by a violent opening of the main door of traditional Newar houses. Newar houses typically have an entrance (Newari, *chenḍi*) that opens directly onto the road and a steep staircase (Newari, *swāna*; Nepali, *bharyāṅ*) leading up to the most important parts of the house; a powerful wooden door closes off access to the first floor, the *māṭan* (Newari). This type of *Khyāḥ*—like the *bāṁā Khyāḥ*, as we will see next—is supposed to live under these stairs.

Another particular kind of *Khyāḥ* is the *kusā Khyāḥ* (Newari, ‘umbrella’), a bird-like spirit that can be heard singing at sunrise, emitting vibrating sounds from the roofs of temples. *Kusā* represents actually an architectural element made of clay used as a gutter on the roofs of many temples. Finally, the *bū khyācha* (Newari, *bū*, ‘field’, *khyācha*, ‘to threaten’) is a scarecrow, sometimes personified as a ghostly creature, which villagers install on the four corners of the fields to protect them from the ravages of wild animals or from thieves.<sup>10</sup>

Today, modernity and the processes of urbanization that closely followed the larger rural exodus of *Parbatiyā*s (Nepali, ‘hill Indo-Nepalese inhabitants’) and Upper Himalayan inhabitants to the metropolitan city of Kathmandu, which thus continues the inexorable expansion of its urban environment, has modified the Newar cultural texture and beliefs. As observed by Damodar Karanjit (Lagan Ṭol, Kathmandu, 25 July 2019), ‘*pahile pahile Khyāḥ bāyo. Aba light āyo ra Khyāḥ chhaina*’ (Nepali, ‘long time ago, *Khyāḥ* was present \[in Newar cities and villages\]. Today, electric light came and *Khyāḥ* is gone’).

### 7.3 Nāsaḥdyaḥ and the genesis of Khyāḥ

Having presented some generalities about the *Khyāḥ* and introduced its different types, I now turn to the myth of its origin, which is intimately related to the figure of Nāsaḥdyaḥ,<sup>11</sup> the dancing form of Śiva regarded by the Newars as the god of music and dance. According to a widespread narrative, Nāsaḥdyaḥ fell in love with a Newar girl named Tara<sup>12</sup> and had sexual intercourse with her, as a result of which she gave birth to a child. Soon the two parents began a harsh dispute about who would keep the child. Then, the woman decided to separate 

*[p. 141]* ^pg154

(Nepali, *chirnu*) the child into two parts: the first having originated the *Khyāḥ*, a being made of only flesh (Nepali, *māsu*) that the woman decided to keep with her; the second, the *Kawancha* or *Kawan* (Sanskrit, *kaṅkāla*, ‘skeleton’), a being made of only bones (Nepali, *nali*), a *bhut-pret* taken by Śiva as a member of his army.<sup>13</sup>

Another version of this narrative is given, in Newari, by Rajendra Shrestha (1999), in an article about music and *dhimay* drums:

Long ago, Nasahdyāḥ took the form of a man and walked to Kabilās Hill. On the way, he fell in love with a peasant woman who was cutting grass. He married her and gave her a son. This child, who was of great beauty, became an object of pride for his mother. She wanted to keep him all to herself. Jealous, Nasahdyāḥ blamed her. They quarreled so violently that they decided to divide the baby in two. With the bones born of his seed, Nasahdyāḥ created an animated skeleton, Kawā. With the flesh born of her blood (*raja*), the mother gave birth to a boneless being, Khyāḥ. Depressed at the sight of such repulsive creatures, Nasahdyāḥ sat on a tree trunk. He began to drum on it. Immediately, the Khyāḥ and the Kawā began to dance. And so the music (and dance) of the *dhimay* came into being.

(My translation from Toffin 1998: 123–124) As the god of arts<sup>14</sup> for the Maharjan-Jyapu caste of Newar, Nāsaḥdyaḥ plays an important role in this etiological myth, which is also relevant for the creation of the *dhimay*, a double-headed drum used by the members of the *dhimay guthi* (Bernède 1997), an organization with multiform functions that incorporate segments of the population and play fundamental roles in the social organization into lineages and castes. Nāsaḥdyaḥ is the central pillar of Maharjan society and its shrines are scattered throughout the geography of Newar cities and villages, always located in a place difficult to access. This formless deity is symbolically represented as a simple hole (Newari *nasaḥ pvaḥ*)—either single or in a set of three<sup>15</sup>—mostly of triangular shape, on the wall of its shrine: the empty space symbolizes the emptiness and the ethereal nature of the music. Usually, on both sides of the hole are often represented, respectively, the *Khyāḥ*, on the left of the deity, and the *Kawancha*, on its right. These two beings are also represented, in the form of paintings or sculptures, next to temples dedicated to powerful deities, such as the *Mātṛkā*s or Bhairava, the terrific form of Śiva. Related to many of these paintings (see Figure 7.1) are two dogs or jackals, manifestations of chaotic tendencies (Wegner 1992: 125), which suggest the relation of *Khyāḥ* and *Kawancha* with these powerful deities. As expressed by Damodar Karanjit, my informant from Lagan Ṭol (Lagan Ṭol, Kathmandu, 25 July 2019), ‘*juṭho chhaina,* *Khyāḥ, Kawancha, bhut-pret āuchhan*’ (Nepali, ‘\[in this place\] there are not impurities, \[so\] *Khyāḥ, Kawancha*, and *bhut-pret* come \[there\]’): these *ātman*s, as he called them, roams around these temples because they like to meet together in clean and pure places. It seems that these figures naturally bear the mark of Tantrism, as well as a particularly strong relationship between Tantrism and

As we have noted in attesting the narrative motif of the Procession of the dead in the Himalayan domain (Armand *et al*. 2016), the *Panchabhāya* (Nepali, ‘five young siblings’), a procession composed of five malevolent female spirits, shows a Tantric substratum that has influenced popular representations. According to Mohan Keimrick,

\[T\]he *Panchabhāya* is a white woman, very beautiful, that wanders in the night-time. She is always preceded by a black or red dog in a fearful attitude and, sometimes, followed by the *murkātta* \[Nepali, a ghost without head riding a black horse, typical of the Nepalese narrative folklore\] that protects her and activates her energy.

(Ḍallu, Kathmandu, December 2013) This narrative enables us to connect the figure of the dog to a Tantric framework, where it serves the purpose of a fearful symbol of protection of energetic deities, thus mirroring the relationship between the *seto Khyāḥ* and the *Mātṛkā*s.

#### 7.3.1 Blood and bones in the constitution of Khyāḥ and Kawancha

As observed by Toffin (1998: 124, n. 18), this myth is interesting as it links the origin of music and dance to a matrimonial disorder and to a separation of the 

*[p. 143]* ^pg156

substances representing the sexual genders. Even more importantly, it gives access to a theory of the body and its substances. Thus, another version of this myth provides the basis for comprehending the nature of these beings:

Once the divine couple Parvati and Shiva had a dispute between themselves on the issue of the right over their child. In the course, they took back the individual share of the substance they had contributed for formation and the birth of the child. While doing so Parvati took back blood, from which she created her own child (without Shiva’s share). Her lone creation turned out to be a boneless man (Khyah). Shiva took back sperm from the child’s body which he created for the child. His creation turned out to become a fleshless man or skeleton body (Kawan). This proved that the human body is constructed by the mixture of woman’s blood and man’s sperm. Blood creates external parts, whereas sperm creates the internal parts.

(Manandhar 2009: 263) This account allows us to address the Newar representation of the human body according to which the bones would be formed by the father’s sperm, while the blood and flesh would derive from female menstrual secretions. This conception does not differ too much from the Bengali *baul* tradition explored by Bhattacharya (2002: 256n7), who confirms that ‘sperm, veins and arteries, bones and brain come from the father, while hairs, skin, blood and flesh are given to the child by its mother’.

Similarly, Toffin (1990) proposes an interesting reflection concerning the opposition between blood and bones in the cultures of Tibeto-Burman language-- speaking Himalayan populations. Bones would be strictly related to patrilineal kinship transmitted through sperm—a fact that supports, among other things, the link with the ancestors. On the contrary, blood—resulting from menstrual blood—would be linked to matrilineal kinship, ephemeral and transitory. The permanence of patrilineal relations<sup>16</sup> results in a societal structure solidly anchored on a ritual order; blood becomes, on the contrary, a symbol of transition and disorder, recalling the putrescible nature of human flesh.

Applied to the myth of origin of our supernatural beings, the relative stability of this representation in the Tibeto-Burman context authorizes us to propose the following scheme:

 i. masculine principle ➔ bones ➔ *Kawancha* ➔ right side<sup>17</sup> ➔ *Preta śakti* ii. feminine principle ➔ blood ➔ *Khyāḥ* ➔ left side ➔ *Devī śakti*

Following the Tantric model analyzed earlier, *Khyāḥ* has inherited the quality of the menstrual principle of the *Devī*, his deified mother, developing a nature not easily ordered according to ritual canons: this would explain his strong connection with deities belonging to disorder, like the powerful group of *Mātṛkā*s or the uncontrollable destructive energy of the terrifying Bhairava. On the other hand, its bony companion, *Kawancha*,<sup>18</sup> was accepted into the group—the army, *senā*—of *preta*s, submitting to the ritual order imposed by its father Śiva Mahādeva.

### 7.4 Bāṁā tayegu : A prepubertal rite among Newar girls

In order to introduce the figure of *bāṁā Khyāḥ*,<sup>19</sup> the last to be treated in my analysis, we need to delve into the ethnography of the initiation rites of Newar girls. The interest in those ceremonies is mainly due to the fact that, during one such rite, the young girls of this ethnic group might meet this particular figure.

Newar girls have to go through two major life-cycle prepubertal rituals: the *ihi* ritual and the *bāṁā*<sup>20</sup> *tayegu* (variants: *barha* and *baḥraḥ*). The first of them, *ihi*, consists of an elaborate ceremony in which a large group of prepubescent girls, belonging to the various Newar castes (apart from those of the Untouchables and Diāḥ Brahmins), are given in marriage to a supernatural bridegroom represented by a *bel* fruit, considered to be Suvarṇakumāra, a form of Viṣṇu or Buddha (Gutschow and Michaels 2008: 93). This ceremony is commonly described as a ‘mock marriage’ (Allen 2000: 211). According to my informant Ramesh Man Shrestha, *ihi* marriage was instituted during the 14th century, when a rapid and violent Muslim invasion hit the Kathmandu Valley:<sup>21</sup>

In Newar communities, child girls are married three times \[the third one is the marriage with a human spouse\]. This because, during the Malla period, Muslim warriors attacked here \[Kathmandu valley\] and used to kidnap and rape virgin girls. In the community, people thought that, if all the girls were already married, Muslim warriors would not rape them. The first marriage is the *ihi* marriage, the marriage with the *bel* fruit: after this marriage, a young girl can have *sindur*,<sup>22</sup> the reddish marriage’s *ṭikā*. If Muslim warriors saw this mark, they would not rape them.

During the second ritual, *bāṁā tayegu*, which is of particular interest to us, the girl joins in marriage for the second time with the sun god, Surdyaḥ. This ceremony is characterized by a ritual seclusion that takes place after the *ihi* marriage and before menarche, the first period of menstruation, and is practiced by both Hindu and Buddhist high castes. *Bāṁā tayegu* literally means ‘to place a barrier’ and, sometimes, Newars use the term *bāṁā chwanegu*<sup>23</sup> (Newari, ‘to stay behind a barrier’) to indicate the same ritual. Allen (1990: 192) considers that, just as *ihi* may be described as a mock marriage, so the *bāṁā tayegu* is a ‘mock-menstruation rite’. This premenstrual rite is most closely related to *guphā basne* (Nepali, ‘sitting in the cave’), a prepubertal ritual realized by the high Hindu Indo-Nepalese castes, Bāhun-Chhetri, which involves 20 or 22 days of seclusion (Bennet 1978): the two are so similar that Kunreuther (1994) considered *bāṁā tayegu* as a modified version of Indo-Nepalese one. In the Newar ritual, a group of girls, aged between 9 and 13, is confined for 12 days within a room: during this confinement, the girls are not allowed to see sunlight nor to meet any male members of their family.<sup>24</sup> At the end of the 12-day confinement, they are led blindfolded to the rooftop or to the courtyard where they are ritually shown to the sun: this ceremony is called *bāṁā pikāyegu* (Newari, ‘taking out from confinement’).

*[p. 145]* ^pg158

Research conducted by Manandhar (2000) is especially useful because, as a Newar woman, she had undertaken the rite and provides us with information collected as an ‘insider’. Hence, on the fourth or the sixth day of confinement, a ceremony called *koṃchikãn sāykegu* is performed: the wife of the oldest male in the family, *thakālinakī*, makes an effigy of cotton called *bāṁā khyāḥ*, which is placed in a niche (*gwakhãnpwā*), or at the ceiling in the confined room. She offers to the effigy—and, later, to the girls—one bowl of homemade face washing powder (Newari, *koṃ*), another one full of mustard oil (Newari, *chikãn*), a comb (Newari, *kakīchā*), and other cosmetic objects (*ibid*.: 224– 225). The girls have to take care of the effigy and offer to it a part of the food they eat during the confinement. The effigy of the *bāṁā khyāḥ* has the form of a doll made of white cloth so that the girls can perceive it in the darkness of the room.

If the *bāṁā Khyāḥ* is not satisfied with the regular offerings (Newari, *khyāḥbvaḥ*) given to it during the period of seclusion, it may decide to appear from the sixth day. However, as noted by Gutschow and Michaels (2008: 175), the girls may decide to evoke it even from the second or third day:

One of the visiting elder girls switches off the light and the girls will listen in the dark for any unusual noises to ascertain the presence of the ghost. The scene ends in them teasing and tickling each other. Once the light is on again, one of the girls will admit to having see the white shadow of the ghost and any disorder in the room will be attributed to it.

Thus, our informant, Ramesh Man Shrestha, told us that the shapes drawn by the light smoke of the *dhup* (Nepali, ‘incense’) in the dark can give the illusion to the girls that there is a presence moving in the darkness. According to a group of women we interviewed in Kirtipur (1 July 2018),

During the *bāṁā tayegu*, one can meet the *bāṁā Khyāḥ* \[they laugh\]. To pass the time, the girls play different games, they tell stories and act out little plays, flattering the doll. One of these games is called *Khyāḥ mhitegu*, the game of the *Khyāḥ*. The girls use the powder of a *kolaḥ* seed \[Newari, ‘the residue of the mustard seed after pressing’\] which has a whitish color: we use it to put makeup on our faces. \[…\] We also offered to the *Khyāḥ* a plate with grain, *kwatā*, and it comes to eat during the night. \[One of the women adds:\] During the *bāṁā* \[*tayegu*\], I did not see the *Khyāḥ* but we were very afraid: we always saw a shadow in the room. All the girls have this feeling during the *bāṁāḥ*: one of my sisters is quite sure that she saw the *Khyāḥ*. During my *bāṁāḥ*, with my friends, we had fled on the bed to stay all together as close as possible: suddenly, the bed collapsed because of the weight of us, eight frightened kids \[she laugh\].

Poonam Singh (Jyāṭā, Kathmandu: 25 July 2019) reports that during this period of seclusion, the *Khyāḥ* comes to play (Nepali, *khelnu*) with girls: if it comes to 

*[p. 146]* ^pg159

play with them, they will be lucky, as this is a very positive sign. However, if the *bāṁā Khyāḥ* is not satisfied, it may take out his claws and come up, in the night, to lie on the chests of the sleeping girls (Allen 2000: 231). Hence, it will appear in its function of incubus (Cathiard and Armand 2014: 71).

#### 7.4.1 Bāṁā Khyāḥ as a deflowering agent

An important aspect regarding the *bāṁā Khyāḥ* concerns the difficulty of accurately identifying its sexual identity. According to Pradhan (1986), the *bāṁā Khyāḥ* is a feminine effigy, but Manandhar (2000: 225) observed, during her seclusion, that it is rather ‘a male figure or a couple in a pose with sexual overtone’. This supernatural being has gone through a process of sexual mutation: from a masculine original nature, the tradition would have operated a sort of transformation into a feminine being,<sup>25</sup> fearing that it might actually have sexual intercourses with young girls who, at that time, are still virgins (Allen 2000: 231).

In this context, Allen (*ibid*.: 233) interprets the *Khyāḥ* as a deflowering agent, symbolizing the beginning of sexuality. As observed by Manandhar (2000: 225), the elderly women tease the confined girls, asking them if the *bāṁā Khyāḥ* comes to sleep with them and if it loves them (Newari, *barha khya napan dyan mawala?* *Wan mayaya mawala?*). This seclusion rite might take the form of a symbolical transition that transforms the girl ‘from a pre-sexual to a sexual being’ (Bennet 1978: 33). This idea is conveyed by the fact that the prepubertal girl enters into the ‘cave’, a symbolical representation of the womb, and after the 12-day seclusion, she comes back to the sun as a woman. According to this interpretation, *Khyāḥ* plays a double function: as observed by Gutschow and Michaels (2008: 185), ‘inside the cave the girls have to control the dangerous forces of the ghost, which threatens to make them pregnant. But as presexual beings they entertain an ambiguous relationship to the ghost, which is also a playmate that receives an equal share of all the offerings’. Finally, Manandhar (2000: 225) suggests that *bāṁā tayegu* is a sort of ‘symbolic romance’ between the girl and the *Khyāḥ*: during the seclusion, girls receive lessons in sexual conduct and are prepared for conjugal life. Hence,

by the way of teasing, she has a lesson of doing make-up to please *barha khya* by respecting and making herself attractive and cultivating cheerfulness. She is preparing for the life with her future husband. During these days the neighbors and friends come to play with the girl. One of their games is acting out dramas. By the means of these plays the girl is made aware of potential family problems and how to handle in-laws. All these experiences prepare her to be a good wife with *barha khya* as her symbolic husband. (*ibid*.: 225)

### 7.5 When rituals fail… Dead girls and Khyāḥ in Newar folklore

In this section, I discuss a liminal situation that can occur during the *bāṁā tayegu*, which highlights the potential dangerousness of this seclusion rite. In rare cases, 

*[p. 147]* ^pg160

a girl involved in the rite may die, thus failing to properly complete it. In this tragic situation, her *ātman* takes the form of a *Khyāḥ*, most often that of a *Kālo Khyāḥ* or a *Bāṁā Khyāḥ*. Hence, we are confronted with a new category of *Khyāḥ*: it would no longer be an autonomous being, generated by the divine union that we discussed earlier, but rather a decorporated spirit suspended in a transitional condition where the girl’s *ātman* can no longer continue its journey through the reintegration into the *saṃsāra* cycle, with the acquisition of a new physical body, nor through the merging into the *mokṣa* (Armand 2019).

Within the spatial and temporal liminality of the *bāṁā tayegu*, the sudden death of a girl does not hinder the strict restrictions imposed by the ritual: the dead body of the girl cannot be brought outside of the house for a normal celebration of funeral rites (Nepali, *antyeṣṭi*) because it might come in contact with the sunlight, which is forbidden until the end of the ceremony. The body should therefore be buried below (Nepali, *tala gaḍne*) the *swāna*, the steep wooden stairs that lead to the upper floors in traditional Newar houses.<sup>26</sup> As Leena Shrestha told me (Ḍallu, Kathmandu 27 March 2013),

If the girl died during *bāṁā tayegu*, she cannot be cremated in the sun: she needs to be buried in this very building. The room within which she has been kept. … They \[her family members\] have to open out the downstairs ceiling. Men are not allowed to dig the room: women have to dig it because no man can see her during *bāṁāḥ*. They have to dig the floor and then pass the dead body through the downstairs ceiling. They bring the dead body on the first floor and they dig again the downstairs ceiling and so on. They cannot bring the body outside the house: it has to be buried under the house itself. If this process is not done in the good way, the *ātman* of that girl will stay longer in the house: this *ātman*, this girl, will try to attack her family members.

Since the girl’s body is buried under the stairs, the inhabitants of the house have to offer a daily *pūjā* to the *Khyāḥ* to prevent it from harming people. Moving from the Kathmandu Valley up the hills of the Central Valley of Nepal, two Newar informants living in the village of Sāno Gāun (Salyantar, Dhading district), Binod and Ishwor Shrestha, confirm the fact that if the *pūjā* is not accepted by the spirit, the latter may appear as a black shadow waiting below the wooden stairs to grab (Nepali, *samātnu*) the legs of people passing by. Lall (2007: 195) more accurately calls this being *yan Khyāḥ* (Newari, *yan*, ‘place’), adding that it can also use the spinning wheel at night, leaving the inhabitants of the house petrified.

Furthermore, these spirits are also involved in sleep paralysis experiences (cf. Armand *et al*. 2017). The Newari term for this sleep disorder is *khyāla tyāḥwagu*, where the term *tyāḥ* indicates the action of pressing and crushing realized by the *Khyāḥ*, a typical feeling during a sleep paralysis experience. It appears as an incubus-like being with a facetious and mocking character. Our informant from Sāno Khokana (Lalitpur district) told us that, when a person is resting, a *Khyāḥ* might be amused by placing a *parāl* (Nepali), a rice straw,<sup>27</sup> on his chest: it will 

*[p. 148]* ^pg161

leave, a few metres away from the sleeper, a small bag full of silver. When the person wakes up from his sleep, he finds himself crushed by the rice straw, which will weigh so much on his chest that it will prevent him from breathing and from performing even the simplest of movements. If the person succeeds in overcoming this overwhelming paralysis, he will be able to retrieve the bag and earn the reward.

Goodman (1992: 28–29) relates a more elaborated version of this story: a young man, wishing to earn the *Khyāḥ*’s reward, enters a haunted house in Bhaktapur carrying with him *gānjā* (Nepali, ‘smoking hemp’), sweets, and a rope, assuring that he can capture this supernatural being. As expected, this being approaches him, in a threatening manner, to ask what he is smoking. The young man offers him some *gānjā* and some sweets, but when the *Khyāḥ* asks for more, he tells it that he does not want to give anything anymore because he fears that, once the sweets are finished, the being might kill him. The *Khyāḥ* reassures him of its intentions and tells him that, if he wants, he could bind it with a rope to avoid any possible attack. Once the little trickster is securely bound, the young man tells the *Khyāḥ* that he will not release it for fear of being killed. The *Khyāḥ* then offers him to take its *manachintay*, a pouch that can give him anything he wants. The young man accepts, releases the *Khyāḥ*, who immediately runs away before the cock crows, and asks for immortality. Normality will then be restored by the intervention of deities.

#### 7.5.1 Pacifying the harassing Khyāḥ

In the previous section, I have presented accounts by informants relating that the *Khyāḥ* can manifest itself in a harassing form and haunt houses and their inhabitants. The experience collected from B.D., an informant of Jyāṭā (Kathmandu, 17 September 2013), highlights, once again, the incubus-like role played by this supernatural being:

\[I\]t was almost fifteen years ago, when I went to sleep in the daytime. In a corner, I saw that someone was coming: it was like black and it walked like this, you know. So suddenly, I could not move, I tried to go but I was sleeping and I could not say anything. There, it was like dancing. Then, finally, I got up and I ran away. So, that was really the moment when I saw it was a *Khyāk*. So, usually it comes during the daytime. After that, I was very afraid and I never sleep again in the daytime. It was really the moment of the *Khyāk*. I was sleeping on this side \[supine position\]. Then there was a corner \[on the ceiling\], the corner was tall and *Khyāk* just came down, just like there, you know? Then I could not get up. You know, I could not see, but I ‘saw’ that this thing was really a *Khyāk*. \[…\] Suddenly, I could not move and I was soundless. \[…\] I still could not move and when it left me I could move again. … When it disappeared from the wall, then I moved.

How can these particular types of *Khyāḥ* that haunt the places they inhabited when they were alive be appeased? In her remarkable work, Stone (1978: 47) highlights the powerful role of food as ‘symbolic marker’ in Bāhun-Chhetri societies of Nepal: ‘with food, god are worshipped, ancestors sustained, and malignant spirits pacified’. By distinguishing among these three functions of food in Hindu ritual practices and translating them to Newar culture, we can consider the necessity to ‘feed to pacify’ *Khyāḥ*s. Hence, Damodar Karanjit, our informant from Lagan Ṭol (Kathmandu, 25 July 2019), explained to me that it is possible to bring offerings for *Khyāḥ*s in a specific crossroad in Kathmandu, near a place named Yanga iti (Newari, *Yan*, ‘Kathmandu’; *ga*, ‘deep’, *iti*, ‘well’), where *Khyāḥ*s and other *bhut-pret*s usually meet. These offerings are related to a *bau tayegu* (Newari) and comprise *besār* (Nepali, ‘turmeric’), *chāmal* (Nepali, ‘husked but uncooked rice’), *dāl* (Nepali, ‘lentils’), *kālo lugā* (Nepali ‘black cloth’), *kālo til* (Nepali, ‘black sesames seeds’), and *agar batti* (Nepali, ‘sticks of incense’).

Moreover, it is possible to request the help of a Buddhist *guruju*, a sort of priest and healer pertaining to the Bajrāchārya and Śākya high castes: using mantras, he can offer *agar batti* and *chamal* to feed a *kālo Khyāḥ* (Rojan Maharjan, Ṭhahiṭī, Kathmandu: 24 July 2019). A Hindu *tāntrika* (Armand 2021) can also realize an *agni śānti pūjā* (Nepali, ‘offering for the pacification through fire’), or *kālī khane* (Nepali, *khanu* ‘eat’), which ‘cooks’, like a second cremation, this ‘raw’ spirit (Armand 2019: 87–91).

### 7.6 Conclusion: Narratives, beliefs, and rites among Newars

In this chapter, I have highlighted the richness of Newar narrative folklore concerning the figure of the *Khyāḥ*, and presented the various interpretations that this ethnic group attributes to this supernatural being, going from a Tantric framework regarding the energetic nature of the *Khyāḥ* to a ‘popular’ approach. Among the Newar communities of the Kathmandu Valley, the boundary between such domains is not clearly differentiated, insofar as we can speak of a permeable border that provides a potential communication path between mainstream religions, both Hindu and Buddhist, and shamanic approaches rooted in the cultural substratum of Himalayan populations. Both dimensions are combined with more specifically Tantric elements.

Today, folklore does not enjoy a high reputation in the humanities and social sciences, being a victim of the ideological abuse that German nationalism and the *Volkskunde* of the 1920s did to this category. However, one could use the framework of narrative folklore to describe the traditional belief systems of Newars relating to the existence of supernatural beings. Such an approach leads the researcher to consider the inferential methods of his or her informants as fully valid and based on plausible processes of observation and interpretation of reality. Thus, beliefs about the supernatural become reasonable because they develop from an experience perceived as real by our informants; they are then interpreted according to empirically valid models—those pertaining to their own culture; finally, they are verbally transmitted, as narrations, to the other members of the group and to the researcher himself.

It is through these narratives, often in the form of experiential accounts, that the researcher can develop a comprehensive view of conceptions about the supernatural and, in our case, a systemic understanding of the multiple facets of *Khyāḥ*. Moreover, these accounts highlight the cognitive categorization of this being and its related practices in the domain of the ritual, as it is understood in contemporary Newar communities. Narratives about supernatural constantly refer to belief systems by linking a whole arsenal of propitiatory or apotropaic rites directly attached to them. This is what I have observed in the initiation rituals of the Newar girls, especially when *bāṁā tayegu* takes a dramatic turn: offering *pūjā* results in the appeasement of a *Khyāḥ* that haunts human reality. Finally, these three domains—belief, narrative, and ritual practice—intertwine in a multiple relationship and allow us to better understand the complexity of *Khyāḥ* in the Newar society.
