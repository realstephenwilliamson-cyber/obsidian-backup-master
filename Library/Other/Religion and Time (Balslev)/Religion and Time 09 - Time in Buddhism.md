I. THE PROBLEM OF TIME AND ITS DIMENSIONS 

## Time in Buddhism

Our awareness of time is bound up with our awareness of the alteration of things. Alteration implies the combination of contradictorily opposed predicates in what appears to be one and the same thing at one and the same place. Only time can apparently remove the contradiction by ordering the predicates one after the other. [^1]Common sense, thus, relies upon the idea of time including simultaneity, succession and pace so that our experience of the world of change may be intelligible. [^2]Successful practical activity requires that we should be able to understand the process of change as subject to some order which may be observed, remembered and anticipated. The sense of time, thus, appears to be given not only in our instinctive practical activity with its accompanying intellectual constructions but also in such aspects of our self-awareness as memory and anticipation or prescience. Nevertheless, it has continued to be a specially obscure area for philosophers. Is time real? Is it an independent substance or a dependent mode or relation? Is there a timeless reality? Science has been interested in the relation of time to matter, motion and space; religion in its relation to creation, self, suffering and salvation. Buddhist discussions on time began in the context of the religious quest of transcendence and in course of time moved on to metaphysical, logical and dialectical issues. They also had to reckon with the cosmological context of time which had a strong earlier tradition.

Ill. TIME IN BUDDHIST RELIGIOUS EXPERIENCE 

Buddha's notion of time was not derived from Cosmology or speculative metaphysics but from introspective reflection over experience. If we return to watch ourselves we would discover a constant succession of ideas and we seem to live in this stream of consciousness. We seem to be passing away every moment and sense time as a unique and irreversable process in which we move toward death. Temporality as the mutability or mortality of the subjective being or consciousness is the source of the deeper kinds of suffering (viz., *PariTJ,ama-du~khata* and *sainskara-duhkhata).* [^18]Both the subject and the object of desire are involved in change willy nilly and this is the reason why happiness is fleeting and turns into suffering. More basically, we desire *to be* without realizing that being is necessarily destined *not to be.* Our very being is involved in commotion and evanescence. Thus temporality is ingrained in subjective being or the self and since suffering is ultimately rooted in it, the quest of the spiritually sensitive and ambitious person can only be for Immortality or the conquest of time. So Buddha set out to discover what is eternally quiescent *(anuttaram fanti-vara-padam).* The enemy he sought to conquer was *Mara* or Death. As explained above, the cycles of cosmic and individual existence depend on the force of *karman* which is ultimately rooted in desire. It is as desire that *Mara* or Death lurks in the human heart. IV. TIME AND REALITY IN THE ABHIDHARMA

VI. TIME AND THE DIALECTICIANS 

Whether it is philosophically tenable to hold to the reality of the unconditioned as well as the conditioned, of eternal as well as the temporal, without reducing the eternal to a mere idea or the temporal to a mere delusion, is an unavoidable question. The early Buddhist schools faced the problem in trying to explain how a stream of conditioned experiences in time could 'attain' to *NirvarJa* which is a reality but not in time. [^84]They glossed over the fundamental difficulty of explaining how the conditioned *(sarhskrta)* and the unconditioned *(asarhskrta)* could both be elements of reality (i.e., *dharmas)* in the same basic sense. [^85]The Sautrantikas proposed not to distinguish unconditioned and transcendent being from non-being but they did not see the dialectical difficulties in the conception of an empirical and determinate reality which is totally dependent on being encompassed logically as well as temporally by non-being. [^86]If all relations are intellectual constructions and superimpositions, [^87]the real becomes strictly inconceivable and cannot be identified with the moment *(krarJa).* Nor can reality be conceived as a plurality of elements each with its own specific essence or nature. The fact that the nature *(svahhava)* of an entity cannot be conceived except by relational dependence on another shows that it is not a reality but an intellectual construction. [^88]Nor can the existence or non-existence attributed to the entities be real because these attributions cannot be made independently. If the entities are devoid of reality (i.e., *funya),* time too becomes unreal. In fact, motion, change and causality present insuperable contradictions<sup>92</sup>and time is inseparable from these. <sup>93</sup>Besides, time has special problems of its own. Since there is no moving or changing body apart from motion or change, 94 they can only refer to successive discrete moments, each different from the other. Motion and change become intelligible only when the notion of a continuum as an identity in difference is superimposed on separate moments. But there is no separate reality corresponding to the notion of the continuum. Since what is real is only momentary, it cannot really move or change. Again, since an identity is reversible, the identity of being with activity also means that activity is nothing but simple being. [^95]Causality then becomes simple temporal succession. In the absence of any influence or activity between cause and effect, how can there be dependence between them or invariance in their succession? If causality were to be real it would be impossible to explain the relationship of cause and effect as one of either identity or difference. As for time, its three divisions into past, present and future have no independent reality since each can be defined only in relation to the others. Time cannot be comprehended in itself as an unmoving whole. Nor can it be approached as the moving moment since that cannot be distinguished from some specific existence. Time is, thus, notional, not real.
