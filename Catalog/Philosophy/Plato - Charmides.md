## About

A dialogue of Plato, narrated by Socrates, on sophrosyne (temperance, self-control). Back from the fighting at Potidaea, Socrates questions the handsome youth Charmides and his guardian Critias about what sophrosyne is, and the search ends without an agreed definition.

- **People:** Socrates, who narrates the conversation to an unnamed listener; Charmides, Plato's maternal uncle; Critias, Charmides' cousin and guardian, later one of the Thirty Tyrants; and Chaerephon, Socrates' excitable friend.
- **Setting and dramatic date:** the wrestling school (palaestra) of Taureas in Athens, opposite the shrine of Basile. Voula Tsouna (2022) calls the dramatic date one of the earliest in Plato: just after the battle at Potidaea, which many commentators place in 432 BC and she thinks more likely 429 BC.
- **Composition:** Wikipedia reports that Stallbaum put it before the rule of the Thirty, around 405 BC, and that most scholars date it around 388 BC. Tsouna says it is generally placed in the 380s and notes Dušanić's date of 382. The IEP lists it among the early dialogues, written after Socrates' death and before Plato's first trip to Sicily in 387. None of these sources doubts its authenticity.
- **Argument:** the definitions tried are quietness, modesty, minding one's own business and self-knowledge; Wikipedia adds knowledge of good and evil. Britannica says the inquiry turns into hard questions about whether knowledge can be knowledge of itself. It ends in aporia.
- **Place among the dialogues:** an early, Socratic dialogue, by style and by its inconclusive ending (Wikipedia, Britannica). Tsouna traces its links both to other Socratic dialogues and to the middle and late works. Stephanus pages 153a–176d.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia notes that Critias' later part in the Thirty gives its questions about self-control an ironic edge. Tsouna's open-access commentary from Cambridge includes a new translation.
