## About

Baruch Spinoza's (1632–1677) Latin treatise on religion and the state. It reads the Bible as a historical document, explains prophecy and miracles naturally, separates theology from philosophy, and defends the freedom to philosophize.

- **Writing:** Wikipedia says he began it in 1665 and connects it with the imprisonment and death of his friend Adriaan Koerbagh; Steinberg (SEP) reports the Koerbagh link as generally supposed. Nadler (SEP) says he set aside the *Ethics* to finish it. In a letter (Ep. 30) Spinoza gave three aims: to answer charges of atheism, to oppose the theologians' prejudices, and to defend the freedom to philosophize.
- **1670:** published anonymously in Amsterdam by Jan Rieuwertsz. The title page named Hamburg and the publisher Henricus Künraht, who had died in 1605. Spinoza stopped the printing of Jan Hendriksz Glazemaker's Dutch translation, ready by 1671.
- **Contents:** a preface and 20 chapters, most on religion and the last five on the state. Spinoza denies that Moses wrote the Pentateuch; Nadler reports his view that one later historian, most likely Ezra, compiled the Torah and the historical books. He treats the Torah as the constitution of the ancient Hebrew state.
- **Politics:** natural right extends as far as power; the state arises from a social contract in which people transfer their right to the sovereign, though how this fits his naturalism is disputed; the sovereign controls the outward practice of religion; and because controlling another's beliefs completely is strictly impossible, and right is coextensive with power, the sovereign has no right to it, though seditious speech may be curbed (SEP). Wikipedia says he favored democracy, meaning any representative government.
- **Reception:** Jakob Thomasius attacked it in 1670, and Nadler reports one critic calling it a book "forged in hell". Wikipedia says the Dutch Reformed synod of Dordrecht condemned it in 1673 and it was banned in 1674.
- **Editions:** English translations by Robert Willis (1862), R. H. M. Elwes (1883), A. G. Wernham (1958), Samuel Shirley (1982), Jonathan Israel and Michael Silverthorne (Cambridge, 2007) and Edwin Curley (2016).
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says it laid groundwork for ideas about liberalism, secularism and democracy.
