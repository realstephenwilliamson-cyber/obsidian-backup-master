## About

Maurice Merleau-Ponty's (1908–1961) study of perception and the lived body. Its central thesis is the primacy of perception: reflective thought rests on a bodily, pre-reflective engagement with the world.

- **1945:** published by Gallimard as *Phénoménologie de la perception*; the SEP says it was completed in 1944. It was his main doctoral thesis, defended in July 1945 with *The Structure of Behavior* as the complementary thesis.
- **Structure:** an Introduction, "Classical Prejudices and the Return to Phenomena"; Part One, The Body; Part Two, The Perceived World; Part Three, Being-for-Itself and Being-in-the-World, which the SEP says treats the cogito, time and freedom.
- **Main ideas:** it rejects both empiricism and intellectualism, which start from dispersed sensations. It describes the body schema and bodily intentionality as an "I can" rather than an "I think," using cases such as Schneider, a brain-injured patient studied by Gelb and Goldstein, and phantom limbs.
- **Background:** it draws on Husserl (including unpublished manuscripts), Heidegger, Gestalt theory and the psychology and neurology of the day. Wikipedia sets it beside Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943).
- **English:** Colin Smith's translation (Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1962), and Donald A. Landes's (Routledge), which the publisher calls the first new translation in over fifty years. Wikipedia's infobox and the SEP date Landes 2012; Wikipedia's text says 2013, and Routledge lists an August 2013 release.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia says it established Merleau-Ponty as the pre-eminent philosopher of the body (Bernasconi) and counts it a major statement of French existentialism. A. J. Ayer found its arguments against sense-datum theory inconclusive.
