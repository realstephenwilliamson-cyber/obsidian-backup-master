## Chapter 5: Crossing the boundaries of sex, blood, and magic in the Tantric cult of Kāmākhyā 1

*Paolo E. Rosati*

### 5.1 Introduction

The temple complex of Kāmākhyā stands on Nīlācala (Blue Mountain) peak in the Brahmaputra plain of Assam, a geographical and socio-cultural bridge between the Indian subcontinent and mainland Southeast Asia.<sup>2</sup> This region is considered a stronghold of Śākta Tantra and the temple of Kāmākhyā the core of South Asian *śaktipīṭha*s’ network (Eliade 1971: 305; Urban 2011: 231–232).<sup>3</sup> Kāmākhyā, who is equated to Kālī in mediaeval Tantras (*Yoginītantra* 1.15.1–2; 

*Kāmākhyātantra* 1.15, 10.4), is a multifarious goddess who is either a caring and lovely mother or a dreadful and dangerous mother (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 80.52–54a). The wide divergence in her traits suggests that she was an early mediaeval socio-- religious construction that intersected Vedic, Brahmanic, folk, and tribal elements (Borkataky Varma 2019: 181–182; Dold 2004: 90; Rosati 2020: 92–93; Shin 2010; Urban 2010).

Kāmākhyā is worshipped in her non-anthropomorphic form as a *yoni-*stone housed inside the dark and restricted *garbhagṛha* (‘womb chamber’) of her temple, which is placed under the ground’s level and reached via more than a hundred steps downward. This sacred cave was probably built during the reign of Surendravarman (450–485 CE; Sharma 1978: 2–3). The *yoni* is not only the symbol of goddess Kāmākhyā but also, according to the local Purāṇas and Tantras, it is the goddess herself embodied in the *yoni-*stone (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 62.75; *Kāmākhyātantra* 1.4).

From a metonymic perspective, Kāmākhyā’s *garbhagṛha* is very suggestive: the *yoni* is submerged by the water of a natural stream that flows underground and oozes out from an inlet on the *yoni-*stone, being thus strongly evocative of a woman’s vulva surrounded by its sexual fluids; the surrounding chamber, in turn, recalls the maternal womb. This sexual symbolism is intensified during the three-- day Ambuvācī-melā—the main festival in honour of Kāmākhyā falling on the month of *āṣāḍha* (in June or July)—that celebrates the goddess’s annual menstrual cycle. The festival attracts many thousands of devotees and tourists belonging to several religious and ethnic strands: Śākta-Tantric, Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and tribal people such as the bare Naga *saṃnyāsin*s. During this period, the goddess, through her menstrual blood, is believed to give back to the earth the power she 

*[p. 99]* ^pg112

incorporated during the daily blood offerings in order to vivify the soil. For the first three days of the festival, the temple’s doors are closed due to the pollution of the goddess’s menses, and no devotees are admitted inside; on the fourth day the doors are opened for the pilgrims’ *darśan* (‘seeing’).<sup>4</sup>

The Ambuvācī-melā very well exemplifies the Kāmākhyā-*pīṭha*’s close connection with feminine sexual symbolism,<sup>5</sup> whose roots lie in the primitive Śākta traditions centred on women as those who transmit norms, values, and traditions to the offspring (Bhattacharyya 1996: 1–2). However, no evidence corroborates the central position of women in non-Brahmanic traditions.<sup>6</sup> Independently of whether Śāktism and its focus on women and womanhood was exclusively a non-Aryan tradition or not, it challenged the Brahmanic patriarchal view that was developed throughout the centuries in the Indian subcontinent.

### 5.2 The intersection of Śākta-Purāṇic and Kaula traditions at Kāmākhyā

The literary origin of the Kāmākhyā temple and its Śākta cult of the bleeding *yoni* is found at the intersection of the mediaeval Epic-Purāṇic myth of Satī’s death and Kaula theology.<sup>7</sup> This emerges from an analysis of the later Tantric section of the *Kālikāpurāṇa* (Ch. 52–76), a Śākta Purāṇa devoted to the worship of goddess Kāmākhyā compiled in northeastern India around the 9th–11th centuries.

The more mythological part of the *Kālikāpurāṇa* (Chapters 1–51) preserves a far more traditional cosmogony of Kāmākhyā-*pīṭha*, which links the sacred site with the mythology of Satī that culminated in the goddess’s self-sacrifice and her corpse’s dismemberment by the gods.<sup>8</sup> In this myth’s version, Dakṣa decided not to invite Śiva (his son-in-law) and Satī (his daughter) to the sacrifice because they were both considered *kapālin*s (‘skull bearers’) (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 16.29–30, 17.12), a status that associated both deities with impurity, as well as with the margins of the Brahmanical tradition (Doniger 1971: 280–281). Satī, furious about their exclusion from the sacrifice, decided to go to the sacrificial arena where she renounced her body by performing *tapas* (‘sacred heat’) (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 16.47–49). Śiva, who became hungry because of his lover’s death, destroyed the sacrifice with the help of his *gaṇa*’s army led by Vīrabhadra. Thereafter, Śiva took Satī’s lifeless body on his shoulders and began to wander through the universe. The gods, wanting to stop the destructive fury of Śiva, pervaded Satī’s corpse and dismembered it (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 18.39–40).<sup>9</sup> Hence, Satī’s limbs fell on the earth originating seven *śaktipīṭha*s; among them, the *yoni* fell on Nīlācala (18.41–43).<sup>10</sup>

The *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya*—an eminent Kaula text compiled in northeastern India between the 9th and the 11th centuries—and the *Kālikāpurāṇa*, through its Tantric section, systematized the cult of 64 *yoginī*s associated with Kāmākhyā. While the Tantric tradition before its Kaula reformation was centred on the worship of Śiva-Bhairava and his Śakti or on the worship of Kālī alone in the 

*[p. 100]* ^pg113

cremation ground (*śmaśāna*) surrounded by *yoginī*s (White 1998: 172–174),<sup>11</sup> with the rise of the Kaula school around the 10th century, the Tantric system’s ritual praxis shifted from blood sacrifices to mystic-erotic practices centred on the *yonipūjā* (‘worship of the vulva’; Sanderson 1988: 664–666). One of the main characteristics of Kaulism, which recognized Śiva and Śakti as the primordial cosmic principles, was the oral transmission of knowledge from the guru to the disciple in order to maintain its religious gnosis secret (Dyczkowski 1988: 62–63; see *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* 6.7–8).

The *Kālikāpurāṇa* preserves an alternative myth on the origin of Kāmākhyā, which reinforces the Kaula ideology within the Purāṇic context:

When the sun was in the house of Virgo on the ninth day of the dark half of the moon (of that month), Mahāmāyā in olden times manifested herself in her ten-armed form, called Ugracaṇḍā, together with the millions of *yoginī*s. On the full moon day of the month Aṣāḍha, Dakṣa commenced performance of a sacrifice lasting for twelve years; all the gods were invited to that sacrifice. However, I \[Śiva\] was not invited to the sacrifice by the noble Dakṣa accusing me as the ‘skull-bearer’ (*kapālin*); and Satī was inviting either saying that she was his (skull-bearer’s) wife. Because of this Satī became enraged and she gave up her life. Having thus abandoned her body, Satī afterwards revealed herself in the fierce form (*caṇḍamūrti*). While this sacrifice of twelve years was still going on, on the ninth day in the dark half of the moon when the sun was in the house of Virgo, Mahāmāyā had given up her form as Satī, assumed a terrible form and then she being associated by tens of millions of *yoginī*s, the retinue of Śaṅkara \[Śiva\], and with Śaṅkara himself carried on the destruction of the sacrifice. The Goddess herself had destroyed the sacrifice of the noble Dakṣa. The violent rage of the Goddess having been subsided the gods performed a unique worship of the Goddess. (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 61.2–10, trans. Shastri 2008: 453) In this version of the myth, the goddess after her sacrifice assumes a dreadful form and, together with a band of *yoginī*s, the army of Śiva and Śiva himself, destroys the sacrificial arena. She is no longer only the one who triggered the destruction of *dakṣayajña*, but she actively participates in the destructive action. The *yoginī*s emerged as her attendants, thus corroborating the hypothesis of a Kaula stratification over the original Śākta-Purāṇic tradition. This variation of Satī’s story puts together, for the first time, the myth of the goddess Satī and the cluster of *yoginī*s. Satī, furthermore, lost her lovely and peaceful traits: indeed, when she renounced her body, she was angry and assumed her terrifying form (*caṇḍamūrti*) to destroy the *dakṣayajña*.<sup>12</sup>

In order to justify the association of the goddess Kāmākhyā with the *yoginī* cult, the *Kālikāpurāṇa* narrates that when the *yoni* fell on Assam, it split in many parts:

On this sacred most seat, named Kubjikā-pīṭha, which is Nilakūṭa, there the Goddess resides with me in secret. The *yoni* of Satī, which had fallen on this 

*[p. 101]* ^pg114

sacred most place was broken into pieces, and then the vulva of Satī turned into stone, in which the goddess Kāmākhyā herself resides. Should a mortal being ever touch that stone (in the shape of *yoni*) attains immortality. Being immortal he resides in the abode of Brahmā, and if he stays there attains *mokṣa*.

(*Kālikāpurāṇa* 62.73b–76a, trans. Shastri 2008: 458) Shin (2010) suggests that each part of the original *yoni* stands at the origin of a *yoginī*. In the Tantric section of the *Kālikāpurāṇa* (54.35–45, 63.37–43) are also preserved the two most ancient lists of 64 *yoginī*s,<sup>13</sup> thus reinforcing the link between the goddess Kāmākhyā and the Kaula cult of *yoginī*s (62.91–95) and, at the same time, working as a cultural bridge linking the Purāṇic concept of *śakti* to Kaulism:

In this place eight eternal *yoginī*s, Śailaputtrī and others \[…\] always remain surrounding the goddess (Kāmākhyā) in her primary form \[…\] Guptakāmā, Śrīkāmā, Vindhyavāsinī, Koṭīśvarī, Vanasthā, Pādadurgā, Dīrgheśvarī and also one known as Bhuvaneśvarī \[…\] these eight deities are the Goddess’ own *yoginī*s; they are known by their *pīṭha*’s names. All sacred places are here in the form of water in the pond called Saubhāgya-sarovara, which though small in size, produces merits.

(*Kālikāpurāṇa* 62.91–95, trans. Shastri 2008: 459) The textual description of goddess Kāmākhyā’s human-like form shares various iconographic traits that belong to the *yoginī*s, such as her connection with sexual intercourse and meditation:<sup>14</sup>

The goddess Kāmākhyā stands on a white ghost, wears a rosary, and a *siddha-* *sūtra* in her two hands; her other two hands are in boon-granting and safety providing postures; she is of saffron-yellow colour, bedecked with gems and ornaments; she resides in the wisdom and meditation (of devotees), adequately manifested she is worshipped by Brahmā, Indra and others; she is engaged in sexual intercourse; her favourite mantra is preceded by *candra* and *bindu*.

(*Kālikāpurāṇa* 72.63, trans. Shastri 2008: 547) Therefore, the *Kālikāpurāṇa* emerges as a complex source, which stratifies Śākta, Purāṇic, and Kaula elements, and reveals a close association between the *yoginī*s and the flowing water inside the *garbhagṛha* of Kāmākhyā.<sup>15</sup> Not only did the *yoni* become the non-anthropomorphic image of the goddess but also the symbol of the *yoginī*s. Kāmākhyā lives inside the *yoni* (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 62.75; *Kāmākhyātantra* 1.4), but she also assumes the form of red water (*Yoginītantra* 1.11.37a). The *yoginī*s, who were previously connected to the cremation ground (*Hevajratantra* 1.7), became the guardians of the *yoni* (Rosati 2017a: 8), thus developing a connection with the aquatic symbolism. From there, the *yoni* was appropriated as the core element by the Kaula school, being described as the ‘mouth of the *yoginī*s’, the 

*[p. 102]* ^pg115

essence of the Kaula tradition (*Tantrāloka* 29.122a–26a, 127b–28b). It was by drinking the sexual fluids from the human vulva that the Kaula practitioner obtained spiritual and mundane powers. This close connection between the *yonipīṭha* and the *yoginī* cult was also described in the mediaeval *Kaulajñānanir* *ṇaya* (8.23–28), in which the *yoginī*s are said to arise from the performance of mantras.

The Tantric section of the *Kālikāpurāṇa* not only presents Purāṇic and Kaula religiosity but also mediates the Śākta-Purāṇic ideology deriving from Satī’s corpse as the origin of the multiplication of the goddess into the Kaula Tantras of northeast India, such as the *Yonitantra* (8.8–9).

### 5.3 The affirmation of Kaula Tantra at Kāmākhyā

The origin of the *yoginī* cult is associated with two religious traditions both at the margins of what is called mainstream Hinduism: the cult of *mātṛgaṇa*s, who were crystallized in the cluster of the seven or eight *mātṛ*s (‘mothers’) around the end of the 6th century (Shin 2011), and the cult of village folk goddesses (Dehejia 1986: 68). Dehejia speculates that the *yoginī*s were absorbed into Kaula sects from local cults, a process questioned by Hatley (2014: 209–210), for whom, if ‘formative influences’ existed, they should be researched within ‘the early Śaiva and Buddhist esoteric traditions’ (*ibid*.).

Whereas the Kaula sources pointed out the connection of the goddess Kāmākhyā with the sex-related practices linked to the circle of 64 *yoginī*s, we do not find evidence of this sexual aspect in the visual art of mediaeval Nīlācala, as no *yoginī* temple was found there, although several sculptures of isolated *yoginī*s were discovered throughout the hill. Furthermore, the sculptural pattern associated with the iconography of the *yoginī*s is based on their death-related symbolism, which belonged to the earlier Vidyāpīṭha tradition, in which the Kāpālika tradition of *śmaśāna*s was pervasive (Sanderson 1988: 670). The Vidyāpīṭha culture, on the other side, ‘mark\[s\] a shift from the pacific deity Sadāśiva to the skull-bearing Bhairava and his wild female companions’ (Hatley 2016: 37). According to Vidyāpīṭha texts, the consumption of sexual fluids was necessary to invoke and propitiate the *yoginī*s, who were called upon through the performance of ritualized sexual intercourses (*maithuna*); those, however, were not central practices in Vidyāpīṭha Tantras and were aestheticized just as in Kaulism (Sanderson 1988: 679–680).

The Kaula school shifted the main focus of rituals from blood offerings and the consumption of prohibited substances (such as raw meat and alcoholic beverages) to a so-called mystic-erotic praxis centred on the sexual worship of *yoginī*s in their human form (White 2003: 8–12). The sexual rites were the most secret practices of the Kaula reformed ritual praxis, which were reserved for a restricted number of initiates. The Kaula ritualization of sexual intercourse was covered in secrecy and therefore usually performed during the night in remote and almost inaccessible places (Dyczkowski 1988: 6–7). The religious secrecy of sexual rites—which excluded those who were not part of the *kula* (‘clan’) from the obtainment of Kaula gnosis—possibly influenced the lack of sculptural 

*[p. 103]* ^pg116

representation of sex-related scenes and symbols within the *yoginī*s’ iconographic patterns.<sup>16</sup> The sexual symbolism, however, was included within death symbols such as severed heads, which symbolize not only the Brahmanicidal act *par excellence*—that is the one performed by Śiva against Brahmā (*Śivapurāṇa* 2.2.29.20)<sup>17</sup>—but also the castration and consequent loss of manhood.

When Kaulism emerged, the blood sacrifices replicating the split of Satī’s corpse in order to release the *śakti*—an act that reflects the main purpose of Tantric devotees (Urban 2001: 783)—were flanked by sexual rites that symbolized the endless union of Śiva-Śakti (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 62.1–3) since Śiva had reached his wife in his *liṅga* shape in every *pīṭha* when her limbs fell on the Indian subcontinent originating her Tantric network of *śaktipīṭha*s (Padoux 2011: 184– 187). Hence, the Kaula system integrated the cult of the *yoginī*s after the mediation of the esoteric traditions related to the cremation grounds, as may be evinced by the presence of Kāmākhyā in the more ancient list of *pīṭha*s in a Buddhist esoteric source (*Hevajratantra* 1.7.12) and by its association with Heruka (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 63.135–37a). With Kaulism, the transmission of religious knowledge to the disciple through the ‘mouth to mouth’ practice raised to prominence (*Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* 18.22b); thus, the *yonipūjā* became ‘the “primal sacrifice” (*ādiyāga*)—one of the six types of “clan sacrifice” (*kulayāga*)—and Tantric initiation’ (White 2003: 106). The ‘mouth to mouth’ transmission consisted in the ritual drinking of sexual fluids (*yonitattva*) from the nether mouth of a *yoginī*, a ritual called *rajapāna* (*ibid*.). The consumption of *yoginī*s’ sexual liquids (*Kāmākhyātantra* 4.22; *Tantrāloka* 29.122a–26a, 127b–28b) became an act necessary also to obtain the powers called *siddhi*s (‘accomplishments’; see the following).

Although sexual rites assumed a very central place in the Kaula system, blood sacrifices did not fall into religious oblivion. The Kaula Tantras, indeed, preserved *yoginī*s’ death-related symbolism. They were described as hungry for animal and human blood and flesh (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 67.3–6, *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* 11.14–17), and the adept’s initiation to the Kaula was often performed in secret places, such as cremation grounds, during the night. The sexual ritual was homologated to the slaughtering of sacrificial victims (White 2003: 17); in fact, according to the *Yonitantra*, blood and sexual rites were two sides of the same coin, and they should be performed together:

Merely by worshipping the female sexual organ \[*yoni*\] the worship of the Goddess as Power \[Shakti\] is performed. The adept should worship with the blood flowing from the sacrifices of birds, etc. and with the words ‘*yoni*, *yoni*’, while reciting his prayers.

(*Yonitantra* 3.16–17, trans. Urban 1995: 68)<sup>18</sup> The *yonitattva* (‘vulva’s essence’), which was considered equal to *tattvam utta* *mam* (‘sublime essence’; Schoterman 1980: 27), not always corresponds to sperm (*śukra*) mixed with menstrual blood (*puṣpa*) or feminine semen (*rajas*) but sometimes refers only to the female sexual liquids (Sacco 2014: 71). The consumption of *yonitattva*—which is supposed to be obtained through the worship of a *yoni*, 

*[p. 104]* ^pg117

ideally a menstruating one (*Kāmākhyātantra* 3.60)—and, for homologation, of ritual blood was placed on the same plane as the consumption of *soma*, the ‘nectar of immortality’ (White 2004: 11).<sup>19</sup>

At the temple of Kāmākhyā, the *yonitattva* seems to correspond to the powerful liquids of the goddess, which were necessary for practitioners to obtain the desired *siddhi*s and *mokṣa* (‘liberation’). The *Kālikāpurāṇa* is silent regarding sexual rituals, although it describes metonymically the eroticism of the Kaula esoteric initiation rite<sup>20</sup> through the emphasis on bathing in and drinking the sacred water of the *yonipīṭha* as an inestimable source of powers:

Those gods took their bath in vaginal water and sipped water from that water. Thus they derived extreme pleasure. The gods became highly astonished, their sense turned nimb \[sic\], they kept on praising again, the *yoni* of Kāmākhyā. … If a person takes his bath in the water oozing out the vagina and sips it he rever \[sic\] borns on this earth again and at last attains liberation \[*nirvāṇam*\].

(*Kālikāpurāṇa* 72.84–89, trans. Shastri 2008: 549–550) It may be speculated that the *Kālikāpurāṇa* marked the Kāmākhyā-*pīṭha* as a place where any devotee, without sectarian distinctions, could come to worship the goddess and obtain her boons. On the other hand, the Kaula Tantras described sexual rites as performances restricted to those belonging to the *kula* that should be kept secret to outsiders.<sup>21</sup>

### 5.4 Sexual fluids, women, and magic

British colonizers often described northeastern India as a land of witchcraft and black magic, as Tantra was widespread in this part of the Indian subcontinent. Blood sacrifices, which were performed at the Indo-Myanmar border up to the 1970s (Barua 2013: 173–188), headhunting culture, which was widespread among the tribes of northeastern India and upper Myanmar, and orgiastic rites, which were inherited by the mediaeval Kaula school, corroborated the infamous reputation of northeastern India. However, the idea that black magic was widespread across northeastern India lies in the mediaeval Kaula tradition. In fact, according to the *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* (22.9–11), every woman in northeastern India (Kāmarūpa) was a *yoginī* and her worship was necessary to obtain several *siddhi*s and other powers:

It is said that female *sādhaka*s, adepts in *yoga*, dwell in Kāmākhyā-pīṭha. If one joins with one, one achieves Yoginī Siddhi, obtaining consciousness, mind, the ability to assume various shapes, and *aṇimā* with the other of the eight *siddhi*s. Whatsoever is desired may be obtained here. … Similarly, the fivefold essence of *siddhi*, which is secret, may be obtained in these places. This should never be revealed.

(*Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* 16.7–10, trans. Bagchi and Magee 1986: 86)

*[p. 105]* ^pg118

The practice of divinizing women and, conversely, humanizing *yoginī*s, was already stratified in the Vidyāpīṭha texts (Serbaeva 2016: 16); however, the transformation of the *yoginī*s from supernatural beings to ‘flesh-and-blood’ women with supernatural powers was established in Kaulism (White 2003: 13–27). The Kaula practitioners created a ‘world of ecstatic delirium’ where the boundaries between human women and divine counterparts cross each other, superimpose, and intertwine (Sanderson 1985: 201). From then onward, the female practitioner (*yoginī*) in the Kaula system became a key ingredient not only to reach the gnosis but also to obtain every desired power or *siddhi*. Kaula female practitioners became counterparts of the *siddha* (‘perfect being’), the Kaula male practitioner, emphasizing continuity between Tantra and local ancestor and fertility cults.

With the Kaula, thus, the woman was placed at the centre of religious life as the only one able to award *siddhi*s and preserve the *kula*’s tradition through her sexual fluids; the power of cultural-religious transmission and continuity was in women’s hands (White 2003: 18–21, 276). Northeastern mediaeval Tantric texts depicted the woman’s role in the sexual rites either as a ritual object or as a semi-- divine being (Biernacki 2007: 11).<sup>22</sup> In the early Kaula Tantras, such as the *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* and the *Kulāṃ̇avatantra*, women were depicted as ritual tools rather than goddesses, necessary to the *siddha*s in order to obtain *siddhi*s. The *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* described the female Kaula practitioner as a *yoginī*, as well as a prostitute:

One should fill \[a vessel\] with blood together with an equal amount of semen, as well as with *kuṇḍgolaka* secretion. This is combined with honey and clarified butter. \[Menstrual\] blood, a woman’s nectar (*vāmāmṛtam*), \[and\] semen are mixed with alcohol by the brahmin practitioner. It is \[also\] mixed with \[the essence of\] the *buka* flower and the \[fermented\] extract of the *kṛṣṇa* flower. That great-souled one who is accompanied by his female consort \[thereby realizes\] a state of intoxicated bliss. … Having worshiped that \[mixture\], which is placed in a conch shell or pot, in proper sequence, O Goddess, the Master (*ācārya*) of one who is resolute in his devotion \[i.e., the initiate who is being consecrated\] … \[should proceed as follows\]: cannabis, and all of the other consciousness-altering plants … should be wrapped in red cloth together with the five precious gemstones. \[These are accompanied by a food offering of\] molasses, milk, yogurt, liquor, and sugarcane juice. He should distribute \[these\], in sequence, into that which gives sweetness and that which eats babies \[i.e., the mouth of the female consort in this rite, the Yoginī\], into one mouth \[that of the initiate\] after the other \[that of the Yoginī\] … Together with a prostitute or a maiden, or likewise with his own hands, the preceptor should place “that which is to be raised into the head” \[i.e., sexual fluid\] \[into the mouth of the initiate\]. Thereafter, he \[the initiate\] becomes a yogin.

(*Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* 18.15a–23a, trans. Bagchi and Magee, amended by White 

*[p. 106]* ^pg119

This connection between prostitutes and sexual rites in order to obtain *siddhi*s seems to be a unitarian view in the early Kaula texts. However, it developed in a non-unitarian way in later northeastern Tantras (Biernacki 2007: 13–15): while the ca. 17th-century *Kāmākhyātantra* (3.59–61) and *Cīnācāratantra* (3.9–10) claim that a prostitute’s *yoni* was best suited for the sexual ritual and the obtainment of *siddhi*s,<sup>23</sup> another group of Tantras (e.g. *Bṛhannīlatantra*, *Māyātantra*, *Yonitantra*), compiled between the 16th and the 18th centuries, raised women to the rank of *deva*s, through their identification with the *puruṣa* or pure spirit rather than with the *prakṛti* (‘substance, nature’; Biernacki 2007: 6–8): ‘women are gods, women are the life breath’ (*Bṛhannīlatantra* 6.75b; *Māyātantra* 3.12–20; *Yonitantra* 7.16; cf. *Tantrāloka* 15.554d).

Unfortunately, *yoginī*s’ history in pre-Tantric traditions is built on speculations that originated from the analysis of esoteric Buddhist, Vidyāpīṭha, and Kaula texts. We have no idea of how the *yoginī*s were integrated, not only into the Tantric traditions but also into the folk religiosity of the villages’ mothers—who were considered foremothers of the *yoginī*s (Dehejia 1986: 68)—and the *mātṛgaṇa*s and their later development (around the middle or the end of the 6th century) into the *saptamātṛ*s cluster (Shin 2011). Bhattacharyya (1996: 128) suggested that the *yoginī*s were ‘priestesses who were supposed to be possessed by the goddess’, a fact that would explain why the Kaula texts intersected the imaginary of human and divine *yoginī*s. In fact, they not only preserve a religious tradition related to copulation with, and blood offering to, *yoginī*s, but also a tradition of *yoginī*s as preceptors and teachers belonging to ascetic orders (Dyczkowski 1988: 65; Sherma 2000: 42).

In a recent article, Davidson (2017: 4) outlined several magic-agents acting in ancient and mediaeval India without any connection with ‘specific sectarian traditions associated with tantrism’. He also proposed a dichotomy between who obtains *siddhi*s through religious practices and who obtains *siddhi*s through the performance of a ritual or spell. Magic would be related to ‘an ideology or behavior’ rather than a ‘social form’ (*ibid*.). Therefore, *siddhi*s should not be acquired through a spell or ritual that one accidentally learned but only through religious practices that were taught by a guru or another specialist (*ibid*.: 5). However, since magic skills were handed ‘down over the generations’ (*ibid*.: 13), I suggest that this transmission is a type of initiation, albeit restricted to a nuclear or extended family. Following this assumption, because the Kaula sexual rituals were reserved to those belonging to the *kula*, the *siddhi*s’ acquisition through the worship of the *yoni* of the *yoginī*s—as the element necessary to the transmission of the gnosis, through its *yonitattva*, over *kula*’s members—seems widely related to magic and the *yoginī*s to magic-agents.

The term *siddhi* has been variously translated as ‘accomplishment, performance, fulfillment’ (Monier-Williams 1899), ‘suprahuman magical power’ (Lorenzen 1991: 83), ‘power’ (Sanderson 1988: 680), ‘supernatural effect’ (Sanderson 2009: 124), ‘mental perfection’ (Kinsley 1997: 47),<sup>24</sup> ‘supernatural ability’ (Urban 1995: 82), ‘physic power’ (Davidson 2002: 200), ‘supernatural power’ (Einoo 2009: 18), ‘Tantric power’ (Samuel 2008: 304), ‘perfection’ 

*[p. 107]* ^pg120

(White 2000b: 632), ‘supernatural enjoyments’ (White 2009: 11), ‘supernatural powers’ (White 2012: 12), ‘yogic attainment’ (Urban 2010: 69), and ‘the supernatural powers which arise either directly or indirectly as a result of the practice of yoga or tantric rites’ (Mallinson 2012: 327). Considering these definitions, *siddhi* appears to be linked to three different phenomena: physical, mental (supernatural), and magical ones.

According to Dehejia (1986: 53), the Kaula practitioner worshipped the circle of the *yoginī*s in order to obtain ‘occult powers’ while *mokṣa* seems not a primary aim for those who worship the *yoginī*s. According to several Tantras, there are eight great *siddhi*s (*aṣṭamahāsiddhi*s):<sup>25</sup> *aṇimā*, the power of minuteness;<sup>26</sup> *mahimā*, the power to become of gigantic size;<sup>27</sup> *laghimā*, the power to become weightless, being thus able to levitate and to leave one’s own body; *garimā*, a power related to excessive weightiness; *prākāmya*, the power to hypnotize; *īśitva*, the power to control people’s and other beings’ bodies and minds; *vaśitva*, the power to control nature; *kāmavaśayitva*, the power to realize every desire (*ibid*.: 53–54).

In the Tantric section of the *Kālikāpurāṇa* (56.51–65) eight *siddhi*s are described, which do not correspond with the ‘traditional’ ones: *guṭikāñjana*, a collyrium that enables to see a buried treasure; *pātālasiddhi*, the power of diving underground; *pādalepa*, an ointment enabling to move secretly anywhere; *rasāyana*, the power to transform baser metals into gold or find an eternal life elixir; *khaḍga*, a sword that guarantees success in battle if the right mantras are performed; *antaradhāna*, the power of invisibility; *bhūcara*, the power to move swiftly everywhere; *khecara*, the power to fly (see van Kooij 1972: 23–25).

The *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* (8.31–44) mentions several *siddhi*s, which can be obtained if the Kaula practitioner worships the *yoginī*s in the right order. These *siddhis* are miniaturization, the capacity to attract and subjugate people, the ability to take possession of people’s will, the power of entering into peoples’ bodies, the power of knowledge of past and future, the power to obtain things that one desires, enjoyment and liberation, the power of paralyzing people and speaking every language heard or unheard, the ability to control wind and create silence, the power to live in harmony with the *dharma* (‘laws of nature’; Mukhopadhyaya 2012: 59), the power to obtain material things, the power fulfil desires and obtain liberation (*mokṣa*), the power to paralyze an enemy’s army, the power of freedom from birth and death’s bondage, the power to fulfil one’s own desires, the power over death, the power to paralyze, and the power of delusion.

In the later northeastern Tantras, *siddhi*s are only touched upon; in the *Yonitantra*, *Kāmākhyātantra*, and *Cīnācāratantra*, indeed, no *siddhi*s’ lists are accounted for. According to the *Cīnācāratantra* (3.8–9, 41; 5.43), those who worship the goddess in the *yonipīṭha* will obtain the eight great *siddhi*s—miniaturization (*aṇimā*) is the first among the eight, but the text does not name the other seven *siddhi*s, nor does it enter into their details. The *Yonitantra* and the *Kāmākhyātantra* are vague regarding the *siddhi*s focusing on the connection between *siddhi* with the *yonipūjā* and its secrecy.

*[p. 108]* ^pg121

It seems that *siddhi* blends physical and mental powers into magical ones. Indeed, through the Kaula sexual ritual, human and superhuman plans intersect each other, thus channelling magical powers into the Kaula cosmos. The *yoni* was the channel that allowed the Kaula practitioners to overcome the natural order and to obtain magical powers, which could be physical, such as *aṇimā*, or psychic, such as *prākāmya*. Both types of *siddhi*s are magical since they overcome the laws of nature. The Kaula practitioners, through the obtainment of *siddhi*s, are able to control natural elements, people’s bodies and minds, and transform their own bodies and thus influence future events and particular behaviours.

The *yoni*’s sexual fluids, which are described as *kuladravyam* (‘clan’s fluid’), *kulāmṛta* (‘clan’s ambrosia’), and *yonitattva* (‘vulva’s essence’), are the fundamental elements in order to obtain *siddhi*s. The *yoginī*, within this mystico-- magical universe, was botha woman and a goddess who was the main cultic object of the *rajapāna* ritual (see *Kāmākhyātantra* 4.24). This ritual underlies the Kaula’s view of the *kula* as a ‘state of bliss’ which can be obtained following the *bhoga* (‘enjoyment’) path in opposition to the *yoga* (‘renunciation’) path (Dehejia 1986: 32).

Sexual and other organic fluids are the most powerful substances to be used in ritual praxis or alchemical works, although they are ambivalent and highly impure (White 2003: 67). The menstrual blood among the sexual liquids has been considered either an impure substance or a highly auspicious, powerful, and sacred element that encompasses both auspicious and inauspicious aspects, yet neither one exclusively (Appfel-Marglin 1985: 40–44). This idea of impurity related to organic fluids is linked to the concept of bodily integrity, according to which substances that flow over the boundaries of the body are polluting and dangerous. The menstruating Kāmākhyā and her circle of *yoginī*s reinforce the idea that menstrual blood is an inestimable source of power (*śakti*), which is sought by Kaula devotees in order to obtain several *siddhi*s.

The secrecy of the Kaula sexual ritual was probably due to the fact that *siddhi*s are incommensurable magical powers, which are dangerous and probably deadly for those who are not part of the *kula*. On a socio-political ground, the sacerdotal caste of the *kula* wanted to maintain the exclusive privilege of being depositories of secret knowledges that could be used by the kingship for political purposes (Urban 2010: 73), thus marking an indissoluble link between South Asian kings and Tantra.

### 5.5 Sacrificial blood, buffalos, and tribespeople

In the Tantric context of mediaeval Assam, only kings were allowed to perform or authorize blood sacrifices, through which blood emerged as a magical tool: with blood offerings (*balidāna*) a king was able to reach heaven, defeat his enemies (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 84.79–81) and maintain a peaceful kingdom (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 85.13–14a). Performing blood sacrifices, the king was in very close relation with terrifying divine beings, such as the *yoginī*s and other blood-thirsty deities often associated with blood and cremation grounds (Rosati 2017a: 10–12).

*[p. 109]* ^pg122

Although blood offerings are often considered an anti-Vedic praxis, animal sacrifices were performed also by the Vedic people since 1500 BCE but were ‘gradually removed from mainstream Hindu traditions’ (Urban 2010: 57). Blood sacrifices were a fundamental part of the Kaula system: indeed, in the remaining *yoginī* temples across South Asia, there are no images of sexual practices, whereas their iconographic pattern is centred on severed heads and skulls, suggesting that blood sacrifices were the main rituals (Davidson 2002: 181). In Kaula ritual praxis, the offerings of prohibited substances to  *yoginī*s, such as meat (*māṃsa*) and blood (*rudhira*), were fundamental (Dehejia 1986: 57).<sup>28</sup>

At Kāmākhyā-*pīṭha*, there was a subversion of the Vedic sacrifice that foresaw the suffocation of the ritual victim in order to prevent the spread of blood, through the ritual decapitation that today is performed daily to worship Kāmākhyā. Furthermore, the animals that are offered to the goddess are not domesticated (as prescribed in Vedic texts) but wild (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 55.3–6, 67.3–5; *Kaulajñānaniṃ̇aya* 11.14–17; *Yoginītantra* 2.9.158, 2.7.170), and thus deemed impure in orthodox Hindu traditions. Urban (2010: 62–63) pointed out that the offering of wild animals has roots in the northeastern Indian tribal traditions, in which sacrificing non-domesticated animals to worship deities and ancestors was a common custom. Among the animal sacrifices, the buffalo offering is the most powerful, not only in Hindu Tantric traditions but also for several tribes of northeastern India and upper Myanmar, such as Nagas, Kukis, Garos, and Khasis.<sup>29</sup> The sacrifice of either a *gayal*, also called *mithan* (*bos frontalis*), or a *gaur* (*bos gaurus*)<sup>30</sup> to the goddess incarnates dark and dangerous powers related to the opposite of the divine and death imaginary (Biardeau and Malamoud 1976: 146–147)—a fact underlined by the well-known myth of Mahiṣāsura:

When a buffalo is offered to the goddess Bhairavī or to Bhairava, he should worship the sacrifice with this mantra: ‘Just as you are hostile to the horse and yet bear Cāṇḍikā, so too you will kill my enemies and bring me prosperity, O buffalo. You are mount of Yama, you are imperishable with an excellent form. You give life, wealth and fame—hail to you, O buffalo!’ (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 67.57–58, trans. Urban 2010: 63) Although slaughtering an animal is a taboo—because of the implicit pollution and danger associated with this ritual act—in Assamese Tantra, it is performed by Brahmanical classes probably because several powers lie in the sacrificial blood; in fact, ‘it is through offering sacrifices that devotee obtains liberation (from the bondage of the world), the heaven and a prince gets victory by conquering his enemies’ (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 67.5b–6a).

The offering of human blood and head to Kāmākhyā is the sacrifice *par excellence*:<sup>31</sup>

\[I\]f a human being is sacrificed following the rules laid down on that behalf the goddess remains pleased for the full one thousand years, and when three 

*[p. 110]* ^pg123

men are sacrificed for one hundred thousand years. With the human flesh the goddess Kāmākhyā and Bhairavī (Bhairava?) who assumes my shape are pleased for three hundred years. An oblation of blood of the sacrifice when purified with the relevant mantras always turns into ambrosia; the goddess Śivā partakes the head and flash of the sacrifice. Therefore an expert adept should offer the read heads of the sacrifices (besmeared with blood) to the goddess when she is worshipped; the flesh is to be offered as eatables *bhojya* and used in *homa* oblation to the fire. An expert adept should not offer raw flesh in worship, except the reddish head, because that becomes ambrosia.

(*Kālikāpurāṇa* 67.18–21, trans. Shastri 2008: 537) Hence, according to the Assamese Tantric tradition, the sacrificial victim’s head is the most powerful offering to goddesses at Kāmākhyā. However, nobody could perform a sacrifice of a human being or of an animal without the king’s permission (*Kālikāpurāṇa* 67.116–18).<sup>32</sup>

Blood spreading, the beheading of ritual victims and offering heads are ritual actions linked to northeastern India and upper Myanmar headhunting tribes, their funerary ceremonies, and the ‘theory of the importance of soul-matter as a fertilizer’ (Hutton 1928: 405–406). The tribal headhunting practices, however, were related not only to spirituality but also to ‘social prestige’, ‘heroism and manhood’ (Zou 2005: 80, 89):

\[H\]ead-taking was essential to marriage in so far that a buck who had taken no head, and could not wear the warriors’ dress at festivals, not only found it exceedingly difficult to get any girl with pretensions to good looks or to self-- respect to marry him, but was held up to ridicule by all the girls of his clan. (Hutton 1921: 165) The main belief subsumed within the head symbolism is that the head is the seat of the soul (Onians 1994: 94–98), an idea that connects the headhunting tribes with fertility cults and magic. In fact, the source of fertility was identified ‘to lie in the soul of the dead’ people. This aspect also reconnects to manhood because a man who was unable to come back to his home with an enemy head does not have a ‘surplus soul-matter from outside’, thus being ‘less likely to be fertile’ (Hutton 1928: 400–403).

The Tantric sacrifices of animals and human beings at Kāmākhyā-*pīṭha* preserve the local magic tradition related to fertilization. The goddess, who is fed through the ritual victims’ flesh and blood, rewards the human world through her menstrual blood that pervades the earth, which is vivified through her divine liquids (Caldwell 1999: 113). Therefore, menstruations are metonymically puzzled with sacrificial blood and death symbolism; in fact, on the symbolic level, the offering of blood intersects with the worship of ancestors and cults related with earth’s fertility. This link is reflected not only in common ritual performance but also in the obtainment of some magic powers, such as seeing into the past or 

*[p. 111]* ^pg124

communicating with dead people (see Borkataky Varma 2016: 77). Thus, the Kaula adept was transformed into a medium able to communicate with creatures of the underworld.

### 5.6 Conclusion

From the mediaeval period at Kāmākhyā-*pīṭha* emerges a close connection between the Tantric worship of the goddess and supernatural and magic powers, several of which are described as *siddhi*s. According to texts and rituals, these magic and supernatural powers are indissolubly linked to sexual fluids and human blood—both connecting the most extreme Tantric practices to the death imaginary of ancestral and fertility cults whose origin can be traced back to Śākta substrata.

At Kāmākhyā-*pīṭha*, it seems that the encounter of Brahmanism, in its larger meaning, and tribal traditions results in the crucial role of women as the agents able to manipulate prohibited substances and master magic acts. Sexual liquids, and more specifically menstrual blood, are related to the woman’s fundamental role in the preservation of the *kula*’s secret gnosis. With the rise of Kaulism, indeed, there was the affirmation of dreadful *yoginī*s not only as divine and dangerous attendants of the goddess but also as humanized deities who were able to preserve and transmit the gnosis’ secrecy of the *kula* through their sexual fluids, such as the obtainment of several *siddhi*s.

At Kāmākhyā, Kaulism reversed the Vedic concept of sacrifice—to worship Kāmākhyā and her *yoginī*s, human beings and wild animals were offered, which were slaughtered rather than suffocated. The victims’ blood, flesh, and head were considered the favourite substances to feed the goddesses, an ideology that originates from northeastern tribal cultures, in connection with death and fertility. More specifically, for northeastern tribespeople, the enemy’s decapitation was a brave act that allowed the performer to seize the enemy’s soul, which was believed to reside inside the head, in order to reinforce his manhood through the fertilizing power of the soul-matter.

In conclusion, the idea that magic and supernatural powers could be transmitted only over generations of those who belong to the *kula*, and that they could not be learned without an initiation, is integrated within this ideology of ritual subversion. It is through the subversion of social rules and the breaking of taboos that magic and supernatural powers are triggered, and are restricted to the Kaulas, who are the only ones that are endowed with the ability to manipulate dangerous and polluting substances without negative consequences.

According to Urban (2010: 62), while *gaur* is identified with *mithan*, actually *mithan* (or *mithun*) is another name for the *gayal* that is the domesticated species of buffalo. However, during my fieldwork, I was able to attend only a few buffalo sacrifices at Kāmākhyā, of what I suppose were *gayal*s. In many studies, these species of *bos* are called water buffalos, although the water buffalo belongs to *bubalus* taxon: *bubalus bubalis* is the domesticated species, while *bubalus arnee* is the wild one.

	31 Today, human sacrifice is completely substituted in South Asia by the animal sacrifice in left-hand Tantra traditions or by coconuts (that is a metonymy of head offerings) and other floral and vegetal offerings in right-hand Tantra.

	32 A series of prescriptions regarding the blood sacrifices are accounted in the *Kālikāpurāṇa*: see in particular 67.48b–68.
