## Concluding Reflection

 I 

have decided to not conclude this book with the typical summary, summarizing what has been achieved. The main theses and the unfolding of the project have been suggested in the introductions to the book’s different parts and need not be repeated. I would like, instead, to conclude with a ref lection, one may even call it a homily, based upon a hassidic homily, that allows me to frame where we have come to in another language. Rabbi Mordechai Yosef Leiner of Izbicza offers the following commentary in his *Mey Hashiloach* on the term “graven image” in his commentary to Exodus 34,17:

 Do not make for yourself a graven image. The graven image are principles. And this is what Scripture teaches—when you have explicit understanding of the heart, do not look to principles to act upon them, but through the understanding of your heart you will know how to act in every detail. 

 This teaching shows how idolatry can work within. Routines, principles, guidelines, opinions, and more can all become fixed in ways that make the stuff of religion no longer transparent to the divine will, to God’s presence. The antidote is a living relationship with God, who is known in the heart, and from there He provides guidance as to how one is to act in every detail. Divine direction replaces the fixity of structures that have the potential of becoming idolatrous. 

 The subject of idolatry can itself become the victim of an idolatrous attitude. Our concern with idolatry can lose interest in the living understanding that comes from God, through our understanding, intuition, and discernment, and become a matter of ruling, of stagnant attitudes. To see idolatry only as a concern with image worship or to use it as a yardstick for evaluating the other based on principles that leave no room for internal spiritual discernment is itself a movement of “idolatrizing idolatry.” We have lost much of the spiritual meaning of what Avoda Zara means because we have viewed it primarily, even exclusively, in terms of outward acts that themselves were instruments in processes of othering. In such othering, little room is left for the understanding of the heart. 

 The encounter with another religion is also one that requires an understanding of the heart, lest our approach to the other religion itself become a graven mental image. Understanding of the heart requires listening, learning, being open, and above all discerning God’s presence in a given situation. We need to shift the idolatrous attitudes in us as a condition for discovering God in the other. 

*[p. 206]* ^pg208

 Herzel Hefter has made us aware of the problem of idolatry within, as it relates to the Hindu concern with the dynamism of the image. The fear is fixity, the loss of dynamism, that is ever present to God’s will and to the dynamic ways in which He appears and surprises us. The fixity of our Torah views is but a counterpart of the image of the Divine the other makes, and whose fixity evokes in us the fear of idolatry. 

 One way of describing what I hope to have achieved in this book is not only to have removed, or lessened, or cast doubt, about the ascription of Avoda Zara to Hinduism. If Avoda Zara is as much an attitude as it is a position taken, I hope that the lengthy exercise in gaining multiple viewpoints and reframing our lead question time and again will allow the Jewish reader to open up the very category of Avoda Zara not only in conceptual terms but also in terms of how it serves as an approach to the other. Thus, our real gain in this project is not liberating Hinduism but liberating Avoda Zara from a narrow discourse. 

 Avoda Zara kills. This is true spiritually for the worship of another god that removes one from the source of true life. But it is also true for our internalizing the regard of Avoda Zara as we view the other, of making it our own perspective, of owning it so that we lose true regard, the regard of the heart, ultimately the regard of God for the other. To view the other primarily through the eyes of Avoda Zara is to make our own look deadly, cursed by the fixity of perspective that sees the Other as other. What we need is a different regard, one that removes the idolatry within the heart as a condition for engaging the other. Whether or not a formal pronouncement will be made one way or another does not alleviate the spiritual obligation of confronting idolatry not only outside but also within, and not only within our religion but also within our attitudes, our hearts. 

 The difference between fixity and dynamism is process. What is idolatrous is a block to continuing evolution, unfolding, to the continuing reach of God’s transformative touch. I therefore do not wish to conclude this book with any fixed conclusions, though there surely are some important ones. Rather, I prefer to highlight the nature of process and how this book facilitates the process of future students, by spelling out parameters and questions that will inform future study. Its most important conclusion is the invitation to further study and its most important contribution is the tools it provides for aiding an ongoing conversation. 

 We have moved from ruling to discernment, and with Rabbi Leiner we move from legal principles to the understanding of the heart, open to the Divine. Any encounter must be lived in this way. Hinduism makes it particularly easy for us to do so. If, as has been claimed, Hinduism makes God’s omnipresence a foundation for its spirituality, and if it features the quest for God as a primary conscious driver of the spiritual life, it provides a subject for consideration, encounter, and dialogue that forces us to remember God, even as we engage in halachic reasoning and to turn to the understanding of the heart, even as we seek to apply the principles of the old in novel configurations and situations. In important ways, the encounter with Hinduism may force us to be less idolatrous in our own view of reality.
