## On the Relationship between Individual and Society Today

*13 December 1957*

Ladies and gentlemen,

When I agreed to give the concluding talk in this series of lectures<sup>1</sup> I decided to offer the observations that I am about to deliver under the title of ‘Individual and Society Today’. Now this title, I have to admit right away, is rather too grand. I simply wanted it to express the general field in which the thoughts I wish to communicate find their appropriate place, but of course I certainly harboured no intention of providing a summary outline or ‘projection’, to use an already highly compromised contemporary expression.<sup>2</sup> In the first place it is quite evident that a two-hour talk would be utterly insufficient to discharge such a task, but I have to point out in any case that my own theoretical position already prevents me from believing in the possibility of providing such an outline, or such a total perspective, on the issue in question. And it will clearly emerge from what I am going to say to you – or at least that is my hope – precisely why I do not believe such a thing is even possible.<sup>3</sup> Thus I have decided instead simply to indicate certain key sensitive points with respect to the relationship between human beings and society, while eschewing any intention to provide a comprehensive systematic perspective on this question.

Now sensitive points in general, as you know, are points that can prove to be somewhat painful, and so I would just like to warn you that the things I am going to say, to the degree in which they touch on serious matters, have a certain tendency to hurt. I believe it is worth saying this right away, because I am well aware that a number 

*[p. 37]* ^pg44

of claims I shall be making here stand in sharp opposition to many of the established intellectual attitudes that characterize the contemporary climate of thought in Germany, and it is almost inevitable that these claims will provoke certain strong defensive reactions. I would simply ask you, as far as this is possible, to try and avoid such defensive reactions. That does not mean that I want you simply to swallow what I am saying, as if it were some piece of dogmatic wisdom, which it certainly is not. But I would ask you at least to consider these thoughts which you may find disconcerting, and which run counter to the overall tone of the prevailing intellectual atmosphere here, and to reflect on them rather than resorting to the automatic response that says, Well, that is just the talk of an ‘oversensitive intellectual’, or simply appealing to the judgement that this is all rather ‘exaggerated’. Now the charge of exaggeration is something I have already heard so often in my life that I am rather tempted to embrace it, and indeed to express the view that exaggeration belongs to the essence of truth in the sense that the smooth, unruffled and superficial appearance of conventional opinion is precisely what is to be rejected. Where truth does not seek to penetrate to the extremes, it certainly seems more agreeable and generates a more comfortable climate of consensus, but thereby also finds itself more or less consigned to sterility. I also want to say in advance that the views I shall present here are quite opposed to much of what is currently advanced in the context of German social science and in German philosophy of the most various stripes. Thus I would ask you not simply to assume dogmatically that the common views you are already familiar with are more likely to be true in comparison to my own, for in both of these fields there is actually no single established or confirmed theory or teaching. The truth is only to be found here through the power of thought and the power of the argument itself.

I also wish to point out in particular that the things I am going to say to you are very different from the views that Arnold Gehlen has probably already presented to you in a lecture that I was unfortunately unable to hear myself;<sup>4</sup> and unfortunately he is not here today either, for this might otherwise have led to a very fruitful discussion of the issues in question.<sup>5</sup> But I think I am familiar enough with his own theoretical position to be able to say something about it in this connection.

Finally, I would like to suggest, or ask, that you try and avoid a kind of fallacy that is very commonly encountered in these matters: namely the tendency, when someone touches on a sensitive wound (as my friend Franz Böhm undoubtedly did only yesterday),<sup>6</sup> to hold this person responsible for the wound which he has pointed out, 

*[p. 38]* ^pg45

instead of seeking the cause in the condition that is under consideration. The French Enlightenment thinker Helvétius once said that the truth never harmed anyone except the person who first expressed it,<sup>7</sup> and I hold this to be a general maxim that one should observe whenever critical ideas and critical theories are at issue.

Now, ladies and gentlemen, in order to introduce you to the particular climate of what it is that I want to explore with you here, I would like to draw your attention to a difference of tone and emphasis that is connected with one quite particular word, namely the word ‘Man’, *der Mensch* in the sense of human being. Towards the very end of the First World War, which undoubtedly quite a few of you, like myself, lived through when we were younger or in our adolescent years, this word ‘Man’ also played a very considerable role. I simply remind you, for example, of a once widely read book by Leonhard Frank with the title *Man is Good*,<sup>8</sup> or of the book by Ludwig Rubiner which was entitled *Man at the Centre*.<sup>9</sup> When you compare that sort of thing with everything being sent out into the world today under the name of ‘Man’, you will discover that a fundamental and, I would even say, crucial difference now prevails in this regard, even though one might initially imagine that man or the human being represents a kind of constant that evinces a certain stability in contrast to something as inwardly mobile and dynamic as society. Let me say straight away that I regard this idea – the idea of the invariance, of the stability, of the immutability of human nature in contrast to the mobile and dynamic character of society – as a fallacy, as a regression to habitual forms of thinking which philosophy and science alike, which I would not, however, simply oppose to each other in the way that Gehlen does, have long since overcome. Today you constantly encounter expressions such as ‘it is all ultimately about Man’. But when you look more closely, you soon find that it is less a question of man properly being at the centre of consideration and more of man as the centre of economic concerns. I think there is actually a book entitled *Man at the centre of Business*<sup>11</sup> – this is not something I have just invented on the spot, as you might be tempted to imagine. I think the difference of tone and emphasis alone is the decisive thing here.

In the period towards the end of the First World War, and in the very early years of the Weimar Republic, when I would say that society still showed a relatively open character, when one had the feeling that everything was still possible, the expression ‘Man’ effectively suggested, in contrast to the war, to all that had been perpetrated, to all petrified relations and conventions, that direct human relations might now be re-established in the light of freedom.

*[p. 39]* ^pg46

Today, however, all this talk of man generally just relates to the domain of social welfare. In other words, all of this talk today springs from what I have described as the ‘administered world’,<sup>12</sup> from the fact that individual human beings, individual subjects, through the preponderant power of the social and institutional relations which confront them, are no longer human beings properly speaking, are no longer subjects properly speaking. And when people talk about ‘Man’ or ‘the human being’ today, this basically means, in the context of such dehumanization or reification, that we need to be securely integrated and accommodated – something which we as official employees experience, myself included, and indeed all of us experience directly in the functions that we perform on a daily basis. The English expression ‘human relations’, and the idea of promoting ‘human relations’, rather drastically indicates, I think, the distinction I have been talking about. If you compare the ideas of Leonhard Frank or those of Rubiner with the idea of promoting ‘human relations’, you will immediately recognize the irony that appears here, which springs precisely from the fact that the idea of developing the relations between human beings institutionally or promoting them ‘from above’ already negates the demand that man or the human being as such is at the centre or should lie at the centre, along with the ideological attraction that was also actually exercised by all this talk of ‘Man’ being the crucial question at issue.<sup>13</sup>

Now I don’t want to overshoot the mark here. I have certainly said that, in a sense, I embrace the charge of exaggeration. All the same, I do not wish to endorse this idea at the cost of abandoning good sense. For the claim that man or the human being really lies at the centre, or that this is what ultimately matters, already involves of course a truth moment of its own. And we have to hang on to this truth moment if we wish to understand the problematic that actually concerns us here. In other words, the petrified relations that we all experience when we recognize the difference between the functions and the compulsion of the objective procedures that we have to apply and the human impulses, these relations didn’t just drop from heaven; for they are all ‘man-made’, as they would say in the English-speaking world, they have arisen out of relationships between human beings, and even the most hardened of institutionalists today would no longer attempt – as people at the time of Filmer, John Locke’s great antagonist,<sup>14</sup> liked to do – to trace these relationships directly back to a divine and rightful order of things; but the institutionalists of today, such as Gehlen, do seek to derive such institutions from the character and needs of the human being as such.<sup>15</sup> Now we might say that it all does actually come down to man, insofar as the task 

*[p. 40]* ^pg47

is to expose the human roots, the roots in the social process, that all these relationships have; and, further, that it all comes down to human beings themselves in that it should be the task of each and every one of us, if we are responsible, to work towards establishing genuinely human relations; that is, to work towards removing this aspect of heteronomy, where we are little more than functionaries, and even, when we occupy so-called leading positions in society, and are capable of achieving very little, towards intervening to assist and improve the life of those in need and seeking precisely to change that life.

But this thesis that everything comes down to the human being cannot be maintained in a merely immediate sense. The science of man or, as I would much prefer to put this in my own language, the science of society has to recognize that, in the world in which we live, that is, in the historical and infinitely complex world in which we live, the institutions themselves do indeed enjoy primacy. We fall victim to ideology – ‘ideology’ here understood in the precise sense of obfuscation – when we proceed as if these relations and the ways in which human beings specifically relate to one another can be explained directly or immediately in terms of human beings as they are today. For human beings today are largely functions of institutional reality, of the way the world is currently arranged, rather than the other way around, which is precisely why anyone who is serious about the concept of man must not start by saying something such as: ‘Man is always basically the same and the task is to derive or determine what is good from the essence of man.’ Rather, we must attempt to change or influence those moments or aspects of institutional reality which now effectively enjoy primacy over human beings themselves.

Now something else also needs to be said here. There is absolutely no concept, including the concept of man, that could be treated as something existing simply ‘in itself’ independently of the context of praxis in which it stands, of the constellation of factors within which it presents itself; for even the truest theory – including the claim, certainly true *in abstracto*, which is so characteristic of Kantian ethics, namely that what counts is the human being as such, in other words, that the human being should always be regarded as an end rather than as a means<sup>16</sup> – even this claim can itself become a lie and an ideology if we proceed as if it immediately comes down today to human beings as such, to the promotion of human relations in their immediacy, when in fact such immediacy is already condemned to impotence through the prevailing relations between human beings. I would be inclined to say that a concept that seems as general as the concept of man, or that of the essential character of man, is 

*[p. 41]* ^pg48

particularly susceptible to being misused for ideological purposes \[since all concrete historical determinations regarding the essence of man have effectively dropped out of the concept in question\].<sup>17</sup> The untruth here lies in the way we simply appeal in such cases directly to ‘Man’, as if the relations under investigation could be explained immediately by reference to the intrinsic character of the human being as such, when in truth the human beings in question are dependent on the machinery of social life, so that nothing can be directly or immediately derived from their anthropological, psychological or ontological character in a way that would really explain our present condition or the violence which this condition exercises upon us. I would almost say that the concept of man in its abstractness, which God knows possesses no particular emotional colour anyway, nonetheless readily lends itself to ideology today since it functions in our contemporary consciousness as a kind of comfort blanket. In other words, as soon as we hear sentiments such as: ‘Well, in the end, we are all human beings, and that’s what matters after all’, we tend to feel comforted and forget that what matters is not the human being as such, but the concrete relations of actual living human beings and the relationship of human beings to the prevailing institutions and arrangements, to existing property relations and to objective social reality.

You can test this for yourselves. If you express among ordinary honest people even the slightest doubt about whether the human being really does matter, you will soon encounter a certain hostility, almost as if you were the inhuman one for actually doubting this; just as it is characteristic of the present age that those who defend the cause of humanity acquire a reputation for inhumanity precisely because they refuse such heart-warming and transfiguring illusions, while those who constantly talk about the human being all too easily end up transfiguring or justifying inhuman relations precisely in the name of ‘Man’. But I shall say a little more about this later.

When people talk about man as such, we cannot really ignore differences of power and powerlessness or differences that belong to a particular social order. Now today we already have this jargon of ‘the human being’, an entire language that is derived from that evacuated concept of ‘Man’ and that I have termed the ‘jargon of authenticity’.<sup>18</sup> You all know this jargon, and I can perhaps count on your feelings of mistrust whenever it comes up. Expressions such as ‘authentic encounter’ or ‘entering into dialogue’, and all such things, typically belong here, and the strangely hollow character of all these concepts springs from the way they suggest that something could essentially be changed through some immediate coming together of human beings, 

*[p. 42]* ^pg49

whereas even when human beings do come together with one another they are still caught up in the context of the whole. A particularly characteristic expression of this approach is the theory of the I–Thou relation as developed by Martin Buber.<sup>19</sup> The absurdity of this theory is most easily revealed from the fact that people usually address one another with more formal pronouns, such as *Sie* in Germany, or with the corresponding courtesy form in France, and use the more personal forms only in special situations; and from the fact that, if people do act as if they really were in an I–Thou relationship to each other, they would be feigning a certain situation among themselves that would instantly be exposed as questionable once it was put to the test, for in the very moment when human beings who are supposed to encounter each other as I and Thou enter into ‘genuine dialogue’ they will still address each other, if they have been properly brought up, in a more formal way.

Now however matters stand in this regard, you may still believe in all of this that I have merely described and attacked a vaguely characterized intellectual climate that is simply a concern of the journalistic sphere. And you will rightly expect in the context of these lectures that I go beyond such general considerations. And this actually brings me to a crucial point, since I believe that in terms of the most recent developments, and specifically of recent developments in the social sciences, we can see that these things that I have described in a preliminary fashion in the context of the general cultural climate are also true for the sphere of the sciences themselves. What I want to point out, above all, is something that is very characteristic of what today is called ‘the science of man’.<sup>20</sup> I am talking about its tendency to adopt a highly critical attitude to psychology, a tendency that is particularly marked in Germany. It is as if the aura, the nimbus that surrounds the essential being of ‘Man’, as something somehow established and thus confirmed from above, is now being played off against the experience of actual or empirical individual human beings, so that from this perspective the experience of concrete individual human beings is effectively demeaned, as if this experience were intrinsically destructive, or God knows what else; in other words, it is suggested that such individual experience is already tantamount to an offence against the dignity of the human being (especially when the instinctual or drive-structures of human beings are specifically acknowledged and explored). Thus the concept of man is exploited, on the one hand, to allow us to abstract to a large extent from the instinctual character of the human being so that the concrete and specific motivations of human beings fade from sight in favour of a doctrine of human invariance; on the other hand, 

*[p. 43]* ^pg50

this kind of science of man also tends to hypostasize the so-called element of ‘evil’ in human beings – in other words, to proceed as if specific negative aspects and features inevitably and essentially inhere in man as such. It is almost as if one were trying today to fashion some kind of ideology out of the notion of original sin, as if one were to rejoice, if I can put it this way, over the sinful essence of man; for once we have revealed the originally evil nature of man in anthropological terms, we can then proceed to show why we also need to curb freedom and exert pressure to keep this evil element in human nature under control. Just let me add here, *en passant*, that in many respects, including the aspect we have just been talking about, the current so-called science of man resembles a spectral repetition of certain ideas that were all very common in the early period of bourgeois culture; and now that bourgeois society to a large extent has come to despair of its own conception of freedom, we find that these ideas are disinterred and begin to lead a spurious ghostly life.<sup>21</sup> In this whole sphere one could speak of a kind of resurrection, albeit one that exhibits all the features of what is merely secondary and attenuated.

But I don’t wish to go too quickly here either, so I want to point out there is also a truth moment to the anti-psychological direction of contemporary anthropological thought, one that we should definitely not lose sight of. For the claim that what actually matters far more than the psychological life of the individual is the overarching objective relations of society when it comes to explaining fundamental modes of human behaviour. The realm in which, as psychological subjects, as particular individuals, we act is relatively limited and belongs solely to the private sphere. We might almost say that we only really react psychologically to the extent that we are consumers, while wherever we are involved in the processes of production we must ignore what we call our own psychology and behave in a rational way, or at least in accordance with objective ends and demands. Nonetheless, it seems to me that it would be an utterly fateful error to try and turn this psychological concept of man into something static and absolute, and thus simply oppose it to that of society.

I find myself in the rather awkward position today, given the current state of the discussion about these matters, of having to mount a challenge, for a second time, in a fight that was already fought out long ago. And it is surely one of the most telling symptoms of our age that decisive achievements dating back to the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, from Helvétius, whom I already mentioned, and D’Holbach<sup>22</sup> through to Hegel and Marx, have been 

*[p. 44]* ^pg51

effectively forgotten, so that we now happily carry on talking in an essentially dogmatic fashion as if, to put it crudely, Kant had never existed. This is connected in turn with that general crisis of education and culture that you all certainly know about, and also with the loss of the sense of historical continuity, something which my colleague Hermann Heimpel in Göttingen has rightly and emphatically insisted upon.<sup>23</sup> Perhaps I might say that these elements, namely the decline of education and culture and that of historical consciousness, are not themselves just mere cultural facts, as it were, but are closely bound up with a regression of consciousness as such, one that increasingly deprives this consciousness of the capacity for critical thought and self-reflection and increasingly drives it into a posture of simple accommodation. But here I would draw your attention expressly to the fact that the controversy over the nature of man that I have been discussing here was one that was already being fought out over a hundred years ago, although there is actually almost no contemporary awareness of this. I may surely be permitted, therefore, in the context of this particular series of lectures, to say something more about this question here.

In this connection I would just like to read you a few passages from Marx’s early writings, in particular from the so-called ‘Fragments on Economic and Philosophical Questions’<sup>24</sup> and from *The German Ideology*.<sup>25</sup> I should perhaps add that the situation regarding Marx today is really very curious. On the one hand, as soon as the name of Marx is mentioned, and passages from his work are even quoted, one is immediately accused of wanting to endorse the oriental despots who have established themselves in the Eastern Zone and the Ost bloc as a whole, and developed a gruesome despotism by appealing to Marx. I would emphatically reject this kind of accusation from the start, and would also question the right of such people to claim the legacy of Marx for themselves as if it were their private property. What they have made of Marx is an appalling distortion and has almost nothing to do with the actual texts as we have them. In this regard I can only encourage you to look at the texts themselves and avoid conflating the balderdash that is presented over there as ‘dialectical materialism’ with what Marx was really talking about. On the other hand, in the context of the social sciences, and particularly in the most important of the social sciences today, one constantly faces the danger, when we speak of Marx – and indeed similarly in the case of Freud – that we simply get the response: ‘Yes, but that is all quite obsolete; these are all just things from the nineteenth century’, or we are told, I don’t know, that this is all too ‘naturalistic-mechanistic’, or whatever the current insults may be, and that ‘we now employ 

*[p. 45]* ^pg52

the idea of “wholeness” and are much more modern, and have gone beyond all these old-fashioned notions.’ Now it is not my task, ladies and gentlemen, to offer you any philosophical lectures here, and I would not want to try your patience with anything of the sort, but I would at least encourage you to maintain a highly sceptical attitude in the face of such claims. Whenever we are assured today in relation to any cultural and intellectual phenomenon – and in a very different sphere this is also true of Ibsen,<sup>26</sup> for example – that it is entirely out of date and no longer holds any interest for modern people like us, whenever we hear this kind of essentially automatic response, you can be sure that there is in reality some sensitive point or wound here, something that has not in truth been really dealt with, and that the mere reference to the passing of time, which in itself is something quite abstract and cannot simply be related to the content of a theory without more ado, is supposed to relieve us of the need for any further reflection on the matter in question.

Now I would just like to quote you a few passages from Marx which clearly show that this controversy we have been talking about was already being fought out a century ago, indeed well over a century ago. I must simply mention in advance that the materialist philosopher Feuerbach had attempted, over against Hegel, to limit the concept of ‘spirit’, which for Hegel was ultimately synonymous with the totality of being, and basically replace it with the concept of ‘Man’.<sup>27</sup> And Marx – and this is probably something that many of you do not know and may well find very interesting – responded to this, we might say, by returning in a sense to Hegel. In other words, in contrast to the materialist Feuerbach, we find that Marx established a certain connection to the tradition of German idealism precisely by showing that this concept of man, of the human being, should not itself be absolutized, should not be taken up in isolation, and arguing instead that this concept of man must itself be thought through as something socially produced, as something intrinsically dynamic in character.

I shall just read you a couple of relevant passages from Marx which may rather surprise you. Thus he says, and I quote from the Landshut edition here:

So the *social character* is the general character of the whole movement; *just as* society itself produces *man* as *man*, so it is *produced* by him. Activity and consumption, both in their content and in their *mode of existence*, are *social* activity and *social* consumption. The *human* essence of nature exists only for *social* man; for only here does nature exist for him as a *bond* with other *men*, as his existence for others and 

*[p. 46]* ^pg53

their existence for him, as the vital element of human reality; only here does it exist as the *basis* of his own *human* existence. Only here has his *natural* existence become his *human* existence and nature become man for him. S*ociety* is therefore the perfected unity in essence of man with nature, the true resurrection of nature.<sup>28</sup> Or in another passage we read: ‘It is above all necessary to avoid once more establishing “society” as an abstraction over against the individual. The individual *is* the *social being*.’ This is an allusion to Aristotle’s doctrine that man is by nature a *zōon politikon*, an essentially social being.<sup>30</sup> ‘His vital expression – even when it does not appear in the direct form of a *communal* expression, conceived in association with other men – is therefore an expression and confirmation of *social life*.’<sup>31</sup> And in another passage, one which very clearly expresses the difference I have been specifically emphasizing in relation to modern anthropology, Marx writes as follows: ‘For not only the five senses, but also the so-called spiritual senses, the practical senses (will, love, etc.), in a word, the *human* sense, the humanity of the senses – all these come into being through the existence of *their* objects, through *humanized* nature. The *cultivation* of the five senses is the work of all previous history.’<sup>32</sup> Or consider the following remark: ‘Just as everything natural must *come to be*, so too *man* has an act through which he comes to be, *history*, but he is conscious of this history, and as a conscious act of origination is also a self-sublating act.’<sup>33</sup>

Now I think that these quotations are sufficient for now. Perhaps I might just add that the most important demand that Marx raises in these early writings is precisely that we have to move beyond the abstraction of ‘Man’,<sup>34</sup> that we have to attend to the concrete human being, and thus try and understand the concrete human being in the process of interaction with the society through which the human being is produced and which the human being produces in turn.

Thus you can already roughly see the extent to which – since I believe these thoughts already actually imply a telling critique of contemporary theories of anthropological invariance – the extent to which theories of kind are untenable. Perhaps I can just summarize some of the most important points so that we can fully grasp and appreciate the implications of the view I have been putting forward for you here today.

The first thing is that it essentially undermines the idea of invariance itself, the idea of something eternal or permanent about the human being. But I also want to say this: I do not wish to be misunderstood here, as if I were somehow claiming that there is no persisting 

*[p. 47]* ^pg54

or invariant aspect to the human being at all. That is far from my intention and, naturally, would be an absurd thing to claim. I hope that no one believes I am capable of such foolishness. For of course human beings, throughout the course of history as we know it, are certainly characterized by a range of things – such as the number of vertebrae we have, the constitution of the human body itself, or that peculiar prematurity at birth that people are so keen to invoke today – all those things which the anthropologist Portmann has especially emphasized<sup>35</sup> and on the impressive discovery of which anthropology today essentially relies. However, it is not really a question of denying these so-called invariants but, rather, of understanding how they assume a completely different function in accordance with the specific constellations, with social reality as a whole, within which they occur. It is all very well to talk about the ‘homeless’ character of the human being, of the extremely underdeveloped nature of the human embryo, when we consider the embryo *in abstracto*, in the medical setting in which it may come into the world; but what makes the difference between life and death is whether this embryo is actually born in a desert, and whether it needs goodness knows what other contingent things in order to survive, or whether it is already born under conditions where this homeless character actually really appears as a possibility only at the extreme margins, while at least in our own society today the newborn fortunately finds a home soon enough. And it is much the same with all these things; so I am not trying to deny these invariant features but simply insisting that they assume a quite different value or significance depending on the point at which they appear in history. And I would also add that when we abstract from this and say things such as: ‘Yes, but it still really comes down to the changeless aspect here, for everything else is purely accidental, a mere addition to the essential as it were,’ we have already deceived ourselves precisely about what is essential, namely the relation between the human being and society. When we concede, as modern philosophical anthropology does, that man is a *zōon politikon*, or when we strongly emphasize this fact, this itself already essentially implies that we cannot simply content ourselves with acknowledging this *in abstracto*. We need to pursue and explore the social character of the human being precisely by determining the relations that obtain between the human being and society at every level. In other words, we must recognize that we cannot simply focus on the individual as such, detached from society, for the individual is determined right down to its innermost composition through society, and further that it is actually much harder to determine what is truly individuated, that which differs from the merely social, than it is to determine the opposite.

*[p. 48]* ^pg55

It follows from these reflections on society that there can be no question of considering the human being directly in any immediate sense precisely because all relations between human beings, and this probably from a very early stage of human history, are socially mediated, whereas the so-called bare or immediate reality of what human beings are in themselves, or of how individual human beings relate or behave to one another, is a marginal phenomenon in relation to this essential social existence. The pathos that springs from this abstract idea of man as such is little more, I would suggest, than an echo of theology, of the doctrine that God created man in his own image, but loses its justification once we no longer have the courage to say that this doctrine is what ultimately lies behind it. Modern philosophical anthropology would be especially loath to admit this since it wants to regard every human being as a purely natural being, even though it lends to ‘Man’ that transcendent pathos which only belongs in the realm of theology.

Let me just say here, before we take a break, that there is a further deceptive element to be considered that is also essentially connected with this pathos, namely the notion of ‘inwardness’. In all the views we have been discussing, it appears as if the human being must essentially be understood from within, from the innermost nature or character of ‘Man’. But we know today that the category of ‘inwardness’ itself, as we encounter it especially in Protestantism and in its secularized form in the German philosophical tradition, is something that has emerged historically. For if you have even a little knowledge of the ancient world you will already be aware that it had no such concept of inwardness at all; and you will realize that any attempt to derive the essence of the human being from the idea of pure inwardness is entirely deceptive precisely because the human being ‘interiorizes’ itself and becomes an individual, becomes a subject, in the emphatic sense only by determining itself in relation to other human beings – in other words, in relation to society; or, as Hegel puts it, only by othering or ‘externalizing’ itself<sup>36</sup> and thereby producing itself as a singular individual being for the first time. And this remarkable interrelationship between inwardness and society is not adequately grasped or even recognized by the all too ‘inward’ conceptions of man which haunt contemporary notions such as that of the ‘image of man’.<sup>37</sup> I think we have reached the halfway point in our discussion and need to take a short break. When we resume, I shall try and clarify the things I have been talking to you about with reference to a series of more concrete points, and I may then be able to provide a critical interpretation of certain exemplary theses of philosophical anthropology.

*[p. 49]* ^pg56

\[Pause\] Ladies and gentlemen, I would now like to show you in a slightly more concrete way that the appeal to the fundamental and invariant character of man, as distinct from the concrete social determinations of human existence, is associated with quite specific social views and attitudes, and indeed with oppressive and repressive ones. I shall lay this out for you and then show how far the category on which the concept of invariance principally depends, namely the category of the individual, is in fact socially mediated, and how far the basic theme of this lecture, namely the individual and society, itself involves a reciprocal social relationship. And then, if there is time, I shall address a couple of particularly relevant concrete aspects of the issue, such as the question of the family today, or the question concerning the organization of society or our relationship to social institutions. All this will inevitably be somewhat fragmentary in character, but I would ask you to bear in mind that all I can do here is to develop a certain model of thinking for you, one that can help to clarify these questions in terms of the view I am presenting; I certainly cannot offer you something resembling a so-called system, and that not least because I am extremely sceptical with regard to the concept of system itself.

In the following remarks I refer to Arnold Gehlen’s book *Man* and cite from the fourth edition, which came out in 1950. And you will be able to see from this how the things we have been talking about really have exercised a significant influence on contemporary anthropology in its most recent form. Right at the beginning of the book we read the following: ‘Just as anatomy is a general science of the structure of the human body, so must a comprehensive theory of man be possible. Since we are never in doubt as to whether or not a being is a man and since, furthermore, man does actually constitute a true species, we are justified in our expectation that a general anthropology should have a clear, well-defined subject.’<sup>38</sup> Now I would say that this claim involves the same mistake as many such seemingly obvious statements inasmuch as its very universality serves to conceal the real problem; that is, we do not actually need to deny the fact that human beings constitute a particular species and that human beings are characterized by certain shared features, but this does not allow us to conclude that we have learnt something truly essential simply by recourse to these universal features. For, in the various contexts and constellations in which human beings live, these aspects or ‘moments’ may signify quite different things, and if we assume some such universal character on the part of human beings, one 

*[p. 50]* ^pg57

that ignores or abstracts from history and society as their true and inner defining characteristic, it soon becomes possible, without even realizing it, to ‘smuggle in’, as Kant would say,<sup>39</sup> all sorts of criteria that are supposed to apply to the human being in itself, things which in reality certainly cannot be ascribed to human beings as such.

Gehlen claims that ‘man represents a quite unique and unprecedented project on the part of nature.’<sup>40</sup> Now it is hardly coincidental that this language is vaguely reminiscent of Heideggerian terminology, in which the concept of ‘project’ \[*Entwurf*\] plays an important role.<sup>41</sup> And this language is entirely characteristic of Gehlen. On the one hand we are presented with a naturalistic conception of the human being – that is, one that ignores the possibility of any theological notion of creation. And this is decisive for the content of Gehlen’s doctrine, since the basically negative conception of man that he defends specifically springs from the fact that any thought of transcendence in relation to man has already been rejected. In spite of this, a certain ‘project’ is still ascribed to nature itself – though a nature that we can hardly regard as anything but the kind of blind force or mere urge that we find in Schopenhauer<sup>42</sup> or the later Scheler.<sup>43</sup> In other words, what is here ascribed to nature, as it were, is an idea of man that is apparently defined once and for all and before which we must somehow bow down, something invariant, even though the concept of project or projection in its very meaning presupposes someone who projects, a subject, or at least something minded in character. In other words, this is an attempt to ontologize nature itself, if I may use philosophical language here, to interpret nature itself as if it somehow harboured certain pure ideas to which we must basically submit – an approach which already excludes the possibility that we could ever change these structures that are supposedly given and defined by nature. Here you can see the inconsistent and fractured character of such thinking, as well as the ideological function that it is almost predisposed to assume. For if nature does indeed reveal such a ‘project’, even though we have no idea of who is projecting anything here, there is nothing left for us to do but to obey this project of nature, namely to become what we already are anyway – a demand, by the way, which almost all so-called existential philosophy appears to endorse today, a demand for the mere repetition, or an encouragement to such repetition, of what we are anyway, at the expense of the thought of changing the relations in which we live and thereby also changing ourselves.

Now you can pursue this even further if you consider the following quotation, for example: ‘this incomplete state of “unreadiness” belongs to the physical conditions of man, to his very nature, and in 

*[p. 51]* ^pg58

this regard he is a being that requires training or *discipline* \[*Zucht*\]: raising, self-discipline, training, this entire process of acquiring and maintaining certain forms of behaviour belongs to the very conditions of existence of a being that is not already fixed or determinate from the start.’<sup>44</sup> Here you can almost tangibly grasp that inner connection of issues that I particularly wanted to draw to your attention here. Gehlen is alluding to the theory, which enjoys such popularity today, that the human being is intrinsically incomplete and under-prepared for survival, that the human neonate is incapable of maintaining its life in the way in which some other small creature that has been cast into the world is more able to do from a relatively early stage of life. But it is really astonishing to behold just how much has been derived from this really rather modest biological insight, and how much work it has been required to do, and I would like to encourage a comparable astonishment on your part too: one might initially think that what these small needy human animals that come onto the world really need, on account of their unpreparedness or dependency, is precisely love and protection. At least I have to confess that this rather sentimental view would be my first reaction to the thesis in question. But Herr Gehlen seems to think otherwise, since for him the idea that this particular being comes into the world in a fundamentally unprepared and underequipped manner instantly provokes associations with the concept of training or ‘discipline’ \[*Zucht*\]. In other words, ‘if you love your children, you have to discipline them’, and the thought here clearly applies to very young children. But I am inclined to think that the concept of ‘discipline’ is being misused in a double sense here: on the one hand to suggest that the establishment or encouragement of certain natural qualities, of what were once described as ‘racial qualities’, should be regarded as a proper task for humanity, although in reality this idea belongs to the crudest form of naturalism and is actually something inhuman. Gehlen does in fact also speak of ‘breeding’ or ‘raising’ \[*Züchtigung*\], but then says that the intrinsic debility of man makes it imperative to raise and shape human beings, to discipline them, in other words, rather than respect their freedom. We could also put this by saying that this kind of biological anthropology, since it establishes that we are not free beings when we first come into the world, actually fails to draw the conclusion that we should become free beings through education, through the way we are brought up, through the way we shape and organize our life. For the naturalist position once again finds expression here in the idea that, since we are originally unfree, this unfreedom actually needs to be strengthened and repeated, that in a sense we have to bow before the power of institutions, even if 

*[p. 52]* ^pg59

we cannot understand what they mean and when they really work against our own interests. All this is somehow derived from some original and allegedly invariant human predicament. You can clearly see here that the apparent neutrality of the original project that the human being is supposed to represent, a neutrality achieved only by abstracting from society, directly encourages us to derive and justify quite specific social forms, which are themselves transitory, along with all those heteronomous forms of discipline and obedience that oppress us and work against us. And behind all this there lies the extremely widespread intellectual habit that I would also particularly like to warn you against in this connection, namely the belief that what once or originally existed is therefore something unchangeable.

The anthropological current of thought that now regards itself as so modern – which I would fundamentally refuse to characterize in this way – has in this respect emphatically fallen back behind the insight of Hegel<sup>45</sup> in particular, but also that of Nietzsche<sup>46</sup> – that what has subsequently emerged can also be something original – in other words, that what has come forth goes beyond what originally was in the beginning and what has always been; we should stop trying to swear by the simply given. For reason and freedom themselves are things which have come to be, although they also somehow arise from our engagement with nature but, in turn, essentially go beyond the mere state of nature through the process of reflection.

Now I just want to read you a couple of other passages which clearly show how incoherent this whole construction basically is. For it essentially attempts to justify the order of what actually lies beyond nature, that is, an order based on reason or one based on freedom, by appeal to mere nature, an approach which contradicts the very meaning of that order. Thus in a much later passage of the book, one that is very reminiscent of Hobbes,<sup>47</sup> we read this: ‘The claim presented at the beginning of this book, that man is a *creature of discipline*, is thus confirmed here. The chronic task intrinsic to human existence, the task that is posed anew in every generation, namely the task of forming or channelling of instinctual life’ – i.e., the renunciation of the instincts, to put this in straightforward language – ‘is necessarily discharged through education and upbringing, and then through human self-discipline in ever new ways under ever new conditions. This process of formation is epitomized by what we call *morality*, which is itself, as we have shown, a biological necessity which is quite unique to human beings.’<sup>48</sup> In other words, to translate this more simply once again, the fact of such renunciation, the fact that we cannot fulfil our natural needs in the world in which we live, is not taken to imply that the world should be organized in such a 

*[p. 53]* ^pg60

way that this renunciation is no longer required – and in the world in which we live today, considering the current state of the means of production, this possibility can certainly be envisaged. However, the conclusion that Gehlen draws from this socially established condition of renunciation, which expresses nothing but the degree of social pressure that is exerted upon us, is that, precisely because human beings receive too little from the hand of nature, they should also receive too little for all time to come as well, and that they must take the requisite self-discipline and self-internalization upon themselves and thereby renounce the fulfilment of their own existence. You can immediately see here how such an anthropological doctrine of invariance can easily be combined with reactionary social ideas, especially with the idea of the unavoidable necessity for repression and containment.

In the light of these few suggestions and observations I think I do not need to take the critical analysis much further here. I had actually intended to say a lot more about all this, but I rather overestimated, as one does, the time available to me. So I can only encourage you to pursue the thoughts I have presented here and think them through for yourselves in your own way. Then you will clearly recognize what is actually going on with this contemporary talk of ‘Man’, namely that is basically attempting to persuade us – on the basis of the intrinsic deficiency of our original nature as human beings – that we should make our peace with the deficiency of the relations in which we live. And this tendency in modern social science to turn the given into the norm, precisely because we lack the power to think beyond it, and effectively to repeat the given within ourselves, this seems to be appropriate target of critique and certainly not something to be positively accepted. And this, I would add, is the very point where I also differ, decisively and methodologically, from Herr Gehlen, the point where I claim that such critique falls to philosophy, to the self-reflection of science, and that in this regard the mere pursuit and acquisition of scientific knowledge is not a higher stage of Enlightenment that takes us beyond philosophy but something that actually falls back behind philosophy.<sup>49</sup>

Now I did promise you to explicate these things more closely with reference to the concept of ‘the individual’. The singular biological being still falls short of what we mean by the individual, for the concept of the individual is a concept of reflection. What I mean by this is that the human being does not become an individual just by being born. It is something that comes about when this singular being, through its own consciousness, knows that it is something distinct from the amorphous species to which it belongs, something 

*[p. 54]* ^pg61

that posits itself in contrast to the species, and it is only in becoming conscious of this contrast, by maintaining and sustaining it, that it first becomes a conscious social being. The idea of a rational society thus intrinsically presupposes this process of individuation, something for which there is no room whatsoever in a philosophy such as Gehlen’s. This concept of the individual, as I pointed out before, is something that has emerged historically, and I would say – although one could raise certain objections in this regard, since it involves a rather complex argument that I would certainly not want to oversimplify here – that this is a concept of relatively recent date. In other words, I do not wish to go into the question how far such a category of individuality was already present in classical thought. There is much to be said both for and against this thesis. Perhaps I will be able to say a little more about this later. In any case, what we generally understand by the word ‘individual’ is hardly older than the end of the medieval period or the beginning of the Renaissance. Thus there is a specific sense in which the first individuals we know of are Petrarch or Montaigne, or the figure of Hamlet, and I would add that the concept of the individual also stands in a very specific relationship to death: the idea of the absolute value, of the substantial character of the singular human being in the West only really emerged at the moment when the belief in an eternal life, a belief that relativized and positively transcended this concept, had begun to fade. In a certain sense the ‘individual’ actually presupposes something like Hamlet’s ‘The rest is silence’<sup>50</sup> as its necessary condition. But I do not want to continue this any further now. But at least we can say that this concept of the individual – and this is certainly not a new idea – is essentially bound up with the beginnings of bourgeois society as a society based on exchange in which the individual agent, through acts of exchange that extend over a whole lifetime, makes his behaviour commensurable with that of others and, through this comparable process of exchange, also secures his own role and status and establishes his position in his own eyes. Now I believe it is possible to clarify this difficult idea, which I can present here only in a dogmatic fashion for lack of time, if you just recall how the qualities we have become accustomed to describing as qualities of the individual are closely connected with specific virtues, indeed with what we used to call bourgeois virtues, and this will help you to appreciate something of the historical character of the category of individuality itself. And then we have the concept of autonomy, of deciding for oneself, which of course presupposes that human beings are no longer serfs, mere dependent functionaries of their feudal lords, but are now, in the context of a free labour market, capable in principle of shaping 

*[p. 55]* ^pg62

their fate through their own behaviour and course of action. We can say that there is really only such a thing as individuality to the extent that this category of individuality is socially respected, that is to say, to the extent that the individual also maintains himself as the adequate form in which the human being – namely this singular biological being – can genuinely live in society. You can also think of the concept of personal identity in this connection.

Now if you have spent any considerable time in America, as I have done, and have had the opportunity for frequent contact with black people in menial roles of one sort or another – in other words, with human beings who have not internalized the form and structure of bourgeois society as deeply as most of us have done – you will notice that their identity is not so rigid, that they do not always have the same consciousness of temporal continuity in their lives, for example; if they have earned a few dollars, they thank God for it and spend them right away, telling themselves that they will still manage to get by somehow – whereas the sense of self-control through time is more connected with the acquisition or accumulation of capital, with caution in money matters, with planning for the future, with all these concepts that are associated with bourgeois society in particular. We find something similar with regard to what we have become accustomed to describing as firmness of ‘character’, which also presupposes that we can also resist society in a certain sense, can pursue our own course in an autonomous way and exercise some control over our lives and the world. The capacity to think ahead and anticipate things also belongs here, and the sense of independence, along with what we call ‘civil courage’. I believe that if you reflect on the concept of the individual in the emphatic sense you will all see that these aspects that I have just listed in a rather rhapsodic fashion belong together in this context and are things that we cannot simply abstract from. And it will be equally clear to you that we are not dealing with invariant factors or unchanging fundamental properties of the human being as such. On the contrary, we are talking about specific features and characteristics that have been produced through very concrete social relations. This has extremely far-reaching implications and seems to me to be quite decisive for how we think about the relationship between individual and society today. For just as these categories in terms of which the individual has been formed have emerged historically, so today we are on the point of losing sight of these qualities precisely because they are no longer respected or acknowledged by society. Thus there is in fact something like a crisis of the individual, and there can only be a crisis of the individual because this concept, as I pointed out in very general terms right at the beginning, is itself 

*[p. 56]* ^pg63

socially mediated and therefore also undergoes decisive modifications along with the substantive character of society itself.

Today, in a world which, as I already said, is basically an administered world, where the most powerful economic concerns prevail, and in which these dominant economic relations are also reflected in the fact that there are countless sectors not directly connected with economic life where human beings have now largely become objects of central planning and administration, the relevant qualities of the individual are no longer respected. The principal thing that matters for human beings today, in the world in which we live, if they want to assert themselves, is something else. Today it has become far more important for human beings to accommodate themselves to the relations that are much more powerful than any individual than it is to assert themselves in any way. Anyone who has been cast into a relatively alien cultural environment, as I have experienced this in my own life as an emigrant, will feel far more strongly, as long as you remain in your own milieu, the pressure that is exerted on the individual to accommodate, to renounce one’s own individuality and the autonomous capacity to decide things for oneself. Now it would be false to imagine – here I come back to the aforementioned idea of the reciprocal mediation of individual and society – that especially among younger people – that is, people who have no longer grown up within the categories or the structures of an individualistic society – this pressure to accommodate and the counter-tendency to individuation were merely something external. It is quite true that individuality is experienced by the majority of people today as a burden, and the stronger this individuality is developed, and the more human beings today actually possess those qualities that, in terms of the Kantian or Hegelian ideal of the citizen, were once positively valued, the more difficult it is for such human beings to find their way in life, and the more they tend to be branded as loners or eccentrics. In this connection I remember a man I was once acquainted with, whose fate I know something about: a German emigrant who was employed as the regional director of some major financial business in America, but who lost his job because, they claimed, he was too active, too keen to get ahead, too ready to become involved in all kinds of things that were not really his concern, and thus of no use to their business. This man effectively fell victim to his own individuation in the modern world.

Now this is not just some random external fact but something that is already starting to be reflected within human beings themselves, within the younger generation; in other words, the thought once expressed by a character in one of Brecht’s plays, namely ‘I just don’t 

*[p. 57]* ^pg64

want to be a human being’,<sup>51</sup> seems in countless cases today to have replaced the bourgeois ideal of the categorical imperative; that is to say, in an act of defiance human beings today negate their own individuality, deny the wish to remain individuals, precisely in order, in this way, to fit in better with the world. I might just add in passing that here lies the key for understanding certain problems such as youth crime, those who are described as ‘half-weakened’ egos, and ‘fads’ such as ‘rock ’n’ roll’ and the like. We must try and explain these things more concretely in terms of particular social dynamics rather than simply complaining that, ‘Well, they have nothing to rely on and no real parental support at home.’ This is a far too general and entirely inadequate form of explanation. No one wants to be a subject because no one is a subject, and because the possibility of being one has already been cut off.

Ladies and gentlemen, I think it is very important at this point to say something more about a concept that will surely already have sprung to mind for many of you, and which almost automatically comes up in this context, although it is one that seems highly questionable to me in this connection. And this is the concept of ‘massification’. The noted Heidelberg psychologist Alexander Mitscherlich, whose writings may well be known to some of you here,<sup>52</sup> once began an essay by recounting an anecdote that I would also like to mention here.<sup>53</sup> You need to picture a huge mass meeting, in the Sports Palace in Berlin or some comparable stadium, where a demagogue is delivering his speech in the most modern fashion. You should imagine a combination of Hitler and Billy Graham, for example.<sup>54</sup> He gives a ranting address to the audience and finally at the climax of the speech cries out: ‘And who and what, my friends, is to blame for all this? The masses and the masses alone!’ He is instantly met with the deafening applause from 30,000 people.

I think that this succinctly captures the problem with the contemporary concept of massification. For this very concept belongs among those other deceptive concepts which help to conceal the truth about the relationship between the individual and society today. It is not really a question of massification. The issue is not that human beings intrinsically behave as masses, or that they tend to form masses. Nor is this a matter of mere quantity, for the concept of mass is itself a qualitative concept. In other words, the society in which we live robs labour of distinctive quality, objectively deprives human beings, in the manner I have described, of the possibility of determining themselves as individuals, and thereby demeans human beings by reducing them to a mass. That human beings today constitute a mass does not reflect some merely natural quality on their part but 

*[p. 58]* ^pg65

is something that has been done to them, and there is no reason to believe that they will always have to remain a mass. The great religious thinker Kierkegaard once said that we can do whatever we like, that we can produce statistics, we can treat human beings like cattle, but we cannot judge human beings like cattle, and the task is to release them from this blind herd by helping them to become genuinely self-aware.<sup>55</sup> But one should not absolutize this concept of mass either, for the talk of ‘mass humanity’ amounts to little more than crying ‘Stop, thief!’; in other words, it is like telling the victims, those who are so subjugated that they can no longer act as independent beings at all, that is just the way they are like everyone else and acting as if this were down to the unworthiness and wretchedness of their own nature. I regard such thinking as immoral in the deepest sense, and if you will forgive my passion in this matter, I would say that we should all do everything we possibly can to avoid encouraging this idea of the mass. The concept is indeed extremely seductive, for the phenomena we are talking about here, the people who rush to see idiotic films, the people who believe that what Billy Graham is offering them is actually religion, the people who pour over their illustrated newssheets, the people who are ready at any moment to embrace any dictator, whether it be Hitler or Krushchev – they all certainly display the characteristics that are captured by the idea of ‘mass humanity’; but we tend to break off reflection prematurely at this point, failing to recognize that the reason people have these characteristics lies, to return once again to this, in institutional reality, in the way that society in its current form hardly leaves any other possibility open to people, and in this respect we cannot hold them fully responsible. Thus one could say that the dynamics of bourgeois society itself, which once helped to produce ‘the individual’, now manifests a tendency to dissolve the individual once more. For since the individual, with the qualities it once possessed, can today no longer fulfil a useful social function in the existing social order, it now regresses to this pre-individual, to this pre-individuated stage or condition, and this social phenomenon of regression is precisely what is reflected in the concept of massification. In a world of universal fungibility, in a world, to put it rather crudely, where every human being is already effectively beginning to be replaceable by any other human being, there is no longer really any room for the individual in the classical sense, and the being of the majority of people is increasingly defined by the world we inhabit as a ‘being-for-another’. And if we are constantly being preached to about ‘being’ today, this is simply a way of drowning out the sense that being, namely the being-in-itself of human beings, has been liquidated in favour of a 

*[p. 59]* ^pg66

mere being-for-others. We hear more perorations about ‘being’ in the world in which we live the less there is actually anything like being among human beings themselves. Thus the concept of being has been debased into little more than a complementary ideology.

Now you might still say that things are surely not yet so bad where the individual is concerned, for while all the things we have mentioned may be true for the professional sphere, this is not the case for the sphere of private life, of our inner life, or, to put this more simply, for the sphere of consumption. But I regard this view as too comforting and optimistic. In the first place I believe that human beings basically express and realize themselves through social labour, that what they are in themselves essentially transpires in the sphere of work. In a higher sense we are what we are in the context of our work rather than what we are in the momentary context when we ignore or abstract from our work and thus from our objective social function. But quite apart from this thought, which Hegel and Goethe already both insisted so strongly upon,<sup>56</sup> it turns out that the belief that we really are our own masters in the sphere of consumption or in the sphere of private life has also already proved to be an illusion. We can certainly say that the concept of the private sphere today has effectively degenerated into what is captured by that appealing expression ‘leisure’ or ‘free time’, where the former concept now simply appears as an appendage of the social mechanism, and one which also only serves to express the impotence of the private sphere. You can already sense this in the contemporary German word *Freizeitsgestaltung* – literally: ‘the organization of leisure’ – where the slightly humorous overtones this expression has come to assume is the most welcome thing about it. But consider how things stand with this ‘leisure time’, with the life outside work that is left to individuals in their own right in this world, when the ‘organization’ of such time now effectively becomes a task – for this clearly points right back to the sphere of production, to the sphere where our time is not free at all. Quite apart from this, the so-called sphere of consumption in our intensely concentrated and highly organized form of economy is already ‘administered’ to a very considerable degree; in other words, the goods and the possibilities of life which are available to the vast majority of people in terms of their free time are already socially pre-formed, the range of choices is remarkably limited, and this social pre-formation is already oriented to a certain conformism, that is, to the de-individualization of human beings, thereby expressly turning them into the mass consumers they are then subsequently denounced for being. Thus the famous Lieschen Müller character, who has become the prototype of humanity in the mass, is defined not just by 

*[p. 60]* ^pg67

the suggestion it would clearly be too much to expect a little typist to have a real ‘personality’, although this person has no possibility of actually realizing herself as a person, but also by the way this Lieschen Müller first becomes a ‘Lieschen Müller’ by the very films she is forced to watch; and the most horrible thing about all this is surely the moment when a film such as *Lieschen Müller* actually makes fun of Lieschen Müller, which is exactly the kind of thing we can expect today.<sup>57</sup>

In other words, if you imagine that my idea of the diminution or the liquidation of the individual is far too negative, and indeed exaggerated, I think you simply have to apply these reflections to the only sphere in which we still believe in something resembling ‘individuals’, and you will see how far the concept of individuality has been drained of life. The conclusion to be drawn here – and I think it is important to recognize this – can be expressed as follows: if in response to the concept of so-called ‘massification’ and the phenomena I have been describing you simply appealed to human beings to look within themselves, to decide to become individuals, such an appeal, articulated *in abstracto*, would clearly be absurd. The substance that the concept of individuality still retains in spite of everything could not be saved simply by enjoining people to embrace such individuality in a world that deprives them of it and prevents them from realizing it.

At this point I would also like to say a few words about another category that enjoys considerable popularity today, namely the category of the personal example or ‘role model’.<sup>58</sup> There is a well-known passage in *Faust* – and I hope you will not take me for a typical old schoolmaster if I start quoting *Faust* to you – where we are told that a ‘good man, in all his obscure striving, / Is still aware of the path that’s right.’<sup>59</sup> I think you only have to reflect for a moment and compare this observation with what you constantly hear today about the urgent need for ‘role models’. You will immediately appreciate how impoverished the category of the individual has become. Goethe still expresses the trust that individuality is something substantial, that in the individual human being and in society at large we already encounter the qualities that can show him something like the right path, the right course of development, the right way to behave. But as soon as we imagine by contrast that we already have to have a so-called role model, where we soon find that the much invoked idea of ‘discipline’ also plays a remarkably important role, we discover that the substantial character that human beings are supposed to acquire, the substantial character of what they might actually become, has already dissolved. The question immediately 

*[p. 61]* ^pg68

arises as to who exactly is supposed to provide or project this role model, and the idea of ‘projecting’ such a model for an autonomous human being, for an autonomous personality, already implies a kind of heteronomy. In this way ‘personality’ itself is already turned into a question of planning and administration, which is then supposed to ensure that the right personalities are duly produced, even though it belongs to the essence of personality that it should develop the kind of characteristics that escape such administrative planning. Once such a role model has to be constructed, has to be expressly produced, instead of something that is implicitly present in human beings, already somehow aware of where it is taking them, then the rule of the administered world has extended to the very realm which seemed to offer the prospect of healing the wounds of the administered world itself. One cannot abstractly decree what the human being is meant to be, what the human being is meant to aspire to, if we are not to fall back behind the great insights of the philosophical tradition, behind Hegel in other words. There is no immediate role model for human beings, nothing according to which we could orient ourselves today. If there were something substantial that resembled such a thing, it could only be unfolded through the developed critique of society, and through the critique of the individual already implied in the developed critique of the former. For perhaps I could just put it this way, ladies and gentlemen: we do not really know how human beings are meant to be, and even if we wanted to project some positive model of society, even if we wanted to proclaim that society should be precisely this way rather than that, such a positive model of society would only take us back to a position that lags behind all those thinkers who have shown that the idea of a right order of human life cannot consist in conjuring up an ideal image of things and then forcing the world to conform to it. Rather, the task is to discern those objective possibilities, already implicit within society, that might point the way to a better order of things, and encourage these possibilities with as much freedom and spontaneity we can muster.

But it would be a great misunderstanding, ladies and gentlemen, if you were simply to regard what I have been suggesting here a kind of defeatism. It is quite true that we do not know what the ideal model of humanity is, and that, if we are completely honest, we do not know what the ideal model of society is; nonetheless, in light of the current state of development, we probably could, somewhat surprisingly, say more about the positive character of what society might yet be than we could about the positive character of human beings in that society. But we do know exactly and precisely what is wrong about 

*[p. 62]* ^pg69

society, and thus about ourselves, about each one of us. This means – and here once more, in conclusion, I would emphatically invoke the philosophical tradition, and the Hegelian tradition in particular – that we have the possibility of measuring our society and our own existence, our life as a whole, up against its concept, as in the short time available to us I have already tried to illustrate for you, in a rather fragmentary way, with reference to some specific examples. For we can certainly recognize the points where our society fails to live up to its own concept, namely that of a perspicuous association of human beings for the protection and preservation of their own life; we can also recognize the scars of human beings, real individual human beings, their weakened egos,<sup>60</sup> the illnesses to which they are prone, their neurotic character, even their psychotic tendencies which appear to be getting stronger today. We can clearly recognize all these things that are wrong, even if we cannot claim to possess the abstract concept of what is right. No serious psychiatrist or psychoanalyst would ever dare to tell us that he or she knows what the right kind of normal, healthy, fulfilled human being is, and this precisely because such a concept of the right kind of human being cannot be conceived independently of the world in which the human being lives, independently of the world to which the human being is essentially related. But, in spite of this, any decent psychologist or psychiatrist will be able to tell you, in relation to a particular human being, where the wound is to be found, where the hurt or pain is, and the same thing is true of society. It may well be true that all possible abstract social ideals have fallen by the wayside today. But I believe we certainly can know that a society that has split apart into two rigidly opposed power blocks that are capable at any moment of falling upon each other, and even, against the will of the vast majority of those who live in them, of ultimately destroying human beings altogether, we can certainly know that such a society contradicts its own concept. And we can also recognize the madness of a humanity that today already possesses the technological means to transform the world into a paradise where no child, where no old woman, anywhere in the world, still needs to go hungry – the madness of a world that probably commits the greater part of its entire social product to the creation of weapons of mass destruction. We certainly do not need to have some positive ideal image or model of society in order to recognize this madness for what it is. And I am more than happy to embrace a negativism which insists that the only positive thing possible for us today is to try, as far as this lies within our power, to put an end at last to this negative reality, to the inhumanity that is already with us, and to the threat of yet further inhumanity.
