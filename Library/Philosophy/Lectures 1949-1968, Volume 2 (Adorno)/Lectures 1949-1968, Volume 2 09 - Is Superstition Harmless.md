## Is Superstition Harmless?

*30 November 1963*

Ladies and gentlemen, In the context of these academic lectures, and in the presence of so many serious individuals here who are thoroughly versed in rational methods and procedures – as one would naturally expect of any professionally recognized teacher or official – it may perhaps astonish you to hear somebody talking about such an unserious subject as superstition in the contemporary world, and especially to hear this coming from someone who I think I am permitted to say is generally just as concerned with serious things as you all are. I should just say in advance that I am not really interested in talking about superstition as such, or about its content, or even in directly refuting superstition, something that I regard as irrelevant here. I would simply remind you that Leibniz, in the baroque period, when alchemy and astrology still exercised considerable power over people’s minds, that Leibniz already declared – even before the great Enlightenment period – that among all the sciences the only one that he despised was astrology.<sup>1</sup> I am discussing these phenomena, therefore, not on account of their importance as such, nor for the purpose of criticizing them or exposing their falseness in the spirit of the Enlightenment, for all that has long since been done. At the same time, I think that one should not underestimate the immediate significance of superstition, for although the statistics compiled by the institutes of opinion research vary somewhat, it would seem even according to the most conservative estimates that about 30 per cent of the population in West Germany believe in astrology, and many 

*[p. 144]* ^pg151

more believe that there is at least something to astrology.<sup>2</sup> Such a figure is not as unimportant as we might initially imagine in relation to the position of contemporary consciousness. But the reason why I want to talk about all this is really a sociological one. I would like at least to point out certain aspects of superstition that seem to me to be quite instructive with respect to the condition of society as a whole. And I would like to present superstition to you in its current socially effective form as a kind of model or symptom of a state or condition of consciousness that is extraordinarily widespread today. The phenomenon of superstition allows us to observe, as in a test tube, a series of social and especially psychosocial tendencies that in other areas are much less harmless than superstition appears to be. Now there are certainly many who will incline to the view that superstition is indeed harmless. Now I should say right away that I do not share this thesis of the harmlessness, and what I want to explore with you here has no other purpose than to show you why I do not regard superstition, or rather the mechanisms that lie behind the commercialized superstition of today, as harmless at all. But first of all I just want to say something about the attitude that is often expressed in this assumption of harmlessness and is also quite characteristic in itself. For when educated and insufferably enlightened people talk about things such as superstition, this usually tends to happen with a certain good-humoured irony: ‘Well, of course, we know there is nothing to all this, we know people are just trying to take us in for whatever reason and just want to deceive us, but what does it really matter?’ Now I believe that precisely this kind of irony, which is immensely common, is a very contemporary attitude and one, incidentally, that plays a considerable role among the superstitious themselves. This is worth seriously reflecting upon, for it represents a compromise between mistrusting the phenomena that we treat so ironically and mistrusting with good reason, although we also let these phenomena pass since they play such a considerable role in society; we accept them and subject ourselves to them, as I would almost put it, simply because they are there and because so much lies behind them. And we do so by making a sort of mental reservation, by smiling at them, even while acting as if we had nothing against these things. This attitude is an expression of a general tendency to neutralize the work of the mind, a tendency we also see around us in the neutralization of culture as a whole. And this neutralization, this tendency to do no more than register cultural and intellectual things of whatever kind, to transform them into cultural goods, to reify them, if I can use this word here, rather than taking them seriously, and relating to them as rational and independent individuals. I also 

*[p. 145]* ^pg152

regard all this as a symptom of a very problematic and questionable condition. And I want to warn against hiding behind all of our supposedly superior culture, under the cover and protection of which all kinds of troubling things may flourish on account of the neutralization I am talking about.

Now there is a question about how in our enlightened times something such as superstition is even possible to the extent that it is, since it so clearly contradicts the condition of enlightenment itself. We can say in reply that, while enlightenment in one sense certainly extends to the whole domain of what Scheler would describe as ‘controlling knowledge’,<sup>3</sup> in other words, to the whole domain of technological civilization and its effects, this process of enlightenment, for reasons that it is not possible to explore fully here, encounters a limit in the psychological condition of human beings themselves. For because human beings are not actually in possession of themselves in the way that a genuinely enlightened society presupposes, there are countless irrational areas that still persist in our rationalized society and in the administered world more generally. And precisely because these areas effectively contradict rationality, they come to assume a poisonous and inwardly untrue and problematic character. But in addition, in order to understand the role of contemporary superstition properly, you must be clear that we are talking not about a primary phenomenon here – that is, about particular superstitious ideas that are immediately present in human beings themselves – but about what I have described as ‘second-hand superstition’;<sup>4</sup> in other words, about ideas which are cranked up and boosted by material interests, essentially by the interests of journals, magazines and newspapers in order to increase their sales and spread their influence. Here again, I believe, we must beware of interpreting this whole complex of factors in far too superficial a manner. Attitudes of this kind can undoubtedly be found among the mass of people today to a very considerable degree, but they are then also mobilized by the standardized form of superstition that is manipulated from above. And while these institutions would probably just insist that human beings are what they are, that people want to be deceived, as the Latin proverb has it,<sup>5</sup> they use this to their own advantage in order to keep human beings in a state of dependency.

Here you can already see why phenomena such as ‘second-hand superstition’ are so relevant from the sociological perspective. Here in a nutshell you can study something of the mechanism of contemporary mass culture, which also consists in leading and influencing the mass of people in a particular way, in making them more dependent, precisely by exploiting certain factors in the masses 

*[p. 146]* ^pg153

themselves. We might almost say that, in all mass culture, ‘the masses’ actually represent the ideology of those who wish to exploit or manipulate them. What makes this possible in the first place is the condition that I would like to describe as ‘socialized semi-culture’. In other words, the human beings who are prone to superstition, if I can say a few words about the appropriate phenomenological characterization of such people, are generally those who have lost the naivety, the innocence of spirit. In other words, these are people who are no longer firmly rooted in traditional religious ideas but who have developed a degree of critical consciousness and no longer just accept what they are told, who want to see what is really going on in the background. At the same time these are people, in terms of their education and their psychological constitution, who lack the power or sometimes even the will to penetrate what is currently happening around them or to see through the often opaque processes of society. Thus they readily turn to some kind of ersatz rationality, such as that currently represented by superstition, and are exceptionally receptive in general to such superstition, along with other heteronomous belief structures of our time such as fascism and what is known in Eastern Europe as ‘diamat’.<sup>6</sup> The presuppositions for all this are already present in the current state of our society as a whole, a society which is certainly capable of conveying information to a previously inconceivable extent through modern media of communication, but which has proved quite incapable, for profound structural reasons, of allowing those who are provided with all this information to become the kind of rational and autonomous human beings who would themselves be able to draw the relevant conclusions from all this information.

Now I would just like to point out a couple of apparent advantages that people derive from this socialized and commercialized superstition. Here I shall be concerned less with individual astrologers or people of that kind, or with any of the particular magazines and publications in this area – which is really a subject in its own right – than with the socially relevant dimension of superstition and the role that this plays in the astrology columns of countless newspapers and the illustrated magazines that are so popular. I have already pointed out that superstition promises quick and premature solutions in a society like ours where there are no longer any direct relationships between human beings, no ‘face-to-face relations’ as they say in America, where functional relations have effectively taken the place of more immediate and spontaneous relations between people. Thus while it requires extraordinarily difficult, persistent, and often discomfiting or even disturbing reflections about one’s own fate 

*[p. 147]* ^pg154

in society if one is to achieve any self-understanding in this society or any understanding of one’s so-called social role in it, astrology offers a kind of spurious orientation through the implicit claim: ‘Well, it’s all written in the stars; you must just look there to see how everything is already really in order.’ This ready response dispenses with all culture and education, which demands considerable effort, and promises people what they are already constantly encouraged to believe by the modern world of advertising and the current arrangement of society – namely that I am about to get, or will at least very soon get, everything that I hope for in the world, whereas of course in reality what one gets from the stars – which may not exactly lie, but certainly fail to tell the truth because they actually say nothing at all – is just another illusion. But there is also something else going on here, which is connected with the spurious solution of problems that is offered by superstition. Social psychology has actually shown how far we are justified in talking of the ‘ego-weakness’<sup>7</sup> of human beings in the context of society today – in other words, of the way they tend increasingly to become centres of conditioned reflexes, without being able to offer much resistance out of their own resources to this process. I do not intend to go into the psychological mechanisms of this ego-weakness here, but I would like to draw your attention to a very simple sociological aspect of this question that I think will be illuminating even for those of you who are not specifically interested in or already oriented towards the problems of depth psychology. For you are already aware that the range of possible decisions open to all of us today has in fact been remarkably diminished by the superior power of institutions, whether we are talking about major economic groupings or the highly concentrated powers of administration. If I may simply appeal to your own experience, and I may also say to our own experience, that as so-called high level employees in the educational system we constantly discover that we do not in reality enjoy that sovereignty or freedom of decision-making that we are expected to exercise by virtue of our position, and that above all we also expect of ourselves. For we are constantly restricted by innumerable conditions, not only by the need to follow rational procedures and regulations but also by the compulsion to reach speedy decisions, by situations where we have to choose between forced alternatives and leave absolutely no room for considered or independent decisions. In other words, we continually find ourselves hemmed in precisely where a truly free and autonomous decision is most expected; in countless cases our decision just comes down to transferring one document from the left-hand pile of things to do to the right-hand pile of things done. If those of us who belong to a fairly privileged 

*[p. 148]* ^pg155

group in society can recognize this experience of dependency and limited opportunities for decision, one can imagine what things are like for human beings in more difficult and oppressive circumstances – human beings for whom commercialized superstition is almost made to measure. Superstition of this kind relieves them – at least formally and symbolically – of the decisions which they are constantly being called upon to make or endorse, although they already know and feel that in reality they are not in a position to make these decisions at all. The psychodynamic achievement, so to speak, which is accomplished by superstition lies in obscuring the difference between the decisions that are expected of each of us as a citizen in a free and autonomous democratic society and the actual unfreedom that, for internal and external reasons, makes it enormously difficult for us to make such independent decisions. This contradiction, from which we all constantly suffer, is resolved by astrology, at least in appearance, since it takes the burden of our decisions away from us by acting as if these decisions had already been made for us, by the stars, whose authority has been silently and unquestioningly assumed from the start.

And there is another thing here. You will all be familiar with those claims, currently much in vogue, that, after the eclipse of the major religions, society and existence no longer possess any real meaning for human beings. I would like to leave aside for now the question concerning this so-called meaning of things and simply recall the aperçu that the moment we start asking after the meaning of one’s own existence, after a meaning that is not already implied in that existence, everything is already effectively lost, and that the so-called problem of meaning begins right there where we have to ask about it. But I do not wish to pursue this point here. In any case, astrology offers human beings a kind of ersatz meaning, once again a compromise solution in the psychological sense, for while people are sufficiently enlightened that, generally speaking, they no longer really believe in a notion of divine providence that reflects the personal will of God, they cannot accept the idea that this is all there is to existence. And the trick played by astrology lies simply in presenting the imperatives we are already subjected to in our real existence – the things we suffer from, the things we feel lack meaning – in presenting this alien and for us meaningless necessity as if it were the very meaning we are looking for. At the same time, in the entirely naturalistic vein of enlightened consciousness, the authority that once had its place in religion is now ascribed to what is merely natural, namely to the stars and their constellations. In astrology, therefore, we find something like a regression to the religions of nature, an element of 

*[p. 149]* ^pg156

vitalistic pantheism, a remnant perhaps of the monism that flourished fifty years ago. But this whole mentality now is so authoritarian, in other words, so concerned that we should swallow the story we are being offered, that it never makes fully clear to people the underlying assumptions that are in play here.

In this connection, incidentally, it is very significant, and definitely worth emphasizing, that the great religions, with very good reason, have always adopted an extraordinarily critical position in relation to superstition. Thus if superstition also presents itself today with an almost religious claim to authority, this is an extremely questionable development not only from the perspective of enlightened thought but also from that of theology. For such superstition explicitly deifies that immanent character of life, that merely natural context of life, against which the idea of a transcendent divinity originally stood and still stands today. Superstition is thus a kind of naturalistic religion, or a religion of nature, to which people are being encouraged to regress; and this idea of regression, which I have now come to, may also help you recognize a truly essential aspect of superstition: that it is an attempt to exploit that element of regression – those tendencies towards infantile regression that are extraordinarily powerful in human beings today – or even to strengthen it by recourse to the infantile conceptions that are involved here. But is also characteristic that the superstition which encourages such regression and discourages rational behaviour, which has what we could call a parasitic relationship to religion, also has a parasitic relationship to science. In other words, it almost always presents itself as if it represented some kind of scientific knowledge, thus simultaneously exploiting both the irrationality of human beings and their faith in science. It exploits their conviction that what transpires in the realm of science enjoys a sort of absolute dignity. And this claim to scientific standing is extremely shrewd, for there is no doubt that astrology does exploit scientific knowledge in a way. On the one hand astrology exploits astronomy, for the astrologist will usually take great care not to say anything about the stars and their constellations that is not factually correct in relation to the day or the hour in question. Only recently I read about a remarkable legal judgement that I might just draw to your attention here, although many of you may well be more familiar with the case than I am. The judgement declared that an astrologist who wants to pursue his profession legitimately has to possess sufficient knowledge if he is not to be regarded as a charlatan.<sup>8</sup> In other words, he must really be able to identify the relevant astronomical conjunctions with precision – as if this were already the rational or scientific issue, for the point is 

*[p. 150]* ^pg157

not whether someone can correctly describe the stars and constellations but whether there is any intelligible relationship between these things and ourselves. On the other hand, what astrology tells us about human beings is also borrowed from science. In other words, astrology presupposes a form of popular psychology which, among those who like to combine these things, very often goes along with an extremely shrewd, hardboiled and sceptical understanding of people and their instincts; thus, in the psychological sphere as well, astrology really does draw upon something resembling a scientific knowledge of human beings, but it uses what it learns from depth psychology in the opposite sense, that is, it uses this knowledge to confirm and strengthen human beings in their irrationality. The spurious character of this apparently scientific status, this claim to scientific authority, in reality consists simply in this (although this ‘simply’ carries considerable weight and bears upon the whole issue): that both these spheres, the experience of popular psychology on the one side and the science of astronomy on the other are tacitly treated – without this being even remotely acknowledged – as if there were some intrinsic connection between them; but there is so little evident connection here that, statistically speaking, as friends who specialize in these things have told me, the claim could not even be refuted in principle; for there are too many variables involved to prove a connection between the celestial domain and that of psychology, and it would be extraordinarily difficult to find any scientific way of checking any such claims.<sup>9</sup> I just hope there is no devotee of astrology among you who will conclude from the fact that we cannot check something that there must be something in it after all.

Ladies and gentlemen, this is what we mean by pseudo-science. I believe that it is hard to underestimate the effect and significance of this phenomenon. I was once incautious enough to appear in a television broadcast<sup>10</sup> – some of you may even have seen it – where I said something about the social critique of superstition, while an astrologist was also independently recorded for the same programme, without us exchanging any words at all.<sup>11</sup> This astrologist, who appeared wearing a cosy jacket, lived in a beautiful, comfortable, tranquil and somewhat modernized farmhouse complete with thatched roof; he set to work with his instruments, pointing out various constellations, doubtless quite correctly; he exuded a certain sense of inspiring dependability and human warmth, whereas I was filmed on the premises of a modern scientific institute, seated behind a desk, where I gave the impression of a cold-hearted and mean-spirited rational individual. Thus the whole setup, if I may put it like that, was effectively pre-determined in favour of the astrological 

*[p. 151]* ^pg158

personality rather than the arguments I was trying to present. This deceptive combination of staged and even mendacious human warmth and intimacy – the kindly old uncle so concerned about the fate of human beings – with a kind of rational scientific reliability – he works with sextants after all and gazed so thoughtfully at the movements of the stars – all this procures a mass effect and a mass appeal that can hardly be welcomed or endorsed. When I said earlier that this whole question is less harmless than it appears to be, we can really make sense of this claim only by looking more closely at certain insights of depth psychology. For if we accept the thesis that we all suffer today, that we all suffer considerably from the heteronomous character of the social order that bears down on us, then we will all harbour certain destructive tendencies of an unconscious nature. We somehow wish unconsciously to find some peace at last, or, to put this very crudely and bluntly, that everything will just blow up and disappear. And this unconscious will is what is exploited by astrology, in that it responds to all kinds of persecution fantasies that human beings already have and plays with the thought of catastrophe and disaster that lies in the unconscious of all of us, a thought moreover that, in the paranoid state of the world today, which could literally blow up at any time, is constantly being suggested to us. And astrology plays with these destructive fantasies. It almost gives people the hope for such a global explosion, while simultaneously giving them the hope that, if they behave in the right way, if they submit to the stars, perhaps the explosion won’t actually happen.

I described this as a paranoid mode of behaviour. The analogous case from the psychiatric field is that of a person with persecution mania who sets fire to a house but still makes sure there is a bucket of water to hand to put out the fire he has just started. In a way this behaviour of the paranoid personality resembles the way that astrological columns function in the newspapers, or at least what these columns are designed to intimate in an extremely rational and cunning manner. And finally there is another essential function at work here, since modern superstition, as I already mentioned, is promoted by the mass media and standardized through what I have described as ‘socialized semi-culture’. This function enables human beings not to feel so alone, to feel that they are really involved in things, to feel that they belong. If you just take a look at the ‘Lonely Hearts’ column in the newspapers, which is well worth doing, you will see the disturbing frequency with which those seeking so-called partners advertise their star signs as a Taurus or a Virgo, or some other rudimentary sign drawn from the animal world. And one has the feeling that these people somehow already experience themselves 

*[p. 152]* ^pg159

in terms of those idiotic definitions that according to popular astrology are supposed to capture the true character of a Taurus, a Virgo or a Gemini; or that even if they feel they amount to nothing or belong nowhere in particular they are still gratified to be a Taurus or a Virgo, the way we all feel gratified to belong to the same generation, after all.

With these things I have perhaps already suggested a structure on the part of astrology that is highly characteristic of our contemporary culture, and one that is exploited above all by mass culture and the culture industry as a whole. For we might say that astrology is really a division of the culture industry, one that divulges something of what the culture industry does not betray with the same naivety in its more polished or developed products. For what astrology offers people are also ersatz satisfactions. Whereas astrology promises human beings all kinds of good things from the stars, although these things never arrive – for they are usually of such a trivial character that they can come true or not, or it is a matter of indifference whether they do or not – these astrological columns are always organized in such a way that the act of promising itself essentially stands in for the fulfilment of what they announce. In other words, human beings derive pleasure from being promised something good; they also derive pleasure from playing with the idea of downfall and catastrophe, and they derive pleasure from feeling directly and personally addressed by means of pseudo-individualization. Thus the friendly newspaper astrologist aways speaks in the highly confidential tone of someone who takes the fate of a little old lady in some godforsaken place right to his heart as he tells us we really need to be especially careful between the hours of one and two. All of these things already offer human beings the gratification that they can hardly expect from reality. Here I would draw your attention particularly to the way that astrology plays with narcissism, with human vanity, to put it simply. Thus the advice which astrology imparts to people, the whole manner in which it addresses them, already seems to lend them some importance or significance, firstly by acknowledging them, by appearing to concern itself with their specific lives; and, secondly, by addressing human beings – namely the masses that astrology is aimed at – as if each individual person were a particularly valuable, talented and conscientious human being. This way of playing with human vanity, this representative way of confirming powerless and constantly humiliated human beings in the existence they already have, is surely one of the most potent effects at work here, just as contemporary collectivism and emotive nationalism also essentially involve similar narcissistic mechanisms. In short, powerless human 

*[p. 153]* ^pg160

beings who are also largely aware of their own insignificance become something in their own eyes by becoming ‘Soviets’ or ‘Germans’, as members of one collective or another, until finally labels such as ‘Taurus’ or ‘Virgo’ are sufficient to individualize them. A further aspect here – and something that astrology also shares with many sectors of the culture industry – is the way astrology promises help to those it individualizes by providing advice that will supposedly lead them to cope better with their lives. But when you look at this advice it generally amounts to nothing but trivialities, and while this all transpires as if real human problems were being seriously addressed, and while real human problems are indeed constantly mentioned, the advice and help which is offered is vacuous; it is intended merely to give people the feeling that they matter, that they are being thought about, without any real consequences flowing from this. And it is this vaguely counselling role on the part of astrology that allows certain people to argue – including among them a quite well-known social psychologist colleague of mine<sup>12</sup> – that the whole thing is quite harmless and should perhaps even be welcomed as something positive. It is doubtless true that no one is harmed if we are told – especially on a day when the astrologer knows the traffic will be particularly heavy – that we should take special care in the car. Advice like that cannot in itself hurt anyone. However, I think that even this advice, apart from being rather daft, is not as harmless or as positive as it gives itself out to be, for it still implies that the stars are somehow responsible for our driving regulations; and the mechanism of stultification that is cranked up here, when the rules we all more or less have to obey are presented as if they explicitly needed the permission of the stars, this mechanism of stultification can only exercise a negative effect in itself. Of course, no one astrological pronouncement is enough to make people more unintelligent, as it were, and if we tried to study stultification of this kind it would be very difficult to establish any useful results, since it would be almost impossible to identify the stupefying influence of this mechanism in particular cases. But you should not forget that superstition is only one of these stultifying mechanisms, and that there is a continuum here that stretches all the way from the influence of the stars through the eye-catching affairs of Soraya<sup>13</sup> and Margaret<sup>14</sup> to the complete distortions of reality we find in the majority of films; and that people are already so oriented to all these branches of the culture industry that there is actually little alternative to accepting or adapting to this process to some degree or other. And this acceptance or adaptation is the whole idea behind these media. I have to say of course that not everyone really believes it is particularly important whether someone 

*[p. 154]* ^pg161

is a Taurus or a Virgo, or that Soraya is the most important person in the world. We can only explain this by recognizing that, while people do believe in this swindle, they never wholly believe in it, that they mainly feel a kind of active ambivalence that also sees the swindle for what it is, so that this ambivalence to some extent lacks any real consequences (like the ideology that is being disseminated here). Nonetheless, one can hardly avoid the feeling, however difficult it would be to prove this objectively, that the whole culture industry as a system already tends to produce something like the type of person which it claims it is there to serve.

Now along with this appeal to vanity there is also the appeal to anxiety. But the latter is tempered in order not to frighten people too much. Thus while there are always threats, there is also support from the stars, both equally fictive. One can interpret this in social terms as a reference to extremely dependent human beings and those who certainly cannot be held responsible for their own fate. The situation is read in such a way that the stars serve to relieve human beings of their dependency. The underlying assumption behind the whole procedure cranked up by astrology is this: everything can be remedied or improved by private or personal behaviour, as long as the individual behaves as the stars suggest – an assumption that is completely contradicted of course by the inability of the individual as such to change, in any serious or independent way, the relations and conditions in which we are caught up in the administered world.

Here I would like to make all this a bit more concrete and say at least a few words about the specific phenomenon of astrology columns which I systematically investigated when I was in America. So the astrologist is confronted by a rather difficult task in this connection. For he has to project the fiction, as I mentioned earlier, that he could somehow personally know all the people to whom his columns are addressed, or that he could somehow personally share their concerns. This is what facilitates the transference situation, the personal bond which the astrologist wishes to establish with his victims, so to speak. This is naturally extremely difficult, for in reality he does not know them at all. Now it is very interesting to note – and in studying these things it is hard not to admire the psychological acumen of these astrologers – just how cunning they are in assessing their victims and anticipating how they will react, in judging how to frame the situation. In other words, what the astrologist says must always be concrete enough to sound as if it indicates something specific with respect to specific individuals, but not too readily refutable. Two things help in this connection. First there is the appeal to vanity and the need for happiness. If the astrology column satisfies 

*[p. 155]* ^pg162

elements in some way, it immediately connects with psychological human needs that are so powerful that people are unlikely to give much thought to the truth or untruth of what they are told. Then we must also remember that at least the conflicts which the astrological column addresses and exemplifies in relation to human life must be sufficiently typical in terms of real widespread conflicts that people can readily recognize themselves in the conflicts in question. In fact, the astrology columns are generally extremely adept in this regard. Moreover, the pseudo-individualization of what is in truth entirely standardized is already also a universal characteristic of the culture industry and its products, like the majority of commercial films; and although such articles are presented as if each one of them represented an individual and specific work of art involving individual and specific people, we always get the same skeleton decked out with different images or painted in different colours.

So one has to present typical situations involving irresolvable conflicts. Now the formal structure is generally framed in such a way that the conflicts in question – although the astrologists are perfectly well aware that they are in principle irresolvable – are spuriously resolved insofar as incompatible elements are separated and distributed in temporal terms. If the changing constellations of the stars already turn the abstract temporal factor into the crucial determining ground for the deliverances of astrology in general, here time once again – which lets the grass grow over many a wound, as the fine old proverb has it – is treated as the basic governing principle by astrology. In this way the astrologists pick up on a particular aspect of ego-weakness that I have already mentioned, namely the tendency of human beings with a weak ego to transfer all the problems they have from themselves, from their particular personality, to some abstract temporal factor. The contradictory demands which are typically made upon people are thereby transposed onto the temporal level: thus the ‘either–or’ gets reinterpreted in terms of a ‘first–then’ structure in accordance with a very simple and empirically identifiable schema, the schema of work and leisure. The advice that astrology generally offers us is nothing but a duplication of what we are generally obliged to do: we usually have to work throughout the day, but we are permitted to relax in the evening. Now astrology, especially in its highly rationalized American form, as we find it in American newspapers, tells us: ‘In the mornings we have to be realistic and discharge the tasks of life; in the evenings we can allow ourselves something more and have a good time.’ But then we find the good time once again standing in for work. In other words, we mustn’t go too far, for the good 

*[p. 156]* ^pg163

time we are meant to have, the things we are meant to enjoy, are supposed to consist in the so-called simple pleasures of life, pleasures which are so enthusiastically recommended as an ideal for poorer people because they generally have little access to anything beyond these simple pleasures. Now there is a very strong insistence on the strict separation of work and pleasure here. It is as if the principle of the division of labour is being applied to the inner psychological economy of human beings: when we work we have to behave solely and strictly in terms of the reality principle, in terms of our own interest and advantage, and must avoid besmirching this with any foolish, that is to say, useless and independent thoughts. Now the ideal of purity plays a very significant role in all these astrology columns. But the happiness that people are supposed to enjoy in their free time is only a means to an end and already suggests the principle of ‘strength through joy’; and according to the astrology columns even our pleasures are supposed to bring practical advantages with them. Thus in one of the American newspapers that I analysed in considerable detail<sup>15</sup> the men are encouraged to go out and enjoy themselves in the evening with an attractive young woman and the women to do the same with an appropriate man, but there is aways inevitably a reminder that significant social advantages may eventually spring from such contacts. This is how the reality principle appears in the context of these practices. We also see how contradictory demands are reduced to a common formula when, on the one hand, we are encouraged to embody the strong individual, in accordance with the American ideal of ‘rugged individualism’<sup>16</sup> – the ruthless, realistic, competitive type; while, on the other hand, we are also supposed to adapt and fit in, which actually corresponds to the social reality where the economically independent type of individual is increasingly marginalized by functionaries and official employees of one kind or another. This too exhibits two phases, as it were, where the other-directed orientation, as Riesman calls it,<sup>17</sup> becomes increasingly evident, while the idea of the inner development of the individual human being tends to recede into the background. Now this outward, other-directed aspect leads to a systematic overvaluation of extremely limited practical concerns, which connects with the petit bourgeois character that these things were meant for. While this tends towards characteristics of meanness and pedantry, it also expresses a highly realistic attitude insofar as the activities most people are able to pursue are usually restricted to rather insignificant and superficial things, such as washing the car on Sunday mornings and the like. This development is cranked up massively today. The prevailing mentality here is what we could perhaps best describe as 

*[p. 157]* ^pg164

an ‘unrealistic realism’. Human beings are induced to participate in realistic activities and forms of behaviour which themselves, considered more closely, are so indifferent or insignificant that they make no difference in the context of the reality people are trying to fit in with. We have only to think of the games people have come up with, where once a year the employees take the place of the boss. The people in astrology columns are being treated like these employees who get to play the role of the boss every now and then. Thus people who almost certainly belong to the poorer sections of the population are encouraged to produce schemas or diagrams of how they would most like to increase or dispose of their income. The pleasure taken in these and similar fetishized things in comparison to the wretched income that may not turn out to be enough to live on – the only thing that would spare people from participating in this kind of nonsense at all – this fetishized pleasure only deceives us about how little there is to add up and play within the first place. An important role is also played here by the way we are advised to act behind the scenes where our bosses or superiors are concerned, an expression of the fact – especially in America, where the concentration of capital is so much more advanced than it is here – that people no longer believe they can get ahead in the normal competitive manner, or no longer believe they can advance in terms of the performance principle, so that they now have to exploit rather shady contacts and connections instead. This is all connected with certain fateful tendencies as our society moves towards a more closed and hierarchical social order. This is a question that we cannot really go into here for shortage of time.

When we consider the economic advice typically offered in such columns, we find that two mutually contradictory suggestions tend to be presented. On the one hand, one is advised: ‘Be conservative, stay strictly within your limits’; on the other hand, one is told: ‘Be modern, consider new investments.’ This basically implies the following: if you don’t take risks, if you don’t invest, you will go under in our competitive society; but if you invest too much, you will also go under; and one doesn’t know what to do either way. One gets the feeling that people are encouraged to resort to credit companies and similar institutions because they are good for the economy as a whole; at the same time people are warned against doing so because of the danger of provoking an economic crisis. In other words, the contradictions in the economic structure itself are transferred to the behaviour that people are recommended to adopt. Here again the astrology column exhibits a bi-phasic character, pursues a twofold path: one should be careful about money by day but splash out by 

*[p. 158]* ^pg165

night. This effectively implies that everything will balance out in the end, while the real problem here is never addressed.

It should be noted that so-called human relationships play a great role here, and especially the sphere of family, where the family always appears as a power that needs to be obeyed. In the background we glimpse a picture of grumbling or admonishing family members, and the astrologer’s advice is to deal with this spectre of the family as adroitly and craftily as we possibly can, without any real warmth or affection being acknowledged in these relationships. And when there is talk of friends, these are generally identified with associates or superiors. And I have also noticed something strange, and rather remarkable, in this connection, namely the fact that we are always advised to keep our new friends close but to be very wary of old ones, something which is clearly connected with sober respect for reality. In other words, according to this superstitious system, we should rid our life of anything that does not procure immediate advantage for us and restrict ourselves to what we can get here and now in accordance with the principle of ‘give and take’, as they say. From details such as this, which I have simply offered as an illustration, you can see that such advice is anything but harmless.

Now, ladies and gentlemen, I am drawing our discussion to an end. In the short time available to us I have unfortunately not been able to mention all the aspects of this question that I wished to draw to your attention. But I would just like to say this in conclusion: those who regard astrology and the mentality that I have been describing for you as essentially harmless will probably excuse their view by saying that the messages and pieces of advice that we receive here, with their false claim to rationality,<sup>18</sup> are in fact fairly reasonable and can at least furnish some order and stability for people. While the life we live is indeed rather chaotic and unstable, this claim to provide some order can only be to the good, and it is surely good if human beings are kept in order in this way, however murky the sources involved may be. In response I would just say this: in the first place, the concept of order is by no means intrinsically good in itself, for the glorification of order as such, irrespective of its shape and content, is already the expression of a weakened heteronomous consciousness which no longer cares whether the world is shaped in a rational way in accordance with our own insight but is only interested, as Brecht says,<sup>19</sup> in finding something to hold on to. And if what we hold on to is imposed so blindly from outside that we cannot recognize ourselves there, and where this order amounts to little beyond blind accommodation, then the appeal to order is utterly devalued. In short, we end up propagating an order which essentially 

*[p. 159]* ^pg166

contradicts the real interests of the human beings who are supposed to be embraced by this order. And the really problematic thing about this symptom, and the reason why I regard the mentality promoted by astrology as anything but harmless, is this: that it encourages this feeling for order in terms of a completely vague and undefined sense of dependency, without making the slightest attempt to justify this order which people are supposed to accept. I believe that those conservative apologists of astrology who appeal to the argument in favour of order should be the last people to endorse Plato’s argument, whose ethical doctrine fundamentally implies that subjective attitudes and forms of behaviour are only legitimate when they also possess objective truth. But here, in astrology, the objectivity of truth is not addressed or explored but merely assumed in a blind, dogmatic and essentially untrue way as something that already lies in the stars. But I take the view that an order that is intrinsically untrue, that cannot intrinsically be justified in the eyes of human beings, cannot possibly be good, as is constantly claimed, for those human beings who look to such an order. This is the fundamental reason – and you must forgive me if I speak in philosophical terms here – the truly philosophical reason that has brought me to recognize that astrology is the symptom of a general and extraordinarily fateful tendency, and that what we can read off from it is not its immediate effect or influence but the overall tendency of our world that simply reveals itself here, a tendency that is anything but harmless.

But it is also – and I would like to conclude with this thought – anti-democratic in a profound sense. For the democratic state in which we live rests on the assumption that the process of will-formation here must be realized on the part of mature, self-conscious and autonomous human beings. But if these human beings – on whose independence, capacity for judgement and reason the shaping of reality depends – are already so diminished by a system that finds perhaps its crassest expression in the phenomenon of organized superstition that they are no longer capable of such independent will-formation, of such rational decision; or when all these techniques, for which I have provided an example here today, effectively hinder people in our society from becoming autonomous human beings in the first place, then we see that this whole theory, in its very form and independently of any specific content, stands in fundamental opposition to the idea of democracy itself. And when people say: ‘Well, in a democracy one should just leave everyone to their own tastes and their own devices, so if people want their astrology, why not give it them?’, this is profoundly mendacious, for the end and object on which these techniques of manipulation ultimately 

*[p. 160]* ^pg167

converge is simply human unfreedom and blind obedience. And I would think that the idea of democracy is hardly strengthened by such an attitude but, in the long run, can only be undermined by such an attitude. Hence I believe it is only right that you should give full critical attention to this entire complex of questions, in relation to which I have been pleased to investigate just one particularly crass symptom, in a test tube as it were.

I thank you all for your attention.
