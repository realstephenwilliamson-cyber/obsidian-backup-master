## 6. Epistemological Applications of the Theory of Recognition

TOG

The affairs \[sthiti\] of humanity are born of the recognitive synthesis \[anusamdhāna} of experiences \[jñāna}\] which are different from one another and do not \[by themselves\] apprehend one another. These \[affairs) would be destroyed if there were not the Great Lord, who contains within \[Himself\] the infinite forms of the universe, is unitary, has the nature of consciousness, and sesses the Cognition, Memory and Semantic Exclusion \[apohana}

Saktis.

—IPK 1.3.6-7, 1:136-39

We now tum to the Śaivas’ attempt to demonstrate the theory of recognition explained in the previous chapters in the arena of epistemology. This chapter will not attempt to survey the enormous range of epistemological issues treated by the Pratyabhijñā thinkers.' It is concerned rather with the implementations of a simultaneously argumentative and ritual agenda. I remind the reader of some of the homologous schemes in terms of which we have come to understand this agenda. The Śaivas endeavor inferentially to elicit the recognition of identity with the Supreme Lord by showing through transcendental inquiry that all the items of experience are one’s Śakti—who is Herself one’s self-recognition. This is a purification of conceptualization, which reintegrates the Lord’s self-recognition fragmented in ordinary linguistic ascription. It is a removal of the obstacles that obscure the ever-existent fact that everything is one’s self-recognition as Śiva.

As explained in chapter 3, the two chief Śaktis that are invoked in summaries of the Pratyabhijñā inference—operative in the areas of

*[p. 108]* ^pg128

epistemology and ontology—are those of Cognition and Action. In the two main philosophical sections of the Pratyabhijñā texts, the Book on Cognition and the Book on Action, a number of subsidiary topics are treated; the reality underlying these topics is often given designations

as various subsidiary Śaktis.

The passage of Utpaladeva quoted above divides the modalities of the Lord’s self-recognition operative within the area of epistemology into three chief Śaktis. These are Cognition per se, Memory, and Semantic Exclusion (apohana) (which will be explained below).? This chapter will consider the basic arguments by which the Śaivas attempt to explain each of these topics in terms of modalities of the Lord’s self-recognition designated as separate Śaktis. Like the Śaivas, I will devote most attention to the Cognition Śakti.?

Cognition as akti

I will begin the discussion of the first topic with some general remarks. The main word being translated as cognition is jñāna.\* This word is a derivative of the root jñā, and is related both to the word “knowledge” and to the word “cognition.” In treating the subject of cognition, the Śaivas are mostly concerned with perception (referred to as pratyakṣa, “direct perception,” and anubhava, “direct experience”).5 For the sake of convenience, I will use the terms cognition and perception somewhat interchangeably.

Another point is that cognition is understood in Sanskrit epistemological discussions to occur in an episodic manner—it is not a disposition. Though cognition is episodic, information gained through it can be retrieved through memory. A further characteristic of Indian understandings of cognition is that each episode must yield new information. Thus, although memory may be completely valid in its recovery of the information from an original cognition, it is not classified as a separate means of cognition (pramāna).”

The arguments by which the Cognition Śakti is demonstrated may roughly be divided by two terminological focuses. First, there are arguments centering around the term prakāśa. This is the same word as “self-luminosity” without the reflexive prefix sva; it may accordingly be translated as “light, illumination” or “awareness.” Generally I will use the latter gloss, and I will gloss cognate verbs as “appears.” Second, there are arguments centering around the term vimarśa and cognates derived from mrś, which have been discussed in the previous chapter. Despite much attention to these two categories of terms in studies of the Pratyabhijñā theory of cognition, I believe that they have not been properly understood. Even where there is acknowledgment of their ultimate identity, they are discussed almost like two separate epistemological “juices” that are being mixed.” There has not been a careful examination of the precise logic of their relation in the context of the overarching problematics of the system.

I would characterize the relation of the arguments about perception centering around the different terms as follows: The arguments utilizing the term prakāśa are largely concerned with a reduction of perceived entities to the fact of awareness itself. Vimarśa and related terms do not describe a factor in experience supplementing prakāśa. Rather they clarify that the awareness to which objects have been idealistically reduced is necessarily recognition. The force of the arguments together is that the perception of objects is an expression of the

Lord’s self-recognition.!??

I believe that each of these phases of argumentation elucidate corresponding moments in the classic Christian philosophical theology of logos. While a more thorough demonstration of this claim will be made in the last chapter using the example of Bernard Lonergan, a few comparative remarks will be offered here to facilitate an initial appreciation of the Śaiva ideas.

The Idealistic Prakāśa Theory

The arguments centering around the term prakāśa seem to be most directly inspired by the idealistic Buddhist school of Yogācāra or Vijñānavāda.” The opponents in these discussions are those who

*[p. 110]* ^pg130

believe in the existence of objects external to awareness, particularly the Naiyāyikas in the Hindu tradition and the Sautrāntikas in the Buddhist. The formulations of the Buddhist logicians, whose ambiguity about whether objects are external to awareness has given them the epithet Yogācāra-Sautrāntikas, are temporarily left aside.

The Saiva discussion of prakāśa develops some of the meanings of their own doctrine of self-luminosity (svaprakāśatva). As stated in chapter 3, the backgrounds to the Saiva understanding of this concept include both the Advaita Vedāntin understanding of self-luminosity and the Buddhist logicians’ understanding of the component of self-consciousness (svasamvedana) integral to each experience." Our present concern is not the Śaivas’ utilization of this idea to explain the simplicity of monistic experience. Important now is the Śaivas positing in this concept, like the other two schools, a self-aware subjectivity that epistemically grounds every cognition, so that one “knows that one knows.”

The thrust of the prakāśa arguments is that this component of subjective awareness integral to every cognition constitutes the objects

of these cognitions. Utpala states:

If the object did not have the nature of awareness \[prakāśa\]), it would be without illumination \[aprakăśa\], as it was before \[its appearance\]. Awareness \[prakāśsa\] cannot be different \[than the object\]. Awareness \[prakăśatā\] is the essential nature of the object."

The argument is based on the analytical fact that we can only be aware of that of which we are aware. For this reason, it is held to be impossible for there to be an object outside of awareness. An object is nothing but the awareness of it.”

The Śaivas further take up an attempt to infer the existence of external objects. This follows the epistemology of the Sautrāntika school of Buddhism, which may be classified as “representationalist.” According to these thinkers, awareness is in itself without any differentiation.’ On the other hand our experience does present a diversity of appearances—for example of different colors such as blue and yellow. External objects are inferred as the causes of this diversity. Utpala states the basic Sautrāntika inference:

The cause of each of the various manifestations is unknown. This necessitates the inference of the external \[object\]. For awareness, which is undifferentiated, cannot cause the marvelously diverse manifestations.”

*[p. 111]* ^pg131

These external objects cause such manifestations as their reflections

(pratibimba).”?

The Śaivas respond on the basis of the understanding held by the majority of Indian philosophical schools that the inference of a relationship between a cause and an effect depends upon the observation of their invariable succession in a series of direct perceptions.” As Utpala explains, the Sautrāntika inference simply lacks the necessary

experiential foundation:

Inference is definitely not accepted regarding something which has not appeared previously. . . . There has never in any way been the manifestation of an object external to manifestation. Therefore there is also not the establishment of it by inference.?

Abhinavagupta makes an additional reductio ad absurdum argument based on the understanding that inference itself is a kind of awareness. This reductio shows the necessity of the Saiva alternative.

Abhinava explains:

Inference, as a conceptual construction \[vikalpa\], is \[a kind of\] awareness \[prakāśa\]. If the object such as blue, etc., is not comprehended even by this \[kind of awareness which is inference}, then it cannot be inferred. If \[the object\] is comprehended \[by awareness\], then it has the essential nature of awareness \[prakāśa\], according to the principle “The object would lack illumination \[aprakāśa\], as it did before.”? It is not external. Therefore, any means of cognition \[pramāna}\] which is adduced to prove that there is an external object, on the contrary, only proves its non-externality. Thus it is contradictory.”

According to the Śaivas, the representationalist inference is not only unfounded and illogical, but also epistemologically useless. Ordinary behavior can be accounted for without the hypothesis of exter-

nal objects. Abhinavagupta states:

You attempt to establish external objects with a problematic conjecture \[kaṣtṭakalpanā, i.e., the inference\]. Nothing can be accomplished through those {conjectured external objects\]. For worldly behavior is established solely through those manifestations \[ābhāsa\], which you have admitted. There is no worldly behavior with something which is always an object of inference.”

The prakāśa arguments elucidate an analogous idealistic conception in the Christian philosophical theology of logos. This idealism

*[p. 112]* ^pg132

directly follows from the explanation of the origin of all things from God’s internal or “subjective” process of self-recognition as discussed in the previous chapter. As Augustine affirms, “It is true of all his creatures, both spiritual and corporeal, that he does not know them because they are, but that they are because he knows them.” To repeat part of a quotation already made from Anselm:

Hence, when the supreme Spirit expresses himself, he expresses all created beings. For, both before they were created, and now that they have been created, and after they are decayed or changed in any way, they are ever in him not what they are in themselves, but what this Spirit himself is.7

Again, for Thomas, God knows all things within Himself:

Whatever effects pre-exist in God, as in the first cause, must be in His act of understanding, and all things must be in Him according to an intelligible mode: for everything which is in another is in it according to the mode of that in which it is.”

Knowledge in general is just a formal cause that must be followed by an act of will to bring things about; however in God the connection is more direct because His knowledge and will are one:

Now it is manifest that God causes things by His intellect, since His being is His act of understanding; and hence His knowledge must be the cause of things, in so far as His will is joined to it. Hence the knowledge of God as the cause of things is usually

called the knowledge of approbation.”

The reduction of phenomena to their foundation in divine subjectivity is a persisting feature in a variety of metaphysical understandings of the Christian logos, whether the explanation is made in terms of Platonic Ideas, the Aristotelian divine act of intellect,” or Hegelian logic.”

I believe that Bishop George Berkeley's notorious formulation that esse est percipi only thematized and emphasized this conception. Granting the inevitable differences, many of Berkeley's demonstrations of this thesis are quite analogous to the Saiva prakāśa arguments. Basing himself on the notion that all we directly experience are “percepts” or “ideas” (in the broad sense including percepts), he contended that it is impossible to posit the existence of such ideas apart from a

subject. Here is a representative explanation:

*[p. 113]* ^pg133

That neither our thoughts, nor passions, nor ideas formed by the imagination, exist without the mind, is what everybody will allow.—And to me it is no less evident that the various SENSATIONS, or ideas imprinted on the sense, however blended or combined together (that is, whatever objects they compose), cannot exist otherwise than in a mind perceiving them—I think an intuitive knowledge may be obtained of this by anyone that shall attend to what is meant by the term exist when applied to sensible things. The table I write on I say exists, that is, I see and feel it; and if I were out of my study I should say it existed—meaning thereby that if I was in my study I might perceive it, or that some other spirit actually does perceive it. There was an odor, that is, it was smelt; there was a sound, that is, it was heard; a colour or figure, and it was perceived by sight or touch. This is all I can understand by these and like expressions.—For as to what is said of the absolute existence of unthinking things without any relation to their being perceived, that is to me perfectly unintelligible. Their esse is percipi, nor is it possible that they should have any existence out of the minds or thinking things which perceive them.”?

As the Śaivas refuted the Sautrāntika representationalism, Berkeley contended that Locke’s position that separate substances cause ideas is unfounded.” Berkeley also held that the very conception of an object external to awareness is a “manifest repugnancy.” He explains:

When we do our utmost to conceive of the existence of external bodies, we are all the while only contemplating our own ideas. But the mind, taking no notice of itself, is deluded to think it can and does conceive bodies existing unthought of or without the mind, though at the same time they are apprehended by or exist in itself. A little attention will discover to any one the truth and evidence of what is here said, and make it unnecessary to insist on any other proofs against the existence of material substance.”

As the Śaivas contended that valid worldly behavior can be accounted for solely in terms of experiences—so did Berkeley understand his immaterialist hypothesis as supporting common sense and behaviors, along with science, on the basis of ideas alone.

However, Berkeley, as much as the most realist Aristotelians, affirmed the existence of phenomena outside of the subjectivity of the limited individual. He avoided solipsism, and guaranteed the regularity of experience by positing the continuous existence of the ideas constituting the world in the divine subjectivity:

*[p. 114]* ^pg134

The question between the materialists and me is not, whether things have a real existence out of the mind of this or that person, but whether they have an absolute existence, distinct from being perœeived by God, and exterior to all minds. This indeed some heathens and philosophers have affirmed, but whoever entertains notions of the Deity suitable to holy scriptures, will be of another

opinion.

. . . Every unthinking being is necessarily, and from the very

nature of its existence, perceived by some mind; if not by any finite created mind, yet certainly by the infinite mind of God, in whom “we live, move, and have our being.”

Here we must consider an important point that is connected with the prakāśa arguments, but discussed in other sections of the Pratyabhijñā texts. This point also marks a divergence of the Śaivas from most of Christian orthodoxy, and puts them in line with the more monistic expressions of Western idealism, for instance, of Eckhart, Fichte, Hegel, and others. The Śaivas contend that there is in the ultimate perspective only one subject. In the introductory chapter, while discussing the presence of the Cognition and Action Śaktis in all beings, Utpaladeva and Abhinava explain that the cognition of others is supposed or trusted (ühyate) from their observed action.\* However, this understanding of the cognition of others is only provisional. Abhinava reduces the presumed awareness of others to one subject in the same way that objects have been reduced:

Cognition does not appear as \[an object other than oneself expressed} “this.” For “thisness” \[as externality to awareness\] is non-cognition. Something which has appeared with one nature could not have appeared with another. Therefore, only cognition appears. And that which appears has only the nature of “I.” Even the cognition of others is nothing but one’s own Self. Otherness is entirely due to accidental attributes \[upādhi\] such as the body, etc. And that \[an accidental attribute such as the body\] has been determined not to be other \[than awareness). Thus everything falls under the category of the subject. The subject is really unitary. And He alone exists. Thus it has been said: “There is only the awareness of one’s Self as oneself and others.”” Therefore, beginning with “Bhagavan Sadăśiva cognizes” and ending with “The worm cognizes”—there is only one subject. Consequently, all cognitions \[by apparently different

subjects really\] belong to that \[one\] subject.”

The Śaiva arguments are so strongly idealistic that a repugnant solipsism is replaced by a conception of a universal awareness. Individual

Once the principle of esse est percipi is accepted, it appears more economical to argue in this manner than to attempt to justify the existence of other selves.” I will offer some final remarks on this divergence between the Pratyabhijñā and mainstream Christianity at the end of chapter 8.

Returning to the discussion of perception per se—according to Abhinavagupta, the refutation of external objects as causes of the diversity of perception establishes the Śaiva doctrine of the Lordship of awareness.“ The idea that objects are nothing but a single awareness entails that they are emanated by it. The capacity of emanation was explained in the third chapter to be the basic significance of Sakti, the definitive characteristic of Lordship.

Utpala presents the Śaiva explanation, supporting the theory with

a widely accepted analogy:

Indeed God \[deva\], whose nature is only consciousness, can, from the force of His will and without material, cause the mass of objects which are contained within Him to appear \[prakāśayet} as external. \[In this creation He is} like a yogin.”

Abhinava explains Utpala’s analogy as follows: The creations of a yogin are held to be real, not merely fantasy, dream or the like. The yogin produces them without material and other external causes such as are observed in a potter's production of a pot. Again, Abhinava explains that it is because consciousness creates objects through mere will, that Utpaladeva uses the expression “God.”®

The Integrality of Vimarśa

Thus the prakāśa arguments, as demonstrating that the subject has the character of emanation that is called Śakti, in themselves already point toward the inferential conclusion of the identity of the perceiver with the Lord. However, the Śaiva position has not been fully distinguished from that of the Vijñānavāda/Yogācāra. The arguments of the Yogācāra-Sautrāntika Buddhists against recognition considered in the fourth chapter have not been answered.“ They are answered with the arguments centering around vimarśa and other terms derived from the root mróś—which show that awareness is necessarily a process of recognition.

Now, corresponding to the identification of Supreme Speech with the Lord’s self-recognition (as explained in the last chapter), the Śaivas develop these arguments out of earlier discussions of Bhartphari. Thus Abhinava supports the Śaiva thesis with excerpts from the Vākyapadīya:

*[p. 116]* ^pg136

It has also been said by the honorable Bhartrhari: “There is no cognition in the world which is without the accompaniment of speech. Every cognition is experienced as if permeated by speech. If there were to end the eternal condition of cognition having the nature of speech, awareness could not be aware. It is this \[condition that cognition has the nature of speech} which makes possible recognitive judgment \[pratyavamarśini\].” And also: “It \[speech\] is the consciousness of all beings subject to transmigratory existence, and it exists both internally and externally. If there were an end to that \[speech\], \[one\] would be observed to be unconscious, like wood or a wall.”s

Śiva'’s Supreme Speech/self-recognition and Bhartrhari’s Word Absolute are monistic principles of interpretation from which the universe is said to be created. According to both the Śaiva and Grammarian philosophies, the primordial unity of the source necessarily pervades and interrelates both poles of ordinary interpretations and the states of affairs to which they are recognized to apply. By their heredity, interpretation must be the way to reality and reality must be what is interpreted. The Western logos as a grounding order of reality and rationality issues the same transcendental entailment, whether in Greek, Judeo-Christian or post-Enlightenment, secularized expressions. The arguments against relativism propounded in chapter 1 using the insight embodied in Epimenides’ paradox and the theories of Davidson and Apel should all be seen as reflecting on this larger Western tradition of logos. Those arguments all demonstrate that we cannot make sense of interpretations that do not refer to a noncontingent reality.

The Church Fathers contended that all knowledge is achieved through the presence in humans of Christ in the form of logos

spermatikos. As Justin Martyr explains:

We have been taught that Christ was First-begotten of God \[the Father) and we have indicated above that He is the Word of whom all mankind partakes. Those who lived by reason are Christians, even though they are considered atheists.\*

St. Augustine’s influential interpretation conceives the divine Word, which contains the Platonic Ideas, as providing an inner illumination by which all humans attain knowledge.” Augustine argues in more directly linguistic terms that humans, as images of God, have within them by participation a very subtle form of word correlated

*[p. 117]* ^pg137

with all epistemic acts. This is the essential language expressed through the media of concrete languages such as Greek, Latin, Hebrew, or

bodily gestures.®

Thomas Aquinas explains that we have in us a preexisting potential knowledge of the order found in various objects of experience:

For certain seeds of knowledge pre-exist in us, namely, the first concepts of understanding, which by the light of the agent intellect are immediately known through the species abstracted from sensible things. These are either complex, as axioms, or simple, as the notions of being, of the one, and so on, which the understanding grasps immediately. In these general principles, however, all the consequences are included as in certain seminal principles. When, therefore, the mind is led from these general notions to actual knowledge of the particular things, which it knew previously in general and, as it were, potentially, then one is said to

acquire knowledge.”

In the contemporary intellectual scene, there are some strong analogies between the Śaiva arguments for the necessity of recognition and Martin Heidegger's conception of truth as disclosure.” While Heidegger affirms the relationship of his conception to Platonic anamnesis and Christian logos theology, he claims its more authentic source as the Presocratic conception of truth as alētheia, “unveiling.” The linguistic disclosure of beings is the most basic cognitive, and behavior-

ally orientative, experience:

All working and achieving, all action and calculation, keep within an open region within which beings, with regard to what they are and how they are, can properly take their stand and become capable of being said. This can occur only if beings present themselves along with the presentative statement so that the latter subordinates itself to the directive that it speaks of beings such-as

they are....

A statement is invested with its correctness by the openness

of comportment; for only through the latter can what is opened up really become the standard for the presentative correspondence. ... But if the correctness (truth) of statements becomes possible only through this openness of comportment, then what first makes correctness possible must with more original right be taken as the essence of truth."

In other words, all seeing is seeing as.”

*[p. 118]* ^pg138

Although Heidegger was trained in, and deeply influenced by theology, he repudiates the Greek and Christian metaphysics of logos as “ontotheology.” The implications of his thought for establishing this metaphysics are thus profoundly ambivalent. Is what is disclosed only historically and linguistically contingent beings or a universal Being identical with or emanated from God? The former direction of understanding leads into (self-contradictory) historicist relativism and Derridean deconstructionism, and the latter back into transcendental theology, for example, of Karl Rahner, John Macquarrie, and David Tracy. David Tracy's synthesis of hermeneutics with Lonergan’s metaphysics reintegrates disclosure theory into the Christian logos theology. Tracy describes the experience of truth with our very term, as recognition.® As I have indicated, the Pratyabhijñā will be comparatively engaged with Derrida and Lonergan in the last chapter.

To return to the Sanskrit texts, one of the vimarśa arguments that Abhinavagupta developed out of Bhartrhari is that cognition must be intrinsically linguistic to account for the possibility of the learning of conventional language.™ Abhinava explains that initially a small boy will have a series of cognitions lacking gross conceptual construction (nirvikalpaka). For example, he hears language as a series of incomprehensible sounds, and he sees the ground with and without a pot. It is due to the fact that objects are generated from the higher form of speech, that the connection is possible:

According to this system, the object actually consists entirely of speech, because it consists of recognitive judgment \[vimarśa\]. .. . Thus things can be verbalized \[śabadanam\] by the entire collection of words. Then a fixed association with a word is created.®

Abhinava compares the linguistic interpretation intrinsic to experience to pointing with the finger. On its basis the boy comes to learn the reference of expressions such as “This is a pot” and “Fetch it.”% Another of Bhartrhari’s arguments concerns the necessary

retrievability of the information gained from cognitions in memory. He gives as examples very simple perceptions such as the sensations of earth and grass while walking quickly, along with states of unconsciousness such as sleep and stupor. It is only if there is linguistic interpretation even in such experiences that one is able to remember them.” Abhinavagupta only briefly mentions this idea in the IPV. He states that one could not recall that one was in a state of stupor if there were not such an inner judgment (parāmarśa).®

*[p. 119]* ^pg139

The chief argument made by Utpaladeva for the linguistic-recognitive nature of cognition recalls Bhartrhari’s discussion of walking rapidly through the grass. Utpaladeva’s argument draws attention to the need for a kind of recognitive ordering in the perception accom-

panying rapid behavior:

There is recognitive judgement \[vimarśa}\] even at the moment of direct perception. Otherwise, without recognitive synthesis \[pratisamdhāna\], how would running, etc., be possible?”

Abhinava states that in the case of running there must be a continuous recognition of the destinations that one successively aims for and attains. The same is the case with other rapid behaviors such as speaking and reading, in which one moves the tongue between the places of articulation.® In these behaviors there is not time for the activation of gross linguistic reference. Abhinava explains:

In these cases, there is not experienced the gross conceptual construction which occurs later. Therefore, rapidity must be due to subtle recognitive judgement \[pratyavamarśa\], consisting of the

interpretive application \[bhāvanā\] of contracted speech.“'

There is a further area of the Śaiva theory of cognition that is a direct outcome of their understanding of the transcendental character of recognition. This mode of thinking is elaborated in a complex manner in a variety of terminologies throughout the texts, and I can only give an idea of it here. The Śaivas invert the Buddhist understandings of the relations of the experiences of universals and particulars. The Buddhists hold that the primary awareness is of unique particulars (svalakṣana), and that the synthetic linguistic constructions invalidly applied to them take the form of universals (sāmāny) or relations (sambandha). The Śaivas, on the other hand, make the recognition of linguistic syntheses primary, and hold that particulars are constructed at a secondary level through the synthesis of these syntheses. I will quote a passage from Abhinavagupta’s discussion of means of cognition (pramāṇa). This explains the construction of particulars through the synthesis of universals with location and time—which, as will be seen in the next chapter, are themselves understood as conceptual syntheses:

There is the means of cognition {pramāna\] through the power of recognitive judgement \[vimarśa\]. Recognitive judgement \[vimarśa\]

*[p. 120]* ^pg140

is animated by the word \[śabda\]. And the word \[sabda\] is employed regarding only a single manifestation which is untouched by other manifestations, such as place, time, etc. \[Examples of expressions for such isolated manifestations are} “pot” and “red.” Therefore, the means of cognition \[pramāăna\] operates regarding a manifestation which is as a universal; for with it there is no mixture of place and time, which would make it into a unique particular \[svalakṣaṇa\]. . . . However, the unique particular \[svalakṣana\] is another, different sort of unitary manifestation. It has the nature of the manifestation of location in the same substratum, of the manifestations of those \[i.e., place, time, etc.\].€

In this mode of explanation the Śaivas attempt to achieve a double victory. The perceptions of both sorts of entities are shown to depend

intimately on synthesis.®

Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta further develop the force of this mode of explanation against the Buddhists in a variety of ways. Thus, they deny the Buddhist position of the primary status of the unique particulars (svalakṣaṇa) by contending that they are apprehended by the synthesis of the universal categories according to taste, purpose, and education.“ For example, a pot will appear as very different things according to its conceived uses as a container for water, a source of profit or a hard object. One who for some reason is broken-hearted will only see that “It is”! The Śaivas also subvert the Buddhist criterion of the reality of unique particulars, i.e., causal efficacy (arthakriyā)—the capacity to do things. A classic example is that only the phenomenal, particular rather than the conceptual fire can burn. For the Śaivas, causal efficacy is just one more concept synthesized into the particular. They further emphasize its secondary character by specifying that it can only be included in the synthesis of an entity when there is also

included its apparent externality.“

Such are the basic arguments on cognition centering around the term vimarśa, and some of those closely connected with them.” Now, as I have already suggested, the idealistic prakāśa arguments make the recognition integral to the experience of an object constitutive of it. The following passage from Abhinava's summary of the Pratyabhijñā system in the first chapter clearly places vimarśa in the algebra of esse est percipit:

Here, as the multiplicity of things are recognitively apprehended \[vimrśyate\], so they exist \[asti\]. This is so because Being \[astitva\] depends upon awareness \[prakāśa\]. That is, there is the manifestation

*[p. 121]* ^pg141

of Being as depending on the recognitive judgement \[vimarsa\] regarding what is brought about through this awareness \[prakāsa\]. For if a thing were not recognitively ascertained \[avimrṣta\], then what could be the answer to the question of why it is not blue, not yellow, not existent, not nonexistent?“ Therefore, something exists as much and in whatever way it is recognitively apprehended \[vimrśyate\] and uncontradicted.“

It is this idealism that gives the Saiva theory greater force against skeptics than merely establishing interpretive realism. Because the awareness to which everything has been reduced is unitary, recognition must be self-recognition.” Because this self-recognition emanates everything, it has the metaphysical and soteriological character described in the previous chapter. The way in which the epistemological argumentation demonstrates the reality of Śiva-Śakti is brought out in a fuller excerpt from the passage quoted in the epigraph of that chapter:

The Sakti of being Creator,” which has the nature of Lordship, contains all the Śaktis. That \[Sakti of being Creator\] has the nature of recognitive judgement \[vimarsa\]. Therefore it is proper that only it is predominant. . . .? The Supreme Lord, who has the nature of awareness \[prakāśa\], makes His own Self into an object of cognition, even though it is not an object of cognition, because the cognizer is unitary. This is deduced \[sambhāvyate\] by means of a firm inference for the proposition \[sambhāvanā\], which includes the demonstration of the impossibility of another cause.” “Therefore” means that this is so by reason of \[His\] creator-hood \[kartrtvāt\], characterized as the Sakti which is recognitive judgement \[vimarśa\]. As He recognitively apprehends \[parāmrśati) His Self, so, because everything is contained within Him, He appears

as \[objects such as\] blue, etc.”

The Pratyabhijñā does not need like Christian orthodoxy to metaphysically distinguish limited recognizers from the cosmic one. The thinkers’ epistemological argumentation constantly leads one to recognize that one is the Supreme Lord on the basis of seeing that all things are nothing but one’s own self-recognition. This is illustrated by a casual remark of Abhinavagupta on a verse of Utpaladeva in the discussion of cognition. Though very brief, I will mention it here because it parallels a more explicit statement by Abhinavagupta in the section on memory. This verse follows a verse in which Utpaladeva identifies consciousness (citi) with self-recognition (pratyavamarśa) and Supreme Speech (parāvāk).” According to Abhinavagupta, Utpaladeva

*[p. 122]* ^pg142

in the verse in question supports his position on the basis of the principle scriptures.” It will be recalled that in explanations of the soteriological recognition treated in the third chapter, Abhinava places the knowledge of the Lord from the scriptures in the position of memory. Utpaladeva’s verse actually predicates of consciousness some

traditional theological qualifications:

She is Creative Trembling \[sphurattā\] and Great Being \[mahāsattā\], and is unqualified by time and place. As the Essence \[sāratayā\], this is She \[saiṣa\] who is said to be the heart of the Supreme.”

Abhinavagupta comments: “ ‘This is She’ \[saiṣa\] indicates the recognition \[pratyabhijñāna\] of Śakti.“” Means and goal are of course identical.

Recognition and Coherence

It is important to understand that the treatment of cognition so far has focused on the ultimate grounds of successful recognitions of interpretation. lt does not address the important problem of how to arbitrate between the diverse recognitions that people make. I direct the reader to a quotation of Abhinavagupta above which states that “something exists as much and in whatever way it is recognitively apprehended \[vimrśyate\] and uncontradicted \[abādhita\].”"® Utpala and Abhinava similarly define a means of cognition \[pramānṇa\] as having the characteristic of bringing about a recognitive judgment (vimarśa) that is uncontradicted (abādhita).” A classic example of contradiction is when what was thought to be silver on closer examination turns out to be mother of pearl. According to the Śaivas, the discovery of contradictions such as this itself depends upon the recognitive act of the Self /Śiva uniting

prior and later experiences.®?

With regard to the generation of particulars through the synthesis of synthetic experiences, Abhinavagupta explains that the limiting factor of such synthesis is also a contradictoriness that obtains between particular manifestations. Again, this contradictoriness is ema-

nated by Śiva's “Fixed Regularity” (niyati) Śakti:

\[There may be the question:\]) “But what is the limit of the mixing of manifestations?” To this it is replied: Only those manifestations of which there is noncontradiction \[avirodha\] become mixed.” For the manifestation of form does not become mixed with the manifestation of air because of contradiction. And that contradiction is

brought about by the Fixed Regularity Śakti.®

*[p. 123]* ^pg143

According to the Śaiva epistemology, the Lord creates the rightness and wrongness in the combinations of what is recognized.

The notion that contradiction (bādha, virodha) between interpretations is an indication of error—or conversely that noncontradiction is characteristic of what is yet unfalsified—is common in Indian philosophy, and should not be difficult for us to accept. What I wish to observe is the significance of this conception in the light of the prakāśa arguments that there are no objects external to awareness. It is not possible to assess the correspondence of recognized interpretations with any external, uninterpreted datum. Noncontradiction thus becomes in effect a criterion of coherence. It is an ongoing assessment of internal as well as external coherence that arbitrates between the diverse recognitions pertaining to perception, along with the claims of scriptures, inferences, and so forth.“

David Tracy, in developing the hermeneutic disclosure theory for theology, has likewise specified additional criteria to evaluate claims of disclosure/recognition. According to him, such claims should be assessed as to coherence as well as practical, ethical consequences.” I agree that the criterion of practice thematizes an important area of common concern. The Śaivas themselves appeal to utility (upayoga) in addition to persistence/coherence (sthairya) in at least one discussion, to defend the reality of action, relation, and other categories involving a unification-of-multiplicity.\* However, I believe that practice is actually also assessed in terms of the criterion of coherence. We should include in evaluations of coherence our best understandings of practical utility and ethics.

Tracy proposes the triad of recognition, coherence, and practice for the mediation of claims in interreligious dialogue. He particularly associates the search for coherence with fundamental theology; for him the transcendental inquiry into ultimate of reality is a search for “ultimate coherence.”” The Śaivas do not similarly explain the whole Pratyabhijñā discourse as a search for the most noncontradictory /
coherent interpretation. However, I suggest that this would be a useful way of describing their along with other projects of philosophical dialogue—as characterized in chapter 1—in the light of the under-

standing of all cognition as recognition.

### The Memory Sakti

The Śaivas again explain the ordinary, as opposed to the transcendental, memory as a modality of recognition designated as the Memory

*[p. 124]* ^pg144

Sakti. The person is demonstrated to remember because of possessing the Memory Sakti of the Lord.® By elucidating this, the Pratyabhijñā enables the student to participate in the Lord's self-recognition.

To proceed, 1 recall the Buddhists’ refutation of the Nyāya understanding of the self as a substratum for mnemonic impressions (samskāra, vāsanā). The Buddhists argued that memory is accomplished through impressions alone, in a phenomenal series without a substratum. The Saivas in turn demonstrate the self-recognitive nature of memory through pointing out features of it which cannot be accounted for by impressions alone. First, they acknowledge that impressions could account for the similarity (sādṭśya) of memory with the original

experience of an object.” Abhinava explains:

By the impression \[samskāra\] which was produced by the previous experience this much is accomplished: That cognition \[which is memory\] is not produced by the object \[of the original experience\]. Nevertheless \[memory\] has that as its object.”

Abhinava analogizes the function of impressions in memory to the way elasticity enables a branch that has been bent to return to its

previous position.”

However, there is more in memory to be explained than its mere similarity to an original experience of an object. If memory were only a sort of copy of the original experience of an object, it would still have the expression typified “this.” “This” is an ascription having the significance of something that is present to perception.” The memory of the object however has the typical expression “that.” That is, it is qualified as previously experienced. Now, according to the Śaivas, such a qualification requires that there is some sort of awareness in memory of the previous experience itself as well as its object.”

In the inquiry regarding this awareness, special considerations for the continuity of the self are brought in, which address the Buddhist challenge to the subject in the Pratyabhijñā inference. These considerations depend upon premises rather foreign to Western understanding. As explained above, Indian philosophy looks at cognition as occurring in an episodic manner. The Saivas contend that because the self-luminosity that the Buddhist philosophers understand to validate each episode is integral to and temporary like the episode itself—it is impossible for one episode, memory, to know the episode

that was the previous experience. Utpala explains:

*[p. 125]* ^pg145

The cognition which is memory is produced from the impression \[samskrti\] of a previous experience. However, since it is contained within itself \[Ztmaniṣtha\]}, it does not make known the first experience. A cognition is only self-luminous. It is not the cognitive object of another \[cognition\], just as the cognition of taste \[is not the object\] of the cognition of color.”

Thus the qualification of the object as previously experienced would be impossible on the Buddhist account.

The Śaivas avoid the problem of objectivizing one cognition to another through the idea of the continuing self. It is the continuous self that enables the memory both to comprehend an original object, and to distinguish it as previously perceived. Utpaladeva explains:

There could be no manifestation of an object which is being remembered, if it were different from the memory itself. Therefore, there is a unity of experiences in different times. This is that Cognizer. The former experience does not appear separately in memory like an object. For there is the manifestation of it as resting in the Self

\[in the manner expressed\]: “1 previously experienced.””®

The qualification in memory of an object as previously experienced is a judgment regarding the experience of the continuing self.

Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta point out another necessary feature of memory that the Buddhists cannot explain without a recognitive connection of experiential episodes. This is the comprehension of the similarity between the episodes of the original experience and the memory

itself:

How is there the ascertainment . . . of similarity? The perceptual cognition \[which is past\] does not cause one to ascertain similarity. Nor does the cognition which is memory. Since \[the original perception and memory\] do not know each other, there is no possibility for the ascertainment of the similarity between the two. And \[according to the Buddhists\] there does not exist another \[i.e., a Self\] which knows both of them.”

This point furthers the Śaivas’ development of a distinctive understanding of self-luminosity in the context of memory. The Buddhist cannot account for a sort of recognition within memory that validates

its authenticity.”

*[p. 126]* ^pg146

The Śaivas emphasize again that the validating self-consciousness that the Buddhists admit cannot be episodic with experiences. An enduring subject is the epistemic ground of both direct experience and

memory. Abhinavagupta explains:

Therefore, this {memory} is possible in this manner: The self-consciousness \[svasamvedana\] of memory is the same as the self-consciousness \[svasamvedana\] of perceptual experience. Nothing other than self-consciousness is able to bring about that \[memory\]. Neither direct-perception nor inference can. Thus is established the principle which is the subject \[pramātrtattva\], which is the uninterrupted self-consciousness, continuing through all that time \[between the original experience and the memory). It is not possible for the one who remembers to be different from the one who

\[originally\] experiences.”

Inasmuch as the recognitions integral to the temporal and validating aspects of memory pertain to the experience of the subject, they are expressions of self-recognition. The Śaivas thus reduce memory to a modality of such self-recognition designated as the Memory Śakti. This is brought out in the following definition of Abhinavagupta:

At some previous time there was an extroverted awareness \[semvedana\] of a manifestation. \[However,\] the essential nature of consciousness \[cit\] of that \[awareness\] is introverted; that remains within His Self at another time. He recognitively ascertains \[parāmrśati) His \[previous\] extroversion towards a particular ob-

ject \[viṣaya\]. Such is the Memory Sakti.”

Memory is a recognition by the Self/Lord of His previous “extroverted awareness” (samvedanam bahirmukham), that is, awareness of an

object.

As explained above, the Śaivas maintain that all perception is nothing but the expression of the Lord’s self-recognition. The Śaiva understanding of memory as the Lord's self-recognition articulates the same notion of a freedom from all extrinsic causality by objects. The following passage of Abhinavagupta more fully explains the process of memory, including the role in it of impressions, in a manner that brings out its total subjectivity:

At the time of the previous experience, an object is made separate \[from the Lord as highest subject\] through association with place,

*[p. 127]* ^pg147

time, and a subject who is other \[than the highest subject\]. It is not dissolved in I-hood.™ It is then as if covered by darkness.'®? Remaining as such \[as the object originally experienced separately from the highest subject, along with its concomitants), it is what is called an impression \[samskāra\]. Then He removes its cover of darkness. When that \[darkness\] is removed, then that \[object\] appears, like before, as separated. It may be objected that it would then appear, like before, as “this.” It is not so. For there is the awareness \[prakāśa\] of it without loss of the connection with the separated body, etc. of the manifestation of that \[original\] time. The recognitive judgement \[parāmarśa\] of the time of the memory, which is a manifestation of this \[present\] time, also does not disappear. Thus the recognitive judgement \[parāmarsa\] “that” is explained to have the essential nature of the recognitive judgement \[parāmarśa\] of both the prior and the later, which are contrary. In this manner, that Supreme Lord remembers.

The Pratyabhijñā agenda of course is to demonstrate this sort of deus ex machina in every sphere of explanation.

Such are the main lines of the Śaiva demonstration of the selfrecognitive nature of memory. However, I must briefly take up one other issue raised in the course of the discussion which is critical to the success of the Śaivas’ argumentative agenda. The Buddhist claims that he does not need to give an adequate explanation of the synthetic features of memory because memory, as a sort of conceptualization, is an illusion.’ This objection in a sense moves the disagreement with the Buddhists to a more radical level than the discussion of cognition. Because the Buddhist logicians, as phenomenalists, accept that there is such a thing as valid cognition, the vimarśa arguments% only have to show that, contrary to their understanding, this cognition must be

intrinsically recognitive.

Here, however, the Śaivas need to establish that memory is valid in order for their demonstration of its nature as the self-recognition/
Śakti of the Supreme Lord to be allowed to come into play. The Śaivas respond to the Buddhist criticism by making the validity of memory hinge upon the necessary validity of something else—ordinary experience and behavior (vyavahāra). Memory functions in the synthesis of personal identity. It is also essential to forming the desire for repeating pleasure that motivates actions such as approaching, leaving, and taking.'\* According to Abhinavagupta, the undesirable consequence of the Buddhist view with regard to worldly affairs necessitates the alternative:

*[p. 128]* ^pg148

Thus \[on the Buddhist view\] worldly affairs would be destroyed. However, they are not destroyed by the mere curse dear to you: “Let them be destroyed.” Since they appear \[prakāśante\], they are established. And an effort must be made to understand them.”

The Buddhist theory is said to contradict the aggregate of what we know. Worldly experience does not disappear just because it is “cursed” to do so.

The Śaivas also attack the internal coherence of the Buddhist account. Thus they claim that the Buddhist theory of impressions itself would not even be sensible if memory were illusory. Abhinava

explains:

Since there is not \[according to the Buddhists’ account, in memory any genuine\] awareness \[aprakăśana\] of that \[original object), nothing can be accomplished by \[memory\] being born from the impression. For, it \[the impression\] is resorted to in order to understand the similarity \[between memory and the original experience\]. However, there is no similarity between an experience which has the nature of the awareness \[prakžśana\] of an object, and an illusion

called memory which does not touch the object in any way.'®

We see here another example of the contradictoriness inherent to skepticism. Skepticism precludes giving an account of anything.

I will finish this section by presenting a passage from Abhina-vagupta that points out the soteriological possibility of experiencing the Lord’s self-recognition disclosed by the discussion of memory. This explanation is made in Abhinavagupta’s commentary on IPK 1.4.3, which was quoted above. Utpaladeva there states the necessity of a unity of experiences in different times, and identifies the unity with the subject in the expression: “This is that Cognizer” (veditaiṣa sah)? Abhinava’s comment parallels his assertion in the cognition chapter regarding the expression “This is She” as indicating the recognition of Sakti. It actually comes first in the Saiva order of presentation, and is probably alluded to in the statement in the cognition section. Abhinava states:

The unity, that is, the recognitive synthesis \[anusamdhāna\] of “I experienced the pot” or “that pot” is undifferentiated from the synthesizer \[anusamdhātr\]. In order to show this, the unity is pointed out with the apposition: “This is that Cognizer.” With “This . . . is that” is made the clear manifestation \[sphuṭāvabhāsana) of the subject-principle \[pramātrtattva\], which is as if covered.!™ By this

As the author has said: “The self-luminous subject-principle had been muddied over by those who obfuscate, talking of this and that. This has been revealed, upon putting those fools in their place by means of clear arguments.”'™

The Semantic Exclusion Śakti

We may now turn to consider the last of our three general epistemological topics—apohana, which I have translated as “semantic exclusion.” This subject is again explained in terms of a modality of the Lord’s self-recognition designated as the Semantic Exclusion Sakti.

I must begin by explaining the idea of semantic exclusion. The Buddhist logicians originally developed this conception. These thinkers had radically divorced the unique particulars from linguistic or imaginative construction. However, they were still faced with the problem of explaining some sort of “coordination” (sārūpya) between language and the particulars as the basis for successful reference in communication and behaviors on the basis of language.” They developed the theory of semantic exclusion in the attempt to explain a “similarity between things absolutely dissimilar.”™? According to this theory, an expression such as “cow” does not positively designate or describe anything in the primitive sense data. Its only referentiality is the negation of what does not fit into the category of cow. The only similarity of the sense datum with the category is that it is not a non-cow.™

The Hindu realists rejected the theory of exclusion as a satisfactory account of semantics. Nevertheless it was generally accepted that the rejection of the inapplicable is an important feature of more positively referential construction. Now, as the Śaivas have already argued for the generation of all experience from the Lord’s self-recognition, their problem is not explaining the relationship between raw sense data and construction. However, in the explanation of conceptual construction at the gross level of emanation, they accept, like the realists, that exclusion is an aspect of more positive reference.”

Abhinavagupta explains the intrinsic character of conceptual construction of the rejection of the inapplicable using the example of

the interpreted cognition of a pot:

When a pot is seen, it is also possible that there could be, in the place of the pot, a non-pot, such as a cloth, etc. \[Such a non-pot

*[p. 130]* ^pg150

would like the pot also\] naturally have a location believed to be suitable; it would produce cognition \[of itself as object\], and would be brought to its location by its own causes." Since the manifestations of both the pot and the non-pot are possible, there is an opportunity for a superimposition \[in which one wrongly takes a pot to be a non-pot\]. Since there is the \[possibility of the\] superimposition of a non-pot, there is the operation of exclusion, which is characterized by negation. Thus the determinate ascertainment \[niscaya\] “pot” has the nature of conceptual construction which is

animated by that \[exclusion\].

The basic point in the Śaivas’ theory of exclusion is that it depends upon a comparative synthesis, or recognition, of what does and does not fit into categories." The Śaivas contend that the Buddhists cannot account for this, because of their understanding of perceptions and concepts as series of entirely discrete events. Abhinava explains

through a rhetorical inquiry:

There may be this doubt: The pot is seen as having a very particular form. How can a conceptual construction \[vikalpa\], as pertaining to the cognition of the pot, effect the negation of the non-pot? For not even the name of non-pot has been apprehended by anyone. How can the mental impression of the non-pot be awakened when the pot is seen? \[This reasoning is accepted by the Śaivas:) True. \[However,\] the Buddhist is to be œnsured in this way, and not us."

Since the Buddhists cannot even account for exclusion, it would naturally be impossible for them to posit it as the sole basis of reference.

Utpaladeva explains exclusion utilizing the term “semantic intuition” (pratibhā), which for Bhartrhari along with the Śaivas describes the revelatory unity of reference grounding all interpretation of

experience:

The determinate ascertainment \[niścaya\] of “that” results from the exclusion of the “not-that” by the cognizer who experiences the intuition \[pratibhā\] of both the “that” and the “not-that.” This \[determinate ascertainment\] is explained to be conceptual construction \[vikalpa\] \[as is expressed\]: “This is a ‘pot.’ "2

The subject's recognition of reference comprehends similarity as well as difference.”

I point out the paradoxical character of the Śaivas’ explanation. Exemplifying the logic discussed at the end of the last chapter, they

*[p. 131]* ^pg151

have in effect explained difference as similarity.'™? Now, the theory of the Exclusion Śakti is an effort to accept and transfigure an issue in semantics and epistemology. However, we have also seen that for the Pratyabhijñā, illusion is not seeing the monistic reality of Siva. Because exclusion is understood by the Śaivas along with other Indian philosophers to be intrinsic to gross conceptual construction, it is assimilated to the explanation of the fragmentation of the Lord's self-recognition. In addressing this problematic it is impossible for the Śaivas to avoid bringing in again the concept of illusion. The following passage by Abhinavagupta suggests the identification of the operation of the Exclusion Śakti in discriminating all subjects and objects from other subjects and objects with delusion:

That \[object\] which is manifested is differentiated from consciousness. Consciousness \[is differentiated\] from that. One consciousness \[is differentiated\] from another consciousness. One object of consciousness \[is differentiated\] from another object of consciousness. This differentiation is not really possible. Thus it is explained to be the mere manifestation of the differentiation. As such, that \[differentiation\] is not ultimately real. For this \[manifestation\] is the ultimate reality of everything which is created. Due to the differentiating all around \[paritaśchedanāt\], there is said to be all-around differentiation \[pariccheda}. The capacity for the manifestation of that \[differentiation\] is the Semantic Exclusion Śakti.'?

The mixture of positive epistemology with some degree of illusionism is puzzling. I refer to the discussion of the Śaivas’ approaches to the logical problem of the relation of unity and diversity at the end of the

last chapter.

Such are the basic outlines of the Pratyabhijñā treatment of semantic exclusion. The reduction of the topic to a form of recognition designated as the Exclusion Śakti again supports the inference for recognizing the identity of the person with the Supreme Lord. Abhinava

explains:

This investigation of the differentiation of manifestations was made in connection with \[the discussion of} the capacity for internal manifestation. From this \[investigation of differentiation\] the purpose in this śāstra, the chief thing aimed at \[abhisamhita\], i.e., the recognition of the Lord in oneself, is established without effort, according to the principle that when one thing is demonstrated its correlative is demonstrated."

The Epistemological Triad as the Foundation

of Ordinary Life

I have completed my survey of the Śaivas’ efforts to demonstrate their theory of recognition in the arena of epistemology. The discussion of memory brought out an important Śaiva position—that the ordinary experience of the world must be basically valid, even if it should be “transfigured.” The Śaiva arguments are transcendental in that they attempt to show what must be the case in order to account for this experience—as analyzed into the topics covered by the triad of Śaktis. The true identity of the individual as the Supreme Lord is the grounding “limit of” beyond the “limits to” found in the study of the topics. Abhinava sums up the triad:

There are all worldly behaviors by means of this triad of Saktis. By this triad of Śaktis of the Blessed One there is the manifestation of \[limited individuals such as\] Caitra, Maitra, etc., who are the perceivers, rememberers, and \[semantically exclusive\] conceptualizers \[vikalpayitr}. For it is \[actually\] He who cognizes, remembers and conceptualizes in this and that form. As it has been said by the preceptor: “Although the establishment of objects is concealed by the individual soul as delimited by the vital breaths and psychophysical complex {puryaṣtaka}—nevertheless that \[establishment\] is {actually} accomplished in the Supreme Self.” . . . If this is not accepted, nothing can appear. Thus is the undesirable consequence. However, \[this world\] does appear. Therefore, this \[the Śaiva understanding of the Lord with His Śaktis\] must necessarily be accepted. Thus there is the avoidance of the undesir-

able consequence.

In the conclusion to the book I will make my final case for the enduring plausibility of these epistemological arguments, along with the

ontological arguments presented in the next chapter.
