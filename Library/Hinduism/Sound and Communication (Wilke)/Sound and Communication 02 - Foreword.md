## Foreword

Anyone speaking about communication — at least in today’s Western cultural area — will not necessarily think of sound as an independent medium of expression and communication, and anyone studying texts is interested in the content and hardly aware that texts also always have a material quality — their sound —and can be perceived in a sensory and emotional way. In Hinduistic India things are different. Since earliest times, sound has enjoyed great cultural importance. “Reading” out a religious text in Sanskrit means “reciting” it in a musically pleasing way and with the utmost care regarding correct pronunciation. In turn, simply listening to the sound of a religious text is already held to be auspicious and purifying. In the scholarly traditions, as well as in everyday life, we find a great focus on the sonic dimension. It is noteworthy that this predominance of sensing the world through sounds is not confined to religious life only, but pervades even the most complex symbolic representations in arts and sciences. Sound may function as an organizing principle in such abstract conceptualizations as those found in grammar, mathematics, and astronomy. At the core of Pāṇini’s ingenious grammar, for instance, lie certain sound codes which re-arrange the Sanskrit alphabet. The alphabet, being structured according to the seats of articulation in a strictly phonetic and highly rational order, likewise inspired the numeric code-systems used by astronomers. Instead of diagrams and numerical tables we find memorizing systems and poetic verses full of alliterations. The same predominance of sound is seen in metaphysics where sound becomes a means of interpreting and understanding the world. The sophisticated cosmologies of the Tantra are also based on the letters of the alphabet and mysterious sound codes serve as models for explaining the creation of the world and language. In various ways sound and its subtle yet very physical quality have been a powerful medium of communication chosen to invoke ordered relationships, furnish ritual effectiveness and generate sources of power and value, and not least to construct “the sacred,” embody assumptions about people’s place in a larger order of things and bring about emotional absorption. 

The greater part of this book resulted from a cooperative project between a scholar of religion who combines an interest in history and theory with work in the field of Ethno-Indology, and a Sanskritist with an interest in the history of religion and philosophy. Both authors have spent longer periods in Varanasi and studied not only at universities, but also with Indian pandits and ācāryas. In this treatise on text, communication and sound in Hindu Sanskrit we propose a new way of accessing Indian cultural history and an expanded method of interpreting text. While doing so we exemplify a recently suggested paradigm in the academic study of religions in Germany — the aesthetics of religion, the interdisciplinary exploration of sensory perception and symbolic representation in religious contexts. The roots of studying the Hindu sound universe go back to the period 1991 to 1998, to Annette Wilke’s investigation of the litanies of the three hundred names and one thousand names of the (Tantric) goddess Lalitā in terms of their literary structure and their performance in private worship and South Indian temple services.' As hymns of one thousand names (Sahasranāma-Stotra) to Tantric and non-Tantric deities are extremely popular in Sanskrit Hinduism, it seemed that what was also needed was a “thicker” description? of the rationality of the purposes and values of this poorly researched form of literature within its own cultural context. Greeting conventions’, linguistic philosophy, and sonic cosmogonies ought also to be included, and most importantly mantra practices, the devotional repetition of one single name, and indigenous aesthetic theories. During the course of this work it became more and more obvious that the litanies can be read as aesthetic narrative theologies, and that in the performative contexts of recitation a major role is played not only by the correct pronunciation, but also by the sensory aspect of the sound. Kapferer’s analysis stresses the highly communicative aspects of ritual performance (performance being itself constitutive of the intended healing result) and the crucial role of the aesthetic media involved (drumming, singing of sacred texts, dance and comic drama). In paying attention to the ritual ensemble, he contends that it is precisely the purposefully shifting arrangement of these different aesthetic forms and the dynamics of their distinct qualities which bring about ritual efficacy. Acknowledging a deep link between cosmology and aesthetics, he reveals how the exorcism rites display many ideas by which Sinhalese Buddhists, especially the urban working class and peasants, comprehend their world. Closer to our study, however, is Kermani’s approach. This scholar of Islam maintains that there is no true understanding of the Quran without an appreciation of its aesthetic dimensions, 1.6. its literary quality and sonic realization. In doing so, he concentrates on the history of reception and on the practice of recitation of only one sacred text: the Quran. By contrast, the diversity of sacred texts in the various Hindu religions do not so much require a single individual study but rather a historical and synoptic procedure, along with the inclusion of largely secular systems such as grammar, theater and poetry. And precisely in the area which is not explicitly religious, it becomes clear just how decisively important sound as a means of communication and expression is in its effects on India’s cultural hierarchy of values. 

Certainly in this book we are not solely interested in the perception of sound but, in a much more general manner, in the manifold and shifting correlations between language and the understanding of the world in Sanskrit Hinduism. The result is a history of the culture and literature of Hinduism which, in ever new variations, makes clear that language is effective in both its terminological, logical aspect and its sensory, emotive aspect. The main function of language is the creation of sense and this takes place on a very great number of levels, both semantic and non-semantic, by making propositions and using symbolic forms. In fact, it will be shown in detail that in many Indian religious traditions non-semantic sounds are also media of the utmost importance for communication. Consequently, we seek to locate our venture in a systematic context continuing the lines of thought of the philosophers Ernst Cassirer, Susanne Langer, and Kurt Hübner, whose explorations included the pre-terminological, the pre-logical and the sensory, and show that these dimensions have their own ways of making sense. Catherine Bell’s remarks on ritual apply equally to our study of language, textual traditions, and their performative dimensions: religious symbols and the symbolic activity of rituals appear too complex to be mere reflections of the social order; they are embedded in and display elaborate systems that appear to have their own logic, and the relationship of this logic to social organization is far from simple and direct. Besides cultural theories we take social sciences, linguistic semiotics, cognitive, aesthetic and perfomance theories into consideration. 

This book tackles a long period of time (from the Veda to the present day), a plurality of traditions, and a great variety of textual sources. This is a daring enterprise given the highly specialized research in contemporary Indology. Yet it is precisely the vast amount of material concerning our subject matter which makes it tempting to strive for a global perspective. Our questions, findings and theoretical framework intend to reach all those interested in cultural and religious studies, but may likewise arouse the interest of Indologists. Although there exist a large number of fine studies on the Veda, Vedic sciences, devotional traditions, philosophy, Indian aesthetics, the Tantra and other areas we want to deal with, the ground we intend to explore by focusing on sonic communication is largely uncharted territory and so far only a few studies exist on single aspects. Writing and Pronunciation of Sanskrit Terms 

In order to facilitate the reading of the numerous and sometimes very long Sanskrit terms, we usually present composites and names of major works in a form which is divided into “padas” (e.g. Rk-Samhitā or bīja-mantra), unless we are dealing with familiar terms such as Rgveda or vowel conjuncts (e.g. vedāīṁga instead of vedā- 144). Sanskrit texts are only given in italics when they are presented for the first time in a chapter, or else when we are moving on to a major argument. Sanskrit terms that are especially frequent in certain individual passages are also only italicized the first time they occur, unless we want to underline an argument. Key terms such as “Brahman” may be capitalized. We interchange the terms Śivaitic and Viṣṇuitic (which sound more familiar to non-Indologists) with the terms Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava (common in Indology). Sanskrit names which are in current popular use are written without diacritical script (e.g. Varanasi or Swami Shivananda). We generally indicate our original sources with the full name of the text. The short-cuts we use in some places, particularly the Veda chapter, occur only in footnotes or citations and are easily identifiable from the discussion of the work in the main text. For instance, references to the Rgveda, i.e. the Rk-Samhitā, will be indicated by the short-cut “RV.”
