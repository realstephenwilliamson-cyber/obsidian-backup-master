The Context of the Fragment ad ĪPK 1.5.4-5:

The Debate between a Vijñānavādin and a Sautrāntika

and the Problem of the Other Conscious Streams

In ĪPK 1.5.4-5,<sup>12</sup> Utpaladeva lets a Sautrāntika criticize the Vijñānavādins’ idealistic thesis that the universe is merely an internal form taken on by consciousness and that phenomenal variety is the result of an internal mechanism of latent traces or imprints (*vāsanā*). In the course of this criticism, not only does the Sautrāntika attack the Vijñānavādins’ position regarding the status of the external object:<sup>13</sup> he also argues that the Vijñānavāda can only lead to solipsism, since in the Vijñānavādin’s perspective, other subjects must be mere internal aspects of my own stream or <sup>12</sup> *tattadākasmikābhāso bāhyaṁ ced anumāpayet* A *na hy abhinnasya bodhasya vicitrābhāsahetutā* AA *na vāsanāprabodho’tra vicitro hetutām iyāt* A *tasyāpi tatprabodhasya vaicitrye kiṁ nibandhanam* AA “\[A Sautrāntika might\] object (*cet*) \[the following\]: the manifestation of this or that \[particular object,\] which is \[apparently\] devoid of cause (*ākasmika*), must lead \[us\] to infer an external \[reality\], for consciousness, which is \[in itself\] undiferentiated, cannot be the cause of manifestations that are varied. \[And\] the varied awakening of imprints (*vāsanā*) cannot be the cause of this \[phenomenal variety\]; for what would be in turn the cause of the variety of the awakening of these \[imprints\]?”

The Vijñānavādin denies the accusation of solipsism by using Dharmakīrti’s line of argument in the *Santānāntarasiddhi*.<sup>15</sup> According to Dharmakīrti, just as in a system that admits the reality of entities external to consciousness, in the idealistic system of the Vijñānavāda, the other streams of consciousness cannot be directly perceived (since a consciousness cannot be directly apprehended by another consciousness but only by itself); yet, just as in a system that accepts the reality of external entities, in the Vijñānavāda, the other streams of consciousness can be inferred. This inference of the existence of other conscious entities or streams of momentary cognitions rests on the fact that we experience in ourselves that when we act (and for instance when we speak), our action is preceded by a will to act. So when we witness an action that has not been preceded by our own will to act, we can infer that this action was preceded by a desire to act that does not belong to our own stream of cognitions – and this other willing entity must be another conscious subject.

To this argument the Sautrāntika replies that the Vijñānavādin’s own presuppositions prevent him from making the very inference that he defends. He thus shows that the Vijñānavādin has no means of establishing the invariable concomitance on which his inference of other conscious streams rests. The Vijñānavādin replies that in fact this necessary relation between the subjective cognition “I want to speak” and the objective cognition “X is speaking” is constantly apprehended in the ordinary world, because our actions are always related to our awareness that they can be witnessed by others: when we act, we act while desiring either to be seen acting or not to be seen acting, but whether we conceal our actions or whether we act ostentatiously, we are aware that our desire to act does not merely result in the cognition “I am acting” (which occurs in my conscious stream) but also in the cognition “X is acting” (which occurs in the other conscious streams witnessing my action). The following passage of the *Viv̥ ti* begins with the Vijñānavādin’s explanation of this very argument, after which Utpaladeva (or, as Abhinavagupta says, the Īśvarādvayavādin, the “proponent of the thesis of the non-duality \[of the universe\] with the Lord”) helps the Sautrāntika criticize the Vijñānavādin’s position. Utpaladeva points out that this position entails a logical circle, because our awareness of the existence of other conscious entities is supposed to stem from our awareness that the others can witness our actions, while our awareness that the others can witness our actions must derive from our awareness of the existence of other conscious entities. He also points out that in fact there is no such thing as an invariable concomitance between the subjective thought “I want to be heard by others” and the objective thought “X is speaking”, because we sometimes experience that we can speak without being heard whether we want it or not: thus the causality relationship invoked by the Vijñānavādin between a subjective will to act and the objective awareness that X is acting cannot be established.

The Śaiva non-dualist concludes from this that the other subject can only be established when the other’s action is perceived both by him/her (in the form “I want to speak/I am speaking”) and by me (in the form “he/she is speaking”), from the supposition that the *same* action is then apprehended in these two diferent forms. For we have to make this assumption in order to justify the unity of the sphere of human practice (*vyavahāra*): if we do not accept it, then we cannot justify our belief that the other subjects share with us the same spatio-temporal determinations and therefore live in a common world, and the notion of intersubjectivity collapses. 

As Abhinavagupta makes clear in his commentary, the Śaiva non-dualists have a solution to this problem of intersubjectivity:<sup>17</sup> according to them, the unity of the sphere of human practice is to be explained as the result of the constant activity of division and synthesis through which the universal consciousness appears to

This thesis (on which see above, nn 22-23) has the advantage of enabling the Vijñānavādin to acknowledge the existence of other subjects while not having recourse to any reality external to consciousness (since the universe is then nothing but a variety of consciousnesses relecting onto each other their own manifestations). But it is problematic for two reasons. First, if it were the case then all the manifestations of objects occurring in one conscious stream should occur in all the others, so that every conscious stream would be simultaneously conscious of all objects. Second, the manifestation of an object in a given conscious stream, which supposedly projects its form onto other conscious streams, must be considered itself as some sort of relection since it appears objectively, in the form “this”, i.e. as separated from the subject perceiving it, and since it has been established that an objective thought such as “he/she is speaking” must be a relection in one conscious stream of a subjective thought “I am speaking” in some other conscious stream. If any manifestation of an object in a given conscious stream is to be considered as a relection of a manifestation in some other conscious stream, then in order to avoid an ininite regress (since my thought “the pot” must be a relection of somebody else’s thought “the pot”, which in turn is a relection of somebody else’s thought, etc.), we could consider that the objective thought “the pot” ultimately amounts to the subjective thought “I am the pot” or “I am manifest \[as\] the pot”, just as my objective thought “he/she is speaking” is in fact a relection of somebody else’s subjective thought “I am speaking”. But then, as Abhinavagupta points out, we would have to admit that all objects are ultimately one and the same thing, namely, the “I”, and phenomenal variety could not be accounted for. The only way to escape the ininite regress is therefore to admit that this phenomenal variety has as its cause the existence of entities that are external to consciousness.
