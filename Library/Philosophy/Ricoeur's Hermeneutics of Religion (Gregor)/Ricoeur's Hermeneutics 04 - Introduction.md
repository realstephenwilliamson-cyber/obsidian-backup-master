## Introduction

Paul Ricoeur is one of the great philosophers of the twentieth century. His thinking is diffuse and covers many topics: the philosophy of the will and action, language and interpretation, metaphor and narrative, psychoanalysis, memory, historiography, law, and politics. While Ricoeur wrote books—and in some cases multiple books—on these topics, he never devoted a whole book to religion, even though he wrote many essays on it. Ricoeur explores religious themes at length in two of his books (*The Symbolism of Evil* and *Thinking Biblically*), and we have a few collections of his essays on religion (*Essays on Biblical Interpretation*, *Figuring the Sacred*, and *Lectures 3*), but he never produced a definitive treatment of his philosophy of religion. This is partly because Ricoeur’s thought on religion was always in process, and also because it was peripheral to his primary philosophical work. Nevertheless, religion is a constant theme throughout his long career, beginning with his dissertation on the place of God in reflexive philosophy, and carrying on through the last decade of his life. This book is an attempt to draw together Ricoeur’s work in the philosophy of religion, identifying the main themes, questions, positions of this work, and following their development through different stages of his career.

WHAT IS THE HERMENEUTICS OF RELIGION?

I use the term “hermeneutics” to characterize Ricoeur’s philosophy of religion, even though Ricoeur never directly describes his own project as a “hermeneutics of religion.” He does, however, use the term to describe Kant’s attempt to interpret religion philosophically in *Religion within the Boundar-*

*[p. 2]* ^pg19

hermeneutics of religion is therefore an attempt to understand religion, in particular the way religious texts, traditions, and practices shape human self-understanding. It is also an attempt to reflect philosophically on the meanings given in religious discourse and religious experience. 

The hermeneutics of religion is a modification of the phenomenology of religion, which attempts to describe the meaning of religious phenomena as they are given in different religious traditions. Ricoeur’s first significant venture in this direction appeared in *The Symbolism of Evil*, where he undertakes what he calls a “phenomenology of confession,” which aims to let the believing soul speak (SE 19). One of that book’s great contributions to the hermeneutics of religion was Ricoeur’s recognition that philosophical reflection has no direct access to the “believing soul.” There is no generic state of religious consciousness independent of specific religious confessions. The phenomenology of religion must therefore take a hermeneutical turn, following a detour through the symbols, myths, narratives, and other linguistic forms in which believing souls have made their confessions of faith.

Ricoeur’s work in *The Symbolism of Evil* focuses primarily on the symbols and myths that constitute the experience of *fault*—that is, the condition of the “bad will” corrupted and held captive by sin. Ricoeur’s detour led through the symbols and myths of Jewish and Christian texts, but also Sumerian, Babylonian, Assyrian, Egyptian, and Greek myths. The majority of Ricoeur’s work on religion focuses on Jewish and Christian texts though, since these are the texts that were formative in his context as a twentieth-century French philosopher, and for his own personal commitments as a Reformed Christian. Ricoeur speaks from the background of the biblical texts that have constituted his own self-understanding.

This focus on Christian texts does not, however, mean that Ricoeur views his philosophical hermeneutics of religion as Christian philosophy. Ricoeur identifies himself as a philosopher who is a Christian, and while he draws deeply on the resources of Christian biblical texts as well as the Christian theological tradition, he does so as a philosopher. Ricoeur’s refusal of the designation “Christian philosopher” is due in large part to his context in French academic philosophy, where the term often denoted either adhesion to Thomism, or a reduction of philosophy to a mere ancillary of theology. Ricoeur’s French academic context was also dominated by the ideal of *laïcité*, with its sharp separation of public and private, state and church. Ricoeur therefore always maintained a “dual allegiance,” both to the public world of academic philosophy and its standards of reasoning, and to his “personal and communitary existence” as a Christian. He cites both cultural and institutional reasons for insisting on the strictly philosophical nature of his work, commenting in a late interview: “It was important to me to be recognized as a professor of philosophy, teaching philosophy in a public institution and 

*[p. 3]* ^pg20

speaking the common language, hence assuming the mental reservations that this entailed, even if it meant that I would periodically be accused of being a theologian in disguise who philosophizes, or a philosopher who makes the religious sphere think or be thought” (CC 150).

This idea of bringing the religious to thought is a fairly accurate description of Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion, which aims to reflect philosophically on the surplus of meaning that is given in religious discourse. The symbols and narratives of the Jewish and Christian biblical texts contain distinctive modes of thinking, different from what one finds in the Greek philosophical heritage, and Ricoeur is seeking a way to bring these distinctive modes of thought into philosophical discourse. He describes this philosophical interpretation as a kind of *approximation* (CI 403). It is not a proclamation of the good news, nor a confession of belief, but rather a philosophical listening to the proclamation and confession. It seeks to hear a word from beyond philosophy, and yet think that word philosophically. In Ricoeur’s words: “This work of thought is a work that begins with listening, and yet within the autonomy of responsible thought. It is an incessant reform of thinking, but within the limits of reason alone. The ‘conversion’ of the philosopher is a conversion within philosophy and to philosophy according to its internal contingencies” (CI 403).

The hermeneutics of religion is therefore also not theology, whether natural or revealed. It is rather an approximation of theological ideas, just as it is an approximation of the meanings contained in biblical texts. Yet it remains a strictly philosophical, not theological, discipline. One of Ricoeur’s most significant philosophical approximations comes from his reading of Karl Barth and what I refer to in this book as the “theology of the Word”—that is, a series of other twentieth-century German Protestant theologians including Rudolf Bultmann, Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Gerhard Ebeling, Ernst Fuchs, and Eberhard Jüngel. The primary influence from this line of thinkers is the idea of the Word of God as an address that comes from outside of the self, constituting faith in an event that is radically discontinuous with the immanent operations of the self. The Word is not an idea that arises from the workings of the human spirit, but a call to an entirely new reality.

This theological idea of a disruptive Word poses a challenge for philosophy, since it is unclear how it shows up phenomenally and registers ontologically. Barth describes revelation as the disclosure of a new reality, a new world that touches the old “as a tangent touches a circle, that is, without touching it.”<sup>2</sup> This revelation cannot be captured in a philosophical system, because every proclamation of the Word is a new event. One of the challenges for philosophy after Barth is to make sense of how philosophy can make sense of such an event. For Ricoeur, the Word is a radically transcendent call, a summons, and yet it is addressed to human beings who think, imagine, desire, and will. 

*[p. 4]* ^pg21

This Word may not arise from the immanent working of history, culture, or human consciousness, but it does enter these phenomenal horizons—becoming a “fact of culture” and registering in the life of a self who is both active and passive, and who has certain capacities to receive and respond to this call. 

Ricoeur is a mediating thinker, which in this case means holding the idea of a transcendent Word together with his commitment to the immanent sphere of thinking consciousness—a commitment Ricoeur developed through his training in French reflexive philosophy. Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion is an extension as well as a modification of the reflexive tradition, and throughout this book we will examine a few of the many ways Ricoeur attempts to balance transcendence and immanence, revelation and receptivity, otherness and selfhood, difference and identity, *alterity* and *ipseity*.

This meeting of the theology of the Word with reflexive philosophy leads to an unlikely alliance. As we will see in chapter 1, the reflexive tradition has typically sought to identify all transcendence as in some way originating in the acts of the self. To confront this picture of the subject with a Word that is anterior and superior to its own acts is to expose an irreparable break in thinking consciousness. And yet Ricoeur remains committed to a modified, chastened form of the reflexive method as a way to pursue self-understanding. Given this goal, hermeneutics is an extension of reflection, but for Ricoeur, self-understanding is not merely intellectual but also ethical, insofar as it aims at the transformation of the self. Reflexive philosophy “seeks to have understanding of self, based on ‘the text which its actions have constituted’ . . . coincide with ‘a regeneration of its being.’”<sup>3</sup> For Ricoeur, this means the regeneration of our fundamental capabilities as human beings. 

RELIGION AND THE REBIRTH OF THE CAPABLE SELF

The theme of human capability comes to the fore in Ricoeur’s mature philosophy, but it is the enduring theme of his work as a whole. He wrote on many topics and problems, but the concern that unites them all is the question of philosophical anthropology: What does it mean to be a human being? In a 1999 lecture, Ricoeur looks back over his career and observes an overarching theme in his work: human capability as “the cornerstone of philosophical anthropology.” “Capability” designates “our power to act,”<sup>4</sup> “*the power to cause something to happen*.”<sup>5</sup> This theme arises in Ricoeur’s first original book, *The Voluntary and the Involuntary*, where he follows Maurice Merleau-Ponty in beginning with the verb *I can* rather than the Cartesian *I think* (FN 9). Ricoeur develops his early phenomenology of the *I can* in his subsequent work, and eventually consolidates the different modes of the *I can* in *Oneself as Another* (1990), which presents a hermeneutics of the 

*[p. 5]* ^pg22

self that focuses on four distinctly human capacities: I can *speak*, I can *act*, I can *narrate*, and I can assume *responsibility* for myself and my actions.<sup>6</sup> This set of capacities forms the core of Ricoeur’s mature philosophical anthropology, returning again and again in his subsequent work, whether in his work on justice (“Who is the Subject of Rights?”),<sup>7</sup> forgiveness (*Memory,* *History, Forgetting*), recognition (*The Course of Recognition*), or religion (“The Addressee of Religion: The Capable Human Being”) (PA 269–89). He sums up this philosophical anthropology with one phrase: *l’homme capable*—the capable human being.

This focus on human capability is not, note well, a simple celebration of human power, ability, and action. The capable human being is also the fragile, suffering, and vulnerable human being. Ricoeur’s idea of the *homo capax* thus refers to the “diverse capacities and incapacities that make human beings acting and suffering beings.”<sup>8</sup> As Ricoeur writes: “All the *I cans* are structured by the idea of a suffering and acting being,” so that “the modalities of potency .  .  . correspond to the modalities of non-potency.”<sup>9</sup> The capable human being is not capable in all things and in all ways. Capacity always exists in relation to incapacity. 

One source of this incapacity is our finitude: We are not omnipotent beings. We act, but we also suffer. We are active, but we are also passive. We make our presence known in the world, but we are also limited in space, time, and power. We are here, not there. We are now, not then. We are strong, and we are weak. We can, and we cannot. We live, and we die. Incapacity is part of being finite. 

There is, however, another kind of incapacity that binds the will—not finitude, but *fault*. This is the incapacity that comes from sin, or what Kant calls radical evil. As we will see in chapter 2, this is the paradoxical form of incapacity the self inflicts upon itself. The will binds itself. Freedom makes itself unfree. How can philosophy make sense of this paradox? 

This is where Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion enters the picture: first, to understand the symbols and myths that articulate the experience of fault, and second, to understand how religion liberates the bound will. In this regard Ricoeur takes an important cue from Kant’s *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason*, which interprets religion in general and Christianity in particular “as offering a fundamental symbolism” to regenerate the will that is corrupted by radical evil (RSV 3). Religion is addressed to the capable human being, in the sense that it aims to heal and restore our fundamental capacities for goodness. Religion is about opening and uncovering “the innermost possibility of my own freedom” (TA 99). It is about the “regeneration,” the “restoration,” the “rebirth of the capable self” (ELRD 146n.4). Religious discourse addresses us through the imagination at the level of our desire to be (the *conatus essendi*) and gives us what Paul Tillich calls the *courage to* 

*[p. 6]* ^pg23

*be* (RB 31). Religion reorients the self toward the good, toward love, and toward justice. 

Religion accomplishes this through the power of poetic discourse. As we will see in chapter 3, poetic discourse is productive: it makes, it creates, and it assists in becoming. This productive power works through the imagination to mobilize our desire to be. Poetic discourse is revelatory insofar as it discloses new possibilities for understanding ourselves and the world. This transformative power is especially present in religious discourse, with its symbols, myths, and narratives exerting a transformative power to liberate the will that is bound by sin, evil, or fault. 

In chapter 4 we will see that Ricoeur draws on several philosophers to develop his hermeneutics of religion, including Spinoza, Hegel, and Heidegger. Ricoeur’s primary influence, however, is Kant, from whom he takes three key points: First, the symbolism of religious discourse has a unique efficacy, working through the imagination to transform the self. Second, no matter how radical evil may be, it is never as original as the good. We may have a radical “propensity” to evil, but we have a more fundamental “predisposition” to the good (MHF 491). Third, Kant’s philosophy of religion is not merely a description of religion as an unchanging fact. It is “a struggle against” the defects of religion (RSV 3). Like Kant, Ricoeur sees this philosophical interpretation as a struggle for the very soul of religion—to critique debilitating and destructive interpretations of religion for the sake of affirming religion in all its liberating, life-giving potential.

THE CRITIQUE OF RELIGION

For Ricoeur, the critique of religion takes two primary forms. The first is the external critique of thinkers like Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud, who present what Ricoeur famously calls the hermeneutics of suspicion, which aims to expose false consciousness in religion. The second form is the internal critique of religion in Barth, Bultmann, and Bonhoeffer. Ricoeur is seeking an interpretation of religion that will be compelling to the late modern self—one that demands neither a fideistic *sacrificium intellectus* nor submission to ecclesial authoritarianism.

Ricoeur’s interpretation of religion is not, however, a purely accommodationist strategy of jettisoning whatever is scandalous to modern readers. Instead, his goal is to encounter that which is genuinely other and even scandalous in these texts so they can address us once again. He makes this point in an exchange with the “post-Christian” theologian Daphne Hampson, who suggests that we “cannot simply return” to sources like Plato and “Judaeo-Christianity,” and asks whether it is “time to think anew.” Ricoeur’s response 

*[p. 7]* ^pg24

is a wager that “it is still possible to return to these sources, because they are still eminently capable of structuring our imaginary self-representation, modelling our paradigms of self-understanding: our memory, our promising, our hoping.”<sup>10</sup>

In this regard Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion is not only a service to the guild of academic philosophy. It aims to bring religious discourse into the wider expanse of the public square to contribute to debates about questions of justice, law, and forgiveness. These questions are difficult in our time, when we have so few shared sources for meaning and authority. Ricoeur thus suggests that a new interpretation of the religious might help in founding a new symbolic order—“no longer on power, but on imagination.”<sup>11</sup> 

This hermeneutics can also be helpful to theology, all the while respecting its limits. It can serve the ecclesial community, not as a proclamation or confession of faith, but by helping modern people to hear, once again, the liberating and regenerating word of address in these texts and traditions. As we will see in chapter 9, this hermeneutics can also assist in the challenge of inter-religious exchange, which Ricoeur presents in terms of translation and interconfessional hospitality.

READING RICOEUR CRITICALLY

Our question for Ricoeur will concern the finer points of his hermeneutical return to the sources, and his philosophical approximation of biblical and theological ideas. It is important to remember that Ricoeur is doing philosophy rather than theology, even at those times when he appears to be entering theological territory. Boyd Blundell observes that there are three Ricoeurs: (1) the philosopher engaged in the larger project of philosophical anthropology, (2) the philosopher of religion, and (3) the author of occasional works of biblical exegesis.<sup>12</sup> While this distinction is helpful, there is also a great deal of continuity between Ricoeur’s work in these different areas. It is true that he will examine religious texts in one area while leaving them out in other areas, but nothing he says in one role contradicts with what he says in another. The most famous example of this is Ricoeur’s Gifford Lectures, which contained two final lectures on religious themes. When Ricoeur published the lectures as *Oneself as Another*, he left out these religious lectures to preserve the strictly philosophical focus of the published book. As we will see in chapter 6, however, there is a clear continuity between *Oneself as Another* and these missing lectures, which remain philosophical all the way through.

What about the boundary between Ricoeur’s philosophy and theology, then? Blundell notes that from a theological perspective, Ricoeur holds positions that are “occasionally outright heterodox.”<sup>13</sup> Should we suppose 

*[p. 8]* ^pg25

that Ricoeur makes philosophical arguments that he would repudiate in theological discourse, that he has a private reserve of theological views that conflict with his philosophical views—in a modern version of the medieval double-truth theory? This strikes me as highly unlikely. I am not convinced that what happens in philosophy stays in philosophy. Ricoeur makes interpretive decisions that have significant theological consequences, showing that his hermeneutics of religion is not merely descriptive but also in some sense normative. We will see this in chapter 5 regarding Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of revelation and the biblical texts, in chapter 6 regarding his account of the relation between the divine call and the human response, in chapter 7 regarding his revisionary interpretation of Christian eschatology, in chapter 8 regarding his attempt to reconcile the God of the Bible with the God of Spinoza, and in chapter 9 regarding central Christian motifs like sin and judgment, cross and resurrection, and forgiveness and justice.

It is not insignificant that Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion is deeply influenced by Spinoza, Kant, and Hegel. Alongside the three masters of suspicion (Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud) and the three *B*s of German Protestant theology (Barth, Bultmann, and Bonhoeffer), this is another trio that has shaped the modern critique of religion. Each of these thinkers—Spinoza, Kant, and Hegel—undertakes a philosophical hermeneutics that seeks to make religion safe for the modern autonomous self. In so doing, each thinker sets up a new, rival religion of his own.<sup>14</sup> 

One key difference between Ricoeur and these modern philosophers is that he does not advocate a model of pure autonomy, but rather one of *the*onomy, which he describes as an autonomy without self-sufficiency and a heteronomy without dependence. He is also not setting up a new religion to rival Christianity—hermeneutics *as* religion rather than a hermeneutics *of* religion. Nevertheless, his interpretations of such key biblical themes as the cross, resurrection, judgment, justification, and forgiveness do entail a significant revision of what has traditionally been the *credo* of the believing soul.

One might suppose this is what it looks like to give a philosophical approximation of religious (whether biblical or theological) discourse. One will end up with symbols, images, or figures of transcendence that are in some way rationalized, secularized, demythologized, naturalized, immanentized—in effect, *neutralized* so that their power derives from the human imagination, desire, and will—but do not participate in a transcendent source: having the form of godliness but denying its power.<sup>15</sup> 

One of my arguments in this book is that Ricoeur’s hermeneutics of religion would benefit from not holding back in this way. Instead of offering thinned out approximations of religious convictions, why not think out of the full depth and scope of their meaning? As we will see in the later chapters of 

*[p. 9]* ^pg26

this book, Ricoeur excludes precisely those deeper religious sources—such as the Trinity, the Holy Spirit, divine judgment, and justification by faith—that would be most helpful for addressing philosophical questions of meaning, love, justice, forgiveness, and human capability. 

To be sure, Ricoeur’s efforts to re-interpret these religious texts and ideas for modernity are often helpful. We cannot return to the first naïveté, but after traversing the desert of criticism, Ricoeur wagers that we can experience a second naïveté in which we can once again hear the address of the text. This requires concessions to modern critical consciousness. The question is whether Ricoeur concedes too much. The only way to answer this question is hermeneutically, through the conflict of interpretations, to put our readings to the test. That is the task that lies before us.
