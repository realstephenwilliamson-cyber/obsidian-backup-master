2 Charnel ground items, *śmāśānika*s, and the question of the magical substratum 

## Chapter 2: Charnel ground items, śmāśānika s, and the question of the magical substratum of the early Tantras

*Aleksandra Wenta and Andrea Acri*

### 2.1 Introduction

The writing of magical recipes (*karma*s) was one of the earliest forms of circulation of knowledge in the Indian subcontinent. Magical recipes are found in various genres of Sanskrit literature, from the *Atharvaveda* through the *Arthaśāstra*, the *Mahābhārata*, and the *Kāmasūtra* to the mediaeval Tantras. Despite the abundance of textual material available to us, comparative research on magic—and, more specifically, ‘Tantric magic’—is still in its infancy.<sup>1</sup> Türstig, in his now classic article on the *abhicāra* or ‘the art of killing by charms’, was among the first scholars to have noticed that magical recipes attested in the Tantras, as well as non-Tantric texts such as the *Manusmṛti* and the *Arthaśāstra*, reveal ‘too many striking similarities \[…\] to deny the existence of an unbroken tradition’ (Türstig 1985: 70). More recently, van Schaik (2020) has noted that, besides the Buddhist Tantras,

sutras and other nontantric scriptures provide rich sources of spells \[…\]. Mantras, mandalas, and ritual objects like protective threads were all part of Buddhist ritual practice before the tantras became popular\[; …\] the literature of spells predated the Vajrayana, and the creators of Vajrayana rituals took aspects of these magical practices \[…\] and integrated them into a system of salvation.

Focusing on the agents of such spells and magical recipes, Ronald Davidson (2017) has investigated possible non-or pre-Tantric prototypes of Tantric magical practitioners who ‘do not appear to have expressed ideologies of liberation or transcendent divinity but were concerned with magical crafts of various kinds’, arguing that ‘some of the dynamics and ritual practices of Indian magicians and sorcerers were appropriated by Tantric groups, so that later forms still exhibit analogous attributes’ (*ibid*.: 1). In this volume, Davidson continues that line of enquiry by focusing on the origins of the employment of skulls (*kapāla*) in Tantric traditions, especially of the ‘skull-bearers’ (*kāpālika*). This chapter takes the arguments of the aforementioned scholars further by concentrating on the comparative study of magical recipes involving charnel ground items attested in Tantric and non-Tantric texts, on the one hand, and by exploring the origins of the 

*[p. 41]* ^pg54

figures of practitioners associated with the charnel ground (*śmaśāna*),<sup>2</sup> referred to as *śmāśānika*s, on the other.

We will examine specific instances of the use of objects and substances procured from the cremation ground in *karma*s found in Tantric and non-- Tantric texts, most notably the *Arthaśāstra*, highlighting the parallels with regard to a ritual technology uniformly employed as part of an early ‘magical substratum’. On the basis of this comparative analysis, we will suggest that groups of ‘charnel ground specialists’ might already have been in existence prior to the emergence and consolidation of Tantric traditions in the post-- Gupta early mediaeval period—especially those belonging to such groups as, or carrying out the observances of, the Kāpālikas, whose practices would seem to overlap with those attested in non-Sanskritic, non-Brahmanical milieus.

### 2.2 Magical recipes, the magical substratum, and animism

While not eschewing the term ‘magic’, we recognize that it imposes a foreign etic perspective on an emic category known under various names (viz. *kṛtya*, *abhicāra*, *yatudhāna*, etc.), and prefer to refer to the practices and techniques described in this section as *karma*s, or ‘magical recipes’. The term *karma*, which appears already in the *Atharvaveda* to describe magical procedures of a similar kind, had continued to be used in non-Tantric and Tantric texts alike and appears to be a feature of an early compositional model for writing magic. Several compositional features suggest that *karma*s were thought of as a specific type of magical ‘genre’. First, the *karma*s often begin with the indication of the precise timeframe when the recipe should begin, e.g. the 14th dark fortnight or during the Puṣya asterism. This presumably highlights the fact that the *karma*s were governed by a parallel knowledge of astral science that organized time into auspicious and inauspicious moments. Second, many recipes, especially in the early Tantras and in non-Tantric texts, such as the *Arthaśāstra*, imply an element of asceticism, requiring the magician to fast for one or three nights before the actual preparation of the *karma* could commence. Thus, in the texts we find such parallel expressions as *trirātraṃ uṣito bhutvā* (‘having fasted for three nights’) or *ahorātroṣito bhutvā* (‘having fasted for a day and night’) to refer to the practitioner who engaged in fasting practices prior to executing a recipe. Third, objects that are being magically manipulated must be empowered by the recitation of mantras; in this case, we find the recurrence of the terms *abhimantritā* or *abhimantrayitvā*. Fourth, the precise methods and techniques that are used by magicians are governed by the cause-effect principle that follows the universal magical laws of contagion, sympathy, and antipathy (sometimes also described as contact, similarity, and contrast). One can see the first law at work in the procurement of the footprint of a victim or the earth on which the target has urinated (see infra). In other words, entities that have been in contact with each other continue to influence each other through some sort of transfer of an essence or property. The law of sympathy is based on the principle of correspondence, ‘similar produces similar’, just like in the case of making a powder from the eyes of the animals that wander at night to 

*[p. 42]* ^pg55

obtain the power of night vision. In contrast, antipathy operates from the understanding of antagonistic forces that are present in the natural world: since the mongoose is the archenemy of the snake, manipulation of items collected from those two animals will cause a fight among people. Finally, the fifth aspect of magical recipes rests on the ‘animistic’ principle that is the understanding that plants and animals have personalities, so to speak. Many plants and trees, as well as animals, are believed to have special powers: suffice it to mention the ‘royal tree’ (*rājavṛkṣa*), the ‘golden shower tree’, the *apāmārga*<sup>3</sup> (‘chaff-flower’ or ‘devil’s horsewhip’), the *sephalikā* (‘night-flowering jasmine’), or the *kapilā-*cow among the animals.

In general, the magical recipes investigated in this chapter possess their own kind of rationality. In this regard, one may quote a passage from anthropologist Hildred Geertz (1975: 83), who stated that magical practices

are comprehensible within the framework of a historically particular view of the nature of reality, a culturally unique image of the way in which the universe works, that provides a hidden conceptual foundation for all of the specific diagnoses, prescriptions, and recipes \[…\]. The common linking element is not a psychological attitude but an ontology.

Here we argue that the knowledge of specific material objects and shared rationality with regard to the methods of their manipulation suggests the existence of an underlying magical substratum shared by texts across genres and time periods.<sup>4</sup> In spite of the fact that this substratum or matrix located in the very materiality and circulation of objects employed in the magical recipes cannot be directly known, but only assumed, this model seems useful in that it allows us to achieve greater historical depth when studying the genesis of Tantric traditions, do more justice to their socio-cultural complexity and diversity, and also cast further light on the relationship between Hindu and Buddhist strands of Tantric magic.<sup>5</sup>

An example of a non-Tantric textual source paying special attention to *kar* *ma*s that parallel those given in Tantric texts is the *Arthaśāstra*, traditionally (yet spuriously) ascribed to Cāṇakya/Kauṭilya, chief minister of Emperor Candragupta Maurya (r. 320–298 BCE). This composite text on statecraft and military strategy, which has been tentatively dated by different scholars to varying periods within the temporal window going from the 1st to the 3rd century CE,<sup>6</sup> has still not received much attention from scholars specializing in Tantric studies, despite the fact that some of the magical recipes contained in this text have parallels with those included in early Tantric sources. The *Arthaśāstra* appears to be an important early source of magical practices in India. As McClish and Olivelle (2012: lxix) put it, ‘Kauṭilya gives more credence to what we might think of as magic—supernaturally effective practice—than to the supplication of independent divine powers’. There is evidence that, at least from the time of the *Arthaśāstra*, magical recipes were thought of as a distinct genre of literature, called *aupaniṣadika* (‘secret practices’). This title first appears in the 

*[p. 43]* ^pg56

heading of the 14th chapter of the *Arthaśāstra* as a collection of four books of magical recipes. The secrecy in which ‘magic’ operated was not incidental: as Dicke (2001: 38) states, ‘\[S\]ecrecy seems to be an inherent characteristic of magic’, and it is fostered by its placement in opposition to the religious orthodoxy that forbids it. Indeed, the *Mānavadharmaśāstra* (9.290, 11.64; see Olivelle 2005: 205, 218), comprising, as it were, the legal tapestry of ancient Indian society, put a heavy fine or even the punishment of the loss of caste on those who dabbled in magic; this may indicate that most of the magicians performed their *karma*s in secrecy.

Who were the magicians? The *Arthaśāstra* speaks of (*atharva*)*vedavid*s, Atharvan Brahmans, or *siddhatāpasa*s, accomplished ascetics, who are also said to be skilled in magic (*māyāyogavid*: 1.19.32cd, 4.3.11, 4.3.44ab). In general, the  text attests to at least three distinct categories of magic specialists: those dealing with incantations (*yogamantra*), those dealing with root-witchcraft (*mūlakarma*)—a non-Brahmanical group often denigrated for using suspect techniques—, and those specializing in cremation ground rituals (*śmāśānika* *karma*). The last group appears to be especially relevant for our present purpose since, at least from the point of view of material culture, there is a striking continuity between the *Arthaśāstra* and Tantric texts, especially with regard to specific organic and inorganic items collected from the cremation ground. On this basis, one could assume that Brahmins would have had a particularly hard time engaging in these magic procedures, considering that contact with corpses was not only forbidden for the members of the highest class of Indic society but also required many purification rituals that would impede on the feasibility of performing these *karma*s. Thus, one may infer that the ‘magicians’ involved in these types of rituals probably belonged to lower social strata, outside those of the literate elites. Insofar as the functions of magical recipes are concerned, many *kar* *ma*s found in the *Arthaśāstra* have distinctively military purposes directed against the enemy—e.g., an army—, which intend to cause death, paralysis, madness, blindness, disease, fever, coma, or poison water, but there are also *karma*s resembling the purposes commonly constituting the repertoire of a Tantric *siddha*, such as invisibility or the *siddhi* of swift-footedness. This peculiar blend of ‘war-- magic’ mixed with an interest in obtaining supernatural powers belonging to the world of fantasy represents a defining feature of magic’s ontology, which is rooted in a blurring of the natural and supernatural, and as such, it also persists in *karma*s found in Tantric scriptures. The *Arthaśāstra* also features a section on the antidotes (14.4) that contains magical recipes directed against magic inflicted by the enemy, such as removal of poisons and cures for madness and various diseases. This demonstrates that there was a conviction that certain practices and objects could reverse the effects of magical procedures—a belief also shared by Tantric scriptures.

The list of ingredients one needed to employ in magical rituals did not only require some kind of botanical knowledge and access to nature<sup>7</sup> but also entailed a belief that certain animals and plants possess a personality—a feature shared by animism. Generally speaking, animism is a worldview or ontology seeing 

*[p. 44]* ^pg57

the world as characterized by ‘a continuity of souls and a discontinuity of bodies’ between human and non-human entities (Descola 2014: 275). Being shared by many nonliterate small-scale societies and ‘indigenous’ people across the world to this day, it has been described by scholarship as being in opposition to organized religions representing ‘high cultures’ and literate traditions. There are many examples of folk animism that have been integrated into organized religions, as epitomized, for example, by the encounter of Buddhism with the indigenous animism of Bon in Tibet that led to the parochialization of the former religion (Samuel 1993), or by the Japanese Shinto entrenched in autochthonous animism, which has developed in connection with the more sophisticated tradition of Buddhism (Earhart 1982). Animism, in its turn, is closely connected with shamanism,<sup>8</sup> a phenomenon that is embedded in a plethora of (mainly orally transmitted) indigenous religions of the Indian subcontinent, such as those of the Austroasiatic-speaking Munda communities. Since some practices and agents found in those indigenous religions overlap with those found in some genres of Tantric sources, for instance, the Gāruḍa-and Bhūta-tantras (see Slouber, this volume), specializing in the healing of snakebites and the exorcism of evil entities, respectively, one may hypothesize the existence of continuities—in the form of appropriation or common origin—between the two traditions.

As a matter of fact, various tribal groups in India rely on shamanic agents for contact with the otherworld, to invoke or drive away malignant entities (for instance, in possession rituals, or *bhūtakarma*s), to perform healing procedures, and to influence the realm of climate, hydric resources, and agriculture. But similar tasks are also carried out by agents that most scholars would not hesitate to call ‘Tantric’—or ‘royal chaplains’/‘priests’/‘ritual specialists’—in literate and politically centralized societies, such as premodern Indian royal courts. As noted by Slouber (this volume), the tightly separate categorization of ‘shamanic’ and ‘Tantric’ agents made by most scholars may reflect a cultural bias rather than actual social realities. And, as argued by Davidson (2017: 26–27), in premodern South Asia, there existed various fluid categories of sorcerers ‘operating outside of the aura of polite society or of received linguistic norms, even if some were patronized by king and court’, who cannot be neatly attributed a sectarian allegiance. Those agents may be regarded as ‘continuing sources for Tantric systems’—for their existence continues, in a transmogrified form and social context, well into the modern period—in spite of their marginalization by Brahmanical hegemony (*ibid*.). Along similar lines, we may ask, with Brunner (1975: 435), whether the Tantric Śaiva ‘*sādhaka-*magician’ (‘*sādhaka-*magicien’) could be the direct descendant of ‘folk magicians’ (‘magiciens populaires’) mastering the science of incantations (*mantravāda*), medicinal herbs, minerals, etc.

### 2.3 Charnel ground items and the question of the magical substratum

We shall now focus on the references to charnel ground paraphernalia and other items, including human corpses and skulls, in Tantric scriptures, as keys 

*[p. 45]* ^pg58

to gaining some insights on underlying socio-religious realities. While these references have been habitually explained as an influence of the Kāpālika tradition of Śaivism, which came to the fore from the 6th or 7th century CE, one might ask: could the Kāpālikas have been just one of the groups that were drawing upon the funerary rituals from an earlier, pre-Tantric magical substratum? In order to answer this question, we shall now look at the magical recipes contained in the *Arthaśāstra*, and compare them with the funerary items that also occur in the early Śaiva and Buddhist Tantras. This comparison, showing that the set of funerary items retrieved from the charnel ground was already present in the *Arthaśāstra*, suggests the possibility that an early magical substratum of funerary rituals might have been already in existence prior to their earliest attestations in Tantric texts.

#### 2.3.1 Human flesh ( mahāmāṃsa)

The ritual use of human flesh (*mahāmāṃsa*) is attested in Śaiva and Buddhist Tantras alike and has been usually labelled as part of the repertoire of the Kāpālika ascetic.<sup>9</sup> The Kāpālikas were often described through the key elements of their antinomian practices, such as the consumption of human flesh, the drinking of alcohol, and the enjoyment of (ritualized) sex. In an early Śāktatantra, the *Brahmayāmala*, the offering of a human victim performed in the charnel ground is a part of the great sacrifice (*mahāyāga*).<sup>10</sup> An early Buddhist Tantra, the *Vajrabhairavatantra*, focuses instead on the consumption of human meat that bestows various supernatural *siddhi*s, which should be undertaken in the cremation ground<sup>11</sup> as part of the ‘nocturnal vigil’ (*niśāṭana*, *niśaparyāya*, or *niśācāra*)—a practice that seems to be prototypically described in both Buddhist and Brahmanical sources.<sup>12</sup> While these are considered the elements of the Kāpālika practice *per se*, meant to emulate the conduct of the ‘ghostly’ form of Śiva, i.e. Bhairava,<sup>13</sup> the use of human meat for various magical practices is more likely to have existed before the advent of sectarian Tantric traditions as part of a shared ‘culture of magic’, which included beliefs and practices associated with the realms of various categories of malignant entities inhabiting the charnel grounds, including *preta*s (‘hungry ghosts’ or ‘unhappy dead’) and *piśāca*s (impure ghosts or demons who feed on human flesh).

Evidence of the use of human flesh as an ingredient employed in *karma*s, specifically aimed at destroying the enemies, is already attested in the first chapter of the 14th book of the *Arthaśāstra*, dedicated to various *karma*s for killing the enemy troops. There we find specifications regarding special types of substances offered to the fires procured from different places. These fires are classified as ‘unobstructed’ (*niṣpratikāra-agni*), which perhaps indicates that there is no recipe that could counteract its effects (see, e.g., 14.1.38). Among them features the offering of human flesh that is to be made into the fire of a funerary pyre (see infra). The use of human flesh for the purpose of destroying the enemies continues in essentially unchanged form in various Tantric scriptures. The *Guhyasamāja*, for instance, refers to human flesh as the killer of all the enemies 

*[p. 46]* ^pg59

(*mahāmāṃsena sarveṣāṃ nāśanaṃ*, 15.78), while the *Vajrabhairavatantra* mentions human flesh several times as a necessary ingredient to inflict a target with madness or to cause his death.<sup>14</sup> In the early Śaiva *Vīṇāśikhatantra*, human flesh is also used as the oblation into the fire, which causes an immediate uprooting of the enemy.<sup>15</sup> The same text (v. 189) also mentions it as the power-- ingredient that brings whatever one desires, and thus equals the benefit of 100,000 sacrifices. In more general usage, human flesh features as an important fumigant (*dhūpa*). This application is attested both in the *Vajrabhairavatantra* and the *Mañjuśriyamūlakalpa*, where fumigation with human flesh in front of the painting (*paṭa*) of wrathful deities is common.<sup>16</sup> The latter scripture gives a recipe where fumigation with human flesh causes the destruction of the enemy.<sup>17</sup> Human flesh features also as one of the objects that is to be sacrificed into the mouth of a corpse during the *vetāla-sādhana*. In this regard, the *Vīṇāśikhatantra* prescribes the following:

Human flesh with unhusked grains added to it, together with milk of a black cow, this mixture he should sacrifice into the mouth of a corpse without delay, until the ghost of the deceased rises and says to him: ‘What can I do?’ One may then beg of him the possessions one desires, O Illustrious One.<sup>18</sup> (Trans. Goudriaan 1985: 117) The *Arthaśāstra* (14.2.44) also provides another recipe that heavily relies upon contact with corpses. In this case, one prepares a paste to be smeared on the camel-skin shoes by obtaining marrow (*majjana*) extracted from pressing the foetuses born from parents belonging to all social classes or dead children buried in a camel-shaped vessel in a cemetery (*sārvavaṃ̇ikāni garbhapatanāny uṣṭrikāyām abhiṣūya śmaśāne pretaśiśūn vā*). This example of *pādalepa* shows a remarkable similarity with the procedures governing the use of animal fat in Tantric texts.

#### 2.3.2 Fire taken from a cremation pyre, from a caṇḍāla, etc .

One of the categories often invoked in magical practices of different genres is vocative fire-offerings, perhaps one of the best-understood categories of rituals thanks to the studies of Richard Payne and others.<sup>19</sup> The use of fire is widely attested in magical recipes of various Tantric scriptures, which often indicate the precise location wherefrom the fire should be taken for the magic to be successful. Among them is the fire taken from a crematory pyre and the one obtained from an outcaste (*caṇḍāla*). Other fires that are thought to be effective in bringing the success of magical *karma*s often come from the houses of lower castes, such as a butcher or a blacksmith. The *Vīṇāśikhatantra* mentions the fire collected from a *caṇḍāla*’s house as bringing about the destruction of the enemies.<sup>20</sup> The same is attested in the *Vajrabhairavatantra*, detailing a *homa-*recipe that kills the enemies made in the fire from a house of a *caṇḍāla*.<sup>21</sup> This understanding is shared by the *Arthaśāstra* (14.1.37), where fetching a fire from a *caṇḍāla* and sacrificing the meat into it causes the killing of the enemy. The later Yoginītantra 

*[p. 47]* ^pg60

*Cakrasaṃvara* (chapter 44, Gray 2007: 351) mentions the fire taken from a *caṇḍāla* or a charnel ground fire as suitable types of fire into which the offerings of sandalwood powder and black mustard seeds should be made if a person intends to bring another under one’s own control. The *Saṃpuṭodbhava* (7.251), an anthology of different Buddhist Tantras, says that the fire taken from a *caṇḍāla* or from a charnel ground will be successful in magic procedures that intend to cause harm. The passage from the 31st *Atharvavedapariśiṣṭa* (Türstig 1985: 92) mentions the *abhicāra* performed in the fire of a *caṇḍāla*, a funeral pyre, or in the fire lighted after a child is born (*sūtikāgni*) into which one should offer sticks of wood taken from the ‘terrible trees’.

#### 2.3.3 Crematory shroud (śmaśānakarpaṭa, mṛtakavāsa, etc .)

In Brahmanical culture, a crematory shroud (*mṛtavastra*, *mṛtacela*) has been associated with agents located at the lower end of the social hierarchy, together with the low-borns and the non-Aryans. In the *Mānavadharmaśāstra*, for example, only the *caṇḍāla*s and the *śvapaca*s (‘dog-cookers’) wear the clothes of the dead and as a social group, they are to be avoided at all costs as the embodiments of pollution (v. 10.52, see Olivelle 2005: 210). Despite its negative connotations, wearing a crematory shroud has been a part of the Buddhist ascetic practice in the Middle Period (Schopen 2007; Witkowski 2017). In this regard, the Vinayas and the early Mahāyānasūtra literature refer (often disparagingly) to a group of low-- status Buddhist monks, the *śmāśānika*s or *pāṃśukūlika*s, who as part of their meditation on impermanence reside in charnel grounds, meditate on corpses, and wear ‘the clothes of the dead’ (*pāṃsukūla*/*pāṃśukūla*, i.e., fabric from a cemetery or used to transport corpses, ‘comparable to the “vile state” of a heap of filth’: Witkowski 2017: 272; cf. infra).

The rag taken from the charnel ground (*śmaśānakarpaṭa*) seems to be an important material object in magic rites across Śaiva and Buddhist texts, but its application in the magical recipes varies. The *Vajrabhairavatantra* refers to the *śmaśānakarpaṭa*—or, as Lalitavajra explains, ‘the crematory shroud taken from a corpse’*—*as the substratum for drawing a *yantra/*mantra that has to be always soaked in poison, blood, *rājikā*, salt, *nimba-*tree and *datura*.<sup>22</sup> In the *Mañjuśriyamūlakalpa* (51.2ab–3), the charnel ground cloth is attested as the suitable canvas for painting the *paṭa* of Yamāntaka; it has to be drenched in blood and thoroughly dried afterwards. The *Vīṇāśikha* prescribes the use of a cloth taken from a dead Brahmin woman as the magical object that, when customized with the name of the target and the mantras and buried in the target’s house causes his death.<sup>23</sup> The *Arthaśāstra* gives a very similar recipe when it mentions the cloth of the dead (*mṛtakavāsa*) as one of the items that need to be buried or planted in the house or in the spot where a man whom he wishes to destroy lives or put on his foot.<sup>24</sup> The Buddhist *Siddhaikavīra* (1.16) gives the procedure of stopping the approaching army where one has to write a mantra on the rag from a charnel ground customized with the names of the generals of the opposing army. The rag has to be placed in the belly of the effigy of Gaṇapati made of 

*[p. 48]* ^pg61

beeswax and buried near the camp of the opposing army. This will stop its invasion. In the *Saṃpuṭodbhava* (7.189, 7.201), a rag of the charnel ground, or alternatively a rag of a madman or a rag struck by a weapon, is considered a suitable canvas for writing the mantras that will paralyze the enemy. Although the majority of purposes for the employment of a rag taken from the charnel ground or a dead person fall under the category of *abhicāra*, their use was not limited to harming victims. In one *karma* of the *Siddhaikavīra* (1.42), the rag of the charnel ground customized with a mantra and a name of a target is employed for protecting a pregnant woman from losing her foetus. In this case, the rag has to be enclosed in beeswax and thereafter placed in a pot and buried in the charnel ground.

#### 2.3.4 Flowers from a corpse (śmaśāna-nirmālya)

In the Śaiva *Guhyasūtra*, flowers from a corpse are one of the ingredients procured from the cremation ground used for making a magical pill (*guḍikā*) that bestows the *siddhi* of travelling long distances:

With one or other of the two dark fortnights, having fasted at night, he should take the flowers from the corpse and any cloth from the cremation ground, lower leg of the crow or the rosary pea from the cremation ground, crush into a ball with a juice of that. Mixing it with *vāgvalī*, he should also add resin of *guggul*, he should make a pill out of it and then it should be recited over ten thousand times. He will cover a hundred *yojana*s \[…\].<sup>25</sup> In the *Arthaśāstra*, on the other hand, burying the flowers from a corpse together with other animal parts at the spot where a man places his foot causes the target’s impotence. The effects of this magical recipe last as long as those items are dug out.<sup>26</sup>

#### 2.3.5 Throwing the cremation ground ash (bhasman)

Cremation ground ash (*śmaśāna-bhasman*) is one of the most common ingredients for drawing a *maṇḍala* in the Śākta *Jayadrathayāmala* and the transgressive Yogatantras, like the *Vajrabhairavatantra*, as well as later Yoginītantras, such as the *Cakrasaṃvara*. The use of ash has been explained by scholars as a legacy of the Kāpālikas and their earlier prototypes, the Pāśupatas. Less known is its usage in magic *karma*s. Throwing the cremation ground ash was a popular magical technology employed for a wide array of magical purposes. The evidence of its existence as a part of the early magic repertoire is found in the *Arthaśāstra*, where a procedure involving the use of ashes for causing people to fall asleep is described. This recipe requires burying in the cremation ground a porcupine quill with three black and white stripes. The animal should be taken out on the 14th lunar day of the next dark fortnight and thrown together with the ash from the cremation ground at the people whom the wizard wants to fall asleep.<sup>27</sup> The 

*[p. 49]* ^pg62

*Cakramsaṃvara* (Chapter 12, Gray 2007: 212) gives the *karma* in which one enchants one hundred times a crematory ash obtained from burning a corpse of a *caṇḍāla* and throws it at a targeted person, who then assumes the form of any being one desires him to be. The *Mañjuśriyamūlakalpa* (52.25–31) contains various recipes that involve throwing of the ash for various purposes. If one throws the ashes enchanted by the mantra-recitation on one’s abdomen, the semen will be arrested (27.47); alternatively, he can throw it on any person he wants to subject to his will (52.25). The *Siddhaikavīra* (1.50) says that casting the ashes taken from the cremation ground at the enemy’s door drives him away.

#### 2.3.6 Skulls of a man killed by a weapon or impaled on a stake

One of the undersides of the tapestry of Tantric rituals, often connected with the Kāpālikas, was the use of skulls or corpses. The great importance attached to them is visible in the detailed instructions regarding the characteristics of skulls (*kapālalakṣaṇa*) and corpses (*śavalakṣaṇa*) found in Tantric texts. For example, Chapter 4 of the *Brahmayāmala* (4.740–779), in the subsection called the *mahāpātralakṣaṇa*, gives list of skulls that are defined in accordance with their external features, their castes, their colours, etc. While some of the skulls, like those having the colour of cow-butter and a glossy appearance, are listed as suitable for those who seek enjoyment (*bhukti*), others, like, for example, the round skull of a woman (*vartula*) with two matching skulls (*dvipuṭa*) are especially favourable for those who seek liberation (*mukti*). Certain skulls should be avoided. Thus, the *sādhaka* should avoid all skulls of the untouchables (*antyaja*) or any skulls of the four castes that have a visible defect. Similarly, the skulls that are blackish, that have some kind of discoloration, or are too short will cause a fatal disease; if the skull is black or red, it will cause the *sādhaka*’s death. Skulls were also believed to bestow various *siddhi*s. For example, according to the *Brahmayāmala*, if the *sādhaka* finds a skull with 100 segments, he will immediately achieve the *siddhi* of *uttiṣṭha*, i.e. either summoning a *vetāla* or flying in the air.<sup>28</sup>

Skulls were employed for a variety of purposes. For instance, they were used as a ritual vessel (*arghya*) that contained (often transgressive) substances offered to the deity. They were also used to build a special type of hearth consisting of three skulls placed horizontally (*trimuṇḍacūlikā*), which was especially popular in the Buddhist Tantras.<sup>29</sup> Or they were employed as material objects in various magical rituals. Skulls or corpses were often classified on the basis of the type of death that had befallen a person. Among the most desirable skulls or corpses, we find those that are classified as uninjured (*akṣata*) or those that have died with unnatural violent death, by a weapon, by being impaled on a stake (*śūlaprota*), or through hanging (*udbaddhā*). For example, the *Mañjuśriyamūlakalpa* gives the following recipe:

On the 14th dark fortnight, one who has fasted for a day, goes to the cremation ground and anoints the *maṇḍala* with the cow-dung which has not fallen. 

*[p. 50]* ^pg63

Having taken a skull from uninjured corpse, and having smeared it with red sandalwood, he offers worship to it with red flowers and incense. Having sat on the skull, he should cultivate the skull reciting the mantra; as long as he does that, the skull will move, then he will become a *siddha*.<sup>30</sup> The *Brahamayāmala* describes the following type of a suitable corpse that is to be employed in Tantric ritual:

On the \[night\] of the eighth *tithi* of the dark half-month, or on the fourteenth, the practitioner should take a corpse, one that has been hung or impaled, \[or\] an undamaged \[corpse of a\] male child, hairless, not too tall, not \[too\] short, not too fat, not \[too\] thin, without sunken eyes, without eyes too large, valorous, a great hero, with all \[desirable\] characteristics.<sup>31</sup> While the pervasive references to these specific types of skulls and corpses in Tantric scriptures has been explained as an appropriation of Kāpālika elements, the presence of the same kind of rationality is also found in non-Tantric texts. For instance, according to *Arthaśāstra* 14.3.4, the skull of a person who was impaled by a stake and a person who was killed by a weapon have special magical properties: if a person wants to become invisible, he has to plant barley seeds in the soil placed in the skull of a man who has been either killed by a weapon or impaled on a stake (*śastrahatasya śūlaprotasya vā puṃsaḥ śiraḥkapāle*) and sprinkle it with sheep’s milk. In another recipe, a wizard who has fasted for three days should plant Guñja seeds in soil placed in the skull of a man killed by a weapon or impaled on a stake. Once the seeds have sprouted, he should collect the sprouts on a ‘new-moon or a full-moon day when the moon is in conjunction with the Puṣya constellation and get rings made out of them. When vessels containing food and drink are placed on them, they do not become exhausted’.<sup>32</sup> These recipes shared with Tantric scriptures the view that certain skulls or corpses had magical properties, which would thus seem to predate the advent of the Kāpālikas.

### 2.4 The origin of skull-magic: Brahmanical, Tantric, or indigenous?

While upholding a strongly orthogenetic model for the origins of Tantra within the Brahmanical fold, Flood (2006: 14) admits that ‘certainly there are elements in Tantric traditions that may well reach back into pre-history—particularly the use of skulls and the themes of death and possession’. Independently taking up this line of enquiry, Davidson (this volume) argues that the Śaivas did not get the practice of carrying skull-cups from the Dharmaśāstras<sup>33</sup> and that, in fact, the use of skulls as implements does not at first occur within Indian religious traditions, but rather at the northern (i.e. Central Asian) periphery of the Indian subcontinent, in a much earlier period. He shows that this practice is attested in archaeological evidence from a plethora of sites throughout Britain and Europe from the Upper Palaeolithic to the Neolithic and Bronze Age, as well as in sites in Southeast Anatolia from 9600 to 700 BCE, and is further supported by textual 

*[p. 51]* ^pg64

evidence from Greek and Chinese sources, which refer to the practices of making bowls out of the skulls of enemies and drinking from them, sometimes coupled with an ancillary practice of the ritual preparation of skulls for an ancestral cult (*ibid*.). We should like to add that headhunting and ritual consumption of skulls as well as ritual cannibalism are characterizing features of the so-- called indigenous religions of Austronesian-speaking people dispersed over an immense geographical area from insular Southeast Asia to the Pacific (Watts et al. 2015). As argued by Blust (1995: 490), proto-Austronesian speakers practiced headhunting in association with war booty, and this practice, as in other parts of the world, ‘was inextricably tied up with religious ideas, in particular notions of human and agricultural fertility resulting from the capture and ritual incorporation of souls or spiritual force from outside the community’. Given the documented pre-and early historical contacts among people across Eurasia as well as, along the maritime routes, across Monsoon Asia,<sup>34</sup> it seems not unwarranted to speculate that ancestral practices involving the cultic use of skulls might not have originated in the Indian subcontinent but were the result of cultural diffusion. In the Brahmanical world order, these practices survived among the ‘outsiders’ living at the margins, i.e. ‘tribal’ people, as well as such groups as the demonized and ambiguous Kāpālikas (which, admittedly, were often not distinguished from tribals),<sup>35</sup> and became rationalized as the 12-year expiation (*mahāvrata*) prescribed by the Dharmaśāstras for the crime of *brahmahatyā* associated with Bhairava.

### 2.5 Who were the pre-Tantric śmāśānika practitioners?

If a similar rationality in the realm of magic, especially with respect to the use of charnel ground objects, skulls, and corpses, is shared by Tantric sources as well as textual sources predating the emergence of Tantric traditions, the questions arise as to whether such groups as the Kāpālikas were drawing upon the repertoire of earlier figures, and whether this repertoire overlapped, and owed to, the practices of indigenous societies intersecting with an ancient cultural substratum of ‘popular religiosity’ predating both Brahmanical and Buddhist literature. Potential prototypical candidates could be the cremation ground specialists referred to as *śmāśānika*s in Tantric and non-Tantric texts.

The possibility that an early magical substratum of cremation ground rituals was already in existence prior to the advent of the Kāpālikas is suggested by Megasthenes’ account: when he came to India in the 4th century BCE as the ambassador of King Seleucus I Nicator at the court of Candragupta Maurya in Paṭaliputra (modern Patna in Bihar), the Greek historian encountered a tribe he calls the Garamanes, which he divided into three sub-groups. First, the forest-- dwellers who are the ascetics abstaining from wine and sex and living on fruit and leaves; second, the mendicant healers skilled in doctoring through drugs; and third, the ‘soothsayers’ (*mantikoi*) and ‘utterers of incantations’ (*epōdoi*), skilled in the rituals pertaining to the deceased and the underworld who go begging from village to village (Dicke 2011: 111). This classification of the Garamanes is quoted by the geographer Strabo in the 1st century BCE, who classifies these three groups 

*[p. 52]* ^pg65

as the mountain *pramnae*, the naked *pramnae*, and the city *pramnae*. The most interesting is the sub-group of the mountain *pramnae* who wear deerskins and carry pouches filled with roots and drugs. They are experts in medicine (*phar* *maka*), incantations (*epōdai*), and sorcery (*goēteía*), and they are distinguished from the naked *pramanae* who practice different forms of asceticism (*ibid*.).<sup>36</sup> Megasthenes’ account of the mendicants specializing in necromancy poses a question about the existence of a pre-Tantric group of cremation ground specialists, which would be in harmony with the *Arthaśāstra*’s reference to magic specialists trained in the cremation ground rituals (-*karmabhiḥ śmāśānikair*, 4.4.14). These specialists could have belonged to what Weber (1967: 295) called a ‘scorned substratum of disreputable magicians preoccupied with the problem of folk religiosity’ existing (mainly) outside of the Brahmanical order.

*Śmāśānika*s (within both the Hindu and Buddhist fold) seem to have positioned themselves in, and developed out of, the territory between the margins (e.g., of the Buddhist monastic order or of Brahmanical social order) and the lay mainstream, coupling individual asceticism with the meeting of the ritual demands of the community. Hindu communities had a necessity to transition their dead from ghosts, or ‘problematic recent dead’ (*bhūta* or *preta*), to ‘harmless long-term ancestral dead’ (*pitṛ*) (DeCaroli 2004: 88), and ‘Buddhist analogues for these rituals such as merit-making for the dead through offerings to the *saṅgha* developed as Buddhists sought to supplant Brahmanical ritual expertise’ (Witkowski 2017: 290n53). Along similar lines, Davidson (2002: 222) argues that for Buddhists, ghosts were ritual opportunities, ‘for Buddhist meditators developed (and continue to develop) rituals for the liberation of the newly departed (*preta*), who may be wandering the earth confused and in need of compassionate assistance’, and points out that this became a ‘Buddhist specialty throughout Asia, with ghost rituals in efflorescence everywhere Buddhist monks took on the responsibility of beings’ liberation’.<sup>37</sup> As Witkowski (2017: 290) summarizes,

There seems to have been a sustained, if complicated, relationship between the Buddhist monk and the cult of the dead from very early on. On the one hand, the exercises of self-cultivation often lumped in with the broader category of cemetery practices (*śmāśānika*), which involved contact with the dead, were viewed by at least some members of the public as unsightly at best and as criminal at worst. On the other hand, monks might be seen as ritual specialists capable of negotiating relations between living and dead, and thus performing a salutary social role in the context of the cemetery. \[… H\]ouse-holders viewed these monks as ritual specialists capable of mitigating the potential for danger posed by spirits which might otherwise have been ignored or improperly tended to \[…\].

Elaborating on the question as to whether the Kāpālikas were just one of the groups drawing upon the crematory rituals from a pre-Tantric cultic substratum, Davidson (this volume) calls attention to the references in the Pali canon to the 

*[p. 53]* ^pg66

*pāṃśukūlika*s as a recognizable group of practitioners who carried a skull-cup as a begging bowl, who were also ‘associated with cults to the *piśāca* spirits who feast on flesh, generally in charnel grounds’. This association with *piśāca* spirit cults seems relevant indeed. Witkowski (2017: 290) argues that the relationship between the *pāṃśukūlika* monk and the dead is

conditioned by two competing ritual attitudes which are not specific to ancient Buddhism but were pervasive in late Iron Age India. The first is the desire to ritually distinguish the world of the dead from that of the living, by requiring rites of purification after contact with another’s physical remains, and in the disposal of the dead in locales outside the zone of settlement. The second is the ancient Indian imperative demanding that those who inherited the legacy of the dead continue to care for their ancestors in order to ensure that the substantial influence of the dead over their descendants was positive and not deleterious to the community of the living.

Given this socio-religious context in the Indian subcontinent going back to at least the late first millennium BCE, and the many references to spirits, spirit-- possession, and offerings to spirits, as well as necromantic practices in the context of the charnel ground found in early Sanskrit and Pali literature, it does not seem far-fetched to propose that *śmāśānika* agents known from later textual evidence reflect Hindu and Buddhist developments of earlier agents adhering to the ‘spirit religion(s)’ (DeCaroli 2004) found in the Indian subcontinent and possibly also throughout Central and East Asia as well as the Indo-Pacific (Reuter 2014). In many of these religious systems, mediumship between, and transition from/to, the world of the living and the world of the dead was a prerogative of the so-called shaman. According to this model, *śmāśānika* specialists might have been gradually absorbed into the ranks of organized Śaivism and Buddhism, and Tantric milieus were the most productive loci of exchange since they were populated by liminal and low-status social agents who did not have to abide by the strict rules of purity upheld by Brahmanism and monastic Buddhism. These agents came to be regarded as ‘specialists of the dead’, qualified to handle impure substances and serve the needs of lay communities not only in terms of funerary rites but also of spirit-related practices, including possession and exorcism, necromancy, and perhaps black magic too.<sup>38</sup>

Suggestive of an enduring link in the Indic imaginary between Kāpālikas and low-status agents frequenting the charnel ground are the several passages in Sanskrit literature that either openly or punningly attest to the meaning of the word *kāpālika* in the sense of ‘cremation ground attendants’—untouchables who gather the corpses and, just like the *pāṃśukūlika*s, appropriate the garments of the dead.<sup>39</sup> Intriguing references to funerary agents possibly related to a cultic milieu that is remindful of Tantric practices have been detected in the Pali canon by Gombrich (1996: 135–164). One such instance is the term ‘Kālī’ used in *Theragāthā* 151 as a proper name (according to Gombrich) referred to a woman who breaks bones and who, in the translation of K. R. Norman, piles 

*[p. 54]* ^pg67

them up (Mudagamuwa and von Rospatt 1998: 178). While to Gombrich this would represent an early reference to the Śākta goddess Kālī adorning herself with skulls, according to Mudagamuwa and von Rospatt (*ibid*.) this is probably a derogatory term referring to a large lady of dark complexion who acts as the ‘female guardian of a cemetery’ (*susānagopikā, chavaḍāhikā*), and who piles up bones of the recently burnt corpses (including a skull broken ‘like a curds-- bowl’). These bones are then used for visualization—seemingly an early form of *aśubhabhāvanā—*by a monk called Mahākāla (!) who meditates in the charnel ground seeking to obtain arhantship. While these references are too fragmentary and ambiguous to be interpreted, as done by Gombrich, as traces of Śaiva/Śākta worship *ante litteram*, they nonetheless seem to suggest the existence of an association between funerary and meditative themes, which in their turn are related to low social agents occupied in the charnel ground, whose names curiously prefigure those of the later Śaiva and Śākta divinities Mahākāla and Kālī.<sup>40</sup> All this seems unlikely to be merely the result of coincidence and may rather be a reflection, however dim, fragmentary, and distorted, of prototypical imaginaries and social realities that were codified in later periods of the Indic socio-religious life.

### 2.6 Conclusion

This chapter has argued that the shared repertoire of *karma*s contained in the *Arthaśāstra* and early Tantric scriptures reveals the existence of commonalities that may stem from an early ‘magical substratum’ out of which various sectarian traditions grew since the late Gupta age. Further research is needed to fully appreciate the cultural and religious context associated with magic in the *Arthaśāstra*, for instance on such elements as the mantras mentioning demons (*asura*) connected with witchcraft, some of which bear names that might have non-Indo-- Aryan origins.<sup>41</sup> These elements highlight the role played by marginal, non-Brahmanical milieus in shaping this shared religious matrix.

An analysis of passages on *karma*s and agents relating to the charnel ground found in pre-Tantric textual sources across sectarian boundaries, combined with insights from archaeology and ethnography, may help us to frame the emergence of Tantric agents as the crystallization of earlier figures carrying out ancestral practices belonging to a substratum of ancestor cults and shamanism—of the kind that is still practiced in modern ‘indigenous religions’ of the Indian subcontinent. For instance, it may be argued that the Kāpālikas, as well as such Buddhist practitioners as the *pāṃśukūlika*s, adopted some elements of the repertoire of earlier groups of specialists in funerary rites that must have been widespread among the general population and that a process of cross-fertilization might have involved Kāpālika, Buddhist, and ‘tribal’ milieus—all situated at the margins of Sanskritic culture and the Brahmanical social order. The same type of cross-fertilization could have involved crematory ground practices and other ancestral practices involving cannibalism, headhunting, and mediumship involving contact with the spirits and souls of the deceased. Admitting to the problem of proving a direction of flow, which may be impossible to accomplish because ‘it would require 

*[p. 55]* ^pg68

historical reconstructions beyond what the literary and inscriptional evidence allows’, Smith (2006: 151–152) argues that ‘from a very early date a South Asian culture of possession must have existed that enveloped Aryan and non-Aryan, brahman and non-brahman’. We would like to advance a specular argument about the existence of a shared ‘culture of magic’, which we can only imperfectly reconstruct from comparing textual passages, but which must have involved socio-- religious realities that were much more complex and varied than what we can appreciate today on the basis of the limited evidence at our disposal.
