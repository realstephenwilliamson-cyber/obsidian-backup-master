### 4.1–11: Promise of rest

*[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-1|Text]], p. 468* · The author’s reading of Ps 95 forms the substance of his argument from 3.7 to 4.11. Although the word “promise” is not used in the Psalm, God does promise rest to the people. The author interprets “today” to make the Psalm speak to his audience, not as a simple cautionary tale, but as proof that God’s promise of rest was not realized by settlement in the land, since God proclaims even “today” “they shall not enter my rest.” Therefore God’s promise of rest must refer to something in the future, whether as a place, a condition, or both. See 4.4n. ^4-1

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-1|1]]:** *Still open*, since the promise went unfulfilled, those faithful to Christ can obtain it. ^4-1-2

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-2|2]]:** *Good news*, gospel message (from Gk “euangelizo”). *Not united by faith with those who listened*, not everyone is in solidarity in disobedience. Moses implores the people not to resist God’s instruction to enter Canaan, but to trust God’s promise to subdue the inhabitants and deliver the land to them. The people are unpersuaded, and their disobedience excludes them from the promised rest. They were condemned to wander in the wilderness until they died. The next generation inherited the land. But the author is not merely talking about the past; then as now, those who rebel against God—even if once favored—will be excluded from their divine patrimony. *Those who listened* thus becomes both Joshua and Caleb in the past, and the followers of Jesus in the present. ^4-2

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-3|3]]:** Again quoting [[Psalms 095#^95-11|Ps 95.11]]. *Foundation of the world*, at creation. ^4-3

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-4|4]]:** *In one place*, [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^2-2|Gen 2.2]], which uses the verb rest to describe God’s rest on the seventh day. By connecting the word “rest” in the Psalm to God’s rest in [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^2-2|Gen 2.2]], the author takes “rest” to be an eschatological reality, where Jesus’ followers enjoy their divine inheritance as eternal life. ^4-4

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-5|5]]:** *Again in this place*, [[Psalms 095#^95-11|Ps 95.11]]. *My rest* in the Psalm is the land of Israel, God’s resting place. Rabbinic sources debate whether [[Psalms 095#^95-11|Ps 95.11]] signals that the wilderness generation will not inherit the world to come**:** Rabbi Akiva cites Num 14 and Ps 95 to suggest that they do not; Rabbi Eliezer cites [[Psalms 050#^50-5|Ps 50.5]] to suggest that they will. (See e.g., *t. Sanh.* 13.10; [[Sanhedrin 110#110b|*b. Sanh.* 110b]].) ^4-5

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-6|6–7]]:** *Today*, both David’s time and the time of the writing of Hebrews. Cf. [[Sanhedrin 98#98a|*b. Sanh.* 98a]], which evokes [[Psalms 095#^95-7|Ps 95.7]] in the context of messianic expectation. *David*, the Psalm’s traditional author. *Much later*, David lived centuries after Joshua; therefore, the settlement of Canaan could not have fulfilled the promise of rest. ^4-6

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-9|9]]:** *Sabbath rest*, Qumran’s 4QShirShabba portrays the heavenly host (together with the Dead Sea community) as *faith in those who listened* b Gk *he* celebrating a heavenly Sabbath. *M. Tamid* 7.4 describes the messianic age as a Sabbath (“a song for the world that is to come, for the day which is wholly Sabbath rest for eternity”); see also *Gen. Rab*. 17.5. Hebrews speaks only of this eternal Sabbath and not of weekly Sabbath celebrations. *People of God*, designating Israel in [[II Samuel 14#^14-13|2 Sam 14.13]]. Gk “laos,” “people,” often refers to Israel in the LXX (e.g., [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^33-13|Ex 33.13]],[[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^33-16|16]]; [[Hosea 04#^4-6|Hos 4.6]],[[Hosea 04#^4-8|8]],[[Hosea 04#^4-12|12]]), and is used of Jesus’ followers in the NT (e.g., [[Acts 15#^15-14|Acts 15.14]]; [[1 Peter 02#^2-10|1 Pet 2.10]]). ^4-9

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-10|10]]:** *As God did from his*, some Jewish and Christian sources indicate that God continues to work even on the Sabbath**:** see [[John 05#^5-16|Jn 5.16–18]]; *Ex. Rab*. 30.9; Philo (*Cher*. 87.90) describes this divine rest as “working with complete ease.” ^4-10

### 4.12–16: Divine judgment

*[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-12|Text]], p. 469* ^4-12

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-12|12–13]]:** A severe, awe-inspiring characterization of the Word of God (i.e., Scripture). It closes the discussion of Ps 95 that began in [[Hebrews 03#^3-7|Heb 3.7]] and functions as a warning about the power of scripture to render judgment. ^4-12-2

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-12|12]]:** *Word of God . . . living and active*, reminiscent of the refrain throughout the Tanakh to “hear the word of the Lord.” That God’s word has vitality is evident from God’s having created the world through speech in Gen 1. *Word of God* as *sword*, see [[Ephesians 06#^6-17|Eph 6.17]]; [[Revelation 01#^1-16|Rev 1.16]]; [[Revelation 02#^2-12|2.12]]. God’s word can separate what is seemingly indivisible. *Soul from spirit*, Gk “psyche” and “pneuma.” “Soul” and “spirit” when used of persons are often synonyms in modern English, but in ancient Greek they refer to two distinct elements or substances that, together with “body” (“soma”), make up a human being. “Psyche” is typically understood to be more intertwined with the body (or flesh) than is “pneuma.” The Hebrew equivalent of “pneuma” is “ruaḥ.” The words have different connotations but overlap in that both can mean “breath” (as well as “wind,” and “spirit” as a force outside of individual bodies). The closest equivalent to “psyche” in Hebrew is “nephesh,” but the semantic difference between “psyche” and “nephesh” is greater than it is between “ruaḥ” and “pneuma.” “Nephesh” is not a distinctive element within a person; it is a person. As such it is not separable from a person’s “body.” There may be an allusion to [[Proverbs 20#^20-27|Prov 20.27]]. ^4-12-3

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-13|13]]:** *Hidden*, no one can hide from God; see [[Psalms 139#^139-7|Ps 139.7–12]]; *4 Ezra* \[2 Esd\] 16.62–63. *Account*, Gk “logos,” rendered “word” in v 12; the double use of “logos” is an implicit argument that scripture demands that one give a personal *account* before God. Here it is used in the context of judgment before God. (Cf. Rom 4.) ^4-13

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-14|14]]:** *Passed through the heavens*, see 1.3–4. Special individuals like Enoch ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^5-24|Gen 5.24]]) and Elijah ([[II Kings 02#^2-1|2 Kings 2.1–12]]) experienced heavenly journeys; Ezekiel and Daniel have visions of heavenly journeys (Ezek 1; Dan 7). Heavenly journeys appear frequently in extra-biblical texts of the centuries surrounding the common era**:** the best-known is *1 En.* 1–36, and in early Jewish “merkavah” or “chariot” mysticism (derived from interpretations of Ezekiel’s vision). *Confession*, faith or confidence in Jesus; see 10.23. ^4-14

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-15|15]]:** See 2.17; 5.2. No other NT text emphasizes Jesus’ sin-lessness to the degree of Hebrews, but the idea is suggested in [[2 Corinthians 05#^5-21|2 Cor 5.21]]; [[1 John 03#^3-5|1 Jn 3.5]]. *Pss. Sol*. 17.36 depicts the Messiah as “clean of sin.” ^4-15

**[[NRSVue Hebrews 04#^4-16|16]]:** *Throne of grace*, on the divine throne, see [[I Kings 22#^22-19|1 Kings 22.19]]; [[Ezekiel 01#^1-26|Ezek 1.26]]; [[Daniel 07#^7-9|Dan 7.9]]; *1 En.* 14. Here it is not clear if the *throne* belongs to God or Christ. The expression, unique in the NT, replaces the “throne of glory” (e.g., [[Jeremiah 14#^14-21|Jer 14.21]]; [[Matthew 19#^19-28|Mt 19.28]]) and highlights God’s (or Christ’s) reign as expressing merciful forgiveness. Later rabbinic texts likewise speak of God’s “throne of mercy” (e.g., [[Avodah Zarah 3#3b|*b. Avod. Zar.* 3b]]). *Boldness*, confidence because Jesus’ intercession makes possible access to God. See 8.1. ^4-16
