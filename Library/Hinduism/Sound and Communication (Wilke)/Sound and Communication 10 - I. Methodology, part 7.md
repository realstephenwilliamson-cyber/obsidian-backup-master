The debates turned into philosophical theological discussions of varying (Brahmanic and non-Brahmanic) schools and confessions that now took a course formally very similar to that in Europe. However, the discursive constellation was quite different than that in Europe. There was no state religion. In classical times the relationship between the king and the religious leaders was not defined in terms of state philosophy or state law, and so neither was there anything like an investiture dispute. The kings usually defined themselves in secular terms, or through only a fuzzy concept of religion, which would not have been possible any other way on a continent like India with multiple religions. Religious groups who felt disadvantaged and over-ambitious gurus with a broad following were a constant potential source of problems and wise kings took appropriate precautions. In the manner in which the nations of the world take one another on today in football championships, so that they do not have to shoot at one another, the rulers sponsored “philosophers” championships” with attractive prizes in which protagonists with differing world-views had to defend them in argumentation. It was generally the king who decided who won, and not a commission of learned theologians as in Europe. That killed two birds with one stone. On the one hand the constant prospect of a large prize ensured that the king could always count on religious leaders being cooperative, and on the other it forced the diverse groups to talk to one another and not only about one another. This way of settling disagreements was also copied outside the courts. Disputes between two religious communities — such as a disagreement over the use of certain religious sites — could also be settled this way in a controlled manner. 

This form of communication becomes tangible in the literature above all in commentaries that are characterized through and through by the dispute style. Formalized presentation and counter-presentation take turns, doubts are put forward and then dispelled, and theses from rival schools are set out and disproved. These fictitious narratives of the disputes also frequently include undertones of sarcastic attacks. Even works such as the Sarva-Darsana-Samgraha (“Compendium of all world-views”) were composed, presenting all the existing world-views of the time. Such works were written not only out of documentary interest, but obviously also to reveal weaknesses in other world-views (those from a different school), so that in a future confrontation in real life one would be better prepared to tackle those with opposing views."

The relationship between science and religion was construed in a far more contrastive way in the Occident than in India. In the following we resume the discussion of natural science and continue the topic of differing socio-cultural conditions impacting on science. As our major interest lies in the relationship between science and religion and historical developments in both areas, we supplement (more explicitly than in the foregoing chapters) Reckwitz? theory of aesthetic and non-aesthetic styles with Michel Foucault’s theory of discursive formations and correlation spaces.“ 

The liturgical and logocentric models and corresponding forms of science, styles and contents are discursive formations and they themselves created such formations. Discursive formations always have to do with correlation spaces in which a statement can acquire meaning or not. Therefore, what can be said and how something can be said not only depend on content, being scientific and the quality of the statement, but are also subject to societal restrictions, previous processes of inclusion in and exclusion, implicit convictions and claims to the truth, which affect the strategic decisions and the persuasive force of ideas. Spectacular discoveries like the spherical shape and rotation of the Earth or the law of gravity, which in Europe led to the modern scientific world-view, are found in India as early as Antiquity and in the early Middle Ages among the astronomer-mathematicians Āryabhata, Brahmagupta and Lalla, but they did not have the same impacts as in Renaissance Europe owing to the different discursive conditions. 

Why was Āryabhata’s postulate of the rotation of the Earth not pursued after the fifth century AD and why was no heliocentric world-view developed? Why did the discovery of the law of gravity by Brahmagupta and Lalla in the seventh century not lead to the development of a scientific system of physics? With European Christianity guiding one’s interpretation it is tempting to think that one has the answer instantly to hand that all of this was due to “ecclesiastical” limitation and the dominance and power ambitions of the Brahmanic Hindu orthodoxy. But the situation is not as simple as that. The philosophical Darśanas are indeed characterized by their loyalty to the Veda, but they are completely different world-views — actually six Darśanas, literally “views,” which were later put together into pairs because in each case only two of them share the same metaphysical foundations. For their part they already demonstrate an astonishing plurality in world-orientation. In this context it is also interesting that one must add another question to those posed at the outset: why did Brahmagupta and Lalla reject Āryabhata’s rotation theory, while accepting that the Earth was spherical, even though this contradicted equally massively the Purāṇic world-view of the Earth as flat? The answer to all the questions is simply this: because the conditions were different. There were certain discursive combinations that were significantly different than those in medieval and Renaissance Europe, which were responsible for the strategic choices and explain why no system of physics was developed, while in astronomy advanced mathematics developed, and in the field of religious discourse advanced philosophies emerged that reflected pluralistic orientations. Both directly and indirectly, the “liturgical model” exerted an effect not only on the formation of aesthetic style, but also on the scientific objects themselves, for instance the fact that astronomy was actually the leading discipline among the natural sciences. At the same time, the liturgical model was not fixed and rigid, and instead allowed considerable opportunities for modeling the “guiding Vedic culture” (“vedische Leitkultur”) within certain limits. Within this discursive formation we find quite different strategies for determining the relationship between science and religion, and different strategies to work through breaks and turning points than those found in medieval and Renaissance Europe on the threshold to the modern age. 

In India in Antiquity and the Middle Ages, the relevance of science to the world and actual practice, and the drive to engage in scientific activity diverged considerably from those in Europe during the Renaissance. All the revolutionary scientific discoveries — that the Earth is a rotating sphere and the law of gravity — were made within the realm of astronomy and not by chance, because there was specific practical interest

here, whereas there was no analogous impetus for the development of physics. In Europe the development of physics and scientific method profited from the interest of the Renaissance rulers in patronizing scientists such as Galileo and Leonardo da Vinci in order to gain benefits in waging war. In India one wanted to make better calculations of the movements of the planets in order to find the right times for rites and important profane acts and to extract the greatest possible gain from them, to be able to make more effective sacrifices, avoid inauspicious influences, and draw up better horoscopes for births, marriages and building houses. The law of gravity and the rotation of the Earth were irrelevant to such matters and were not pursued further. Moreover, Āryabhata’s spectacular postulate of the rotation of the Earth (although it was not a heliocentric view of the universe) was considered far too improbable and outlandish by succeeding generation of scientists, even the supporters of Āryabhata’s school. In view of the flight of birds and other observations, they were convinced of the stability of spatial coordinates. So the theory of the Earth’s rotation remained ineffective because there were no criteria for its validity or ways of integrating it into the accepted theory (“keine Gültigkeitskriterien und Anschlussmöglich-keiten”). But a different theory became widely established, namely that the Earth is spherical (bhugola). Here criteria for its validity and possibilities of integration existed owing to its immediate practical relevance. It was true that the bhugola theory also contradicted the Purāṇic world-view that the Earth was flat, but the calculations were easier with a sphere than with the small thin disc and that was what was important. For this reason the theory also spread among the religiosi, from the Brahmanic astrologer drawing up horoscopes as a private business, to the Purānṇic literature.™!

For another, they themselves were part of an interpretation of the world and of a social and religious practice that prescribed the strategies and boundaries and determined which findings were developed further and which remained ineffective. In this very different correlation space in which science developed in India, the relationship between religion and science was more one of give and take than one of mutual exclusion or a fundamental gap. The scientific questions and debates were determined, just as they are today in the modern quantitative disciplines, not by mathematics as such but by its application to the physical world, and that meant specifically application to the astronomical era. One of the main controversial points was the question of the compatibility and incompatibility of the choices of astronomical parameters and theories with the Purāṇic theory of length and periods in the age of the world (yuga). There were definitely clashes and controversies. Yet religious mythical interpretations of the world and scientific interpretations were not seen as opposites, but instead were continually adapted to one another. They even tended to be interchangeable, because one assumed that both of them had the same truth content, even if the truth content of religion (primarily the

Plofker was correct to comment on the mathematician-astronomers of Antiquity and the Middle Ages by saying: “Since mathematical and scriptural truths were not necessarily treated as separable, agreement with sacred authority was considered to be one aspect of scientific validity.”\*® Calculations contradicting the Purāṇic theory of the age of the world etc. were therefore a problem even for the scientists themselves and ignited the most controversies. There was always freedom of opinion. But it seemed that theories and parameters with even a hint of textual heterodoxy and socio-religious deviance about them were less acceptable because they could not be reconciled with established views. It was therefore less coercion on the part of orthodox Hindus or a fear of being burned at the stake that exerted a restrictive effect, and more one’s own interiorized world-view and the desire for the scientific statements to be compatible with the Brahmanic cosmology. Conversely, however, it was true that whenever the new scientific findings broke with the Purāṇic world-view, Hindu orthodoxy did not categorically reject them on principle. Instead, it tended to be religion that was adapted. It was more the case that the Brahmins once again scoured their Purāṇas when a new discovery appeared to deviate radically from their cosmology. Purāṇa cosmology was adapted to the new knowledge that the Earth was spherical by duplicating Mount Meru as the home of the gods at the North Pole and the home of the demons at the South Pole. Adaptations like these to new scientific theories by the religiosi can also be found in philosophical discourse. For instance, the Pūrva-Mīmāmsā, the strictest form of Veda orthodoxy, was unable to escape the developments in logic, linguistics, knowledge theory and natural philosophy and found itself forced to adapt its understanding of the Veda as an eternal, authorless “knowledge” of invisible things to the rationalistic theories, i.e. to defend its claim to the truth of the Veda as an eternally valid text of revelation in equally rationalistic terms. Rather like a by-product, from this there emerged innovative contributions to research into linguistics that stand up to comparison with modern Western linguistics. 

Unlike the Roman Catholic Church, which had to defend not only its holy scripture but also its monopoly on interpretation against such figures as Galileo and Giordano Bruno, Hindu orthodoxy had greater flexibility in its interpretations. Only the truth of the sacred texts and their eternal validity had to be defended, and this was always possible by means of “translation,” modification of the argumentation patterns, recoding, re-interpretation, paraphrasing, re-writing, mental interpretation, allegorization, etc. With such strategies, the relationship between the religious establishment and science was more one of exchange than one of mutual exclusion. However, the interactive reciprocal relationship between science and religion had certain limits — wherever the boundaries between the differing views could no longer be reconciled so easily. But that had less to do with belief than with the dharma practice of the social order, morals and lifestyle, i.e. the norms of the surrounding Vedic culture that was reinforced, perpetuated and re-invented in the Purāṇas and the laws. 

Like all discursive formations, the scientific statements, styles and functions are always part of a much more comprehensive complex of statements. They are constructed within a network and demarcated from other objects. In classical India one can identify an interactive continuum involving the liturgical model, the function of the Veda as the overall culture, social conventions (dharma rules), natural science, philosophy and theology, which was relatively consistent and intact in the first millennium of the Common Era. Although Vedic rites were hardly being performed any more, and a great plurality of religious and profane meaning orientations was possible, the Veda continued to act as a restricting and legitimizing factor, both visibly and invisibly. Those who desired to be and remain visible and authoritative embedded themselves in this nexus. Ultimately, the Vedic “guiding culture” (“vedische Leitkultur”) retained restrictive power even in the Tantric period. The liturgical model allowed flexibility and even breaks, yet wherever a break appeared, the positions were not permitted to go too far apart. On the other hand, the inexorable rise of non-Vedic ritual cultures created new discursive formations. While the Āgama universe established itself as new liturgical model, it simultaneosly expanded the validity criteria and social acceptability of the overall Vedic culture and steered it onto new paths. Of course, this socio-religious change could not have come about without a real basis in politics and financial power, i.e. royal patronage of Śivaitic ritual experts. As explained above, whereas the sciences were free to move within the boundaries of the liturgical model and yet implicit convictions, practical relevance and functions determined the space within which the scientific statements were realized, the limitations and expansions of the liturgical model itself were dependent on actual political interests. These observations are in line with Ernst Cassirer’s cultural philosophy and his theory of symbolic forms, which he understood as self-created media. It was Cassirer (1874—1945) who first introduced the elementary concept of the culturality of human beings into the discussion! and by so doing enabled one to think of contingency. A key point was his call for a semiotically enriched symbol theory that investigates cultural coding practices. He thus initiated a meaning-oriented (symbolic and semiotic) concept of culture that has since become common in the cultural sciences and one that we also support. 

Cassirer’s symbol concept is a very broad one. In his theory of symbolic forms he stressed the interaction between human beings’ mental energies and their necessary manifestation in meaningful forms. We do not simply perceive a thing, but we always perceive it “as” something. There are no areas without symbols; every mental content of meaning is linked to a sensory sign, and vice versa. Therefore, the mental cannot exist in isolation from the materialization, and neither can reality be disconnected from one’s mental, i.e. meaning-giving processing. According to Cassirer, perception of expression precedes every perception of a thing. Every individual piece of data points to a more complex connection where it can make sense — a symbolic network of language, myth, art, religion, technology, etc. Consequently there is nothing that is presented by the senses (including supposedly purely natural factors such as sexuality, etc.) that is not linked to some preconceptualized idea or cultural meaning. The symbolic forms are the self-created media with which human beings assimilate the world and at the same time transcend it. These symbolic forms — or media structures in Cassirer’s sense — are not fixed, but are in a constant state of flux. Culture reproduces itself in ever new forms.

Because in practice the audible word is so important, language in India has seldom been removed from its acoustic physical nature and its sonic aura. It is revealing that in the Indian context it is difficult to differentiate between words and sounds even in terminology. The central Sanskrit term sabda covers the English terms “sound” and “word” equally. A word is a sound that is linked with a meaning. This sentence could have been written by the Austrian poet Ernst Jandl and today this idea is no longer particularly foreign to us Westerners. In the Indian context a word was always botha body of sound and the bearer of meaning. Although they do not impart meaning in the sense of content, they are still bodies of sound with their own audible expressivity. For the Indian thinkers, language is not only a means of communication, but in a way is also the subject of the communication process. The language itself is envisaged as a person and even individual phonemes as independent personalities or speech subjects with their very own statements. AD) even form the composite “language-cow” and speak metaphorically of the “most high deity, Language” as a “disembodied cow” (anaṁñga-dhainavi). Somehow, the terms appear to be linked by means of associations, and this link is perhaps most informative about Indian views of language. The link is a very old one. It dates back even as far as the oldest surviving Indian texts, the hymns to the gods in the Rgveda, for example in this extract from the eighth book: 

“The cow, who lifts up her voice in the skillfully speaking person, who is attended by all good thoughts, who, being a goddess \[even\] for the gods, moves around \[with them\] — let not the mortal be so silly as to relinquish her.” 

5“ In the hymns of the Rgveda, the term “cow” covers a dizzying diversity of concrete meanings, which are taken up again and again even in the later highly developed literature, such as artistic poetry, and are therefore also listed in the Amarakośa.\*” The most frequent physical phenomena for which “cow” can stand in the Rgveda (apart from the animal) are: “water,” “earth,” “daylight” and of course “language.” In this early usage the term “cow” is clearly a metonym. Everything that has anything at all to do with food or any of life’s resources can be called a cow, just as in German “Fuchs” (meaning “fox”) can stand for cunning, i.e. a cunning person (“schlauer Fuchs”), or “Esel” (meaning “donkey”) can stand for stupidity (“dummer Esel”). Whereas these modern examples may also be taken as metaphoric speech, allegory or figurative language, just as Indian poets liked to speak of the “lotus face” of a beautiful woman (meaning beautiful “like” a lotus), in the Rgvedic world cow and language have a real and substantial relationship, which is why we prefer to speak of a metonym.
