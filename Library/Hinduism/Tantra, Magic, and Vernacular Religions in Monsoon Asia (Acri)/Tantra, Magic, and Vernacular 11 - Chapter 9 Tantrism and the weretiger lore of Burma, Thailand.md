## Chapter 9: Tantrism and the weretiger lore of Burma, Thailand, and Cambodia

*Francesco Brighenti*

### 9.1 Introduction

In the Indo-Burmese border region, Tibeto-Burman-speaking tribal communities such as the Garo, the Karbi, the linguistically diverse ‘Naga’ complex of tribes, several Kuki-Chin-Mizo clans, the Kachin, and others all possess rich cultural traditions concerning the ‘weretiger’, whether this term is understood as indicating a supernatural being belonging to a monstrous race of shapeshifting tiger-- people inimical to humankind or the feline alter ego (or ‘psychic’ familiar) of a religious specialist, a healer, a warrior, or even a commoner. At the same time, several Austroasiatic-speaking tribal communities of mainland Southeast Asia, including the Khasi in the Assamese region, hold traditional beliefs of various kinds concerning the transformation, either physical or psychic, of both living and dead persons into tigers.

After researching this subject—which cannot be discussed in detail here due to space constraints—at some length, I have arrived at the conclusion that most of the aforesaid tribal weretiger beliefs are indigenous to mainland Southeast Asia and the Greater Assam region, and are not the product of Hindu-Buddhist cultural and religious influences originating from the Indian subcontinent—all the more so because weretiger myths and legends are conspicuous for their absence in both Hindu (e.g. Śaiva and Śākta) and Buddhist traditions of India (Brighenti 2021). The same, however, cannot be said of the weretiger beliefs embedded in the magical lore of the (non-tribal) Theravāda Buddhist populations of Cambodia, Thailand, and Burma, which, as will be shown in this chapter, have undeniable links with Tantric ritualism and, in a few cases, even with Hindu and/or Buddhist asceticism. Obviously, these cannot be regarded as purely ‘tribal’ traditions of magic but instead as hybrid traditions that were handed down among urban and rural populations of mainland Southeast Asia whose cultures were to varying degrees influenced by South Asia-derived stream of Hinduism and Buddhism through the centuries.

In addition to describing the weretigers beliefs of, respectively, the Khmer (Section 9.1), Thai/Lao (Section 9.2), Shan (Section 9.3), and Burmese or Bamar (Section 9.4) peoples, this chapter will provide, in its final discussion section, a  historical assessment of the South Asia-derived, Tantric religio-cultural 

*[p. 174]* ^pg187

component that, in my opinion, contributed to shaping the little-known weretiger lore of lowland mainland Southeast Asian societies.

### 9.2 Cambodia: Tiger-transformation and /sməl/ magic

In Cambodian (Khmer) folk magical traditions a weretiger once used to be referred to as a /sməl/.<sup>1</sup> This term, which is rarely used nowadays since the Khmer Rouge regime eradicated most of such ancient Khmer traditions back in the 1970s, actually designates a distinctive type of sorcery as well as its practitioners and the magic spells and incantations they avail themselves of (Aymonier 1883: 183–184; Porée-Maspero 1961: 210). This form of magic is believed to give one superhuman strength and to allow one to give off rays of light (Chuon Nath 1938–1943; Headley, Rath Chim, and Ok Soeum 1997).

The word /sməl/ is, most likely, a Khmer reflex of the Proto-Austroasiatic noun root *\*smuul* ~ *\*smuəl* ~ *\*smaal* (Shorto 2006), originally meaning a ‘shadow’ or ‘reflection’ and, by extension, ‘soul, spirit’ (Paul Sidwell, p. c.). This lexical root was used to form Austroasiatic words for ‘shadow, soul’ across an area extending from South Vietnam to East India. One may compare Modern Khmer /sməl/ with Surin or Northern Khmer /smʌːl ~ smɔːl/ ‘reflection, shadow’ (Chantrupanth and Phromjakgarin 1978), Modern Khmer /səmaol/ ‘(cast) shadow’ (Pou 1992), and the Old Khmer r-infixed (?) form /srəmoːl/ ‘shadow, shade; ghost, apparition’ (Jenner 2009). The practitioner of /sməl/ magic is considered to be able to emit rays of light as a visible manifestation of his power, and this kind of ‘reflective’ luminescence or radiance provides the semantic connection with the common Austroasiatic word for ‘shadow, reflection’. The term /sməl/ may, thus, refer to the visible aura of magically potent objects and/or persons in Khmer (as well as Thai and Lao) folklore that is also mirrored in Buddhism, where sacred objects and Buddha relics are said to emanate sparkling rays which are discernible by persons possessing the appropriate knowledge (kings, priests, hermits, and magicians).<sup>2</sup>

Human-to-tiger transformation, or feline therianthropy, is just one way in which the magical repertoire of people regarded as /sməl/ sorcerers manifests. In rural villages of Cambodia, such people may display their alleged superhuman powers in public by chewing and ingesting broken glass pieces or shards of porcelain plates without getting hurt, breaking iron bars with their bare hands, swallowing fire, or jumping into fire without getting burned, etc. It is his utterance of special magic formulas, likewise called /sməl/, that confers such extraordinary abilities upon the /sməl/ magician and allows him to emit a luminous aura; thanks to such mantras he can, among other things, take the form of any animal he wishes to (Cabaton 1922: 56; Porée-Maspero 1961: 209–210).

Numerous French colonial authors emphasize the aspect of Khmer /sməl/
magic as a means to theriomorphic transformation. To them, a /sməl/ is primarily a weretiger (Aymonier 1883; Leclère 1895; Cabaton 1910, 1922; Guesdon 1930). As to the supposed dynamics triggering one’s physical metamorphosis into a tiger and then back to human form, all such authors provide similar descriptions based 

*[p. 175]* ^pg188

on local folktales of Cambodia and the Khorat Plateau region of East Thailand, along the following lines:

	1\) Typically, a weretiger originates from a wicked, bewitched woman (or sometimes from a man), who is senseless and unaware of the circumstances leading to her animal metamorphosis. She is the ‘victim’ of a /sməl/
magician who, whether accidentally or on purpose, prompts her to anoint herself with a magical oil that automatically causes her transformation into a tigress.<sup>3</sup>

	2\) This magical oil or /tnam/<sup>4</sup> (Khmer for ‘medicine’; Leclère 1895), over which appropriate mantras have been spoken by a /sməl/ magician, and contained in a /kʰuəc/ vial (a small earthenware or porcelain vessel, often used in Cambodia for storing oil that is believed to have magical properties), causes the woman to lose her reason, run into the forest, join the tigers, grow fur and, after seven days, eventually become a tigress herself (Aymonier 1883).

	3\) The magical method used to restore the weretiger to human form would, according to all sources, consist in hitting the prospective tiger thrice with a stick provided with a hook, typically with a carrying-pole, or else with an elephant goad (Cabaton 1922). This action must be accompanied by the recitation of appropriate mantras of the /snae/ (‘love charm’) class (Leclère 1895). Perhaps the man skilled in sorcery who performs this exorcism is a /kruu snae/
(‘sorcerer who makes love charms’, from Sanskrit *guru* ‘preceptor’ and *sneha* ‘love; oil’), a kind of Khmer magical healer who provides ‘love medicine’ and promotes good luck in the form of charms.

	4\) Identical traditional beliefs (of Khmer origin?) about weretigers are reported by Aymonier (1885: 128) and Cabaton (1914: 796) from the Lao-inhabited Khorat Plateau or Isan region in Thailand. In a case recorded in Loei Province, seven days of purification with lustral water, accompanied by the prayers of Theravāda Buddhist monks, followed the alleged exorcism of a Lao weretigress.

	5\) Suspected weretigers who have managed to escape exorcism are identified by their frantic glance and shining, bloodshot eyes. In the past, individuals afflicted with such an appearance were sometimes made object of suspicion and preventive vengeance by a village, and were put to death to avoid the possible effects of their potential maleficence (Cabaton 1922). People suspected of being /sməl/-tigers are said to be indecent, and rude in speech. Some can no longer speak, and growl like wild beasts. Some take off their clothes and run the countryside naked, making obscene gestures to people of the opposite sex they meet (Leclère 1895).

	6\) Sorcerers turning women into /sməl/-tigresses ‘with the aid of magic philters’ are even mentioned as a menace to law and order in a Cambodian legal code of 1723, the *Cbpab Khon Sala* (‘Code for Magistrates’; Leclère 1898, II: 33). Therefore, turning someone into a tiger through the use of /sməl/ magic was once perceived as a real threat to Cambodian society—a crime to be prosecuted by the king’s courts.

*[p. 176]* ^pg189

Some parts or components of the aforementioned magical proceedings may have been Tantric (that is, originating from Hindu-Buddhist magical traditions of South Asia), ‘para’-Tantric (i.e., an indigenized form of Hindu-- Buddhist Tantrism), or merely Tantric-inspired. This is hard to determine, as the content of the mantras supposedly employed by Khmer /sməl/ magicians to turn people into tigers and back is, for obvious reasons, either secret or unrecorded. Yet in Nepal, for example, Tantric manuscripts containing mantras that would enable an adept to convert himself into a tiger are known to Brahmins in Bhaktapur and Pokhara (after interviews conducted at those sites by this author back in 2013–2014).<sup>5</sup> Conversely, the use of a /(dɑɑŋ) rɛɛk/ (Khmer name of a carrying pole with a hook at either end) or, alternatively, of a /ʔɑŋkoh/ (Khmer transcription of Sanskrit *aṅkuśa*, ‘elephant driver’s hook’) for attracting and subjugating a weretiger and bringing him/
her back to human form, attested in all ethnographic sources, appears to have Tantric ritual associations. In the multi-ethnic (Thai, Lao, and Khmer) Khorat Plateau region of East Thailand, wooden sticks with iron hooks or, more specifically, elephant goads, feature prominently in local possession rituals, which may include exorcisms, as is the case with the ritual restoring of a weretiger to human form within the framework of Khmer /sməl/ magic (Benjamin Baumann, p.c.). In Sanskrit Tantric texts, the elephant hook is considered one of the main symbols of the magical power of ‘subjugation’ (*vaśīkaraṇa*) and of the closely related magical power of ‘attraction’ (*ākarṣaṇa*), both often associated with ‘love charms’ and ‘love potions’ (Khmer: /snae/); moreover, in some Tantric Buddhist texts, human beings are said to be attracted to sacred images representing the goddess Tārā or some Bodhisattva by means of the elephant hook included among the latter’s divine attributes (Goudriaan 1978). *Ākarṣaṇa* and *vaśīkaraṇa* often seem to imply the reduction of a person to a state of complete and helpless obedience, which, in the Khmer oral tradition under discussion, is precisely the goal of the exorcism rite performed with the use of a hook and the uttering of magic /snae/ formulas to attract and subjugate a person transmogrified into a tiger and allow him/her to become human again.

An orally transmitted ancient Khmer folktale, published for the first time at the end of the 19th century, gives the name of the magical (Tantric?) technique of shapeshifting as *sūtr manta vijjā kala khluon* (/soot mʊən vɨccie kaalaa kluən/), a hybrid Sanskrit-Pali-Khmer expression meaning ‘recitation of magic formulas for changing one’s body’.<sup>6</sup> It recounts the story of a power-seeking Khmer king and his court who travelled as far as Taxila in India to learn magical arts from a /ʔaacaa/ (Sanskrit *ācārya*, ‘spiritual guide or teacher’) so as to become capable to transform themselves into animals, giants, and various supernatural creatures. Returning through the jungle after studying the magical technique of shapeshifting, they run out of provisions and, in order to catch some prey, turned themselves collectively into a majestic tiger (/sdac klaa/, ‘tiger king’). Yet their new powerful shape made them cease to think about their kingdom so that their collective metamorphosis became irreversible. This is how the first tiger was born (Thierry 1985: 

*[p. 177]* ^pg190

As far as feline therianthropy is concerned, another similarity of Cambodia’s /sməl/ magic to certain Tantric (or ‘para’-Tantric) ritual practices of South Asian origin is represented by the use of a magical oil triggering one’s transformation into a tiger. Some ancient South Indian lower castes, such as the Pāṇaṉ and Paṟaiyaṉ of Tamil Nadu (known, respectively, as Pāṇan and Paṟayan in Kerala), practiced an ‘art of black magic’ termed *oṭi* (‘sorcery’, lit. ‘to break/destroy \[the enemy\]’), whose practitioners were known as *oṭiyans* (‘sorcerers’; Wirz 1954: 33, n. 1). These initiated low-caste sorcerers, whose supreme deity is Nīli (‘the Dark One’), a demonic, fierce, and destructive manifestation of the Hindu Great Goddess (Shulman 1976), are traditionally believed to be able to prepare a magically potent ‘baby oil’ (Malayalam *piḷḷa tilam*), of which the liquid exuded by a human fetus smoked over a fire forms the main ingredient along with a powdered human skull. By rubbing his body with this magical oil or balm, an *oṭiyan* is supposed to acquire the ability to assume the form of any animal he likes—including that of a tiger (Thurston 1909, VI: 124–126). According to an interpretation, these low-caste magicians of Tamil Nadu and Kerala ‘used to follow lycanthropy \[= therianthropy\] as a traditional occupation, undertaking to kill or injure people on payment’ (Thomas 1966: 30).

The notion that a magical oil existed, which could be used to shapeshift from human to animal form, may have travelled from South India to Southeast Asian countries during the mediaeval period, though it is not clear how some low-caste adepts of Śākta-Tantric magical arts, who were actually outcasts, could have been included among the Indians who immigrated into those domains. In any event, magical oils made from human corpses are well-known in mainland Southeast Asia, for instance in Thailand, where the so-called /nám man pʰraay/ (usually rendered in English as ‘spirit oil’), an oily and fatty substance said to derive from a human corpse, and mainly used to make a love philter, is still nowadays said to be extracted by melting with the flame of a candle (called the ‘Candle of Victory’) the fat from the chin and/or the forehead of a corpse—preferably that of a woman who died while pregnant or from childbirth. The rites for the subjugation of the ghost of the dead person (Thai /pʰǐi taay tʰáŋ klom/, ‘spirit/ghost of a woman who died while pregnant’), who tries to come out of the corpse during the oil-extraction ceremony, are reminiscent of Tantric ceremonies associated with the ‘reanimation’ of a corpse through the medium of a *vetāla* spirit (Giles 1938; Anuman Rajadhon 1964: 167–169; Huang 2009). We will probably never know whether the magical oil or ‘medicine’ (Khmer /tnam/) that was reportedly used by Khmer /sməl/ sorcerers to create a weretiger was a kind of corpse oil (Thai: /nám man pʰraay/) similar in concept (from a Tantric perspective) to the fetus oil that was reportedly used by the *oṭiyan* sorcerers of Kerala and Tamil Nadu to turn themselves into tigers.<sup>7</sup>

Because Khmer /sməl/-magic lore holds that women are more subject than men to the phenomenon of tiger-transformation, a further connection can be traced between the weretigresses of Khmer magical folklore and other weretigresses described in oral narratives, myths, and legends of different 

*[p. 178]* ^pg191

Austroasiatic-and Austronesian-speaking peoples of mainland Southeast Asia. For instance, east of Cambodia, some Montagnard tribes of South Vietnam—e.g. the Jeh, Bahnar, Jarai, and Sre—have legends and folktales about maleficent weretigresses (Guilleminet 1952: 420, 451–452; Dournes 1978: 151–153, 159; Gregerson et al. 1987: 26–31). West of Cambodia, i.e. in Thailand, both the Thais and Karens have many legends and folktales about lustful female weretigers (Thai /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/) using their feminine charms to seduce males and devour them (Ratanakul 1984: 2 and n. 3). Thus, the theme of a seductive female weretiger appears to be deeply embedded in Indochinese folklore at large.

### 9.3 Thailand: The /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ and other types of weretigers

Just like in Cambodia, in Thailand, the physical metamorphosis of a human being into a tiger is often regarded as a magical procedure enabled by the knowledge of specific mantras. Such a traditional belief may betray an influence from esoteric, either Tantric or ‘para’-Tantric magical doctrines and practices of South Asian origin on mediaeval Thai sorcery. For example, a Thai folktale recorded by Coyaud (2009: 72–73) stresses the pivotal role of magic spells, taught to an initiate by an old, long-bearded Buddhist forest hermit (perhaps standing for a Tantric *ācārya*) for achieving one’s bodily transformation into a tiger. Such mantras are here called /sǎmrít/ (‘accomplishment, success’), a Thai term borrowed from Sanskrit, probably via the Angkorian Khmer form *samṛddhi* (Jenner 2009).<sup>8</sup> In the Vedic tradition, a *samṛddhi* (‘great prosperity, accomplishment, or success’ ~ *saṃrāddhi* in Pāṇini’s *Dhātupāṭha*) formula was a sort of benediction of the priests indicating the success of a Vedic sacrifice and containing blessings for the enhancement of a king’s power. Through the recitation of /sǎmrít/ incantations, the initiate in this story accomplishes ‘success’ and manages to turn himself into a tiger—the first one to appear on earth (compare the Khmer folktale about the first tiger being created from human beings after the recitation of magical formulas for changing one’s body, which is summarized in Section 9.1).

The earliest Western account of a therianthropic transformation from man to tiger in Thailand is probably that provided by Bastian (1867: 261–262), who reports about a ‘*Suä Saming*’ (Thai /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/) or man-eating weretiger who was a monk (‘Priester’) in a Buddhist monastery situated in the Chao Phraya River Valley. He could change into tiger form at night by reciting certain magic formulas, and he used to eat the farmers who came to the monastery by boat, so that in the end all of the inhabitants of nearby villages were frightened and dared not to come to the monastery, leaving the latter short of food. The same Thai legend is recounted, with slight variations, by Knappert (1992: 299), who sets the action in a monastery near Chiang Mai. In this version of the story, the monk—who, as a result of his long studies, had learnt the art of changing himself into a tiger—prowls in his tiger form the campsites of cattle drivers and devours their fattest calves. At dawn, he resumes his human form. The weretiger is finally killed by a merchant (and magician) who counters him by resorting to a kind of ‘water 

*[p. 179]* ^pg192

buffalo magic’: he fashions buffaloes from bamboo, then mutters magic mantras that turn the bamboo skeletons into real buffaloes, and finally instigates the buffaloes to attack the weretiger. The tiger escapes, but the next day the monk is found dead.

The learned Buddhist monk expert in black magic represents but one of the diverse incarnations a /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ usually manifests through. It can also appear in the form of a crying infant lost in the forest. If a person comes close, the /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ reveals its true form and devours him/her. Yet another form, very popular in Thai folktales, a /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ is supposed to assume is that of a beautiful and lovely maiden approaching a man in the forest whom she, after turning herself into a tigress, devours (sometimes after their lovemaking). This seductive weretigress typically appears as a woman to a hunter watching for game to shoot on a perch in a tree during nighttime. Then, she tries to induce the man to descend and meet her in order to kill him after assuming her tiger shape (Irwin 1907: 30–31). Sometimes the hunter waiting up in his tree stand sees his wife appear underneath the tree, beckoning and calling out to him to come down but, soon after that, he realizes this apparition is in reality not his wife but a weretiger; consequently, he shoots the woman dead, whereupon she turns back into tiger form before his very eyes. Nearly identical tales are narrated around hunting campfires in the jungles of Burma (Sein Tu 1999). According to Irwin (1907: 30), a tiger assuming the shape of a charming woman to tempt a hunter in the jungle at night and devour him would actually be a /pʰǐi pàa/ (‘forest demon’) who ‘appear\[s\] in the form of various animals, with an awkward habit of becoming invisible, or disappearing at will’.

But what kind of weretiger is a /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ exactly? First of all, from an etymological viewpoint, the Thai appellation /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ is a compound of the terms /sʉ̌a/, indicating any big cat species (tiger, panther, or leopard), and /sàmǐŋ/, for which the Royal Institute’s Thai-Thai dictionary (the official Thai dictionary, first published in 1950) gives the following definitions:

	1\) a tiger believed to have been originally a human being with great (black) 

magic skills enabling him/her to change his/her own appearance into that of a tiger; 2) a tiger that has devoured so many people that it has become haunted by the souls (/winyaan/) of its human preys, and that can consequently assume human shape, being in this case called a /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/, that is, a /sàmǐŋ/-tiger (see below);<sup>9</sup> 3) high rank of nobility—a title denoting a king, a city governor, or an administrative officer—in the Mon country, namely, in the Mon kingdom of Pegu (Pali: Haṃsāvatī) in Lower Burma, c. 1287–1552 CE.<sup>10</sup> Definition #1 implies a belief in a close interconnection between therianthropy and black magic, just as in the Cambodian case. Conversely, definition #2 implies a belief in the possibility of a ‘psychic possession’ of a man-eating tiger by the souls of the people it has formerly killed, which finds parallels in several tribal cultures of East India (though not in Hindu myths and legends).<sup>11</sup> Yet the meaning 

*[p. 180]* ^pg193

given in definition #3 is certainly the oldest, for the word /sàmǐŋ/ is generally believed to be a Mon loan in Thai with the meaning ‘lord, master, ruler’ (Gilles Delouche and Mathias Jenny, p. c.).

The indigenous Old Mon title *smiṅ* ~ /smøŋ/ (‘king, prince, headman, lord’) is first attested in inscriptions from Pagan in Burma dating from c.1100 CE (Shorto 1971: 394–395). This term was later borrowed into Thai as /sàmǐŋ/ and into Lao as /sámǐŋ/. In the latter language, too, this Mon loanword has the same double meaning it has acquired in Thai since Lao /sámǐŋ/ denotes botha ‘dignitary, governor, king’, and ‘a tiger who was formerly a man, but who was put under a spell’ (Kerr 1972). The Mon meaning of ‘king, lord (etc.)’ appears to denote the power or potency associated with the phenomenon of tiger-transformation since only a really potent ritual expert is able to metamorphose into a tiger—a really powerful creature and apex predator—and control the transformation.

It is not clear why the Siamese adopted the ancient Mon title *smiṅ*, borrowed into Thai as /sàmǐŋ/, to designate a weretiger. Why did they not choose a native Thai term meaning ‘king’, ‘noble’, or the like instead? Quite significantly, there is yet another Thai term for weretiger, /sǎaŋ/, which is considered a Mon loanword. It designates a legendary tiger-like animal or, to put it better, ‘an old tiger which can transform itself into a man, or vice-versa a magician who can turn himself into a tiger’ (Anuman Rajadhon 1964: 173n1), just like the better-known /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ does. A /pʰǐi sǎaŋ/ is a fierce and malevolent, tiger-like ghost, spirit, or demon in Thai folklore. The same terms are found in Lao (/sǎaŋ/ ‘\[mythical\] animal resembling a large tiger’, and /pʰǐi sǎaŋ/ ‘evil spirit in general’; Kerr 1972). According to Jenny (2013: 12), Thai (and Lao) /sǎaŋ/ is a likely loan from Old Mon (where the form -*siṅ* ~ /*-*søŋ/, meaning ‘lion’, is attested in inscriptions from Pagan dating from c. 1100 CE; Shorto 1971: 121) of the Old Indo-Aryan word *siṃha* (‘lion’). However, the meaning ‘lion’ is not found in Thai and Lao dictionaries, most probably due to the fact that lions never existed in mainland Southeast Asia; accordingly, the Sanskrit/Pali term for lion was applied there to a mythical (were-)tiger.

Therefore, the Central Thai or Siamese language has not one but two different terms for weretiger—/sàmǐŋ/ and /sǎaŋ/—that were formed on the basis of some pre-existing Mon words. Yet another term for weretiger found in Thailand, /sʉ̌a yen/,<sup>12</sup> commonly occurring in the Northern Thai language spoken in the Lan Na region with the apparent meaning of ‘cool/evening tiger’ (perhaps in the sense of ‘calm, relaxed’?), may be in fact a corruption of the Shan or Tai Yai term for weretiger, /sʰɤ<sup>1</sup> jen<sup>4</sup>/ (Shan /sʰɤ<sup>1</sup>/ ‘tiger’; /jen<sup>4</sup>/ ‘to pass from one condition of being or character to another’; Moeng 1995). Thus, there seem to be no purely native Siamese (or Central Thai) and Northern Thai terms for ‘weretiger’; all of the currently used ones were either borrowed from Mon or from Shan.

It is also not known whether the ancient Mons of the Dvāravatī culture of Thailand, the cradle of Buddhist Thai culture, had weretiger beliefs that might have been later assimilated into the successor, Tai-speaking cultures (the 

*[p. 181]* ^pg194

Siamese, Lan Na Thai, and Lao ones). The Mons certainly believed in the existence of tiger (Mon: *kla*) demons called ‘*ka*(*nd*)*o*(*k*) *kla*’ (Shorto 1971: 39) and in tiger-spirits causing sickness called ‘*wāsu* (= the mythical chthonic serpent Vāsuki of the Hindu tradition?) *kla sarāi*’ (Halliday 1917: 107). Moreover, in Nyah Kur, a Monic language of central Thailand spoken by a remnant of the Mon people of Dvāravatī, there is a term for tiger, /j ɔ̀ɔŋ-péeɲ/
(contracted form: /mpéeɲ/), whose literal meaning, ‘grandmother-grandfather’, is indicative of a very ancient Monic semantic association between tigers and ancestors (Diffloth 1984: 112) which finds many parallels in tribal Austroasiatic languages of mainland Southeast Asia. Hence, in spite of the lack of any inscriptional or iconographic evidence, one may speculate that the ancient Mons also entertained beliefs about weretigers or else about ‘supernatural’ tigers, as many other Austroasiatic-speaking peoples of mainland Southeast Asia do still today.

According to Gilles Delouche (p. c.), the earliest written attestation of the term /sàmǐŋ/ in Thai occurs in the Siamese narrative poem *Lilit Phra Lo* (c. 1500) in the name of Pu Chao Saming Phrai (/pùu jâw sàmǐŋ pʰraay/), a very powerful magician whose name can be translated as ‘venerable lord (or ‘grand old man’), king (Thai /sàmǐŋ/) of spirits/ghosts’, who could, among other things, metamorphose into various animals. This literary text was possibly a Siamese adaptation of an earlier work composed in Northern Thai in the Lan Na kingdom, which had previously undergone a strong Mon influence (Delouche 2000). In the *Lilit Phra Lo* Pu Chao Saming Phrai is a hermit, a sorcerer, and a mountain lord who gains strong magic powers from meditation until he is able to control spirits, animals, human beings, and nature. He is also ascribed the ability to change at will the appearance of animals and people, including his own. He is, therefore, portrayed in the poem as botha transformer and a shapeshifter. In the *Lilit Phra Lo*, the epithet Pu Chao Saming Phrai seems to be almost a fixed phrase, used as a predictable block of oral poetic structure (Bickner 2020). Kongkananda (1982: 79–81) holds the view that Pu Chao Saming Phrai was not simply a magician but an ideal ‘indigenous Thai god’ symbolizing ‘two different sets of value systems simultaneously’—namely, Buddhism and animism (i.e., Thai ghost-lore).

In the Royal Institute’s Thai-Thai dictionary, the appellation /sàmǐŋ pʰraay/
(Thai) ~ /sámǐŋ pʰáay/ (Lao) is equated to /jâw pʰǐi/ (‘lord of spirits’), the name of a tutelary or guardian spirit of the jungle and wilderness otherwise known as /aarák/ (from Sanskrit *ārakṣa*, Pali *ārakkha*, ‘protection’; Anuman Rajadhon 1964: 154). Giles (1932: 187, 189) freely translates /sàmǐŋ pʰraay/ as ‘lord of tigers’ or ‘tiger king’, though a /pʰraay/ is not a tiger but a potent death-associated spirit in Thai folklore. The name of this local guardian of forests may have been invented as a response to the ancient legend of Pu Chao Saming Phrai and the role this figure played in the *Lilit Phra Lo*. In today’s Thailand, Pu Chao Saming Phrai is venerated at some temples as a tiger-faced hermit (/rʉʉsǐi nâa sʉ̌a/), and amulets depicting him as a tiger-headed /rʉʉsǐi/ (‘hermit’, from Sanskrit *ṛṣi*) seated in yogic posture are very popular throughout the country. Furthermore, still today, 

*[p. 182]* ^pg195

Thai /rʉʉsǐi/ or forest hermits, often encountered as long-bearded and long-haired men wearing a tiger skin or a garment printed with tiger stripes, are believed to possess a wide range of supernatural powers, including that of transforming themselves into animals.

In sum, there appears to be a close connection between the name of Pu Chao Saming Phrai, the great magical shapeshifter portrayed in the *Lilit Phra Lo* some 500 years ago, and the common Thai term /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/, indicating a weretiger. Both names share the lexical element /sàmǐŋ/, itself an Old Mon loanword. It seems likely that the phenomenon of feline therianthropy became known in everyday speech as /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ (/sàmǐŋ/-tiger) in accord with the magical shapeshifting abilities of the /rʉʉsǐi/ (or god?) Pu Chao Saming Phrai, which provided a literary model for it. The original Mon meaning of the term /sàmǐŋ/ (‘lord’, etc.) was lost, and /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ became simply a well-known designation for the phenomenon, in which /sàmǐŋ/ stands metonymically for Pu Chao Saming Phrai or any other supernatural shapeshifter. Thus, the word /sàmǐŋ/ became permanently associated with the weretiger complex of Thailand.<sup>13</sup>

### 9.4 Burma: Shan weretigers (/sʰɤ 1 jen 4 /)

The court chronicles of several former Shan princely states of East and Upper Burma record one and the same foundation legend stating that a great ‘White Tiger spirit’ (Shan: /tsaw<sup>3</sup> sʰɤ<sup>1</sup> pʰɤk<sup>2</sup>/, lit. ‘lord-tiger-white’) was the ancestor of all Shan ruling families (Milne and Cochrane 1910: 44; Mong 2004: 371). This legend possibly constitutes a relic of a former totemism (Seidenfaden 1958: 44), which also seems reflected in the fact that some Shan royal houses are exogamous lineages of the Tiger clan (Leach 1954: 216). Many hereditary Shan princes or *saohpa*s (Shan: /tsaw<sup>3</sup> pʰaa<sup>5</sup>/, lit. ‘lord of the heavens’) of the past included the honorific /sʰɤ<sup>1</sup>/ (‘Tiger’) in their throne names. The title of ‘White Tiger’s Head’ (Shan: /ho<sup>1</sup> sʰɤ<sup>1</sup> pʰɤk<sup>2</sup>/) was conferred on the *saohpa*s of the Shan principality of Hsenwi by the Emperor of China at a very early date (Scott and Hardiman 1901a: 183, 191). Still, nowadays a tiger head is the national emblem of Shan States.

The aforementioned foundation legend maintains that a blind princess from China (probably Yunnan is meant) was put on a raft and set adrift on a river by her parents. She was found by a white tiger (some versions claim it to be a weretiger prince, about whom see next) who cured her blindness. They got married and had four sons, all of them bearing names beginning with the title /sʰɤ<sup>1</sup>/
(‘Tiger’) since they were the children of the White Tiger spirit. Each son built a city of his own on either side of the Irrawaddy River. The meaning of this legend is that the princess must have married some Tai/Shan ruler who belonged to one of the lineages of the Tiger clan (Scott and Hardiman 1900: 202–203; Scott and Hardiman 1901b: 780–781; Milne and Cochrane 1910: 19; Hom and Tun 1997; Mong 2004: 14; Hsen 2009: 12).

The official chronicle of the principality of Hsenwi holds that one of the names of that state was ‘Hsenwi Four Tigers’ (Shan: /sʰen<sup>3</sup> wi<sup>1</sup> sʰi<sup>2</sup> sʰɤ<sup>1</sup>/)—probably a reference to the four legendary sons of the White Tiger spirit who ruled the Shan 

*[p. 183]* ^pg196

country in the beginning, called /paw<sup>3</sup> mɤŋ<sup>4</sup>/ (‘chiefs of the country’) in that chronicle (Scott and Hardiman 1900: 227). They probably represented as many Tai-- speaking ruling lineages from western Yunnan who moved westward to lay the foundations of the Mao-Shan states in Burma and were called ‘Tigers’ (Milne and Cochrane 1910: 15–16). Rispaud (1966: 219), however, contends that the Shan phrase /sʰen<sup>3</sup> wi<sup>1</sup> sʰi<sup>2</sup> sʰɤ<sup>1</sup>/ (‘Hsenwi Four Tigers’) actually originated from a misunderstanding, on the part of the Shans, of the similar sounding Chinese title of *xuanwei shisi* 宣慰使司 (‘Pacification Commission’) conferred on the ruler of the Hsenwi State by the Ming government in 1404 through a seal of office used thenceforth by the *saohpa*s of Hsenwi—an expression that perhaps fit well with a pre-- existing legendary tradition concerning the four Shan princes, sons of the White Tiger.<sup>14</sup>

In addition to this, ancestral tiger worship among the Red Shans of Upper Burma is evident in the veneration of the ‘White Weretiger Prince’ in the ancient town of Mohnyin at the Buddhist shrine built on the spot where his bones were allegedly buried by his sons, which is known as the White Tiger Prince (Burmese /ʧá pʰyù mí*̃*/, lit. ‘tiger-white-king’) pagoda (Sein Tu 2004c; Hla Maw 2017: 131, 163). Assimilated to the white tiger who marries the blind princess in the historical legend mentioned previously, this ‘tigerish’ man could transform himself into a tiger through magical knowledge in order to easily hunt game animals in the wild. Representations of beneficent tigers are found on the portals or on the platforms of Buddhist pagodas in areas populated mainly by Red Shans who trace their legendary origins to this weretiger progenitor, such as at Mohnyin and Mogaung in the Kachin State. According to Sein Tu (2004a), all this would point to the existence of a kind of ancient ‘weretiger cult’ among the Red Shans, which appears to be a benign one. Indeed, among the Shans, weretigers are generally not supposed to attack human beings or domestic livestock, in the same way as the legendary White Weretiger Prince only preyed on jungle animals (Sein Tu 2004c).

The Shan highlanders of northwestern Thailand, or Tai Yai, attribute a human being’s ability to shapeshift into were-animal form to two different orders of supernatural phenomena: the hosting of a /pʰi<sup>1</sup> pʰɤ<sup>5</sup>/ (lit. ‘witch spirit’, a type of hereditary and contagious spirit-power that permanently resides within members—particularly female ones—of cursed clans and makes it possible for hosts either to have a ‘spirit double’ that can be sent to distant locations and to turn themselves into animals, mainly into tigers; Tannenbaum 1993: 67–69)<sup>15</sup> and the mastery of secretive, esoteric (Tantric?) techniques of therianthropic transformation learnt through the teachings of some spiritually potent Buddhist hermit. The latter magical techniques are best described in a Lan Na Thai folktale entitled ‘The Weretiger of the Shans’ (Thai: /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ kʰɔ̌ɔŋ tʰay yày/), which recounts how a Shan initiate can become a /sʉ̌a yen/—a Thai term translating as ‘cool/evening tiger’ that, as pointed out in Section 9.2, is most likely a loan from Shan /sʰɤ<sup>1</sup> jen<sup>4</sup>/ (‘weretiger’). The method, said in different oral traditions to have been taught to man either by the ‘Great White Spirit’ (i.e., the ancestral White Tiger?) or by a ‘master’ (i.e., a Tantric *ācārya*?), would consist of the following steps (Keely and Price 1972: 55–62; Sein Tu 2004a; Coyaud 2009: 113–114):

*[p. 184]* ^pg197

	1\) The master tattoos the appropriate talisman with sacred *yantra* designs and magic mantras (Thai /kʰaa tʰǎa/, from Sanskrit/Pali *gāthā* ‘verse, stanza’) on the initiate’s shoulder.

	2\) The initiate undresses and recites the mantras etched in his talisman on his clothes while sitting in the *samādhi* (‘meditation’) pose. Knowledge gradually arises in him; his body shakes violently, and he writhes to the left and to the right.

	3\) He entrusts the bundle of his clothes to a companion, who must hide the clothes (and himself) in a nearby tree.

	4\) He must urinate, making a circle with his own urine, and then throw himself fully naked on the ground, rolling from side to side. Shortly thereafter, he becomes a tiger.

	5\) The weretiger goes hunting in the forest and later brings the killed animals back in his mouth, dropping them off in the circle he had made with his own urine.

	6\) Then his companion, sheltered in the tree with his clothes, throws the latter, protected by incantations, down to him. As soon as he sees his own clothes, the weretiger becomes human again.

	7\) Practitioners of this magical art can control their own consciousness and will not kill people or their cattle, just wild animals. They will take care of people walking through the forest so that they are not harmed and will protect Buddhist monks who come to practice *dharma* in the middle of the forest.

Tattooing with tiger figures inscribed in *yantra*s is a widespread magic practice among Tai-speaking peoples; for instance, the Dai (= Tai) Lue people of Xishuangbanna Prefecture in the extreme south of Yunnan (China), culturally and linguistically related to the Shans of Burma, believe that a legendary tiger king (Dai Lue ‘Bashehong’ = /pʰaʔ<sup>2</sup> sɤɤ<sup>1</sup> xoŋ<sup>5</sup>/, ‘holy tiger’) was a master of tattooing and that those with tattoos can turn themselves into tigers (Ziquan 1989). The recitation of mantras to trigger one’s own transformation into a tiger is, on the contrary, most likely due to the influence of Hindu-Buddhist (Tantric?) ideas. However, it must be pointed out here that some nearly identical magical methods for metamorphosing into tiger form and back are recorded across a wide geographic area stretching from the Assamese region in India to Upper and East Burma and northwestern Thailand. In particular, tales about tiger-transformation current among the Tibeto-Burman-speaking Kachin people of Upper Burma include the motifs of the recitation of magic spells as a prerequisite for the transformation, the undressing and rolling on the ground of the would-be weretiger, the tree-climbing of the weretiger’s assistant in order to protect himself from the prospective tiger, and the weretiger’s return to human form after showing him the clothes he had taken off (Girsham and Thomas 1971; Dunlop 1979). In Northeastern India, the Garos of Meghalaya, linguistically related to the Kachins, allegedly had similar beliefs; among them, the mantra allowing one to turn himself into a tiger is known as the Narasiṃha (Sanskrit for ‘man-lion’) mantra (Brighenti 2017).

### 9.5 Burma: Bamar weretigers (/θamá̃ ʤá/)

Little is known about traditional Burma (Bamar) beliefs about weretigers due to the latter’s extreme rarity in our days, but it is usually assumed that their bodily transformation is made possible by their knowledge of magic, just like in the case of the Khmer, Thai, and Shan weretigers expounded on earlier.

In Lower Burma, a weretiger can be generally called /lù θá sá ʤá/ (lit. ‘man-- flesh-eat-tiger’), or else simply /lù ʤá/ (‘man-tiger’). A better-known Burmese term for weretiger is /θamá̃ ʤá/, rendered in English as ‘man turned into a tiger by black magic’. The origin of this compound is difficult to assess. Literally, it would translate as ‘*kuśa-*grass tiger’ (Bernot 1992; Myanmar Language Commission 1993), with *kuśa* (a synonym of *darbha*) being the Sanskrit name of a sacred graminaceous plant having medicinal properties and used in certain Vedic ceremonies.<sup>16</sup> According to the description of a Burmese watercolour painting provided by an anonymous Christian missionary,<sup>17</sup> a *thaman chah* (/θamá̃ ʤá/), here translated as ‘tree tiger’—a gloss similar to, but not exactly the same as ‘*kuśa-*grass tiger’—would be ‘a fabulous monster supposed to reside in caves in the deepest forests’. Burmese medicine-men who, after seeking for an unspecified, poisonous wonder-working herb, at last find it but neglect to pronounce the proper mantras when plucking it up, are here said to be punished by being transformed into tigers. Likewise, a Burmese text maintains that ‘if one prepares \[with certain herbs\] the drug which rejuvenates and that does not succeed well, one becomes a /θamá̃ ʤá/’ (Bernot 1992: 41). The term /θamá̃ ʤá/ also designates a kind of creeper according to Judson (1921: 1004).

Therefore, there seems to be a linguistic connection in Burmese between the main term for weretiger and the one for the sacred *kuśa* grass, although there is no clear-cut explanation for such a linguistic usage. Abbott and Khin Thant Han (2000: 134) tell us that, for some indigenous groups of Burma, tiger-transformation is the result of eating a certain (which one?) kind of grass. All in all, the link between the Burmese terms for ‘*kuśa* grass’ (/θamá̃/) and ‘weretiger’ (/θamá̃ ʤá/) cannot be ignored, though a popular etymological hypothesis links this term to the name of the Taman (or Htaman) tribe of Upper Burma instead. For example, Keely and Price (1972: 55–62) implicitly suggest that the Burmese term /θamá̃ ʤá/ may actually indicate a ‘Htaman’ or ‘Htamanthi’ tiger, Htaman (Burmese: /tʰamá̃/—not the same word as /θamá̃/) being the exonym given by the Bamar to a small, virtually extinct, and deeply ‘Shan-ized’ Tibeto-Burman-speaking tribe living in the town of Tamanthi (or Htamanthi) in the Upper Chindwin Valley. They could allegedly turn themselves into tigers using a magical procedure very similar to that described for the Shans in Section 9.3, and certain villagers in Upper Burma still claim descent from a man who was so metamorphosed (Grant Brown 1911: 306; Maung Shwe Zan Aung 1912: 55; Rabinowitz 2001: 18). Besides being the name of their present headquarters, Htamanthi is the alternative exonym for the Htamans (Grant Brown 1911: 308).<sup>18</sup> The originally Shan form of this tribal name, ‘Tamansai’, would be a Shan formation of /t<sup>h</sup>am<sup>3</sup>/ ‘cave’, 

*[p. 186]* ^pg199

/mon<sup>3</sup>/ ‘be happy’, and /s<sup>h</sup>ɤ<sup>1</sup>/ ‘tiger’ (Huziwara 2016: 2, n. 1). Hence, Shan \*/t<sup>h</sup>am<sup>3</sup> mon<sup>3</sup> s<sup>h</sup>ɤ<sup>1</sup>/—that is, ‘Tamansai’, would mean something like ‘a cave where tigers enjoy their lives’. Here there may be a connection with the description of the Burmese *thaman chah* (= /θamá̃ ʤá/, see above) as a ‘fabulous monster supposed to reside in caves’.

Practically nothing is known about the ritual proceedings which would supposedly allow a Bamar initiate to transform him/herself into a tiger. At any rate, Maung Htin Aung, the first Rector of Rangoon University after independence, claimed in an article of his (Maung Htin Aung 1969) to have been abducted in his youth (in 1916) by the headman of a village in Pakokku District to be initiated into a pretended ‘cult of the weretiger’. The headman, who had a reputation of being a /θamá̃ ʤá/, carried the child into the forest at night, where they met a group of people. Together, they sacrificed pigs and fowls to the /naʔ/ spirits of the forest, the protectors of tigers,<sup>19</sup> and then got drunk with toddy, danced, and sang invocations to the ‘tiger-men’. The men then poured a few drops of their blood into the toddy pot and asked the child to drink from it, but the pot fell on the ground and broke into pieces. Due to this bad omen, Maung Htin Aung’s initiation into the weretiger ‘brotherhood’ failed; yet he, after taking shelter in a tree, could witness the ‘transformation’ of the men below who went down on all fours, crawled, growled, and roared like tigers (though in the article he candidly states he could not see the men’s bodies metamorphosed into animal form due to darkness). This theriomorphic power, as Maung Htin Aung writes, may be either inborn or acquired through sorcery, but, since the magical techniques for acquiring it are obviously secretive, it is not possible to know whether they included elements of Tantric magic (such as the recitation of special mantras, etc.) or not.

### 9.6 Discussion

If, as pointed out in the previous Sections, there is indeed a (Hindu-Buddhist) Tantric component—the use of esoteric mantras, etc.—in the magical phenomenon of tiger-transformation as traditionally conceived of by Buddhist populations in lowland Southeast Asia (Cambodia, Thailand, and Burma), where do the origins of this esoteric component lie?

Given its virtual absence in both Hindu and Buddhist literary and ritual traditions of South Asian provenance, the overall origins of this phenomenon must lie with pre-and proto-historic Austroasiatic-speaking peoples of mainland Southeast Asia, who were the carriers of an archaic ‘possession’ complex in which tiger-- spirits, tiger-ancestors, ‘tiger-shamans’, and tiger-shapeshifters (i.e., tiger-people or weretigers) played a prominent role (Brighenti 2021). But what about the Tantric (‘black magic’) element associated with accounts of the magical phenomenon of tiger-transformation in, respectively, modern Cambodia, Thailand, and Burma?

As far as Cambodia is concerned, Tantric esoteric practices of South Asian origin, designed to manipulate occult powers and activate potentialities 

*[p. 187]* ^pg200

within the practitioner’s own body, may have crept into the local magical lore at any time after the formation of the Khmer Empire. Tantric elements were embedded in Mahāyāna Buddhist and (Brahmanical) Śaiva and Śākta doctrines and ritual practices sponsored by many dynasties across the Indian subcontinent in the mediaeval period, including the Pālas of Bengal (8th to 12th centuries) and the Bhauma-Karas of Odisha (8th to 10th centuries), whose maritime contacts and exchanges with various Southeast Asian kingdoms are well-known. Although Briggs (1951: 246–247) states that such South Asia-derived Tantric traditions never struck deep roots in Cambodia, they did spread to the Khmer domains together with the aforesaid doctrines from around the mid-8th century (Harris 2005: 11–12; Sanderson 2003–04). The dominant framework of Khmer Buddhism, the Mantrayāna or Vajrayāna school (Woodward 2004: 352), came to occupy a less central place in Cambodia only after Theravāda Buddhist traditions rose to prominence in the 13th–14th centuries. Yet, Khmer ‘para’-Tantric occultism, including the /sməl/ type of sorcery (allegedly employing a magical oil consecrated with mantras to achieve theriomorphic transformation) discussed in Section 9.1, appears to have continued underground, so much so that the indigenous, non-- Theravāda Khmer form of black magic is known (and feared) throughout Thailand still today.

As for Thailand, the Siamese may (or may not) have derived their beliefs about weretigers (/sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/), guardian spirits of forests associated with tigers (/sàmǐŋ pʰraay/), and tiger-like, shapeshifting mythical animals (/sǎaŋ/) from the Mons as speculated in Section 9.2. If the Thai term /sàmǐŋ/ was borrowed from a Peguan (that is, a Mon) source, and the use of this Mon loan to refer to a weretiger spread progressively to the whole of Thailand and to Laos, one could hypothesize that oral traditions about the /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ originated from some earlier, unattested traditional Mon beliefs about weretigers, as if the Mon people were regarded by mediaeval Thais as ‘masters’ in the magical art of tiger-transformation. According to this interpretation, the term /sʉ̌a sàmǐŋ/ could have originally denoted a specifically Mon ‘Tiger Lord’ (whose legendary archetype could have been the powerful shapeshifter Pu Chao Saming Phrai) by virtue of the very choice of a Mon word for ‘lord, ruler’ to designate this supernatural tiger. Be that as it may, the learning of special magic spells (mantras) and the esoteric teachings imparted by forest hermits in many ways similar to Hindu ascetics (Thai: /rʉʉsǐi/), forming a prerequisite for one’s own metamorphosis into a tiger according to Thai oral traditions, can hardly be traced back to the Mons, who had embraced Theravāda Buddhism since the beginnings of their recorded history. Indeed, if such techniques of transformative magic are to be labelled as ‘Tantric’, then they cannot have arisen within the Theravāda Buddhist *saṅgha* (‘order’). Rather, a Mahāyānist form of Buddhism deeply imbued with Tantric doctrines is more likely to have influenced the development of such Thai esoteric practices, allegedly resulting in therianthropic transformation.

Tribal groups speaking Southwestern Tai languages, who later evolved into the Siamese, Lan Na Thai, Shan, Lao, Dai Lue, and other peoples, apparently arrived 

*[p. 188]* ^pg201

in northern mainland Southeast Asia towards the end of the first millennium as adherents of Mahāyāna Buddhism mixed with Tantric concepts and practices (Stump 2008: 155–156). This form of religion, which was superimposed on tribal Tai animism, dominated (at least among the local elites) their source region to the north—that is, the multi-ethnic kingdom of Nanzhao in Yunnan (c. 738–902), whose southern extremes the Tai tribes inhabited for some time, also as recruits in the Nanzhao armies, during their rapid westward migrations from their earlier homes in North Vietnam in the 8th–9th centuries (Chamberlain 1972; Backus 1981: 51–52; Yang 2011: 329–332; Evans 2014: 244–247). The Nanzhao kingdom is thought by some scholars to have transmitted the form of Tantric Buddhism that was current in the Pāla domains of Northeastern India, which it had formerly received through the Southern Silk Route (the overland trade route connecting Yunnan to Magadha through Upper Burma, Greater Assam, and Bengal), to the tribal ancestors of the Lao, Thai, and Shan peoples (Dommen 1995: 6; Yang 2011: 330). Since esoteric Buddhism represented the heart of the Nanzhao Buddhist tradition, which made large use of protective Sanskrit magic spells and formulas (mantras, *dhāraṇī*s), handed down by Mahāyānist-cum-Tantric monks known as *ācārya*s (Sørensen 2011), it seems possible that the late-first millennium Tai tribes that would later become the Siamese, Lan Na Thai, Shans, etc. applied ritual practices borrowed from this esoteric tradition to their own native forms of transformative magic, which perhaps already included therianthropic transformation, even before they migrated to Burma, Thailand, and Laos, and converted to Theravāda Buddhism. This putative religio-historical process would explain both the mantra element and the initiatory role assigned to seemingly Tantric learned monks and hermits within the non-Theravāda magical phenomenon of tiger-- transformation, as described in Thai and Shan folktales.

A similar religio-historical process may ultimately have occurred in Central Burma. Indeed, the proto-Burman tribes, who during the 9th century descended to the central plains of Burma from the mountain areas of Yunnan situated to the west of the Middle Mekong, and who had previously served in the Nanzhao armies like the Tai tribes mentioned earlier (Luce 1985, I: 101–103), initially adhered to Mahāyāna Buddhism, including its Tantric expressions. In this, they were no different from their Pyu predecessors in Central Burma, who had received Mahāyāna-cum-Tantric Buddhism through their overland contacts with Northeastern India (and, possibly, with Nepal as well) via the Southern Silk Route. As is well-known, it was only from the reign of Anawrahta (Aniruddha) of Pagan in the 11th century that Theravāda Buddhism became the dominant religion of Burma (Stump 2008: 153–155). Prior to Anawrahta’s reign the Ari monks/priests (written Burmese: *arañ*, from Pali *ariya*, ‘noble’), most likely representing a ‘heretical’ and ‘corrupt’—obviously, from a Theravāda standpoint—Mahāyānist-cum-Tantric Buddhist sect that also fostered proto-Burman animistic beliefs and bloody sacrificial rites, had been prominent in Central Burma (Duroiselle 1918). Although it is not possible to expound here on the controversial origin and nature of the Ari monks/priests of Burma, the fact that their ‘deviant’ ritual practices included a system of sorcery whose aim was ‘the attainment, 

*[p. 189]* ^pg202

by spiritual means, of desired objects, whether material or otherwise’ and which ‘by means of mantras, charms and alchemy sought to realize dreams of invulnerability, invisibility, etc.’ (Duroiselle 1918: 93) makes them good candidates for the introduction of Tantric elements (if this is indeed the case) into the ill-known /θamá̃ ʤá/ (‘weretiger’) Burmese tradition of transformative magic discussed in Section 9.4.
