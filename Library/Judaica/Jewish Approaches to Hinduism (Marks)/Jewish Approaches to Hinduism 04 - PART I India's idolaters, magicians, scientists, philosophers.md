His source seems to be Muslim genealogies elaborating on [[Torah 02 - Noach#^10-1|Genesis 10]]. In the encyclopedic *Tarikh al-rusul wa’l-mulak* (History of the Prophets and Kings), Ibn Jarir al-Tabari (d. 923) writes that Cush married a woman named Qarnabil, who “bore him the Abyssinians, Sindis, and Indians.”<sup>12</sup> Tabari later summarizes his genealogy of Ham thus: “Among the descendants of Ham ibn Noah are the Nubians, the Abyssinians, the Fezzanites, the Indians, the Sindis, and the peoples of the coast.”<sup>13</sup> Later, a century after Ibn Ezra wrote his commentaries, Gregory Abu al-Faraj (known as Bar Hebraeus, d. 1286), would narrate a history of ethnic origins which seems to parallel Ibn Ezra’s concept. Bar Hebraeus was a bishop of Aleppo and an industrious compiler of earlier writings, including Arabic sources. His *Chronicle* of world history names Indians as one of the many nations born from Ham and states that the descendants of Ham had inherited

the whole of the south, from east to west, India central, outer, and southern, Cush, Shebha, Egypt, Lybia, Thebais, Africa, and towards the north Cilicia, Pamphylia, Pisidia, Mysia, Phrygia, Lukya \[Lycia\], Lydia, and of the islands \[of the Mediterranean\] Cyprus, Chios, Sicily, and twenty others.<sup>14</sup>

If Ibn Ezra had in mind historical traditions such as those recorded by Tabari and Bar Hebraeus, he could easily think that the descendants of Ham once comprised more than half the world. According to his comment on [[Daniel 02#^2-42|Daniel 2:42]], the descendants of Ham included the Persians and Babylonians too.

In regard to Egypt, Ibn Ezra’s reasoning apparently went like this: we know that Indians do not “eat or drink anything that is derived from sentient life.” Indians are descendants of Ham. The descendants of Ham once shared a set of common customs and beliefs: “know that Egypt and India are of the descendants of Ham, and these rely on those.” So if Indians today are vegans, all descendants of Ham were, which therefore includes the ancient Egyptians. “The people of India do not eat meat, and so would the Egyptians behave.” And not only do Indians refuse to eat “anything derived from a living creature,” but “they abhor \[*mit\`avim*, from the same root as *to\`evah*\] anyone who eats them.” Indeed, continues Ibn Ezra, Indians so much abhor meat-eating that they prohibit meat-eaters from living with them and when they travel, they avoid anyone and anything that has touched meat. No wonder, then, that the ancient Egyptians abhorred shepherds, for they ate the meat and drank the milk of sheep, and even slaughtered them as sacrifice to their god.

Ibn Ezra’s three comments comparing Egyptian and Indian customs thus assume the existence of an extensive ancient civilization comprising 

*[p. 53]* ^pg66

the descendants of Ham which covered the lands of the Middle East, North Africa, and Asia. This civilization once shared many customs, and although much of it had now changed, in India it retained many of its ancient features. Contemporary Indian customs, having survived unaltered from early times, could thereby help modern people understand ancient customs exhibited by other Hammian peoples of the past.

Now, no Muslim reports about the Indian diet claimed that all Indians abstained from meat and all animal products. The closest claim comes from Abu al-Hasan al-Mas’udi, who spoke only of the Brahmans: “They are in high respect with the Hindus, and form their highest caste and their nobility. They abstain from all animal food.”<sup>15</sup> Idrisi, as I said before, described the diet of the Indians of Nahrwara as rice, peas, lentils, fish, and only animals that had died naturally because these people never killed animals.<sup>16</sup> Thus, Ibn Ezra’s picture of the Indian meatless diet bears a flawed if definite resemblance to ethnological reports in contemporary Muslim writings. However, I failed to find in any of those writings a reflection of Ibn Ezra’s claim that Indians practiced the three biblical gestures for swearing obedience to kings and masters.

Indian beliefs

Three of Ibn Ezra’s comments on the Book of Exodus contain his most explicit statements about Indian beliefs. After reading the *Kuzari*, we will be surprised to discover that Ibn Ezra’s Indians voice philosophical doctrines about a single abstract God.

*[[Torah 16 - Beshalach#^16-1|Exod. 16:1]]* All the nations rely on Israel for this number \[the seven days in the week\] …. But see, with the people of India, who do not believe in the act of creation (*ma\`aseh b’reishit*), the beginning of the days of the week for them is the fourth day \[Wednesday\], since the planet of that day is the Sun Star \[Mercury\] and, according to their words, it rules over them.

*[[Torah 17 - Yitro#^19-9|Exod. 19:9]]* The sages of India give as evidence for their belief \[against prophecy\] that it is impossible for the Name \[God\] to speak to a human being and he would live. And Israel was in Egypt, and among them were people holding this \[same\] belief, so that the prophecy of Moses was in doubt among them.

*[[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-1|Exod. 20:1]] (Short Commentary)* And this is the way of the sages of India who say that the Name does not know the parts \[the particulars\], because if He did know, the one who swears a lie in His name – because the Name hears it, he would fear Him.

*[[Daniel 02#^2-39|Daniel 2:39]]* This is the Kingdom of Ishmael, which spread over the world, so that all Persia and Media turned to their teaching, and Sheba and Seba and Egypt and Africa, and many from India (*hodu*) and most of the kingdom of Cush.

*[p. 54]* ^pg67

Beginning with the last passage, we see that Ibn Ezra knew well the history of Muslim conquests, and was aware that “many” Indians were Muslims. When he speaks of “the sages of India” in general, however, he means non-Muslim Indians.

In two of these passages, Ibn Ezra paraphrases the views of Indian sages: “The sages of India give as evidence of their belief that …” and “the sages of India who say….” They state what they believe about God and why. Ibn Ezra places into their mouths the general term he usually uses for God: *ha-Shem*, “the Name.”

Yet while Indians believe in general in the same God as Ibn Ezra, they reject three doctrines important to traditional Judaism: the doctrine of a unique creation in the past, the concept of God’s knowledge of particular occurrences in the sub-lunar world, and the possibility that human beings can gain knowledge of God through revelation.

Elsewhere in Ibn Ezra’s Short Commentary to [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-1|Exod. 20:1]], we learn that the first Indian doctrine is identical to that of the “sages of Greece,” who also believe that “the Name creates continuously” rather than having created once in the past. In his Long Commentary on the same verse, he extends this doctrine to all “the nations of the world.” Their scholars all believe that God alone created the world but “maintain that the Name continuously creates without beginning or end.”

The second Indian doctrine implies a concept of God as a distant Aristotelian Prime Mover far removed from the goings-on of the world of human beings. In this conception, God knows the world only in its universal unchanging essences or forms, but not all the changing particular instances of those forms. This idea bears upon the important doctrines of providence and retribution since without knowledge of particular events, God cannot guide nations or punish the wicked. Ibn Ezra has the Indians argue, “If He did know the one who swears a lie in His name – because the Name hears it, he would fear Him.” That is, if God really knew particular events, people would fear divine retribution for swearing falsely in God’s name, but since we observe that people continue to swear falsely without punishment, God must not know particular people and events.

Jewish and Muslim thinkers of Ibn Ezra’s time associated these two doctrines with Greek philosophy and its contemporary representatives. Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 1111), the influential Persian theologian, considered any Muslim thinkers who held these doctrines to be infidels. In *al-Mun* *qidh min al-Dalal* (Deliverance from Error), Ghazali identified “the views of Aristotle” as the source of these errors, and Abu Nasr Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina as expounders of Aristotle. Referring to his extended argument in *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (The Incoherence of Philosophers), Ghazali specifies the three worst errors of philosophers as denying bodily resurrection and thinking “that God knows universals and not particulars” and that “the world is everlasting, without beginning.”<sup>17</sup> Ha-Levi, who seems to have been familiar with some of Ghazali’s thought, places two of these 

*[p. 55]* ^pg68

doctrines into the speech of the philosopher who speaks at the beginning of the *Kuzari*:

Likewise, according to the philosophers, He \[God\] is beyond the knowledge of particulars because they change with the times, whereas there is no change in God’s knowledge. Therefore, He is not aware of you …. He is the cause of \[all the\] causes \[involved\] in the creation of every created thing, not because this \[creation\] was intended on His part. Indeed, He did not create man at all because the world is eternal, and man has never ceased coming into being out of the man before him.<sup>18</sup>

This philosopher holds the same two doctrines, eternal creation and divine ignorance of particulars, which Ibn Ezra associates with Greek sages, “nations of the world,” and Indian sages. Later in the *Kuzari*, philosophers are said not to fear God because “they do not believe that if they rob or murder, they would be punished” (4:19); and their view of creation is summarized as “belief in the eternity of the universe” (5:14). Ibn Ezra’s Indians thus look a lot like Ha-Levi’s philosophers.

These doctrines continued to pose important challenges for Jewish and Muslim thinkers during and after the time of Ibn Ezra. Kevin Lim, in his article “God’s Knowledge of Particulars: Avicenna, Maimonides, and Gersonides,” sums up the position of the three philosophers of his title in this way: Ibn Sina (d. 1037) argued that “God knows particulars *via* universals,” Maimonides (d. 1204) that “God knows particulars *qua* particulars,” and Gersonides (d. 1344), a Jewish philosopher who devoted many pages to the subject, that “God knows particulars *via* their intelligible ordering.”<sup>19</sup> Maimonides also allotted a large portion of the *Guide*, eleven chapters, to the question of whether the world is eternal (which he identifies with Aristotle) or was created in time.<sup>20</sup> As for Ibn Ezra himself, he believed that the world had been created once in time, but apparently through emanation, and he held a subtle theory about God’s knowledge of the world. He expressed it enigmatically as “The All \[God\] knows every particular in a general way and not in a particular way” and “the Name knows the particulars of everything in a general way because the particulars change.”<sup>21</sup> So Ibn Ezra would not have considered the Indian view of divine knowledge to be especially implausible, since he himself thought that God’s “knowledge of the particular is only by way of the general” (comment to [[Psalms 001#^1-6|Ps. 1:6]]).

What we have learned is that Ibn Ezra identified Indian thought, characterized by an eternal cosmos and impassive God, with the kind of modified Greek thought that challenged Jewish thinkers of his day. In saying that “the nations of the world” believe in the eternity of the world, he seems to picture Greek (particularly Aristotelian) thought as a worldwide phenomenon.

The third doctrine that Ibn Ezra associates with Indians, the denial of prophecy, sounds like the main doctrine associated with the Barahima 

*[p. 56]* ^pg69

in Muslim writings. But the reason which Ibn Ezra’s Indians give for their belief – that “it is impossible for the Name to speak to a human being and he would live” – is not one of the justifications attributed to the Barahima. Rather, it repeats the fears expressed by Israelites in the Pentateuch, who exclaim, “Let not God speak to us lest we die” ([[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-16|Exod. 20:16]]). The exact words spoken by Indian sages derive from [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^5-23|Deut. 5:23]], “What mortal has ever heard the voice of the living God speak as we did, and lived?” But Ibn Ezra omits “as we did” to convey the Indian rejection of any possibility of survival. Biblical writers had considered auditory contact with God lethal. So was direct visual contact. Ibn Ezra merely pastes these biblical words with their biblical assumptions onto Indian sages.

If Ibn Ezra’s Indians do not sound like Barahima, might he have associated the Indian denial of prophecy instead with the Greek philosophical ideas held by “the nations of the world”? After all, an Unmoved Mover who does not know particular persons and events would also not be expected to intervene in history to convey revelations; and why would philosophers need revelations in addition to their rational wisdom? Indeed, medieval Jewish and Muslim thinkers recognized the tensions between prophetic knowledge and scientific-philosophical knowledge based on a Hellenic framework. So Indian thinkers, as adherents of two other Hellenic doctrines, might certainly be expected to reject prophecy too on the basis of such philosophy. But again Ibn Ezra’s wording of their argument, his use of biblical phrases and assumptions, contradicts this interpretation. Human contact with the impassive God of Aristotle would not cause death.

We must leave this problem unsolved and notice only that Ibn Ezra attributes three doctrines to Indians that reflect Hellenic systems of thought which he believed to be common throughout the world, including Egypt. We should also notice the calm tone of Ibn Ezra’s descriptions of Indian doctrines, a sharp contrast from Ha-Levi’s harsh condemnation, and how different Ibn Ezra’s Indians sound in their rejection of revelation compared with the spiteful Indians of the *Kuzari* who “scoff at those who say they possess a book from God.”

Have we, then, learned all we can about Ibn Ezra’s view of Hindu thought? We have discovered intellectuals who believe in a world of continuous creation and a single God distant from human affairs. These Indians seek knowledge through human reason rather than revelation, and they live in a society of people exhibiting innate justice and honesty. What is especially odd about Ibn Ezra’s view, however, is that it does not reflect Muslim ethnological reports about India; his Indians are not divided into sects and do not worship statues in temples. So from what sources did he draw his ideas about Indian thought? I propose that we shall discover his sources and more fully understand his image of Indian intellectuals when we consult his scientific writings.

God, cosmos, and astrology: Ibn Ezra’s universe

Returning to the first of the Indian doctrines, we notice one additional aspect of Indian thought, one that was central to Ibn Ezra’s understanding of Indians: they believe in astrology and its complex theories of astral influences upon human life. He tells his readers that Indians have their own calendar and that its week begins on Wednesday “since the planet of that day is Mercury and, according to their words, it rules over them.”<sup>22</sup> They hold to the theory that each planet has a particular influence over certain nations and days of the week.

Ibn Ezra himself agreed that Indians lived under the primary astral governance of Mercury. Astrology played a central role in his own thought, and he employed it to interpret events and behavior in the Bible as well as the world of his own day. In order to understand what astrology meant to Ibn Ezra and its relevance to Indian thought, however, we must step back and consider his view of the universe. His comments on [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-15|Exodus 3:15]] and [[Daniel 10#^10-21|Daniel 10:21]] provide useful summaries of that view.

Ibn Ezra’s universe had three levels, which he called “three worlds.” According to his comment on the Book of Daniel, the highest world is that of the One, the glorious Name, beyond time, change, division, place, and body. Elsewhere he writes, “The Name is the One and he creates all and he is all, and I am unable to explain” (commentary to [[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-26|Gen. 1:26]]). In referring to God, Ibn Ezra (like Ha-Levi) usually uses the term, *ha-Shem*, meaning “the Name,” to indicate, without having to write the letters of, the holy and ineffable four-lettered name of God (the Tetragrammaton). But he also distinguishes between “the Glorious Name” (*ha-Shem ha-nikhbad*), as the transcendent Source of the universe (which he sometimes calls merely “the Name,” *ha-Shem*), and ‘*Elohim*, divine actions within the universe, which applies to angels and other agents of God and indirectly to the stars as “the children of *‘Elohim*” embedded in the highest sphere circling the earth.<sup>23</sup> Ibn Ezra calls the planets *ha-meshartim* – the ministers or attendants of God. However, *ha-Shem*, “the Name,” usually refers, in Ibn Ezra’s usage, to God in a generic sense that does not differentiate between the Glorious Name and ‘*Elohim*. This is the sense intended by his statements on Indian ideas about God.

The middle world in Ibn Ezra’s universe contains the angels, and below them the sphere of the constellations, and below it the complex moving spheres of the five planets and sun and moon. Each level exhibits different degrees of change (and change is always inferior to stasis). The “holy angels” are incorporeal and unchanging themselves but impart motion to the stars and planets below them. The stars “are fixed in one sphere” which itself revolves in a simple pattern around the earth. The “five ministering stars” (the planets known at Ibn Ezra’s time) are eternal but their movements change in complex ways.

The lowest world, our own, contains three types of corporeal inhabitants in rising order of importance: metals, plants, and animals, all of which undergo 

*[p. 58]* ^pg71

change. Human beings stand at the top and are “the secret” of this world. They have three souls, two of which die with the body, but the highest soul, called *neshamah*, the rational soul of human beings, is of the same essence as the angels of the middle world and has the potential to achieve eternal life.

Astrology enters this picture when Ibn Ezra writes, “actions of the lower world are initiated by movements \[of the higher world\] and the planets, sometimes for good and sometimes for bad” (comment on [[Daniel 10#^10-21|Daniel 10:21]]). Because of the many movements of the planets, “all creatures in this lower world change in their essence and their accidents” (comment on [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-15|Exod. 3:15]]). In a paragraph about [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^5-26|Deut. 5:26]], he connects these astral forces with God: “The source of all actions and movements is decrees of the Name. The power and nature of all that is found beneath the heavens is determined by the arrangements of the higher ones.” Likewise, “the middle world is maintained by the power of the Name and by his holy angels … Ours, the lowest of the worlds, is maintained by the power of the Name plus the power of the two higher worlds” (comment on [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-1|Exod. 20:1]]). Thus, God governs this lowest world through the mediation of the middle world, in which angels convey motion to the stars and planets, which in turn, through laws of cause and effect, determine the events of the lowest world.

Astrology, as the study of how the stars and planets act upon this world, provides indirect knowledge of God and indeed, all the sciences do the same. In his comment on [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-1|Exod. 20:1]], Ibn Ezra identified science as the surest path toward knowledge of God. Through its study, he writes, one “will see the Name’s work in the metals, in the plants and animals, and in the human body itself.” Then, if one studies “the science of the spheres that are the Name’s work in the middle world,” one will learn, for example, the exact causes of eclipses. Through such study one gains solid evidence about “the ways of the Name,” and then from this, “the intelligent person (*ha-maskil*) will know the Name.”

Ibn Ezra used the phrase, *h. okhmat ha-mazalot*, literally “science of the zodiac signs,” as a general term that included mathematics, calendar calculations, astronomy, and astrology. However, he thought of astrology, sometimes called “science of the judgments of the zodiacal signs,” as a soft science, because it was based on incomplete proofs, that is, “proofs that are the products of analogies, and some proofs derived from experimental evidence.”<sup>24</sup> To Ibn Ezra, however, all four fields of *h. okhmat ha-mazalot* were sciences that studied recurrent patterns in the visible world. (I use the terms “astronomy” and “astrology” in their contemporary meanings as two different areas of study.)

Shlomo Sela has studied Ibn Ezra’s scientific writings with great thoroughness, erudition, and insight, and opened these difficult texts to a wider readership. I follow many of his findings and interpretations here, acknowledging gratefully my reliance on his careful work. Sela divides Ibn Ezra’s scientific books, nineteen in all, into two groups: (1) “works designed to develop techno-theoretical skills related mainly to astronomy 

*[p. 59]* ^pg72

and mathematics,” including use of the astrolabe and astronomical tables, as well as methods for determining the Jewish calendar, and (2) astrological treatises comprised of (a) *Reshit H. okhmah* (Beginning of Wisdom, composed in 1148), a general reference book on the fundamentals of astrology, and (b) “a series of astrological works designed to deal with the various branches of astrology” that together constituted chapters of a unified encyclopedia of astrology.<sup>25</sup> In this latter group we find, for example, *Sefer Ha-Moladot* (Book of Nativities, 1146), concerned with determining the destiny of a new-born child on the basis of the configuration of the stars at the moment of the child’s birth (called geneathlialogical astrology); *Sefer ha-Mivh. arim* (Book of Choices, 1148), on how to choose the most auspicious moment for beginning a specific action; and *Sefer ha-\`Olam* (Book of the World, 1148), on forecasting the fate of nations and cities.<sup>26</sup>

For Ibn Ezra the purpose of astrology was both scientific and practical, and had no relationship to magic. Learning what the planets and constellations have decreed for one’s life allows one to take precautions.

Consider the case of a scholar in the science of the zodiacal signs who observes in \[the horoscope of\] his anniversary nativity that he is bound to come down with a fever at a certain time when Mars enters in the degree of the ascendant. If he takes precautions before the illness comes, abstaining from any hot food and drinking beverages to cool his body, then he will maintain balance in the temperament of his body when Mars enters in the degree of the ascendant.<sup>27</sup>

In this case, you can apply medical remedies on the exact day of a harmful astral influence; in other cases, you could avoid embarking on a sea voyage when your horoscope predicts calamity during travel, or you could adjust the time of sowing when a late drought is predicted for your kingdom.

Ibn Ezra presents a second way of avoiding astral harm, a way he considers more effective and which he emphasizes in his biblical commentaries, and that is by turning to God. The passage just quoted about using a medical remedy to avoid harm from the stars, adds that trusting in God is more effective than medicine because God will “effect causes … that save him from any harm prognosticated in his nativity \[horoscope\].” Ibn Ezra usually uses the phrase “cleaving to the Name” to describe this approach, and in one essay, says that if “a wise soul … forsakes the pleasures of the lowest world and cleaves to the Glorious Name,” then God “will cause a chain of causes to save him” from a destined evil. For example, “if it is in the arrangement of the stars that this man be sterile, the Name will establish for him the power of reproduction and he will sire children.”<sup>28</sup> Ibn Ezra tells a story to illustrate how an entire city can escape a malefic astrological fate:

The configuration of the stars was such that a river would overflow onto a city and flood it or kill people. A prophet came to warn them to return 

*[p. 60]* ^pg73

to the Name before the day of their calamity. So they repented with all their heart. Because they cleaved to Him, He gave them the resolve to leave the city and pray to the Name. They did this on the day the river suddenly overflowed and flooded the city. (Comment on [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^33-21|Exodus 33:21]]) 

Ibn Ezra points out that God’s decree, transmitted through the stars, did not change: the river flooded in a natural way. Yet God saved them.

Hence, studying astrology can decrease human suffering when this knowledge leads to a proper response – either human measures of avoidance or turning to God with all one’s soul. Ibn Ezra says the second approach is more effective because astrologers sometimes make mistakes and human efforts may fail to escape harmful events.<sup>29</sup>

The image of Indians in Ibn Ezra’s scientific writings

Ibn Ezra identified his sources repeatedly in his scientific writings. He referred most often to Claudius Ptolemy, whose ideas he appropriated in modified form through Arabic translations and from abridgments such as the *Centiloquium*. He also drew from translations of the writings of Dorotheus of Sidon, a 1st-century Hellenistic astrologer, and attributed a number of ideas to Enoch (Hermes) and to unnamed Egyptian and Persian scholars. His most important Arabic source was Abu Ma\`shar al-Balkhi (d. 886, Khurasan and Iraq), whom he frequently cited, and the names of numerous other Muslim scholars appear in his books.<sup>30</sup> And he often cites the ideas of “Indian scientists,” whom he considered important scientific authorities.

Here are a few examples of the role of Indian science in Ibn Ezra’s books. *Sefer Ha-Mispar* (Book of the Number, 1146), on arithmetic, explains the decimal positional system (using zero as a placeholder), which, Ibn Ezra informs us, was an invention of Indian scientists.

*Sefer Ha\`Ibbur* (1146), dealing with calculations for the Jewish calendar, makes use of Indian, Greek, and Arab astronomy, and acknowledges the Indian source of its system of decimal placement.<sup>31</sup> It mentions “Indian scientists” five times.

*Reshit H. okhmah*, Ibn Ezra’s central astrological textbook, includes this introductory statement about its sources: “I shall tell you in this book everything agreed upon by the Ancients (*ha-qadmonim*), from the Babylonians to the scientists of Persia and India and Greece, at whose head is Ptolemy.”<sup>32</sup> Only in the second chapter does Ibn Ezra list disagreements among these sources, and only in regard to the figures or forms (*tzurot*) of the stars that appear in the three “faces” (also known as decans) of the twelve zodiacal constellations. For example, he explains that within the constellation of Aries, there ascends in the first face the forms of a woman, a fish tail, a triangle, and an ox, whereas the people of India say the form is that of a dog-shaped face with hands holding a torch and a key; but Kanka the Indian says it is a giant Cushite wrapped in a white cloak.<sup>33</sup>

*[p. 61]* ^pg74

The Latin version of Ibn Ezra’s lost *Sefer Ta\`amei Ha-Luh. ot* (Book of Reasons Behind Astronomical Tables, 1146) recounts a debate among astronomers attempting to determine the length of the solar year. Ibn Ezra cites the Indians first.

The scholars of India (*sapiente vero Indie*) \[compiled their astronomical tables\] according to the days of the world, which are called by them “the days of Sindhind.” They all agree that the \[length of the\] solar year, besides the quarter of a day, contains the 120th part of a day, which is the fifth part of one hour, so that the annual cycle of the sun is augmented by 3 degrees. The astronomical tables of al-Khwarizmi, Ibn al-Saffar, and Maslama were compiled in accord with the scholars of India.<sup>34</sup>

Ibn Ezra’s scientific works thus present Indians consistently as experts in astronomical and astrological knowledge who provided theories and methods useful to Western scholars. He includes Indian views in reporting debates among the foremost astrological authorities.

We learn more about Ibn Ezra’s image of Indian scientists from a narrative he wrote about how Indian astronomy came to the Islamic world. This story appears in an historical introduction to his translation (in 1160) of *Ibn Al-Muthanna’s Commentary on the Astronomical Tables of Al-Khhwarizmi*. The story begins when a great Arab king named Al-Saffah. (d. 754), the first Abbasid caliph, heard about a “distinguished” Indian book named *Kalilah and Dimnah* “concerning counsels for government in the manner of fables from the mouths of dumb animals.” He ordered a Jew to translate it into Arabic, and “when he saw how wonderful the book was, for so it truly is, he yearned for more knowledge of the sciences of India,” and sent this Jew to India, where he met a scientist named Kanka (or Kanaka), whom he persuaded to come to Baghdad. There Kanka taught the Indian numbering system to Arab scholars and helped two Jews translate an unnamed Indian book of astronomy containing the following topics:

The tables of the seven planets, all the procedures for the earth, the rising times of zodiacal signs, the declination and ascending degree of the ecliptic, the arrangement of the astrological houses, knowledge of the upper stars, and eclipses of the luminaries.

(Bernard Goldstein, historian of science, citing Biruni, thinks Ibn Ezra was referring to the *Brahma-siddhanta* written by Brahmagupta in the 7th century, which came to be known in Arabic as *al-Sindhind* and *al-Zij al-Sindhind*.<sup>35</sup>) Then came a Muslim scientist named Muhammad al-Khwarizmi (d. 850), who added Persian time-reckoning to the book and simplified its set of tables, and taught Indian mathematics. “All Arabic scholars of those days did their multiplications, divisions, and extraction 

*[p. 62]* ^pg75

of roots as is written in the book that the \[Indian\] scientist translated for them.” Then a Muslim scholar named “al-Fargani” (Muhammad al-Farazi) added explanations to al-Khwarizmi’s book, and next came an eminent astronomer named Muhammad ibn Muthanna, who wrote a Ptolemy-inspired commentary on al-Fargani’s explanations, and now Ibn Ezra is translating these interpretations of Indian astronomy into Hebrew.<sup>36</sup>

This history of transmission presents Indian political counsel, mathematics, and astronomy as valuable knowledge that an Arab king treasured and Arab scientists studied and adopted. *Kalilah and Dimnah* is called “distinguished” and “wonderful … for so it truly is.” Ibn Ezra’s summary of the content of the *Sindhind* showed his readers that Indian astronomers understood the most important movements of the celestial bodies and how to calculate their positions, and this is supported by al-Muthanna’s words of praise later in the book: “The scientists of India (*h. akhmei al-hindi*) know more in matters of the spheres (*divrei ha-galgal*) than all the scientists of other lands.”<sup>37</sup>

Of special interest is the summary in *Ibn al-Muthana’s Commentary* of the cosmological system underlying the Indian calendar, called “the days of the Sindhind,” which Ibn Ezra translates, paraphrasing Indian scholars and using his special terminology for God:

The glorious Name created the seven planets, their apogees, and nodes, in the first part of Aries and commanded them to go round, giving each of them a fixed motion until they come together again where the Name created them. When they return, the Name will do what he wishes with them. And they \[Indians\] said that the time from the beginning of their motion until their return to the point where they began is 1,577,916,450,000 days.<sup>38</sup>

God, in the Indian view, initiates this immense cycle of motions, intervening only at the times of return.

Sa\`id al-Andalusi, who wrote in Toledo during the 11th century, also reports this Indian concept of cyclical return but adds the elements of universal destruction and re-creation at the time of return. He states that Indian Sabians “believe in the eternity of the world” and

… in the times of return, in the revolutions of the planets and their orbits, in the destruction of all the derivatives of the elements every time the seven planets meet in the head of the Lamb \[Aries\], and in the recreation of the compounds during every cycle.<sup>39</sup>

Al-Andalusi points to three Indian astronomical systems called “the Sindhind, which means cyclical time, the Arjbahd, and the Arkand.” The Sindhind, he continues, was “adopted and further developed” by a group of Muslim scientists that included Muhammad al-Farazi, al-Baghdadi, and Muhammad al-Khwarizmi.<sup>40</sup> Ibn Ezra’s version of this chain of 

*[p. 63]* ^pg76

transmission adds Jewish persona and alters some of the names, but both authors envision Indian thought influencing an important line of Muslim astronomers and astrologers.

Surprisingly, the Muslim ethnological and geographical writings of the time hardly mention Indian astronomy. An exception is Mas\`udi’s *Meadows of Gold*, which speaks of “the book Al-Sind-hind” and the invention of “the numeric system of the Indians,” while also noting that some Indians “believe that the world is regenerated every thousand *hazarwan*” (apparently a *mahayuga*).<sup>41</sup>

The most likely source, then, of Ibn Ezra’s view of Indian thought was the Arabic-language books on astronomy and astrology that he consulted, which presented Indians as intellectuals who possessed expert knowledge about complex scientific theories and methods of calculation. These Indians were not divided into arguing factions and did not worship divine images, but their view of the universe, of stars and planets following predictable laws of cause-and-effect, reflected the cosmos of Aristotle and even more, that of Ptolemy, both of whom conceived of a distant passive God who sets the world in motion but does not intervene in it with retribution or revelation. This is the world of India’s three philosophical doctrines which Ibn Ezra presented in his biblical commentary, and thus the two parts of Indian thought found in his writings, from his biblical commentary and from his scientific writings, combining doctrines of a passive distant God with belief in a realm of complex astrological forces that control the events of this lower world, fit together into a logical whole.

The question of astral magic

Three questions might disturb this view of Indian thinkers as pure monotheist scientists. First, a few vague associations of Indians with astral magic appear in Ibn Ezra’s writings. Did he think Indians practiced astral magic? Second, Ibn Ezra, like Ha-Levi, held ideas about ancient pagan “religions.” Where did Indians fit among those religions? Third, astrologers since the time of Ptolemy, including the Muslim astrologer who was Ibn Ezra’s main source, believed that astrological forces determined each nation’s beliefs. Do we find the same idea in Ibn Ezra’s writings, and if so, what were the implications for India’s religious beliefs?

In his first commentary to the Book of Exodus (the “short commentary,” c. 1143–1145), Ibn Ezra addresses the question of why, when Moses had not returned from Mount Sinai and the Israelites had asked Aaron to make a god (*‘elohim*) for them, Aaron chose to make it in the form of a calf rather than some other animal or object.

*[[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^32-1|Exod. 32:1]] (Short Commentary)* And the Gaon \[Sa\`adiah\] said that the reason for the image of the \[Golden\] Calf is because there are in India people who think that the image will receive higher power (*ko-ah. \`elyon*).

*[p. 64]* ^pg77

Sa\`adiah Gaon (d. 942), an influential rabbi and exegete of the 10th century, asserts (according to Ibn Ezra) that the Israelites were influenced by the Indian practice of fashioning talismans in the form of cattle. Ibn Ezra himself expresses no opinion about this claim. Then, however, about ten years later, Ibn Ezra, in his Long Commentary on the Book of Exodus (c. 1153–1156), returns to the connection between the Golden Calf and astral magic in his comment on [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-20|Exodus 20.20]]. Interpreting “You shall not make with Me any gods of silver or gold,” he says that the odd phrase “with Me” informs us that God has no need of “intermediaries to be with Me,” and that the phrase “gods of gold” warns the Israelites against making the Golden Calf. The biblical verse means (in Ibn Ezra’s words), “Do not make images to receive the power of high ones (*tzurot l’qabel ko-ah. ‘elyonim*) … like the calf that Israel made.”

So does Ibn Ezra himself think that Indians make images to receive this higher power? He often disagrees with Sa\`adiah’s interpretations of scripture, but in this case, in the Long Commentary, he agrees that the Israelites fashioned the Calf to obtain higher power, while he omits Sa\`adiah’s explanation for their choice of forms. The Indian connection with astral magic, ambiguous in the Short Commentary, disappears altogether in the Long Commentary.

“Higher power” (*ko-ah. \`elyon*) has a number of meanings in Ibn Ezra’s commentaries, where it occurs 16 times. Sometimes it means the direct emanation of God’s power,<sup>42</sup> and sometimes a power related to angels in the realm above the stars, as in this sentence: “If the soul (*neshamah*) grows wise, it will stand in the secret of the angels and will be able to receive great power (*ko-ah.* ) from a higher power (*ko-ah. \`elyon*) that received it from the light of the angels” (comment to [[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-15|Exod. 3:15]]). But most often the phrase refers to a power believed to emanate in a special way from the stars and planets when people “draw down” this power for their own benefit, as the Israelites tried to do through the image (*tzurah*) of the Golden Calf. This is astral magic, which assumes the existence of such powers and uses talismans to obtain it. Maimonides called the process “drawing down spiritualities (*ruh. aniyyat*)” from the stars.

Ibn Ezra’s commentaries contain several references to astral magic. He suggests that the objects that Rachel stole from her father, called *terafim* ([[Torah 07 - Vayetzei#^31-19|Gen. 31:19]]), had the form of human beings (*tzurot b’nei adam*) and were designed “to receive power of the higher ones” (*leqabel ko-ah. \`elyonim*); the “higher ones” mean the planets and stars. Interpreting the “molten gods” (*’elohei masekhah*) prohibited by Leviticus 19:4, Ibn Ezra defines them as shapes intended to “receive power from higher ones.” In his comment to Exod. 20:4, explaining “You shall not bow down to them \[other gods\],” he says this law is directed against people called “masters of the images” (*ba\`alei ha-tzurot*), who “think they can draw down the power of the higher ones for someone’s need” by means of the images they construct.<sup>43</sup> These examples apply to Israelites; his comment on [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^4-16|Deut. 4:16]] presents an example of foreign astral magic.

*[p. 65]* ^pg78

There are also nations who bow to and venerate any animal they meet at the start of day, to draw down the power of higher ones, such as \[venerating\] the image of a scorpion \[to heal\] someone who was bitten (Comment to [[Torah 45 - Vaetchanan#^4-16|Deut. 4:16]]).

These “masters of images” (more frequently “scholars of images,” *h. akhmei ha-tzurot*) appear also in several of Ibn Ezra’s scientific books. Sela analyzed their role.<sup>44</sup> They are, first of all, a category of scholars who study, in Sela’s words, “clearly astronomical matters, notably the theory of trepidation (the accession and recession of the fixed stars),”<sup>45</sup> as well as shapes that appear within constellations.<sup>46</sup> But “masters of images” also refers to people who fashion talismans, that is, objects made of certain materials in certain shapes designed to draw down astral powers; for example, they established experimentally an association between each planet and a metal pertaining to it, such as Venus to copper, in order to draw down the planet’s power.<sup>47</sup> The use of images in this way, Ibn Ezra writes in *Sefer Ha-Te’amim*, “is forbidden by the Torah of the Name because it resembles idolatry (*avodah zarah*).”<sup>48</sup> Such magic implies bowing down to “other gods” (comment on [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-2|Exod. 20:2]]) and venerating an intermediary instead of only God (comment on [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-20|Exod. 20:20]]).

Scholars of images are related to India because three times, in two books, Ibn Ezra associates them with Indian astronomy.<sup>49</sup> Here is the clearest example:

*Sefer Ha-Te’amim II, 8.3:* You should know that Indian sages said that one fifth of an hour should be added to six full hours for every year, but this applies only to the images, and there is no need for their \[opinion with regard to the motion of the\] sun, except for those who deal with the wisdom of the images (*h. okhmat ha-tzurot*), and this is forbidden by the Torah of the Name because it resembles idolatry.<sup>50</sup>

In this and two other instances, we are told that people who want to practice astral magic must use the theories and tables of Indian astronomers. They are the only astronomers whose calculations support this kind of astral magic.

However, we find in these passages no essential identification between Indian astronomy and the practice of astral magic, and no actual statement attributing such practice to Indians. In fact, Indian astronomers and astrologers appear in Ibn Ezra’s science books a great number of times without any association at all with image-making. *Sefer Ha-Te’amim I* summarizes Indian views 13 times with no connection to astral magic, and *Sefer* *\`Olam I* cites them six times without such connection. *Reshit H. okhmah* summarizes them throughout its second chapter, and *Commentary on the Astronomical Tables of Al-Khhwarizmi* mentions Indian ideas often, but never in association with scholars of images.

*[p. 66]* ^pg79

I therefore conclude that Ibn Ezra viewed Indian astrology as a scientific system that, like Ptolemy’s, had no essential link to astral magic, even though Sa\`adiah Gaon thought Indians practiced astral magic and even though Ibn Ezra knew that astral magicians relied on certain Indian ideas and calculations.

The astrological study of “religions”

Many of the astrological writings from which Ibn Ezra drew conceived of religion as, at least in part, a product of astrological forces. Ptolemy, Ibn Ezra’s main source of astrological theory, analyzed national character through several methods. One of them employed the concept of quarters (cardines), a division of the inhabited world into four parts, in each of which a combination of three constellations and two planets shaped “conditions of character and customs” of the inhabitants. Ptolemy wrote that the areas occupying the quarter that includes India

are familiar to \[correspond with\] the south-eastern triangle \[of constellations\], Taurus, Virgo, and Capricorn, and are governed by Venus and Saturn in oriental aspects. Therefore one would find that the natures of their inhabitants conform with the temperaments governed by such \[planetary\] rulers; for they revere the star of Venus under the name of Isis, and that of Saturn as Mithras Helios. Most of them, too, divine future events …. Further, they are ardent, concupiscent, and inclined to the pleasures of love.

Being governed by Venus and Saturn, the inhabitants naturally worship these two planets and are erotically inclined. About other nations, Ptolemy asserted, for example, that the celestial combinations ruling Judeans made them godless and bold, and Chaldeans worship the sun and are addicted to astrology.<sup>51</sup>

In this understanding of what I am calling “religions,” the veneration of gods derives from the natural laws of celestial influence. People in each region tend, for practical reasons, to venerate the planetary powers that control their region, which is why Indians revere Venus and Saturn. No religion is superior to another, and religion is not concerned with one higher truth.

Muslim astrologers expanded many of Ptolemy’s ideas. In the 9th century, for example, the great Muslim astrologer Abu Ma\`shar, Ibn Ezra’s main Arabic source, wrote a famous work on historical astrology titled, in Keiji Yamamoto and Charles Burnett’s translation, *The Book of Religions and Dynasties*. This book predicts the future of nations in much more detail than had Ptolemy, based on more complex configurations of celestial objects. Abu Ma\`shar addresses such issues as future rain, prices, wars and riots, epidemics, and earthquakes, as well as the rise and quality of beliefs. 

*[p. 67]* ^pg80

In his chapter on “sunnas and sharias,” he examines the position of Jupiter in relationship to other planets in a nation’s horoscope, “since Jupiter is by nature an indicator of faith, and the differences of faiths in the periods, religions, and dynasties are from its mixture with Saturn and the mixtures of the other planets with it.” For example, if Jupiter is mixed with Saturn, the faith will be Judaism even if the nation confesses some other belief.

If the mixer with Jupiter is Mars, it indicates the worship of fire and the faith of Mazdaism. If the mixer with it is the sun, it indicates the worship of the planets, idols, and the marvelous. If the mixer with Jupiter is Venus, it indicates revealed religion and monotheism, like Islam and the like. If the mixer with it is Mercury, it indicates Christianity and every faith containing antipathy, doubt, and trouble.

Abu Ma\`shar also explains ways to predict the quality of a nation’s faith, that is, whether it will be sincere or false, and how to predict the corruption of a nation’s religion into idol-worship or veneration of fire or the moon.<sup>52</sup> In Abu Ma\`shar’s scheme, we notice how Ptolemy’s set of Hellenistic religions has changed to reflect a later view of the world’s beliefs and heresies, and Abu Ma\`shar has added predictions about the quality of a nation’s faith. As with Ptolemy, however, we see the planets determining national beliefs.

One more Muslim example comes from a 10th-century astrologer from Aleppo named Abu al-Saqr al-Qabisi. The second chapter of his *Introduction to the Art of Judgments of the Stars* summarizes the ways that each planet affects a person’s or nation’s life. In regard to religion, Qabisi specifies that Jupiter in a national horoscope indicates monotheism; Saturn indicates polytheism and idolatry and the sciences of magic and spells; the Sun, pure monotheism (*al-Hanifiya*); Mars, religions of fear and doubt; Venus, religions involving sex, eating, and drinking; Mercury, revelations to prophets; and the moon, Brahman belief.<sup>53</sup> Al-Qabisi’s system of astrology focuses on religious doctrine rather than quality of belief and retains the concept of astral determination of doctrine. Indians follow Brahman beliefs because of the position of the moon in their national horoscope.

In light of these earlier applications of astrology to religious belief, we might expect to find similar ideas in Ibn Ezra’s astrological books that would illuminate his view of Indian and other religious traditions. But the contrary is true. Ibn Ezra actually says very little on the topic of astrology and religion, and this is limited mainly to the quality of religious belief rather than the object of that belief. His longest statement appears in *Sefer Moladot*, a book about casting an individual’s nativity horoscope. He locates the question of an individual’s faith within the astrological category of Houses (or Places), both the third and ninth Houses, showing how faith is governed by certain complex conditions that may occur when a planet occupies those Houses. Thus, if the sun occupies the ninth House and the 

*[p. 68]* ^pg81

person is born in the daytime, he or she will be a pious monotheist (*yerei’* *shamayim*, fearing heaven); but if born at night, he or she will only pretend to piety. Or if Jupiter occupies the third House of a person’s horoscope and the sun also “exerts lordship” over him, he will have no faith at all. The conditions can get complicated, as in this sentence:

If the lord of the ascending sign is there \[third House\] and exerts lordship over it and is not aspected by any of the malefic \[planets\], the person will worship the Name, and his dreams will be true and will not need interpretation.<sup>54</sup>

Ibn Ezra assumes in these predictions that everyone is a monotheist. The stars determine quality of faith but not what a person believes.

All in all, Ibn Ezra wrote much less about astrological forces influencing religions than had his predecessors. For further evidence of this, and to learn how Ibn Ezra thought astrological forces had influenced Indians, let us consider Chapters 2 and 4 of *Reshit H. okhmah*. These chapters specify how each zodiacal constellation and each planet affect terrestrial life in many diverse ways that include illnesses, hours of the day, physique, geographical formations, vocations, types of buildings, types of animals, numbers, colors, mental qualities, and many more ways, sometimes including the quality of one’s faith. Ibn Ezra uses the Ptolemaic theory of “climes” (*gevulim* in his Hebrew) to divide the inhabited world into seven latitudinal bands, with each region ruled by a constellation and a planet. Each nation is therefore governed by the constellation and planet of its clime, but may also be governed by its own particular planet. For Jews, the combination was Aquarius with Saturn, and for Arabs, primarily Scorpio with Venus. The clime in which India is located is ruled by Capricorn and Saturn, but Mercury has particular power over the Indian people. Ibn Ezra states that Mercury indicates the vocations of philosophers, scientists, doctors, scribes, traders, and mathematicians and the human characteristics of speech, thought, morality, wisdom, astrology (*h. okhmat ha-kokhavim*), magic (*kesef*), rapid speech, cunning, poetry, and prophecy (*nevu’ah*). All these features need not apply to all Indians; the idea is that as a nation ruled by Mercury, Indians are more likely to exhibit these qualities, but there are many other astral influences that determine actual behavior.<sup>55</sup> In *Sefer ha-\`Olam*, Ibn Ezra quotes Abu Ma\`shar’s view that Mercury governs the souls of Indians, making them clever and smart, while Capricorn governs their bodies, causing the darker color of their skin.<sup>56</sup>

*Reshit H. okhmah* says nothing of Indian belief. Predictions of faith and worship, in fact, appear only six times in the book, once when we are told that people born in the third decan (face) of Gemini will be middling in their fear of the Name (their piety), and twice again concerning people ruled by Sagittarius and Jupiter, who will include servants (or worshipers) of the Name and pious devotees (*h. asidim*), and further that Saturn rules 

*[p. 69]* ^pg82

over the human act of worshipping the Name, that places for worship of the Name belong to Jupiter, and finally, that Mars, planet of fire and destruction, governs the English and fire-worshippers. As in *Sefer ha-Moladot,* Ibn Ezra is here concerned with the quality of people’s faith and the human act of worship rather than what people believe. The few references to beliefs in *Reshit H. okhmah* portray almost all nations almost everywhere, excepting only the Mars-governed fire-worshipers, as monotheists who fear and worship God.

From this we see again the association of Indians with science, intelligence, and astrology (along with magic and prophecy). As for religion, India’s does not stand out as different from the usual worship of God followed by other people living in all other climes but one. Finally, we have learned that like everyone else, including Jews, the quality of an individual Indian’s faith, whether deep or superficial, can be predicted from her or his horoscope.

Ancient misbeliefs

In his commentaries, Ibn Ezra wrote only a few scattered sentences about ancient beliefs and practices. He briefly explains various practices condemned in the Bible, sometimes tending toward astrological interpretations of them. Soothsayers, he says, were people who looked at clouds and saw shapes figuring the future; diviners, he theorizes, may have been astrologers who declared the future with dangerous certitude; and necromancers entered cemeteries and fantasized shapes in their “imagination and madness, which is an abomination to the Name.”<sup>57</sup> He tells us that the Israelites, while living in Egypt, had sacrificed to demons because this was an Egyptian practice.<sup>58</sup> The Israelites had also venerated Egyptian gods (*gilulim*) while there.<sup>59</sup>

In addition, Ibn Ezra describes practices that remind us of the image of Sabians as worshipers of the stars and planets. He refers in a general way to people who bow down to planets and stars (comment on [[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^33-21|Exod. 33:21]]) and he states that most ancient people venerated the sun and moon (comment on [[Job 31#^31-27|Job 31:27]]). Elsewhere he says that people mistakenly venerated the highest sphere of the cosmos (*galgal ha-’elyon*), the one that propels the lower sphere of the stars, because they thought it was the world’s ultimate creator (comment to [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-1|Exod. 20:1]]). Ibn Ezra also explains the obscure word *h. amaneikhem* in [[Torah 33 - Bechukotai#^26-30|Lev. 26:30]] as temples built by the Israelites for bowing down to the sun (*h. amah*).

Another type of flawed practice was astral magic, and we just looked at examples of this in Ibn Ezra’s writings, mainly in the form of talismans designed to draw down power from the planets or stars. The Golden Calf and Laban’s *terafim* are two examples; another is the practice of unnamed “nations” of making images of animals they meet early in the morning “to draw down the power of higher ones.” Ibn Ezra characterizes astral magic as “close to idolatry” (*\`avodah zarah*), as he says in *Sefer Ha-Te’amim*, 

*[p. 70]* ^pg83

examined earlier. Such practices “are forbidden by the Torah of the Name.” He repeats this idea in his comment on the words “heavens above” in [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-4|Exod. 20:4]]: “The scholars of images make things that are not so, and their actions are close to venerating idols (*\`avodat gilulim*).” The images they make resemble the forms of the constellations (and are thus astral talismans), which is why this commandment prohibits making “any likeness of what is in the heaven above.” Astral magic is “close to idolatry” rather than full idolatry, probably because it does not venerate other gods or reject God directly. Its concept of God, however, is certainly flawed.<sup>60</sup>

However, astral magic was not for Ibn Ezra the worst theological error. Explaining why the first of the Ten Commandments (“I am the Name your God”) precedes the second one (“You shall have no other *‘Elohim* before Me”), he declares that “the sin of one who does not believe in the Name \[God\] is greater than the sin of one who commits idolatry (*\`avodat gilulim*)” by venerating statues of gods while believing in the Name. He then points to Israelite veneration of the Queen of Heaven as an example of believing in God while practicing idolatry. Ibn Ezra further defines this error as “believing in the Name while joining others with Him (*mishtatfim imo ah. er*).”<sup>61</sup> In these sentences, he clearly conceives of the possibility of worshipping God while also venerating lower powers; he condemns such worship but considers it better than entirely rejecting God’s higher existence.

Thus, in these passages about theological error, we see four levels of belief: a full and exclusive belief in the Name who governs the world through intermediary angels, stars, and planets; venerating the stars and planets while also worshipping God; the near-idolatrous use of talismans for astral magic; and denying God altogether and worshipping astral powers instead.

Indian beliefs fit none of these ancient misbeliefs. We saw that in Ibn Ezra’s view, Indians are monotheists and scientists who believe in cause-and-effect processes in the middle and lower worlds. They don’t use astral magic and they don’t venerate astral entities. But I will soon argue that Aristotelians fit an additional category of flawed belief associated with the name *‘Elohim*. Ibn Ezra thought the Bible used the name *‘Elohim* when speaking of the way non-Jews like Jethro knew about God, for *‘Elohim* was the divine name “known in the world before the coming of Moses because everyone acknowledged it” (comment to [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^18-1|Exod. 18:1]]). That is, the entire world knew God through the name that, in Ibn Ezra’s view of the cosmos, refers to God’s agents in the middle world.

Ibn Ezra’s idea of Indian culture and religion: Reprise

It is time to draw together the multiple facets of Ibn Ezra’s image of Indians and their beliefs.

Indian culture exhibits customs symbolizing obedience seen also in ancient Israel, and vegan dietary customs seen in ancient Egypt. The similarity between Indian and Egyptian customs results from a common descent from 

*[p. 71]* ^pg84

Noah’s son, Ham. Indeed, the Hammian nations comprise half the earth’s population.

The Indian people behave with innate justice and civic virtue, and their sages wrote an exemplary book of political advice called *Kalilah and Dimnah*.

Indians believe in God but differ from Judaism in holding to the eternity and ongoing creation of the world, God’s ignorance of particulars, and the impossibility of prophecy. Indians also believe in an extremely old universe in which everything returns to its point of origination after a very long time.

Indian scientists are experts in their fields of knowledge, ranking among the world’s foremost authorities in mathematics, astronomy, and astrology, a source of important theories and calculations. Like most other scientists of the world, Indians believe that heavenly bodies exert a complex web of powers shaping human life.

Indians do not practice astral magic, although the people who do, use the calculations of Indian scientists.

Ibn Ezra’s astrological principles assume that the great majority of people are monotheists fearing Heaven and worshiping God. This includes India.

Altogether, then, we find an image of a civil, ascetic (in diet), and highly intellectual society that believes in a passive distant God, the eternal return of the cosmos, and astrological forces governing the human world.

But Ibn Ezra’s view of Indians contains nothing from Muslim ethnological reports about Indian religious practices – no statues of four-armed gods, ancient temples, sectarian divisions, Brahmins, or sun-worship. Ibn Ezra’s view instead reflects the image of respected intellectuals found in Arabic books of astronomy, and it echoes the opinion expressed by a number of Muslim writers about Indian civility and high achievements in science and philosophy.

This image of India differs radically from Ha-Levi’s condemnation of Indians for their irrationality and quarrelsomeness, ignorant astrological and historical ideas, and Sabian astral magic and veneration of idols. Ibn Ezra instead fulfills his roles of historian of science, astronomer, and anthropologist reporting the customs and views of another culture without judgment.

There remains, however, the question of comparing religions. Ibn Ezra’s view of Indian beliefs portrays them as rather close to his own philosophy: Indians are moral citizens and rational monotheists, and they can protect themselves from harmful astrological forces as well as Jewish astrologers can, and Indians can live a happy life. Their only obvious defect is their Aristotelian doctrines, but this mistake seems minor compared with pagan worship of the sun and moon by other nations. I will argue, nevertheless, that Ibn Ezra, if he had explicitly compared Indian beliefs with his own Jewish philosophy, would have argued for a clear superiority in his own Judaism. Ibn Ezra’s answer will resemble Ha-Levi’s in essence, but Ibn Ezra will reach that answer through his own specific concepts of Judaism and views of the universe, including astrology.

*[p. 72]* ^pg85

How Ibn Ezra would compare his Judaism 

with Indian beliefs: A speculation

According to Ibn Ezra, non-Jews like Jethro knew about God, but only through the name *‘Elohim*, for *‘Elohim* was the divine name “known in the world before the coming of Moses because everyone acknowledged it” (comment to [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^18-1|Exod. 18:1]]). This applies also to the Egyptians, whom Ibn Ezra portrays as astrologers. In this Egypt, Pharaoh uses astrological thinking to explain the plague of the frogs from “a configuration of the stars of the sky.”<sup>62</sup> And when his magicians cannot duplicate the plague of the gnats, they tell Pharaoh, “It is the finger of *‘Elohim* in accord with the configuration of stars affecting the fortune of Egypt.” This means that Egyptians, who were astrologers, knew God only through God’s intermediaries, which is what the term ‘*Elohim* signifies to Ibn Ezra. “Pharaoh did not deny the existence of a creator but only the Name that Moses made known to him” (comment to [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^8-6|Exod. 8:6]]).

We have seen that Ibn Ezra himself conceives of God as the transcendent Source of existence, which he calls “the Glorious Name,” and that he differentiates this from the powers of ‘*Elohim* in the middle world, beginning with the angels, which govern the lowest world through natural laws. But the Glorious Name can bypass the middle world, intervening miraculously in history. In speaking of such intervention, Ibn Ezra always refers to God as “the Name” or “the Glorious Name.” Moses, the greatest prophet, had attained a level of “cleaving to the Name” and was therefore “able to change nature in the lowest world and to perform signs and wonders” (comment to [[Torah 14 - Vaera#^6-3|Exod. 6:3]]). The Exodus itself was a miraculous occurrence.

See, it was the configuration of Israel to continue in slavery in accord with the stars of its constellation. But the Name … with His power brought forth signs \[miracles\] in the lowest world that were not governed by the middle world, and brought Israel out from the rule of the constellations. (Comment to [[Torah 17 - Yitro#^20-1|Exod. 20:1]])

Ibn Ezra characterizes seven remarkable phenomena that occurred during the Exodus as miracles, using the terms *mofet, nisim, ma\`aseh nes, nes be-tokh nes, d’var pele’*.<sup>63</sup> But because Egyptians, like the rest of the world at that time, believed in God as *‘Elohim*, they could not understand these interventions that altered astrological and other natural laws. They also would not have understood how the Name intervenes in the lives of individuals who cleave to Him, whom He saves from harmful astrological configurations.

Coming into sight, then, is Ibn Ezra’s picture of all the pagan nations holding a concept of God like that of the Indians and ancient Greeks: a distant Cause continuously imparting motion to a cosmos of astrological causality. This is why Ibn Ezra can portray all the nations in *Reshit H. okhmah* “fearing Heaven” and worshiping God: they acknowledge God but only in the form of *‘Elohim* with its astrological powers and continuous creation. 

*[p. 73]* ^pg86

Ibn Ezra’s world was thus divided between monotheist pagans believing in *‘Elohim*, and those nations who believed in the revealed Name together with *‘Elohim*. And if some of these pagans, such as the ancient Egyptians, also worshiped astral powers, they could still – in Ibn Ezra’s view – be considered believers in God at the same time. This was not as bad a doctrinal error as denying God altogether.

I now repeat: so what is wrong with this pagan view of the world? Indians rejected prophetic revelation but were good citizens who wrote books of political advice without depending on revelation. They were smart astrologers who helped people avoid astrological dangers.

Ibn Ezra’s answer would be that the revealed Torah provides profound benefits to Jews, including knowledge of the highest world and God’s Glorious Name and a path to eternal life. To all Jews the Torah reveals the existence of the Glorious Name, who created the world once in the past and who intervenes in human history to nullify astral forces. In biblical times, the Torah had taught Jews how to construct divinely ordained objects like the Cherubim in the sanctuary and to perform rituals such as the sacrifices, which would channel astral power for the nation’s benefit. And now, during this time of exile, the laws of Torah provide a wisdom and a path that guides all Jews in behavior that is beneficial to them. Its laws keep Jews healthy, protecting them from illness caused by foods or changes in the air, so that they live longer.<sup>64</sup> Even more important, the laws of Torah “train the heart and lead a person to cleave to the Glorious Name,”<sup>65</sup> for the root of all the Torah’s laws is loving the Name with all one’s soul and cleaving to the Name ([[Torah 18 - Mishpatim#^23-25|Exod. 23:25]] and 31:18).

The Torah even has the power to protect the entire Jewish nation from misfortune, since if all Jews were able to observe its laws fully, they would free themselves from astral forces and live directly under the rule of the Name. “As long as they cleave to the Name, no zodiacal sign exerts any influence on them,” Ibn Ezra writes in *Sefer Ha-\`Olam*. Ibn Ezra defines “cleaving to the Name” as “knowing Him and observing His commandments.”<sup>66</sup> If they do this, God “will overcome the upper configurations.”<sup>67</sup> “But if Israel does not keep the Torah, then their constellation \[Aquarius\] rules over them” and causes harm.<sup>68</sup> The sad story of Jewish life in exile showed that the Jewish nation had never succeeded in obeying the laws of Torah in their entirety, but Ibn Ezra does speak of God nullifying malefic astral decrees for individual Jews who cleave to the Name.

Lastly, the Torah “strengthens, intensifies, and increases the power of a person’s highest soul,” the *neshamah*, helping it to rule over the desires of the body.<sup>69</sup> This function assists especially the intellectual elite among Jews – the intelligent person (*maskil*) and the wise soul (*ha-neshamah ha-h.* *akhmah*) – to follow a higher path. Reflecting Neoplatonic, perhaps mystical, ideas, Ibn Ezra envisions the Torah as a training in moderate asceticism, withdrawal from the world, and intellectual meditation on the Absolute. His short book of religious philosophy, *Sefer Yesod Mora’ ve-Sod Torah* (Book on the Foundation of Awe and the Secret of the Torah, 1161, England), 

*[p. 74]* ^pg87

sets forth a number of “steps of ascent.”<sup>70</sup> The intellectual (*maskil*) will first allow the laws of Torah to control his desires for the pleasures of the world, which Ibn Ezra lists as wealth, buildings, delicious foods, and sexual pleasure. Then, understanding that life is short, one will withdraw from the “vanities of the world” and turn to a program of study that begins with science and moves to metaphysics. But these rational studies are insufficient for completing the upward path. One must also study and meditate upon the Torah of the Name and continue to keep His laws. At that stage, the Name will intervene to open one’s eyes and renew one’s spirit within, transforming one’s soul, and it will cleave to its Creator and be filled to satiation with the joy of His presence. Then when death comes, this transformed soul will enjoy eternal delight with God.<sup>71</sup> Or as *Reshit H. okmah* puts it, “When a person refrains from following his eyes and his heart in their tendency to satisfy his desires, ‘wisdom comes to rest within him’ \[[[Proverbs 14#^14-33|Prov. 14:33]]\] … and after the soul leaves his body, he will ‘inherit substance’ \[[[Proverbs 08#^8-21|Prov. 8:21]]\] and ‘live forever’” \[[[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^3-22|Gen. 3:22]]\].<sup>72</sup>

Indians and other Aristotelian monotheists, therefore, lacking Torah, face disadvantages. They lack a set of guidelines designed for the masses that helps them avoid harmful astrological forces, keeps them healthy, and reveals God’s higher Name while training them to cleave to the Name; these guidelines also enable intellectuals to ascend into the presence of the Glorious Name and gain eternal life. Indians, in contrast, know God only as a distant Cause of natural forces.

Some of Ibn Ezra’s religious poems illuminate the two types of relationship with God, through *‘Elohim* and through the Name. In a book titled *Twilight of a Golden Age: selected poems of Abraham Ibn Ezra* (1997), Leon Weinberger collected 65 of Ibn Ezra’s hundreds of surviving poems and organized them in representative categories. Reading just the liturgical poems in this collection, one notices that many of them describe the cosmos of medieval astronomy, with its sun and moon, planets, and constellations moving in complex ways embedded in their separate spheres, maintained by the angels above them, and all of the heavenly bodies attesting to God’s majesty and power. In one poem, for example, Ibn Ezra imagines the stars as numerous soldiers circling the throne of *‘Elohim* and singing praises; the five planets and sun and moon are officers of the army hastening on their rounds, sometimes in a day and sometimes in months or years. They move in irregular patterns as God has determined for them. And all bear witness to “the reliable *‘El*” (God).<sup>73</sup>

But we also encounter poems that express passionate love and longing for a higher personal God, the Name, in the way of some of the Psalms and Sufi poetry. These poems declare that God is All and everywhere, and yet the poet’s soul yearns to cleave to God. He seeks nothing from God but God’s “face” (presence). The poet uses a vocabulary of desire, love, and physical longing (*h. eisheq, ma’avai, \`agavah, ‘ahavah*) to characterize his per

specify God’s ineffable Name as the object of desire. The poem *‘Ahavat ’Eili* 

*[p. 75]* ^pg88

declares that the more the poet comes to know the Name, the more he feels awe and love. He inscribes the Name on his heart. Another poem declares that God invites us to call His Name, and another that the poet calls God using the divine name revealed to him by the prophet (Moses).<sup>75</sup> Knowledge gained through revelation appears in other poems. One exclaims that whoever meditates on God’s wonders, using his mind and observation together with the words of God’s prophets, will be always grateful to God for His protection. In another poem, written for the holiday of *Simh. at Torah*, the poet discerns God through his mind’s eye (reason) as well as His Torah, “by His splendid power.”<sup>76</sup> Revelation and reason are interdependent; one alone is incomplete. This small sampling of Ibn Ezra’s liturgical poetry reveals the way he could integrate astronomy and science, mystic longing, and the role of revelation into a coherent voice of religious praise and love.

The poems assume a world in which Indians would suffer from a superficial and incomplete knowledge of the Divine. Yes, the scientists of the world, including Indian scholars, can share the poet’s awe and admiration for the grand and orderly universe, and can imagine a power that sets it in motion. This is itself a significant experience. But the poems also assume that only the revelation of God as the Glorious Name provides the deepest encounter with the Divine, arousing personal love and a longing to cleave to the Divine and to inscribe God’s Name upon one’s heart.

Or so I would speculate as to how Ibn Ezra would compare Indian beliefs and his own philosophy of Judaism. When Ibn Ezra compared religions, he would, as with the ancient pagan religions, identify levels of correct belief. But he would be most interested in whether religious philosophies taught a full knowledge of God, enabled “cleaving to the Name,” and guided people into eternal life. That is, he would make what is central to his own philosophy of religion the criterion for comparing and judging other religions.

Ibn Ezra’s picture of Indian philosophy totally omits some important motifs found in Ha-Levi’s picture, such as idolatry and magic, but continues the motifs of the absence of revelation and presence of astrology (although a much more sophisticated astrology than Ha-Levi imagined), and the antiquity of Indian culture. The civic virtue of Ha-Levi’s “good India” reappears in Ibn Ezra’s picture, and Indian science becomes a central theme.

### 3. The kingdoms of South Asia and the hierarchy of religions in Benjamin of Tudela’s Book of Travels

Benjamin’s *Book of Travels*

Sun- and fire-worshippers, dark-skinned astrologers, embalmed corpses sitting on stools, magic sun-wheels, high priests using magic to deceive their people: these are some of the elements of the picture of South Asia that appear in the book *Sefer Masa\`ot* (Book of Travels). This book presents itself as a record of the people and places encountered by a traveler named Benjamin, a resident of the city of Tudela in Navarre, who returned from his travels in 1173. The book begins with the words of an editor claiming that Benjamin passed through “many faraway countries, as is set out in his book. In every place he entered, he wrote about all the things he saw or he heard from truthful men.”<sup>1</sup> The book describes cities and Jewish communities in Italy, Greece, Constantinople, Palestine, Baghdad, Persia, Egypt, and eastwards, including south and eastern Asia.

Although scholars have written extensively about *Sefer Masa\`ot*, its reports about South Asia still need further study. Adolph Asher, the first modern student of the book, speculated in 1841 that Benjamin had “collected notices” about Asia from “Jewish pilgrims” who visited Baghdad, the city farthest east that he actually reached in his travels.<sup>2</sup> In 1907 Marcus Adler, using a superior Hebrew manuscript and re-translating the book, showed interest mainly in trying to identify which actual cities in south Asia the book was describing.<sup>3</sup>

Recent scholars have returned to the question of the sources of Benjamin’s descriptions of Asia. Joshua Prawer (1988) observed that a “large scholarly consensus” agreed that the Asian sections “depend on oral witnesses.”<sup>4</sup> In 2008 David Jacoby argued the more specific claim that Benjamin had learned about Asia from merchants circulating between eastern and western Asia, whom he encountered in Baghdad, Isfahan, and the island of Kish, including Egyptian Jews trading in India.<sup>5</sup> In another recent article, Abraham Melamed speculated that the book’s pictures of south Asia had a different source: they were “no doubt influenced by Muslim texts” while also being the “most imaginative” picture of India in medieval Jewish literature. Melamed could find “no explanation” for the 

*[p. 82]* ^pg95

various elements in the book’s portrayal of India.<sup>6</sup> But he did not identify the “Muslim texts” that had influenced the author, or think about how those texts might help us understand the book’s picture of South Asians. In fact, Melamed wrote no more than a paragraph on *Book of Travels*. Nor did Martin Jacobs (2014), whose recent book *Reorienting the East:* *Jewish Travelers to the Medieval Muslim World*, devoted four chapters to the topic of medieval Jewish travelers “encountering the Other” – which included Muslims, Christians, Karaites, women, Assassins, and blacks. Jacobs’ short paragraph about the south Asian kingdoms of *Book of Travels* appears under the category of blackness – which is an important topic, but the south Asians were “other” in multiple ways, and especially in their worship.<sup>7</sup>

Jacoby believes that Arabic sources shaped several stories in *Sefer Masa\`ot*, and Jacobs finds in the book “narrative motifs that turn up in Muslim-authored works.”<sup>8</sup> All these suggestions about the book’s sources raise questions about its history of composition. The most we can say about this, as Jacoby and Jacobs have demonstrated, is that we know very little for certain.<sup>9</sup> Because we can recognize in the book the work of at least two editors, we cannot even know whether a traveler named Benjamin was the book’s main author, or if he was, how much of his actual reports were modified, abbreviated, or even expanded in the process of editorial rewriting. How much of the book represents elaborations based on oral reports circulating in Europe at the time? And since many printings exist, each differing slightly from the others, we are also reading the work of copyists rewriting for the tastes of their particular audiences. However, like most scholars today, I will study the received text of *Sefer Masa\`ot* in the form of Marcus Adler’s edition of 1907 (based on the fullest extant Hebrew version, MS London), and will speak of “Benjamin” as its author while recognizing its complex history of composition.

As with Ha-Levi’s and Ibn Ezra’s writings, I will compare Benjamin’s picture of South Asian societies with the accounts in Muslim-authored travel reports and geographies of the time. Were Benjamin’s stories about South Asia unusual for his time and society, and if so, what does that tell us? Or if the book’s accounts show similarities, the Arabic versions might clarify what Benjamin was describing. Are his stories as imaginative and inexplicable as Melamed surmised?

That is one way to explore Benjamin’s portrayal of South Asia – from outside the book. But is there any way to learn more from within *Book of Travels* itself about its view of the South Asian societies? This is difficult in a book containing very few opinions about the people and places it describes. One way, however, to infer the book’s view of the South Asian kingdoms is by comparing them with other societies in the book and setting the south Asian kingdoms into the book’s framework of the world’s religions. First, however, we must examine exactly what the author wrote about the two kingdoms of South Asia.

*[p. 83]* ^pg96

The South Asian kingdoms and their 

Arabic parallels: Qulam

The south Asian kingdoms enter *Sefer Masa\`ot*, like all the locations described in it, after a set of travel instructions. To reach the first kingdom of South Asia, containing the city of Qulam, the traveler must sail down the Tigris river as it flows into the Indian Ocean, then sail for six days to an island called Qish, and then sail another ten days to Qatifa, after which seven more days brings one to Qulam.

The book’s account of Qulam begins with two introductory sentences making four brief claims (part A). Five major themes (B–F) make up the body of the account, with the last theme (F) expanding on an element (A1) of the introduction.

… Qulam, the beginning of the kingdom of the \[A1\] sun-worshippers, \[A2\] who are descendants of Kush. \[A3\] They are astrologers, and \[A4\] all of them are black. \[B\] They are trustworthy in commerce. When merchants come to them from a distant land and enter the harbor, three of the king’s scribes approach them and write their names and bring them before him. And the king takes upon himself \[responsibility for\] their property which they lay upon the field without a guard. An official there sits in an office, and the owner of anything lost tells him the marks \[of the lost property\] and he \[finds and\] gives it to him. And thus is this custom (*minhag*) in all his kingdom. \[C\] From Passover to Rosh Hashanah, all the days of the summer, no person leaves his house because of the sun, since there is great heat in that land, and from the third hour of the day and onwards, all remain in their houses until evening, and go out after that. They light lamps in all the markets and all the streets and do their work and commerce at night, for they turn night into day<sup>10</sup> because of the great heat of the sun. \[D1\] Pepper is found there. They plant its trees in the fields and each person in the city knows each one his own orchard. The trees are small and the pepper is white as snow, but when they collect it, they place it in tubs and pass boiling water over it to strengthen it and afterwards they remove it from the water and dry it in the sun, and it turns black. \[D2\] Calamus and ginger are also found there, and many varieties of spice. \[E\] The people of that land do not bury their dead but embalm them with varieties of spice, and place them on stools and cover them with sheets. And every single family has a house in which it places its fathers and relatives, and the flesh dries on the bones and they look like living people, so they can recognize every one of them, their fathers and their relatives, from that day for many years. \[F\] They worship the sun, and they have large high-places (*bamot*) everywhere about half a *mil* outside the city. In the morning they run to greet the sun because they have at each and every high-place (*bamah*) a sun wheel made from various kinds of 

*[p. 84]* ^pg97

magic (*kishuf*). When the sun comes forth, the wheel turns with loud sounds, and every one of them, men and women, offers incense to the sun with a censer in his hand. And this, their path, is folly for them.<sup>11</sup> (*Sefer Masa\`ot* 90-92, my translation)

The name “Qulam” probably refers to the old port city of Quilon on the Malabar Coast and today’s Kollam in the State of Kerala.<sup>12</sup> It was a well-known port for Greek, Roman, and Chinese merchants, and then medieval Arab traders, who knew it as Qulam Malay (Malay probably referring to Malabar), first mentioned in the *Akhbar al-Sin wa ‘l-Hind* in the 9th century.<sup>13</sup> Benjamin never uses the word “India” (*hodu*), but a geographically informed reader would likely infer it.

This is one of the longer descriptions of a city or kingdom that appear in *Book of Travels*. Benjamin offers a broad and detailed picture of the society of Qulam that combines its commercial laws, king’s behavior, weather and hard-working people, major crops and their preparation for human use, treatment of corpses, and acts of worship.

What do contemporary Arabic writings about India show us about the account of Qulam in *Sefer Masa\`ot*? Benjamin’s first claim (A4), the inhabitants’ dark skin, appears in many but not all the Arabic-language accounts. For one example, Idrisi, writing in the middle of the 12th century from Sicily, stated, “As for the people of India, Sind, China and of all those \[regions\] embraced by the sea, their color is either brown or black.”<sup>14</sup> Although (A3) Indian astrology and astronomy was not a major theme in Arabic travel and geographic books, we see from the writings of Andalusi, Maimonides, and Ibn Ezra, as discussed in previous chapters, that educated Jews and Muslims knew Indians as astrologers and astronomers.

Turning next to the five major narrative motifs in Benjamin’s account of Qulam, we find close parallels for the first motif (B) in two Arabic books telling how Indian kings protected foreign merchants. In Nahwara, wrote Idrisi, “the king heartily welcomes travelers and protects their property.”<sup>15</sup> Similarly and at great length, Sharaf al-Zaman al-Marwazi, of 12th c. Persia, wrote that in the land of Jurz (Gurjara), “justice and security prevail,” so that even if someone lost gold on the street, “no one would pick it up.” Arab merchants, he continued, visit this kingdom to trade with its honest, kind, and charitable people. “When their bargain is finished, the king sends someone \[with the merchants\] to protect their goods and escort them out of the kingdom.” The next kingdom, according to Marwazi, is even better. Its king tells visiting merchants and travelers, “Go out as you like. If anything happens to you and if you have any losses, take from me, as I stand surety for you.”<sup>16</sup>

Sun-veneration (F) also appears in Arabic reports. Idrisi stated that of the 42 doctrinal sects in India, the adherents of one of them “worship the sun and prostrate themselves before it and believe that it is the creator and ruler 

*[p. 85]* ^pg98

of the universe.”<sup>17</sup> Abu al-Faraj al-Nadim of late 10th c. Baghdad, called this sect “Dinabaktaniyah” (likely reflecting the Sanskrit words *Aditya*, “sun,” and *bhakta*, “devotees”), and said,

They are worshipers of the sun, for which they have made an idol on wheels …. They claim that the sun is the king of the angels, deserving devotion and worship. They prostrate themselves in front of this idol and walk around it with incense and stringed instruments.<sup>18</sup>

Muhammad al-Shahrastani of 12th c. Persia repeated Nadim’s report, and then, writing about Indian philosophers, added a story that resembles Benjamin’s about the Qulamese rising early to venerate the sun: “It is part of their tradition,” wrote Shahrastani, “to watch for the sun to rise and \[when they see it\] to worship it, saying, ‘How beautiful is your light! How splendid! How luminous!’”<sup>19</sup>

We find, then, these two central motifs of the Qulam story – protection of foreign merchants and sun-worship – closely reflected in the books of four Muslim authors. The details are different enough, however, that we cannot say *Book of Travels* relied directly on the Arabic books or similar oral reports. Rather, at least in the second of these motifs, *Book of Travels* shows a different interest, less about theology and more about magic and exotic machinery.

“High places,” *bamot* in Hebrew, is where the Qulamese stand to greet the sun. This word is associated in Scripture with hills where idolaters venerated their gods, usually through sacrifices and sometimes child-- sacrifices. The term appeared earlier in *Book of Travels* when Benjamin visited a *bamah* in Palestine where ancient ruins of the Ammonites still stood. He observed on this *bamah* a gold-covered statue consisting of a male god attended by a female god to his right and another to his left, all three sitting on a throne. Ammonites had offered sacrifices and incense to the altar of this god. Benjamin called this statue by a biblical noun of utter disgust, *shiqutz*.<sup>20</sup> The *bamot* (high places) of Qulam’s sun-veneration would evoke this association with Ammonite idolatry, and perhaps even Benjamin’s expression of disgust. *Book of Travels* judges this sun-veneration harshly, quoting [[Psalms 049#^49-14|Psalms 49:14]]: “And this, their path, is folly for them.” 

Certainly, India’s heat was well-known to Muslim travelers and geographers, but I could not find in the Muslim books any story similar to Benjamin’s about (C) Indians working at night to avoid the heat of the day. And several books report on the cultivation of pepper trees, but I found no report on (D1) its processing into black pepper. Idrisi, for example, wrote about pepper, but only the appearance of the tree; instead he wrote at length about coconuts and elephants, and very briefly about bamboo, hemp, bananas, and other produce of Indian kingdoms – in contrast to 

*[p. 86]* ^pg99

Benjamin’s sole interest in pepper production.<sup>21</sup> One of the main products traded by Jewish merchants was pepper, so perhaps Benjamin learned about its processing from them.<sup>22</sup> The Arabic books were aware of (D2) Indian spices,<sup>23</sup> and Maimonides also knew of Indian spices, writing that myrrh, cinnamon, and sweet calamus come from the islands of India.<sup>24</sup> Ginger was another important item of trade for Jewish merchants.<sup>25</sup>

But reports of (E) Indians embalming their dead do not appear in the Arabic writings. This motif is, however, found in one early Arabic account of China. Sulayman reported that the Chinese dried the bodies of their dead relatives with quick lime “and place them in some part of their houses” for a year before burying them. They also embalmed the corpses of their kings with aloe and camphor and mourned them for three years before burial.<sup>26</sup> Benjamin or his unknown oral or written sources may have confused distant locales and customs, transferring the story from China to South Asia.

The South Asian kingdoms and their Arabic parallels: Ibrig The second Asian kingdom in the *Book of Travels*, called Ibrig, is difficult to identify today. Benjamin says only that one reaches it after twenty-three days of sea travel from Qulam. The location could lie along the eastern coast of India, southern Burma, or as some scholars have suggested, Sri Lanka.<sup>27</sup>

Benjamin’s description of Ibrig differs from that of Qulam in telling a single integrated story with two parts. It begins with two sentences that generalize about the people’s worship and their priests, which become the subjects of the longer stories that follow.

Its inhabitants are \[A1\] fire-worshippers who \[A2\] are called Dukhbin …. These Dukhbin have \[A3\] priests (*komarim*) in their house of idolatry (*beit \`avodah zarah*) in every place. And these priests are great magicians in all kinds of magic (*kishuf*), and there are none like them in all the earth (or land). \[B\] And in front of the high place (*bamah*) of their house of prayer (*beit tefilah*) is a large valley (or trench), and every day of the year they light there a great fire and call it ’*Elohuta* (Divinity), and \[B1\] they pass their sons and daughters through it,<sup>28</sup> and \[B2\] they also throw their dead into the fire. \[C\] And there are some distinguished men of the land who vow upon their lives \[to die by\] being burned in the fire. And he who vows says to his household and relatives, Behold, I vowed to throw myself into the fire while I am alive, and everyone answers, saying to him, Happy are you.<sup>29</sup> And when the day that he vowed arrives, they make him a great feast. If he is wealthy, he rides a horse, and if he is poor, he walks by foot to the edge of the trench and throws himself into the fire, and all the members of his family rejoice with drums and dancing<sup>30</sup> until he is entirely burned. \[D\] And after three days come two of the high priests (*komarim gedolim*) 

*[p. 87]* ^pg100

to his house and to his children and say to them, “Prepare the house because today your father will come to you to command you what you will do,” and they bring witnesses from the city. And behold the satan (*ha-satan*) comes in his image \[the dead man’s\], and his children and wife come and ask him how he is in that world, and he says, I came to my friends and they refused to receive me until I fulfill my duty to the members of my house and to my neighbors. And he makes his will and divides his property among his children and commands them to pay all that he owes to other people and to receive from other people all that they owe to him. And the witnesses write the will, and he goes on his way and no one sees him again. And because of this fraud and this magic (*kishuf*) that the priests do to them with their magic, they are hardened in their error (*ta\`ut*) and say that there are none like them \[their priests\] in all the world. (*Sefer Masa\`ot* 92–94, my translation)

Compared with Benjamin’s account of Qulam, his picture of Ibrig seems narrow, focused only on worship, self-immolation, and priests using magic to deceive the dead man’s heirs. But it is more dramatic: men volunteer to end their lives bravely and painfully through self-conflagration, fires burn before temples night and day, this fire-god has the name ’*Elohuta*, powerful priests rule their people through deceit and magic, and a satan impersonates a dead family member. We even hear the exact words spoken by the main characters in the stories. But we learn little about Ibrig’s economy and government – only that upon death, the property of the deceased is distributed among heirs and that debts are settled.

Because Benjamin calls the Ibrigans (A1) “fire-worshippers,” Samuel Krauss (1937) and Hans Reisssner (1954) thought they were Parsees,<sup>31</sup> but the Arabic writings point much more probably to an Indian sect that worshipped fire. Indian fire-veneration appears in several medieval Arabic reports. Abu al-Qasim ibn Khurradadhbih (early 10th c.), followed by Idrisi, lists fire-worship as one of India’s forty-two sects,<sup>32</sup> and Marwazi, followed by Muhammad al-Shahrastani, names the sect as the Akhnutriya and describes their ritual in this way:

They dig a square pit for the fire, gather over it and turn round it while they throw into it food, clothes, perfumes, gold, silver, and jewels, whatever they have found …. They say that fire is the noblest of the four elements and the most excellent of them in its substance. They blame him who has burnt himself with fire, saying that he has polluted the fire.<sup>33</sup>

Like Benjamin, this report portrays the fire as a superior entity manifested in a pit.

Though Marwazi says this sect rejected self-immolation, we find other reports of Indians voluntarily throwing themselves into fires. Idrisi identifies 

*[p. 88]* ^pg101

these people as devotees of fire who “burn themselves in it,”<sup>34</sup> and Marwazi lists this practice as one of the “nasty ways” that Indians bring about their own deaths: “some of them burn themselves with fire.”<sup>35</sup> Abu Zayd al-Sirafi provides a long account of the established procedure in India for undergoing self-immolation; it bears a striking similarity to Benjamin’s story.

If someone from the general populace makes up his mind to burn himself to death, he first goes to the gate of the king’s palace to ask permission to do so, then he goes around in the markets. In the meantime, a fire has been kindled for him in a huge great pile of firewood; there are men in charge of this who stoke the flames until they blaze red-hot, as red as carnelian. The man then begins to run around the markets, preceded by people clashing cymbals and surrounded by members of his family and close kin …. He does not alter his pace or show any fear, until at last he reaches the pyre, leaps into the flames, and is burned to ashes.<sup>36</sup>

Benjamin’s story differs in having the person take an oath, and by quoting its exact words. Instead of asking his king’s permission, the person recounts his oath to his family. The fire is a god. But the main themes are the same: deciding to immolate oneself, processing through the city, public celebration and music, complete incineration.

Cremation (B2) was widely reported in Arabic texts. An anonymous observer in an early account claimed that “all the Indians bury their dead on a pyre.” Abu Zayd: “Their custom is to cremate the dead.”<sup>37</sup> Abu al-Hasan al-Mas\`udi concurred, and Idrisi stated simply, “the people of India cremate their dead. They do not bury them.”<sup>38</sup>

Another well-known theme in Benjamin’s story of Ibrig is the Indian use of (D) magic for deception. To repeat my discussion in Chapter 1: Khurradadhbih reported that the people of *al-Hind* used enchantments and conjured delusions to the bewilderment even of intelligent men,<sup>39</sup> and Abu Zayd wrote of Indian “conjurors and illusionists who are masters of their art.”<sup>40</sup> Marwazi included magic in his list of the arts known by Indians, saying, “They pretend that by this means they obtain what they want.” As examples of magic, he listed the use of “imagination and thought” to work wonders, and sorcery that baffles the minds of experts.<sup>41</sup> Nadim wrote similarly, “The art of illusion is a specialty in India, where there are books on the subject.”<sup>42</sup> Moreover, Muhammad al-Biruni specifically associated priests with the use of magic to beguile and control, a central element of Benjamin’s story. These priests, wrote Biruni, kept crowds in “thralldom by all kinds of priestly tricks and deceits,” by which they increased the ignorance of the masses rather than enlightening them.<sup>43</sup>

But the idea that magic can produce wondrous twirling machines that announce the exact time of sunrise (Qulam), and the motif of devious priests summoning a satan (in Ibrig) does not appear in the medieval Arabic reports about India that I have seen. The presence of satans is especially 

*[p. 89]* ^pg102

surprising. Indeed, this last section of Benjamin’s account of Ibrig (D) is written in a dramatic narrative style containing direct quotations and complicated narrative actions. This style implies an author filling in details needed for a vivid story.

One of these details, the odd one about priests causing “the satan” to take the form of a dead man, is reminiscent of Nadim’s information about Muslim “exorcists and magicians” (outside India) who could command “devils, jinn, and spirits” (*shaytin wa jinn was al-arwah*) to obey and serve them because these magicians obeyed Allah. Other magicians, wrote Nadim, claimed to control *shaytin* by committing actions that Allah prohibits.<sup>44</sup> Such an image of sorcerers controlling *jinn* and satans, circulating in Benjamin’s world, could have shaped his account of Ibrig, where priests use magic to summon “the satan” (*satan*, the Hebrew version of *shaytan*). So if he was thinking like Nadim, Benjamin meant merely one particular satan or devil, rather than Satan himself. The motif of Ibrigan magicians summoning devils now begins to sound like sorcerers summoning *jinn* in the *Thousand and One Nights*.

However, *Sefer Masa\`ot* itself contains a long story about a deceptive Jewish magician named David Al-Ro’i. A brilliant scholar living in a city near Persia, he had studied not only Jewish holy books but also the Muslim sciences, including “books of the magicians and sorcerers” (*sifrei ha-h. artumim veha-mikshafim*). Claiming that “there is no sorcerer (*mekhashef*) in the world” like himself, he performed “signs by deceptive miracles” (*simanim b’otot sheqer*) and claimed to be the Messiah, the “king of the Jews.” He planned to lead a rebellion against the king of Persia, which endangered the kingdom’s Jews. Finally, a secret plot brought about his death.<sup>45</sup>

So the world of *Book of Travels* contained magic as a normal power that human beings, including Jews, could learn to wield, although it was clearly associated with the danger of misusing that power. Perhaps Arabic reports about Indian magic, together with popular stories of sorcerers deceiving people through magic and demons, led Benjamin to fill out his story of Ibrig in what seemed a very plausible direction.

One final theme not found in Arabic reports about India is Benjamin’s claim that Ibrig carried out (B1) child sacrifices. In fact, his wording, “they pass their sons and daughters through it \[fire\],” comes straight from Scripture, appearing in numerous biblical verses condemning this practice among the ancient Israelites. Take two examples: “Let no one be found among you who passes his son or daughter through the fire, or who is an augur, a soothsayer, a diviner, a sorcerer” ([[Torah 48 - Shoftim#^18-10|Deut. 18:10]]), and “They passed their sons and daughters through the fire; they practiced augury and divination” ([[II Kings 17#^17-17|2 Kings 17:17]]). Other biblical verses even add the motif of an idolatrous high place (*bamah*) located in a valley, as for example, “They \[Judeans\] build the high-places (*bamot*) of Baal that are in the valley of Ben Hinnom, to cause their sons and daughters to pass through \[fire\] for Molekh” ([[Jeremiah 32#^32-35|Jeremiah 32:35]]).

*[p. 90]* ^pg103

Why might *Book of Travels* have connected child sacrifice with the people of Ibrig? I would speculate that Arabic reports of Indians venerating fire that burned in a trench evoked in Benjamin’s mind biblical images of the Ammonite god Molekh, who demanded child sacrifice. Benjamin would also have recalled biblical high places and valleys where these sacrifices were performed. Many of the scriptural verses about child sacrifice associate it with fire, sorcery, and idolatrous *bamot*. In addition, reports of Indians cremating their dead and immolating themselves, also carried out through fire, strengthened the association between Ibrig’s fire-worship and the Bible’s child-sacrifice. In the background of this identification probably lies an assumption on Benjamin’s part of common customs shared by south Asia and the ancient Near East, as we saw in the writings of Ibn Ezra and Maimonides.

We have seen, then, that nearly all the elements of Benjamin’s pictures of Qulam and Ibrig are reflected in Arabic reports about India. They are not Benjamin’s inventions, as some scholars have wondered. Sun- and fire-veneration, specific ways of venerating the sun and maintaining the fires, kings protecting foreign merchants and their goods, the specific procedure for deciding to throw oneself into fire and then the procedure for doing so, the use of magic – all these elements in the descriptions of Qulam and Ibrig find parallels in Arabic written sources, and two of the parallels are remarkably close in wording: the story of kings protecting foreign merchants, and that of the procedure for self-immolation.

Four elements, however, do not appear in the available Arabic sources: embalming, powerful magic-wielding priests, child-sacrifice, and the processing of pepper. Perhaps the written sources reporting the embalming and priests have disappeared, or perhaps the basis is oral tales that never entered any books. I have also suggested possibilities of confusion within the extant sources or, in regard to child sacrifice, logical inferences from the sources, that led to Benjamin’s stories. He may have learned about the processing of pepper from Jewish merchants who traded it. His pictures of south Asia are therefore not as imaginative and inexplicable as one might first surmise, and they were common in his time and culture.

Even so, Benjamin’s accounts of Qulam and Ibrig do not reproduce exactly the same arrangement and selection of material found in any one of the written sources. This does not necessarily mean that Benjamin’s accounts derive from unknown oral sources, although that is certainly possible. More likely, the author of *Book of Travels* was selecting topics from available material, most of it surviving to our time, which fit his own interests and curiosity. He also enjoyed turning his sources into a good story.

The hierarchy of religions

I turn now to the question of how *Book of Travels* compared religions, and I again use “religion” in the redescriptive sense that I explained previously. 

*[p. 91]* ^pg104

Examining this question will also tell us more about the author’s judgment of Qulam and Ibrig.

Benjamin did not discuss the religions of his world in any systematic way. Unlike Halevi, Maimonides, and Muslim heresiologists, he had little interest in theology but mainly in acts of worship and unusual beliefs. What particularly interested him was what or whom people worshipped and how they worshipped, and whether they followed laws and what kinds. He reported customs that he considered strange and fascinating, and whether a certain people maintained good relations with the nearby Jews.

Benjamin used the Hebrew word *dat* as one way to classify the societies of the world. This word is difficult to define precisely. In Abraham Melamed’s book-length study of *dat* in Jewish literature, he concludes that in rabbinic literature it meant “commandments and customs” (*mitzvot uminhagim*), but changed in the Middle Ages to a more fluid meaning that blurred between theology and law, sometimes either and sometimes both. In medieval Jewish writings, *dat* as law could refer either to the revealed law of Judaism or to the human laws of pagan societies. Jews writing in Arabic used the word *din* as equivalent to *dat*.<sup>46</sup> We saw in the case of Ha-Levi that *din* meant for him a complex phenomenon composed of beliefs, laws, and worship, whether divinely revealed or based on human thought.

Let us look first at Benjamin’s use of *dat* in relation to two non-monotheistic societies, beginning with the Druze. The book calls this people *paganos*, a Spanish word distinguishing them from monotheistic peoples, and one manuscript adds the word *kofrim*, unbelievers. “They have no *dat* at all,” writes Benjamin. This seems to be associated with lack of sexual prohibitions; the men commit incest and trade their wives. They are *shetuf zimah*, “immersed in sexual desire.” But they do hold one belief, that after death good people are reborn as human beings and wicked people as asses and dogs. Benjamin condemns this belief as absurd by quoting the same biblical verse he applies to the people of Qulam for venerating the sun: “And this, their path, is folly for them” ([[Psalms 049#^49-14|Psalms 49:14]]).<sup>47</sup>

Another people without *dat* is the *Balakhzin* (Wallachians), who live in the mountains of Greece. Fiercely warlike and invincible, they ravage and plunder the land of Greece. They kill Greeks, but with Jews, they only rob them. Benjamin concludes, “And they have no *dat* at all.” Here, the absence of *dat* is associated with unrestrained murder and robbery.<sup>48</sup>

Benjamin’s sentence, “they have no *dat* at all” (*‘ein lahem shum dat*), characterizing both the Druze and the *Balakhzin*, implies three aspects of his concept of *dat*. First, there are degrees of *dat*, or else he would not have written “at all.” Second, one can think of *dat* in the abstract and beyond the boundaries of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. If *dat* were limited to the three monotheisms, Benjamin would not need to clarify that the Druze and *Balakhzin* “have no *dat* at all.” Lastly, holding a belief in transmigration, as do the Druze, does not alone function as *dat*; apparently, therefore, laws regulating social behavior are essential to the concept. The only 

*[p. 92]* ^pg105

actions associated with lack of *dat* among the *Balakhzin* are murder and robbery. So Benjamin’s use of the word *dat* in these two cases seems to focus on social behavior, and therefore, *dat* need not consist of regulations revealed by God.

A third nation, the *Benei H. am* living in the Land of Kush, are the lowest human beings in Benjamin’s world. “They lack thought (*da\`at*) like other human beings.” He compares them with animals because they eat grass directly from the soil, wear no clothing, and lack self-control, which leads to the men “sleeping with their sisters and whomever they meet.” The men of Assuan capture the *Benei H. am* and sell them as slaves. Although Benjamin does not say so explicitly, his usage of the word *dat* in relation to the *Balakhzin* and Druze (who “have no *dat* at all”) implies the absence of *dat* also among the *Benei H. am*.<sup>49</sup>

Opposite these societies lacking all *dat*, and at the top of Benjamin’s hierarchy of religions are the Christian, Muslim, and Jewish communities, which exhibit full forms of *dat*. About Christian and Muslim religions, Benjamin says very little, maintaining an objective and impersonal stance. But he does, explicitly if incidentally, apply the term *dat* to each of them. We are told that the Caliph of Baghdad is “the head of the Ishmaelite *dat*,” and that the Girgashites “engage in the Nazarene *dat*” and the *Balakhzin* reject it.<sup>50</sup>

Benjamin’s vocabulary for Islamic and Christian institutions implies a comparison and ranking of these religions. What little he says about Islam appears in his long detailed description of an unnamed Abbasid caliph. His vocabulary conveys both closeness and distance. He uses terms from Jewish religious language to name certain aspects of Islam that appear close to Judaism. Thus, when pilgrims call the Caliph, “Our lord, light of the Ishmaelites and splendor of our instruction,” the term for “our instruction” (*torateinu*) refers to the Quran but echoes the word for Jewish law and teaching, the *Torah*. During the festival of ’Id al-Fitr the Caliph expounds the Quran, which Benjamin explains as “their instruction” (*toratam*). Then Muslim “sages” (*h. akhmei yishma’el*) praise him, and he slaughters a camel, an action that Benjamin also explains in relation to Judaism: “this is their Passover sacrifice (*pish. am*).” In addition, *Sefer Masa\`ot* uses the Hebrew word *qadosh* to describe the attitude of Muslims toward their Caliph; for them he is “their holy king” (*ha-melekh ha-qadosh shelahem*), again explaining Muslim behavior through the language of Judaism. Finally, mosques in *Sefer Masa\`ot* are usually called “houses of prayer,” *batei tefilah*, and twice they are called by the same term used for synagogues: *kenesset*.<sup>51</sup> These expressions present Islam as a religion of prayer. *Tefilah, kenesset*, *h. akhamim*, and *torah* together make for a religion that combines prayer, study, and law in a way that evokes Judaism. Notice, however, that the Muslim *torah* is “their *torah*,” theirs rather than the true and holy Torah of Judaism. The caliph likewise is “their holy king,” holy only in their eyes.

In “Images of the Muslim in Medieval Jewish Travel Literature,” Martin Jacobs (2011) suggests that Benjamin’s use of Jewish terminology for 

*[p. 93]* ^pg106

Islamic religious phenomena “allows for the decoding of alterity – which in turn becomes transformed into familiarity.”<sup>52</sup> But Jacobs also points to two disparaging terms that distance Jewish readers from Muslims; like Moritz Steinschneider (1877) before him, Jacobs explains these terms as “polemical.”<sup>53</sup> First, the Muslim pilgrims who stop in Baghdad on their way to Mecca are called *to\`im*, a word that usually means “going astray, erring,” and could in this context mean “wandering about” (because one is lost).<sup>54</sup> *Book of Travels* uses the same term for Christian pilgrims heading for Santiago de Compostela or Jerusalem.<sup>55</sup> Second, the book in its primary manuscript (that of the British Museum) usually refers to Muhammad as “the Madman” (*ha-meshuga\`*).<sup>56</sup> This derogatory appellation also appears in some other medieval Jewish writings, including two letters of Maimonides,<sup>57</sup> and may have implied Muhammad’s possession by *jinn* instead of his receiving true revelation.<sup>58</sup> On the other hand, the other manuscript versions of *Book of Travels* usually use the name *Mah. mad* (Muhammad) instead of “Madman,” and the primary manuscript itself uses the name *Mah. mad* three times in the story of the Caliph’s respectful treatment of the Jewish “Head of the Exile.”<sup>59</sup>

In the case of Christianity, Benjamin says nothing about ritual, doctrine, or scripture, and none of his descriptions evoke Judaism. He calls Christians “Edomites,” Edom being the rabbinic name for Rome, afterward used for Christendom, or he calls them “Nazarenes” and once, *ha-\`arelim*, “the uncircumcised.” Only his vocabulary for church buildings seems to imply an opinion: he calls them *bamot*, which in this context I will translate as “shrines” instead of high places:

In the one part is the great shrine (*ha-bamah ha-gedolah*) which they call St. Peter’s of Rome …. In Constantinople is the shrine (*ha-bamah*) of Santa Sophia …. And the like of this wealth is not to be found in any other shrines (*bamot*) in the world …. Mount Carmel … where the Edomites have erected a shrine (*bamah*) called St. Elias …. In Jerusalem is the great shrine (*ha-bamah ha-gedolah*) called the Sepulchre.<sup>60</sup>

We have already seen the biblical usage of the word *bamah*, often associated with hills where idolatrous sacrifices were offered to Canaanite gods; and in *Book of Travels*, we have seen the location of Ammonite worship called a *bamah*, which is the same term for where south Asians worship the sun and fire. This, then, is the word Benjamin uses for churches instead of *batei tefilah*, houses of prayer, as mosques are usually called, or *keneisiyot*, assembly places, as he calls some mosques and synagogues. (In *Sefer Masa\`ot* synagogues usually go by the term *batei keneisiyot*, “houses of assembly.”)

Higher in *Book of Travels* than Christian and Islamic religions, however, stands the religious life that Benjamin observes in the Jewish communities he encountered during his travels. Jewish life is characterized by four main virtues – piety (observance of *mitzvot*), holiness, study of the holy books, 

*[p. 94]* ^pg107

and generosity toward other Jews. The Jews of *Book of Travels* exhibit these virtues in community after community. For example, “great sages educated in the Mishnah and Talmud” live in Thebes, and the Jewish men of Lunel are “scholarly, intelligent, holy, and observant of the commandments” and “stand in the breach<sup>61</sup> for all their brothers near and far,” that is, take on the duty of helping other Jews. The phrase “observant of the commandments” (*ba\`alei mitzvot*) in this sentence appears also in Benjamin’s reports about the Jews of Paris, Constantinople, and Qulam – the latter called “good and observant.” Parisian Jews, in addition, study Torah day and night and are hospitable toward “all who pass by and return” (travelers); and they are “brothers and friends with all their brothers the Jews.”<sup>62</sup> When Jews suffer, as in Constantinople and Rudbar, it is because they are bearing the yoke of exile, but they do so with cheerfulness.<sup>63</sup> In addition to these virtues, Benjamin takes note of Middle Eastern Jews with distinguished lineages. In Baghdad, he reports that Rabbi Samuel ben ’Eli, a Levite, is “descended from Moses our teacher” and that the Head of the Exile, Rabbi Daniel ben H. isdai, has a “book of lineage \[that goes back\] to David King of Israel.”<sup>64</sup> And Benjamin also admires communities of warrior Jews (*gibborim*) who rule their own kingdoms in out-of-the-way places.<sup>65</sup> In the entire book he speaks of only one bad Jew, the David al-Ro’i whose story I summarized earlier.

Surprisingly, the virtues exhibited by the Jews of *Book of Travels* resemble the virtues of the Caliph of Baghdad. He is scholarly, having learned many languages and gained expertise even in the Torah of the Jews. He has studied the Quran and can expound it publicly. He observes religious law, as on ’Id al-Fitr. He is generous to others, establishing free hospitals for the ill and demented. He loves the Jews and shows them respect. Benjamin confers on him an array of complimentary adjectives: “like a truthful and trustworthy man,” “speaking peace,” “well-intentioned,” and twice, the adjective *h. asid*, emphasizing (in this context) the man’s kindness. The similarities between the Caliph’s virtues and those of Jewish leaders are remarkable, differing mainly in the Caliph’s more secular morality, lifestyle, and knowledge.

In relation, then, to Judaism and Islam as they are portrayed in *Book of Travels*, *dat* seems to imply a complex system of rules of worship, social laws, and morality, and, if only tacitly, the doctrine of a single revealed God.

However, between the three peoples of *dat* and the three peoples lacking any *dat* whatsoever, *Book of Travels* seems to conceive of a middle realm of nations that neither lack all *dat* nor exhibit the full range of *dat* characterizing the three monotheistic peoples. Recall my earlier argument that the book conceives of degrees of *dat* rather than either its absolute presence or absence; the possibility of *dat* existing outside the three monotheisms; and *dat* implying, at minimum, laws regulating social behavior.
