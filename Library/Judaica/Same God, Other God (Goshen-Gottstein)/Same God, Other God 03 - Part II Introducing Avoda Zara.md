## Part II Introducing Avoda Zara

 Overview 

 Part II introduces the category of Avoda Zara. Chapter 4 makes some historical and conceptual observations regarding the category. The category was formulated in the rabbinic period and is absent in biblical literature. The Bible is concerned with following other gods and faithfulness to the covenant. Continuity with these concerns is maintained through the rabbinic category of Avoda Zara and it is this category that informs Judaism’s attitude to other religions till the present day. Whereas the biblical focus on other gods is clear and requires no unpacking, Avoda Zara is a concept that is both rich and elusive. It describe both the otherness of another god and the foreignness of alternative means of worship. It refers to the domain of worship proper, but its application includes broader processes of othering and identity building, in relation to non-Jews and members of other religious communities. The broader uses make it harder to get at the core of the concept and to define what it is exactly that is wrong or problematic with Avoda Zara. There is next to no discussion of this in rabbinic literature, making Avoda Zara a matter of self-evidence, intuition, or a consequence of intergroup relations, rather than of a reasoned or focused argument in relation to other religions. It is only in the Middle Ages, living in Muslim or Christian societies, that theories and explanations emerge as to what constitutes Avoda Zara and its applicability to contemporary religions. 

 Chapter 5 argues that Avoda Zara is not only a legal category but also the foundation for a mental attitude and approach to the other. Scorn, mockery, and contempt are the attitudinal by-products of the declaration of another religion as Avoda Zara, following biblical precedents of how idol making is viewed. These procedures are phenomenologically similar to Hindu rituals that are practiced till this very day. Avoda Zara functions as a category of exclusion and delegitimation. The chapter poses the question of whether identity formation requires such strategies or if it can be upheld without such extreme tactics. The question is all the more poignant in today’s world, which witnesses a redefinition of relations between religions, with attitudes of respect gradually replacing stereotypes of contempt and mistrust. 

*[p. 26]* ^pg36

 While the classical associations of Avoda Zara do operate as described, we also witness the beginnings of a new paradigm emerging in relation to Hinduism. This is found in the attitude of religiously observant Israelis who are willing to give Hinduism a respectful hearing, seeking its spiritual riches, without necessarily foregoing the halachic concern for Avoda Zara. Such new openness may be possible because of the novelty of the Jewish-Hindu encounter and the fact that it does not carry the bitter historical memories associated with other religions, especially Christianity, previously considered as Avoda Zara. The emergence of this new attitude also provides an opening for reframing the question that might be posed with regard to Hinduism. <sup>1</sup>

### 4 Avoda Zara: Introducing the Category

 The Jewish view of other religions as Avoda Zara was not formed as a response to Hinduism. Contemporary Jewish approaches to Hinduism that make 

this issue the primary lens through which Hinduism is appreciated are carrying over attitudes that are thousands of years old and that were formed in relation to other religions, focusing on religious difference and opposition to other gods as well as to the worship of idols. Since the time of the Second Temple, Jews have imagined the battle against idolatry as a defining feature of the work and message of the founder of the Jewish people, or of the Jewish religion, namely Abraham. <sup>1</sup> 

 The term Avoda Zara is not a biblical term, and thus it does not represent the most fundamental foundation of Jewish thought and practice. In the Bible we find the prohibition of worshipping other gods and of making images, presumably for purposes of worship. Thus, the ten commandments move from the prohibition of having another god (Ex. 20, 2) to the prohibition of making and worshipping images (Ex. 20, 3–4). Presumably, the images are images of other gods, though it is possible to consider images of the covenanting God of Israel as equally problematic. What we have then is an insistence on full fidelity to the God who redeems Israel from Egypt and the ensuing avoidance of alien forms of worship. The framework is one of covenant making, that is, the formation of an exclusive relationship between God and His people. This exclusive relationship leaves no room for other gods. In the framework of covenantal fidelity, other gods are considered real alternatives to God, hence avoiding them and their forms of worship is of supreme importance. In what is essentially a polytheistic, or henotheistic, worldview, covenantal insistence on absolute loyalty makes perfect sense. While one could offer an alternative reading, it makes most sense to assume that foreign worship through images is derivative of the identity of other gods, who are worshipped through images. The identity of the worshipped god as an other is primary, and the inappropriate method of worship is derivative of it. <sup>2</sup> The foreignness of worship is closely related to the otherness of the other god, a notion that is coherent within the biblical worldview. 

*[p. 28]* ^pg38

 The term Avoda Zara was born during the rabbinic period. It is a useful term inasmuch as it captures both dimensions, mentioned in the ten commandments, that lie at the heart of Jewish concern with other religions—the strangeness of foreign worship and the otherness of the foreign god. Strange, foreign, and other are all nuances of the Hebrew *zar* , or of the feminine *zara* . To these one may add wrong, abhorrent, and repulsive, as additional nuances of the term. Avoda Zara thus takes on an entire range of meanings and associations. That all these meanings converge within one term adds to the theoretical and emotional complexity of the term and consequently of working with it in a contemporary context. <sup>3</sup> Therefore, alongside the conceptual unpacking that is the backbone of the present work, one must also consider unpacking the term emotionally. As I shall suggest here, one may own some of the emotional associations of the term, while avoiding others. Greater clarity on both the conceptual and the emotional plane is called for as part of a contemporary engagement with the meaning of Avoda Zara. 

 As useful as Avoda Zara is in expressing a complex relating to otherness with regard to other religions, it is equally unhelpful in ascertaining where the heart of the problem lies and understanding what is actually wrong with it. If one poses the question of what is actually wrong with Avoda Zara, one realizes that the answer is presumed more often than it is articulated. <sup>4</sup> The ambiguity begins, as is already evident, in the collapsing of the strangeness of other gods and of other methods of worship into one term. The breadth and complexity of such usage invite lack of precision and perhaps as a result also facile application of the category. To this should be added the fact that with the possible exception of a sentence or two in rabbinic literature, we do not have a discussion in that literature of what is actually wrong with Avoda Zara. But the question may be superfluous. The religions that would constitute Avoda Zara, the others of the rabbinical world, are indeed other gods, distinct from the God of Israel. <sup>5</sup> In this sense the rabbis stand in a continuum with the Bible. <sup>6</sup> Avoda Zara would then be a continuation of the prohibition of the worship of other gods, by the methods typical of their worship. Unlike the biblical worldview, however, the henotheistic option seems to no longer be an option. There is no room, no legitimacy, for any god besides the one God, known and worshipped by Israel. 

 Otherness is not only the key to understanding how Avoda Zara relates to other gods, but also to how concern with Avoda Zara defines attitudes to the other, that is, to other members of society, the non-Jews, amid whom the rabbis live. One of the striking features of the rabbinic tractate Avoda Zara is that it deals almost exclusively with Jewish-Gentile relations, and not with the theological or practical issues relating to other gods and their worship. These are indeed assumed as part of the complex of otherness, in light of which Jewish-Gentile relations are viewed. <sup>7</sup> But it is precisely how closely these two sets of issues are interwoven that suggests that for the rabbis otherness and, hence, identity are the actual foci of religious concern, rather than preoccupation with theological precision or appropriateness of worship due to God. One may assume fidelity as the basis for separating from others and their gods. However, it is noteworthy that the covenant, the conceptual category that called for such separation and fidelity in the Bible, has all but disappeared from the rabbinic worldview. 

*[p. 29]* ^pg39

Thus, even if a sense of covenantal fidelity endures, the need to separate from the nations and their gods is no longer articulated in terms of the covenant and its demands. Otherness, identity, and the need to separate in faith from all that is not us thus emerge as characteristics of the rabbinic world view and, in a sense, also as the rationale that informs the laws and practices formulated by the rabbis in relation to Avoda Zara. Objection to foreign worship helps establish Jewish identity in relation to the various gods of surrounding cultures, and for the past two millennia has played a similar role in relation to the surrounding cultures of Christianity, and even of Islam. Extending this logic would lead to the position that everything that is within the realm of the other is ipso facto Avoda Zara. <sup>8</sup> The concern for identity is not strictly speaking a halachic concern. Halachic 

discussions during the course of the evolution of rabbinic literature almost never make preservation of identity an out-and-out factor in their reasoning. <sup>9</sup> Nevertheless, I think it is fair to argue that this concern informs much of halachic decision making and the broader Jewish concern with other religions. The attitude to other religions is always informed by the concern that we might learn from them or become like them, thereby losing our particularity, our true vocation, and our faithfulness to what makes us unique in our relationship with God. The battle against Avoda Zara is thus a fundamental feature of Jewish identity and an important safeguard for its protection. Using a metaphor appropriate for a discussion of India, it may well be the elephant in the room. 

 Recognition of the centrality of the concern for upholding identity and the discourse of otherness as constitutive of rabbinic views and rulings is significant for our discussion. It points to the roots of the emotional attitudes associated with Avoda Zara . The authorities that instructed that wigs made from Tirupati hair must be burned draw legal precedent and emotional orientation from talmudic sources and their later iterations in halachic literature. Both the halachic content and the emotional tone resonate up to present day and continue to inform Jewish attitudes to other religions, including Hinduism. 

 Alongside recognition of the major role that otherness and the distance taken in relation to it play, we must also note what is absent from the rabbinic worldview and what changes take place in later generations. As mentioned, there is no philosophical discussion of what Avoda Zara means and what is wrong with it. Related to this is the lack of any meaningful attempt to relate the Jewish God to the understanding of divinity expounded by other religions. <sup>10</sup> What this suggests is that attitudes to other religions, and in particular the possible view of other religions as Avoda Zara, are not always a function of close and careful analysis of the faith content of other religions. Because Avoda Zara came into being as a formative category with so little discussion of what it means, why it is religiously problematic, and how it should or should not condition our attitudes to others, it has continued to flourish and to play a major role, both in halachic and non-halachic discourse, despite diversity and lack of clarity in the possible understandings of Avoda Zara. From talmudic to contemporary times, the declaration of Avoda Zara and the practical consequences of such a declaration were often viewed as self-evident, requiring little further reflection and explication, either in terms of Judaism’s own concerns or in terms of an optimal grasp of the other religion. In 

*[p. 30]* ^pg40

important ways, understanding other religions can be bypassed in the process of pronouncing them Avoda Zara. 

 The attempt to unpack what Avoda Zara is, why it might be problematic, and how contemporary religions are or are not Avoda Zara takes place more than a thousand years after the category came into vogue. The rise of Christianity and Islam placed Judaism before a challenge that is fundamentally different from what confronted it in either the biblical or in the rabbinic period. The religions that Judaism had to deal with no longer proclaim another God. They claim to proclaim and worship the same God, even if, in the case of Christianity, such claims involve a different understanding of divinity than that espoused by Judaism and Islam. This new challenge should have perhaps occasioned far more reflection on the nature of Avoda Zara than it did. But it did lead to the articulation of some novel and important views of those religions, and these provide us with theoretical resources through which we can explore possible Jewish views of Hinduism. 

 The Middle Ages are characterized not only by the new external theological challenges but also by novel developments within the Jewish intellectual tradition. One of the characteristics of medieval thought and creativity in all fields—halacha, philosophy, kabbalah and interpretation—is the advancement of method and an increase in the systematic approach to discussions. It is therefore no surprise that the first systematic attempts to articulate what Avoda Zara is, either in a theoretical sense or with an eye to resolving contemporary challenges, come from the Middle Ages. These theories were formulated when Avoda Zara already operated as a powerful formative category, shaping Jewish practice and the attitudes of Jews to non-Jews. It is therefore understandable that these theories have only a partial impact upon how Avoda Zara actually operates as a category and in practice. If the praxis of Avoda Zara precedes its theoretical discussions and if the attitudes concerning Avoda Zara and otherness have been formed long before such systematic theoretical discussions are articulated, we need not be surprised if any theory will have only limited value in shaping the views and practices of practitioners in relation to other religions. Practice has the power to endure beyond the varieties of theory. Still, theory is crucial for the continuing application of Avoda Zara as a theoretical and practical category and is obviously of significance in the exploration of novel challenges. Thus, even though no single theory or understanding of Avoda Zara has had the power to shape the minds and practices of believers in a universal way, some views or theories have gained great popularity, while others continue to vie for recognition. 

 The present challenge is complex. Judaism encounters religions it had never encountered previously, and it must articulate its views of those religions. The process inadvertently entails some kind of dialogue with all levels of previous tradition. Significantly, the paradigms developed through the encounter with other religions, primarily Christianity, must be tested in relation to new religions. <sup>11</sup> But beyond that, the new opportunities also invite us to continue the process of reflection on the core question of what Avoda Zara is, why it is problematic, and what it ultimately means for Judaism. Once we recognize how late in the day and how partial the reflection on Avoda Zara has been, we realize that every new encounter is also an opportunity for fresh consideration of what 

*[p. 31]* ^pg41

Avoda Zara means. If we are to advance beyond propagation and repetition of age-old habits and attitudes, we must continue the process of examination and reflection of the fundamentals of Avoda Zara and wherein Judaism’s objections to it lie. Novel situations allow us to engage in advancing reflection on these issues precisely because they might present us with challenges, in the form of theological and religious realities, not addressed by previous religious encounters or earlier reflections. This leads then to the question of whether we are actually adequately equipped, theologically and halachically, to deal with the challenge of encounter with new religions—in our case, Hinduism. It is conceivable that the theoretical tools at our disposal, developed late in the day, in a very partial way and in response to other religious realities, may not do justice to the challenges of Judaism’s encounter with Hinduism. Therefore as we apply different understandings of Avoda Zara to the case of Hinduism, we need to be mindful both of how those understandings may be applied and of the very suitability, or lack thereof, of the categories and explanations previously provided for addressing the novel challenge.

### 5 Avoda Zara: Mental Attitudes

 Before delving deeper into an exploration of Hinduism in terms of Avoda Zara, I would like to offer some ref lections on the broader mental attitude 

associated with identification of another religion as Avoda Zara. The sheitel crisis brought to light some of the elements that govern attitudes to other religions and that both feed and are fed by the perception of the other religion as Avoda Zara. Affirmation of another religion as Avoda Zara is, in fact, a tactic of exclusion and delegitimation. Each has a different emphasis. Exclusion points to how one views oneself in relation to the other. It is an important component of Jewish identity politics. Separateness and the uniqueness of Jewish identity are enforced through affirmation of the total lack of value of the other religion and its declaration as Avoda Zara. Following such a declaration one removes oneself, in a variety of practical ways, from contact with the other. Not wearing “offered in India” wigs is but one expression of a broader tendency to avoid all contact with Avoda Zara, and consequently with its practitioners and eventually with all forms of otherness. 

 Delegitimation takes the politics of exclusion one step further. It is not simply that one is erecting boundaries in relation to the other. Delegitimation also makes a claim about the other, and in our case the lack of validity and value of another religion, because it is classified as Avoda Zara. If someone were asked to classify the religions of the world according to their relative value, from the perspective of ultra-Orthodox Judaism, one would be hard-pressed to offer a response. If all, or almost all, religions are classified as Avoda Zara, there is little left to compare or rank. Classification as Avoda Zara sends all religions to the divine recycle bin. Pronouncement of another religion as Avoda Zara is a means of delegitimating that religion. The upshot is that there is nothing of value to be learned or received from that religion. 

 It is important to recognize that in principle exclusion and the protection of group identity can be achieved without delegitimation. While psychologically and sociologically there is a strong link between the two strategies, conceptually it is possible to maintain boundaries without delegitimating the value of the other. The other may have value, even if limited. The other has value in and of itself, and the other’s values may be upheld as appropriate for the other, even if 

*[p. 34]* ^pg43

not for oneself. <sup>1</sup> Recognition of value without delegitimation could open the door to receptivity and openness to learning from the other, provided such receptivity does not infringe on the rules that guide behavior appropriate for the Jew. Clearly, the nature of exclusion is different when it is coupled with delegitimation. The more closely bound exclusion and delegitimation are, the more rigid the boundaries are that constitute identity. 

 Classical attitudes to other religions were formed in the biblical and rabbinic periods. What characterizes both periods is that exclusion is coupled with delegitimation, leading to minimizing contact with the other and limiting mutual influence and inspiration. Both biblical and rabbinic literature develop an ethos of mockery of other religions, and idols are the very subject of that mockery. <sup>2</sup> Prophetic critique of idolatry resorts to the rhetoric of mockery:

 I foretold, and you are My witnesses. Is there any god, then, but Me? 

 “There is no other rock; I know none!” 

 The makers of idols all work to no purpose; And the things they treasure can do no good, As they themselves can testify. 

 They neither look nor think, and so they shall be shamed. 

 Who would fashion a god or cast a statue that can do no good? 

 Lo, all its adherents shall be shamed; they are craftsmen, are merely human. Let them all assemble and stand up! They shall be cowed, and they shall be shamed. The craftsman in iron, with his tools, works it over charcoal and fashions it by 

hammering, 

 working with the strength of his arm. 

 Should he go hungry, his strength would ebb; should he drink no water, he would grow faint. 

 The craftsman in wood measures with a line and marks out a shape with a stylus; he forms it with scraping tools, marking it out with a compass. 

 He gives it a human form, the beauty of a man, to dwell in a shrine. 

 For his use he cuts down cedars; he chooses plane trees and oaks. 

 He sets aside trees of the forest; or plants firs, and the rain makes them grow. All this serves man for fuel: he takes some to warm himself, and he builds a fire and bakes bread. 

 He also makes a god of it and worships it, fashions an idol and bows down to it! Part of it he burns in a fire: on that part he roasts meat, he eats the roast and is sated; He also warms himself and cries, “Ah, I am warm! I can feel the heat!” 

 Of the rest he makes a god—his own carving! he bows down to it, worships it; He prays to it and cries, “Save me, for you are my god!” 

 They have no wit or judgment: their eyes are besmeared, and they see not; Their minds, and they cannot think. 

 They do not give thought, they lack the wit and judgment to say: 

 “Part of it I burned in a fire; I also baked bread on the coals, I roasted meat and 

ate it— 

 Should I make the rest an abhorrence? Should I bow to a block of wood?” He pursues ashes! A deluded mind has led him astray, and he cannot save himself; He never says to himself, “The thing in my hand is a fraud!” 

 Remember these things, O Jacob for you, O Israel, are My servant: 

 I fashioned you, you are My servant—O Israel, never forget Me. <sup>3</sup> 

*[p. 35]* ^pg44

 It seems quite likely that the prophets, indeed the Hebrew Bible itself, did not really understand idolatry and were already at some remove from it. <sup>4</sup> But what is important is that idols serve as the spearhead of a strategy of separation, realized through the tactics of mockery. To a large extent, much of the Jewish attitude to the use of images in worship perpetuates this prophetic attitude. <sup>5</sup> Studies of the religions, mocked by the prophet, reveal that behind the worship of idols lay a sophisticated religious system. <sup>6</sup> Significantly, Hindu worship of idols is in many ways similar to ancient Mesopotamian practices. The similar understanding of the use of the idol as dwelling place for the deity and hence the temporary nature of such dwelling is striking. <sup>7</sup> This makes the challenge poignant. The prophets could only engage in their polemic by either misunderstanding or ignoring the self-understanding of idol worshippers and by presenting them in a way that made their actions seem stupid and senseless. Either way, we must ask whether we can continue upholding this attitude and whether we are willing to bear its billion dollar consequences. If the prophet did not understand, and we have become aware of this lack of understanding, is it religiously upright to maintain the same mocking attitudes, based on misunderstanding and caricature, if there exists the possibility for a fuller understanding as an alternative? <sup>8</sup> In fact, our attitude to mockery and caricature of the other should be fed by the experiences of centuries and millennia of having been misunderstood and mocked by others. We have the choice of either propagating and reciprocating such attitudes, within the confines of our comfortable separateness, or of reconsidering the entire strategy, in view of how it has shaped our own historical experience. One thing seems clear: the double-faced game of protesting a false representation of ourselves while propagating a false representation of the other is not tenable in intellectual, religious, or broader social terms. If we desire not to be mocked, and we seek true respect from others, we must consider how that should affect our own efforts at granting similar understanding to others. In certain circles, this requires a fundamental shift in mentality. 

 The alternative explanation, in theory, for how prophetic critique functions is that the prophets did not simply misunderstand but that they intentionally misrepresented a religion they understood only too well. <sup>9</sup> The dangers of such a strategy are the same as those noted above, except more so. To intentionally misrepresent the other is far graver than to simply misunderstand. Both require us to reflect on how problematic mockery, rejection, and delegitimation are as strategies for dealing with other religions. Further thought is required to account for why they should be avoided on internal Jewish grounds. At the very least, the lessons of history, just mentioned, should provide an incentive to desist from mockery. From a different perspective, our awareness today is global and no longer supports the limited insularity (as separate from distinct identity) that was typical of former ages. There are few people around who would continue to justify a narrow insularity, founded on rejection, mockery, and delegitimation of the other. Global coexistence requires a different mode of religious communities living together. If we take this seriously, it also requires rethinking what Avoda Zara means and how it conditions our attitude to the other. Openness to understanding the other on his own terms may be a prerequisite for a new paradigm that would replace partial understanding, misrepresentation, and mockery. 

*[p. 36]* ^pg45

 Representing the other in a way that is true to the other’s self-understanding, rather than in light of our own canons, which lead to the mockery of the other, might itself be of religious value. Much thought is still required concerning how a fair representation of the other might constitute a religious value beyond the imperative of contemporary global culture and its needs for peaceful coexistence. <sup>10</sup> Some initial thoughts in this direction might include an expanded understanding of the third of the ten commandments, of not bearing false witness to another. <sup>11</sup> Perhaps the quest for truth ought to itself be sufficient as a driving force for seeking to understand and represent the other in a faithful manner. Other ideals may be invoked, including love, the golden rule, and even the notion of judging the other favorably. Obviously, application of any internal religious principle to another religion is a constructive act that requires the will to apply it and the recognition of its importance. As someone who has that will, I would seek to develop a religiously based mandate to represent other religions faithfully. If that is so, we must apply Avoda Zara wisely. I am not suggesting that the category is not relevant. However, we must give thought to how we apply it. The sheitel crisis seems to encapsulate all that is wrong in an age-old attitude and how that attitude can no longer be upheld. One cannot judge the other purely on one’s own terms, without careful consideration of the other, on his terms. <sup>12</sup> One cannot follow the visual as the sole yardstick for appreciating another religion. And the upshot of the declaration of another religion as Avoda Zara itself must be considered. Even if another religion is defined as Avoda Zara, does this mean that it is of no value, that it has nothing to teach, or that it cannot be respected? Avoda Zara need not spell complete lack of value, any more than the designation of food as non-kosher would lead to viewing such food as lacking any nutritional value. That Jews may not be able to derive nourishment from these foods should not impede our ability to recognize that these foods are nourishing; nor should the fact that we are prohibited from eating these foods lead to a denial of their secondary and tertiary benefits (such as through commerce, medicine, and more). <sup>13</sup> We perhaps ought to conceive of laws governing the view of other religions and their worship in a similar manner. 

 Tracing the attitudes to the worship of idols to biblical sources forces us to confront the roots of our attitudes to other religions. These indeed go back to our foundational texts, biblical revelations, and prophecies. Tackling Avoda Zara as a fundamental religious attitude thus touches the deepest religious questions: the enduring meaning of prophecy, <sup>14</sup> the price of exclusivity of religious faith, and the truth and message of Judaism. While rejection of Avoda Zara has remained a constant in Judaism for thousands of years, the scope of what constitutes Avoda Zara and how it is to be treated have changed in a variety of ways. Living in the midst of other religious communities has yielded, as we shall shortly see, positions that accommodate and produce a respectful attitude to those religions. We are today heirs to two kinds of attitudes, two emotional orientations toward the religious other, the one rejecting the other accommodating, perhaps even accepting. One may draw various distinctions in order to account for why at times one attitude prevailed and at times another. Economic, sociological, philosophical, and theological factors all came together in order to form, particularly in relation 

*[p. 37]* ^pg46

to Christianity, an attitude that is either continuous or discontinuous with biblical and rabbinic rejection of idolatry. <sup>15</sup> Ways have thus been found of adopting attitudes that are emotionally different from the rejectionist paradigm. Hinduism presents us with a unique challenge that has been addressed thus far only in the most minimal way. In dealing with a religion that was never part of the Jewish horizon of other religions, what will be the emotional attitude that governs the view of the worship of idols? 

 Several factors come together in considering the emotional attitude to Hindu worship of idols. All previous instances of dealing with the religion of the other, and the consequent proclamation of that religion as Avoda Zara, have been the result of common living or of cultures that have come into close contact and have a common history. Hinduism, as well as other Eastern religions, are different in that they have not been part of the historical Jewish encounter with other religions. Dealing with these religions and their worship takes place when new paradigms for relations between religions have emerged. On the one hand, interfaith relations are founded on respect and acceptance of the other, by virtue of the very fact of the other’s existence and by the common understanding that the world’s religions must move from conflict to collaboration. At the same time, the academic study of religion brings to our awareness more nuanced understandings of the other religion than those that prevailed under the competitive paradigm, under which mockery and rejection flourished. The recognition that Mesopotamian idol worship, the very practice mocked by the prophets, was indeed more sophisticated and therefore less worthy of scorn, is but one example of new understandings that we must integrate into our theological view of other religions. 

 That the attitude toward Hindu Avoda Zara is already different from classical Jewish attitudes to Avoda Zara can be seen from the testimonies of contemporary Jewish religious travelers to India. In reading some of these testimonies, as compiled in a collection of essays titled *From India Till Here* , one is struck by how other paradigms and other foci become more central in conditioning the attitude to the newly encountered religious culture. Avoda Zara is recognized, but it is not the exclusive factor conditioning the attitude of these travelers. Spirituality, wisdom, vitality, and inspiration are all recognized alongside the worship of idols, leading to the challenge of how they might be reconciled. While no answer has emerged as yet, the very willingness to explore the validity of a tradition that is recognized as Avoda Zara is in many ways a novum. It suggests that emotional attitudes do not necessarily derive from the halachic proclamation of a religion as Avoda Zara, but in fact precede it. Under the conditions of cultural openness, receptivity, and dialogue that characterize much of the present Jewish encounter with Hinduism, <sup>16</sup> Avoda Zara does not seem to hold the exclusive sway it did for many former generations. This suggests that attitudes are at least as much a function of times and circumstances as they are an outcome of holding specific halachic views concerning the status of another religion. The Jewish seekers of today who roam around India in search of spiritual meaning, even when they come out of the Orthodox Jewish tradition and uphold its laws, do not seem to share the mental conditioning and the psychological attitudes that characterized earlier 

*[p. 38]* ^pg47

generations. Perhaps this is because Hinduism is distant and not an immediate neighbor; or because the world of contemporary interreligious relations is less confrontative; or it could be that some later attitudes to Christianity have softened Jewish attitudes to other religions, replacing scornful rejection with respectful boundary construction; or perhaps the sociology of different forms of Orthodox Judaism—modern/ultra, diaspora/Israel—holds the key. Whatever the reason, we can detect an attitude shift that accompanies the exposure to Indian reality. 

 The encounter with Hinduism thus brings to a head and provides an opportunity to reflect upon and to consider two fundamentally different attitudes to other religions. The Hindu sheitel crisis carries forth into the twenty-first century attitudes that are thousands of years old. These are based on scorn, rejection, and delegitimation. This mind-set leaves no room for learning, for understanding, or for dialogue. It focuses on exterior appearances, having little time or patience for the self-understanding of the other and for the complexities and nuances of the other’s worldview. The message of Judaism is read in stark opposition to the falsity of idols and what they represent. In this black-and-white view, the encounter can only go one way—ultimately, in the messianic future, all will come to know the truth of Judaism and all forms of false representation of God, Hinduism included, will disappear. Till such time, we continue to practice our spiritual lives. To this view one should perhaps add the hope that while waiting for such messianic fulfillment, others should not view us with the same polarized view and scornful misrepresentation as we grant them. 

 The other attitude that has emerged likely does not reject the halachic status of Hinduism as a form of worship that is forbidden to Jews. However, it does not project the entire scornful and rejecting outlook upon Hinduism as a consequence of this halachic declaration. It is characterized by a willingness to listen and to understand. It deems that there is something to understand, even if the other’s worship is defined as Avoda Zara. And understanding may even hold the key to the realization that what seemed at first sight as Avoda Zara turns out to be more nuanced, possibly not Avoda Zara at all, once a deeper understanding has been gained. Those halachic attitudes that developed a more respectful view of Christianity, even if defined as Avoda Zara, provide this approach with precedent for substituting scorn with respect. Indeed, this view illustrates how much the attitude to another religion is not simply an outcome of the judgment passed on that religion, but a consequence of broader historical factors. 

 The emerging attitude is not simply to ignore idol worship, while emphasizing spirituality, wisdom, and the likes. Rather, it allows a more open dialogue with the very phenomenon of Avoda Zara, without assuming that definition of Hinduism as Avoda Zara is necessarily the end of potential involvement with it. Instead, the recognition that Hinduism is, or may be, Avoda Zara becomes a way of defining the challenge. The challenge is one of understanding, learning, and appreciation of both self and other. Avoda Zara reveals something about ourselves and challenges us to a deeper understanding of what is the real difference between the various spiritual choices, rather than assuming the answer is self-evident by the very nature of different means of worshipping the Divine. <sup>17</sup> I believe that deeper

Later on in this book I will venture some suggestions regarding what could be an enriching dialogue with the Hindu perspective, and what it could teach us also about ourselves and our own particularity. 

 One of the important contributions of the present book to a discussion of Hinduism and of Avoda Zara in general is the reframing of the question with regard to another religion. Rather than ask whether Hinduism is Avoda Zara, we could enquire as to whether it is the same God that is known and approached in both religions. Reframing the question in this way has far-reaching emotional consequences. In theory, as shall be suggested in a later chapter, it is possible for Hinduism to be Avoda Zara, even though it is the same God that is worshipped. Once we admit the possibility of the same God being worshipped in both religions, the attitudinal ground shifts dramatically. Even if various strictures, boundaries, and identity-maintenance strategies remain in force, the scorn and delegitimation associated with the proclamation of another religion as Avoda Zara would disappear. Specific prohibitions and boundaries, expressing some meaningful difference in worldview, would be accounted for by the practical dimension of the application of the category of Avoda Zara. At the same time, the harmful negative attitudinal consequences of such a declaration would be neutralized by balancing that declaration with a revised view of Hinduism, based on the “same God” perspective.
