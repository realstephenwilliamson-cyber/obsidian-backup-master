## The Concept of Political Education

*1 February 1963*

The subject that I would like to talk about here today is a fairly modest one. I would simply like to encourage you to recognize that your political education and culture should consist in more than familiarizing yourself with political procedures, the party political system, the process of political administration, and other phenomena of this kind, or with learning how to pass the examinations by showing that you understand what is required for some legislative proposal or other to become law, or how the Upper House of the Federal Parliament has to proceed in these matters, or goodness knows what else. For the main thing is to come to a living understanding of politics itself – in other words, to get beyond the mere façade of politics – and for this you must develop a social and sociological understanding and thus recognize the real social play of forces that lies beneath these official institutions.

First of all, I just want to say a few words about the phenomenon of ‘personalization’, which seems to me to be an exemplary illustration of the false consciousness that arises once the sphere of politics is cut off from everything else – is treated on its own in such a way that everything is unilaterally focused on the individuals who are involved in politics and make decisions in the political sphere. Of course, there are social reasons for this. The colder or more impersonal the world becomes, the less we are capable of influencing or deciding our fate within society, the more we sense the role of society in this painful regard, and the more indirect or thoroughly mediated the relations 

*[p. 162]* ^pg169

between people also become – and even the closest and most intimate relations between human beings are subject to the law of the market, to the law of exchange, and exhibit an alienated aspect that no individual can avoid becoming painfully aware of in themselves in some way or other in this world. The more these processes develop – the processes which the impressive tradition of philosophy and social theory alike described in terms of social alienation, objectification or reification – the more that human beings, blinded by a kind of false consciousness, tend to try and humanize in their own way a world in which they would otherwise almost freeze to death; in other words, they try to present this dehumanized world to themselves as if it really were a human world, one which directly reflects or depends on human beings themselves. And then prominent individuals of one sort or another come to assume tremendous importance, as if everything depended on which particular person plays this or that role in the political sphere. Thus politics comes to be looked upon, if I may put it this way, in the same way as the illustrated magazines that would have us believe that whether Princess Margaret is pregnant or a royal celebrity like Soraya is about to remarry are issues of the highest importance for everyone, even though such questions, apart from the two or three individuals concerned, are matters of complete indifference to us. Now the role of a particular individual in the domain of politics is indifferent in a similar way. You can observe this phenomenon from the electoral procedure in America: this is entirely personalized, although it is just a question of the ‘tickets’ involved, i.e., the political party programmes that are supported by particular power groups behind them. The more objective, the more reified the forces in question become, the more people tend to personalize the latter. Thus the conflicts between specific constellations of interests and power are transferred to particular persons. This gives rise to spurious or merely apparent solutions to political conflicts, as you can also observe in political life over here, when one unpopular politician is replaced by someone else who might feel or look like a very different character, although in terms of their objective function and effect, of what they stand for or accomplish in the actual political struggle, may not be terribly different from that of their predecessor. Now we often find that this tendency to separate politics from the actual play of social forces means that students of political culture find it hard to relate their political judgement to the real forces at work here but focus instead on the ideas and the moral norms that allegedly stand behind the realm of politics. Thus the questions of law, of correct procedure, along with the secondary conceptual 

*[p. 163]* ^pg170

and intellectual issues involved, effectively take centre stage rather than political reality itself. It is astonishing to discover the extent to which the students, when they were asked about political questions,<sup>1</sup> talk repeatedly about political ideas and concepts and the extent to which they ignore the forces and the conflicts of power that, as we all vaguely know, actually shape our lives. This leads to an idealization of the political sphere as such, where real struggles are turned into merely apparent ones, where what is really happening is either not recognized at all or is downgraded to the level of merely contingent conflicts between individuals. The consequences of this are anything but harmless. For it means that the ideal sphere in which this kind of political ideology moves, namely the struggle or the apparent struggle between concepts, also leads to a kind of idealization of politics itself. In other words, once politics has become a matter of ideas, an idealistic matter, in this way, we ascribe the realm of politics to particular groups which, by virtue of their social position, are supposed to have a certain affinity or proximity to these ideals, and which are therefore supposed to exercise a specific function in this ideal sense, although in reality this is certainly not the case.

So here you have the transition – in other words – from the idealization of politics to the theory of elites, something which, as you know, played a decisive role in fascism and National Socialism. But this is also something – as we have had an opportunity to study at the Institute for Social Research – that clings on tenaciously in the general consciousness of the population in our democracy as well. Thus I can hardly emphasize too strongly, ladies and gentlemen – which is why I am repeating this now – that you should not identify the specific political form under which we live with the real social consciousness of human beings. One can live in a democracy even though one’s consciousness may still be entirely governed by ideas and forms of thought which are fundamentally incompatible with democracy, even when we openly endorse the latter, as very many people do today.

Now the theory of elites has the tendency to turn this apolitical aspect – where we turn away from the real play of forces to focus on the small minority of people engaged in more or less idealistically conceived political activities – into a political issue: into a readiness to cede social control to the elites, to relinquish the consciousness of our own autonomy and responsibility, to renounce even the possibility – extraordinarily difficult as this already seems in reality as it is – of shaping and determining our own fate as social beings in a genuinely free and independent manner. The concept of the mass, which is the conceptual counterpart of the concept of the elite, has certainly 

*[p. 164]* ^pg171

undergone a remarkable development these days. In one of his essays, Alexander Mitscherlich related an anecdote about a demagogue at an enormous public gathering – there must have been 20,000 there – who roars at the end: ‘And who, my friends and fellow countrymen, is to blame for all this? The masses, I say, the masses!’ – followed by ten minutes of thunderous applause from 20,000 people.<sup>2</sup>

Now techniques have been developed – and here National Socialism was particularly adept on account of its racial theories – for casting elite theory in such a way that even the majority, namely those who were not Jews but the overwhelming majority of the population, could feel as if they were the elite, were singled out, were something special. This is one of the remarkable and paradoxical compromise solutions that has been struck between the real conditions of mass society and elitist ideology. From this you can form a fair idea of the relationship between the hostility of the masses and the theory of elites – a theory which was originally developed by the Italian fascist theoretician Vilfredo Pareto.<sup>3</sup> And the potential of this anti-democratic elitist thinking, as we call it in sociology, plays an important role where the analysis of political consciousness is concerned.

In those few courses where the field of social studies includes genuinely sociological categories and questions, i.e., where the students explore the actual play of forces in society rather than just looking at the formal categories involved in questions of method, we found a significant contrast in the study that we mentioned earlier (which is now in the final stages of being written up for publication). We discovered that the proportion of students strongly committed to democracy was markedly higher in these courses than it was among students in the courses where there was no such emphasis on sociological issues. And here we can equate the ‘democratic’ outlook with a decisive rejection of elitist ideas – quite irrespective of which particular groups see themselves as an elite or claim to be one (and this self-understanding belongs to the concept of an elite) – along with the rejection of all authoritarian forms of political life. The relevance and importance of sociologically oriented teaching in political education, specifically in relation to the structures of political consciousness, is also evident from the fact that, in the courses where students learnt something about sociology, the tendency to react in authority-oriented ways, and the susceptibility to prejudices such as antisemitism or extremely negative judgements about black people, is far less marked than in the courses where the students are essentially concerned with formal political issues or political history and sociological themes are not addressed.

*[p. 165]* ^pg172

Thus in social studies with sociology, 42 per cent of the students are committed democrats, with only 25 per cent fitting this description in the courses without sociology. The students whose attitudes are internally contradictory amount to 44 per cent for those who do some sociology and to 54 per cent for those who do not. Those who reveal openly authoritarian attitudes amount to only 14 per cent of the sociologically informed students, with 21 per cent for those who are not.<sup>4</sup> Where one crucial thing is concerned, namely the attitude to authority and to the authoritarian state, the ratios are much more extreme, in the order of 2:1. It is forgotten that democracy is supposed to be a form of society in which human life is realized autonomously in accordance with free will, where the people and the individual are self-determining, where all relations between human beings are defined in accordance with free will. When politics is turned into a special or particular sphere, all these decisions are effectively relegated to institutions and the relatively blind social forces that are bound up with existing property relations. And democracy then becomes little more than a form of legal protection against cases of extreme and arbitrary behaviour, against serious infractions of one kind or another, or merely comes to represent a system of government that we allow an opportunity to govern when things are going well, for under democracy we have experienced a great economic upturn in recent years after all. But if we split the domain of politics off from the life process of society itself in this way, we may well be willing to dispense with democracy, as we already saw under fascism, as soon as we see the prospect, under a quite different political system, of securing a greater share of the global wealth for ourselves. Now I am not saying that this is the actual situation today, for the horrors of fascism and the direct threat to life it represents are too present to us to think that people would seriously contemplate the terrifying choice that I have conjured up here. Yet the possibility of this kind of thinking, on the basis of a formalized conception of democracy, of a renunciation of substantive living democracy, this possibility is extraordinarily strong precisely because we regard democracy as nothing but the totality of constitutional rules and other procedures, and completely lose sight of our own autonomous and independent capacity for social action, forgetting that society itself should be based upon the free and rational will of human beings. We lose sight of this because the preponderant power of the existing institutions in our world is so great that we hardly have the freedom any longer, and certainly not the energy, to imagine that things could be different. But what is really decisive here is that the social dimensions of all this are not exactly unrecognized. You constantly hear talk about the ‘climate 

*[p. 166]* ^pg173

of business’, and people go on everywhere about the importance of so-called interpersonal relations and the like. But that is not the key issue. For what is not properly recognized here is the objectivity of society, the fact that society has its own objective forms and laws that ultimately consist in control over the means of production and in the realities of social power. And the kind of thinking which abstracts the political sphere from these things serves only to dissolve the latter into legal-political forms or the mere thought-forms of individual subjects, so that the concept of the objectivity of society is lost to view. And if I could just sum up for you here what seems to me the real task of a meaningful sociology, it would be this: to awaken the consciousness of the objective social relations and to spread this same consciousness more widely among people so that they can try and improve these relations in earnest.

The great sociologists who adopted a subjectively oriented standpoint, such as Max Weber for example, were still very much aware of this social objectivity, as we can see from the importance that they ascribed to objective phenomena such as the progressive bureaucratization of the world.<sup>5</sup> But, for many others, the field of politics became another special sphere defined by the division of labour, one that is supposedly separate from the life of society as a whole. Given this division of labour, the special sphere is then happily abandoned to the so-called specialists who deal with it on our behalf. ‘He will see to it’, ‘the Führer knows everything’, ‘the Führer will deal with it for us’ – as they used to say in the Third Reich. But something of this still lingers on today precisely through this separation of politics from society in general. And this is accepted in a more or less indifferent or contemplative sort of way as long as we believe there is some clever professor in the Department of Trade and Industry who will ensure that the economic upturn lasts.

On the other hand, those who make politics their own special field in the practical sense, such as ‘the politician by profession’, as Weber puts it,<sup>6</sup> the person who pursues the general good as a particular profession, also become objects of suspicion for this very reason. This neutralization or trivialization of politics finds its precise correlate in a feeling of rancour towards the politician, towards the professional politician – a feeling that is very widespread and deeply rooted in America and is clearly beginning to manifest itself here in Germany as well. It expresses a resentment over the realization that I am not myself doing what I actually should be doing for myself, that the one I have allowed to deal with these things on my behalf is a questionable character who is really just out to fleece me. And this mistrust towards the politician – so coloured by the social relations 

*[p. 167]* ^pg174

that I have already attempted to describe for you – can easily become a powerful means of sowing mistrust about democracy itself, of promoting the kind of attitude that says: ‘We must finally make an end of all this corrupt politics and its associated cliques.’ As the Nazis liked to say: ‘Away with the wretched party system!’<sup>7</sup> In a sense they abolished politics, or, if you prefer, they made it total and abolished it at the same time. It is the strong man rather than the politician who is finally called upon to clean those Augean stables that always belong to the brazen rhetorical repertoire in these cases. This is the sort of anti-democratic tendency that can grow at the heart of democracy itself.<sup>8</sup>

Now I am certainly not saying that a solid knowledge of the so-called democratic rules and procedures – the things we hear so much about from the unloved figure of the official convener or the apparatchik – is something bad or contemptible in itself. I believe that anyone who gets involved in the power play of politics must also be competent and well informed about these structures and processes. For, as I have frequently had occasion to observe, political decisions are rarely taken in a completely open manner but are almost always arrived at through formalized, regulated and seemingly objective forms of procedure. So if one is not entirely familiar with these forms of procedure, if one cannot assess such practices in their own terms and deploy them appropriately, one is more or less hopelessly and unwittingly overwhelmed by their complexities.

But I actually want to say much more than this. One could argue that the domain of politics – and there is of course a well-known theory that has argued precisely this – is just an expression of the superstructure that rises over society.<sup>9</sup> For it identifies the procedures, transactions and individuals involved in the immediate direction of seemingly neutral social institutions and conflates them with social objectivity itself, i.e., with the institutions in which social power actually resides. In this sense, and I repeat the point, one might say that politics is ideology. But I believe – and here I would encourage you to entertain a thought that might initially seem rather hard to square with what your common sense may tell you – that politics is not just ideology, but ideology in the exemplary sense that the process of political will-formation and the political apparatus of power itself also harbour the potential for a change in the general structure, a change that can be brought about only by political means. In other words, if I can put this in a rather extreme form – and I am indeed frequently accused of a tendency to extreme formulations – politics is that shape of ideology that contains within itself the possibility of the dissolution of ideology. Now when I said that politics should 

*[p. 168]* ^pg175

really be understood as sociology, that always means that we can also intervene with every means of political will-formation available to us on the basis of social insight into the underlying social structures.

Let me just say in conclusion that there are situations in which politics does not have to consist exclusively in active and positive participation, for it can also consist in emphatic resistance to a false politics – and perhaps we live at such a historical moment right now. There are times and situations in which positive programmes are discredited or when there seems to be no prospect for their realization, where true politics, one which serves the concept of a right society, has contracted to this form of resistance.
