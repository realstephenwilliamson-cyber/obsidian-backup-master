**1.** Wherever ten Jews live,[^1] it is necessary to establish a place for them to congregate for prayer at the time of each prayer service.[^2]  
This place is called a *Beit K'nesset*.[^3] The inhabitants of a city can compel each other[^4] to construct[^5] a synagogue and to purchase scrolls containing the Torah, the Prophets, and the Sacred Writings.[^6] ^11-1

**2.** When a synagogue is built, it should be built only at the highest point of the city [as implied by [[Proverbs 01#^1-21|Proverbs 1:21]]]: "She cries at the head of the public places."[^7] It should be built [so that] its height exceeds [that of] all the other buildings in the city [as implied by [[Ezra 09#^9-9|Ezra 9:9]]]: "to lift up the house of our God."  
The[^8] entrance to the synagogue should open only on the east [as implied by [[Torah 34 - Bamidbar#^3-38|Numbers 3:38]]]: "...And those who camped before[^9] the sanctuary on the[^10] east."  
In [the synagogue], a *heichal*, where the Torah scroll is placed, should be constructed.[^11] The *heichal* should be constructed in the direction to which the people pray in that city,[^12] so that they will face the *heichal* when they stand to pray.[^13] ^11-2

**3.** A platform is placed in the center of the hall,[^14] so that the one who reads the Torah[^15] or one who gives a sermon[^16] can stand on it, so that all the others will hear him.[^17]  
When one positions the *tevah* which contains the Torah scroll,[^18] one should position it in the center of the hall,[^19] in the direction of the *heichal*[^20] and facing the people. ^11-3

**4.** How do the people sit in the synagogue? The elders sit facing the people with their backs toward the *heichal*. All the people sit row after row, each row facing the back of the row before it. Thus, all the people face the sanctuary,[^21] the elders, and the *tevah*.[^22]  
When the leader of the congregation stands to pray, he stands on the ground, before the *tevah*,[^23] facing the sanctuary[^24] like the others.[^25] ^11-4

**5.** Synagogues and houses of study should be treated with respect.[^26] They should be swept clean[^27] and mopped.[^28]  
All the Jews in Spain, the west,[^29] Babylonia,[^30] and *Eretz Yisrael*,[^31] are accustomed to light lamps in the synagogue[^32] and spread mats over the floor to sit on. In European communities,[^33] they sit on chairs. ^11-5

**6.** No lightheadedness - i.e., jests, frivolity, and idle conversation[^34] - should be seen in a synagogue.[^35] We may not eat or drink inside [a synagogue],[^36] nor use [a synagogue] for our benefit,[^37] nor stroll inside one.[^38]  
On a sunny [day], one should not enter [a synagogue to seek shade] from the sun, and on a rainy [day], [one should not enter a synagogue to seek shelter] from the rain.[^39] [However,] the sages and their students[^40] are permitted to eat and drink in a synagogue[^41] because of the difficulty [observing the prohibition would cause them].[^42] ^11-6

**7.** It is forbidden to calculate accounts in [a synagogue], unless the accounts are connected with a mitzvah:[^43] for example, the collection of charity,[^44] the redemption of captives,[^45] or the like.  
[Similarly,] eulogies should not be recited inside them, except a eulogy that involves many [of the inhabitants of the city]; for example, [if] there were a eulogy of the great sages of that city for which all the people would gather together and come.[^46] ^11-7

**8.** If a synagogue or a house of study has two entrances, one should not use it for a shortcut,[^47] i.e., to enter through one entrance and leave through the other to reduce [the distance one] travels, because it is forbidden to enter [these buildings] except for a mitzvah.[^48] ^11-8

**9.** A person who has to enter a synagogue to call a child or his friend should enter and read [a portion of the written law] or relate a teaching [of the oral law] and then call his friend,[^49] so that he will not have entered [a synagogue] for his personal reasons alone.[^50]  
If he does not know [how to study], he should ask one of the children[^51] [to] tell him the verse he is studying[^52] or, [at the very least,] wait a while in the synagogue[^53] and then leave, since spending time [in the synagogue] is one of the aspects of the mitzvah as implied by \[[[Psalms 084#^84-5|Psalms 84:5]]]: "Happy are those who dwell in Your house."[^54] ^11-9

**10.** A person who enters [a synagogue] to pray or to study is permitted to leave by the opposite door to shorten his way.[^55][^56]  
A person is permitted to enter a synagogue[^57] [holding] his staff, [wearing] his shoes,[^58] wearing [only] lower garments,[^59] or with dust on his feet.[^60] If it is necessary for him to spit, he may spit in the synagogue.[^61] ^11-10

**11.** Synagogues and houses of study that have been destroyed remain holy [as can be inferred from [[Torah 33 - Bechukotai#^26-31|Leviticus 26:31]]]: "I will destroy your sanctuaries."[^62] [Our Sages explained]: Even though they are destroyed, they remain[^63] holy.[^64]  
Just as one must treat them with respect while they are standing,[^65] so must they be treated [with respect] when they are destroyed[^66] with the exception of sweeping and mopping them. [When destroyed], they need not be swept or mopped.[^67]  
If grass grows in them, it should be pulled out and left there[^68] so that it will be seen by the people [in the hope that] it will rouse their spirits and rebuild them.[^69] ^11-11

**12.** One should not tear down a synagogue in order to build another in its place or in another place.[^70] Instead, one should build the [new synagogue] and then, one [may] tear down the [previous] one[^71] lest unforeseen difficulties arise [which prevent it] from being built.[^72]  
This applies even to a single wall of [a synagogue]. One should build the new wall next to the old wall and then, tear down the old wall.[^73] ^11-12

**13.** When does the above apply? When its foundations are not ruined or its walls are not leaning perilously.[^74] However, if its foundation is destroyed or its walls are leaning perilously,[^75] it should be destroyed immediately[^76] and [then, efforts to] rebuild it should be begun immediately throughout the day and night lest times become difficult and it remain destroyed.[^77] ^11-13

**14.** It is permitted to transform a synagogue into a house of study.[^78] However, it is forbidden to transform a house of study into a synagogue because the sanctity of a house of study exceeds that of a synagogue[^79] and one must proceed to a higher rung of holiness,[^80] but not descend to a lower rung.[^81]  
Similarly, the inhabitants of a city[^82] who sold a synagogue[^83] may purchase an ark[^84] with the proceeds.[^85] If they sold an ark, they may purchase a mantle[^86] or a case[^87] for a Torah scroll with the proceeds. If they sold a mantle or a case, they may buy *chumashim*[^88] with the proceeds. If they sold *chumashim*, they may buy a Torah scroll with the proceeds. If they sold a Torah scroll, the proceeds may only be used to purchase another Torah scroll,[^89] for there is no level of holiness above that of a Torah scroll.[^90]  
The same [laws apply] to [any money] which remains.[^91] ^11-14

**15.** Similar [principles apply] if a congregation collected money to build a house of study or a synagogue or to purchase an ark, a mantle or a case [for a Torah scroll], or a Torah scroll, and desired to change [the purpose for which] all the [funds] had been [originally] collected.[^92]  
It is forbidden to change [the purpose for which the funds will be used][^93] except from a matter of lesser sanctity to one of greater sanctity.[^94] However, if [the congregation] accomplished the purpose for which they had [originally] collected [the funds],[^95] they may use the remainder for whatever they desire.[^96][^97]  
All the components of a synagogue[^98] are considered[^99] like the synagogue itself.[^100] The curtain hanging before the ark[^101] is considered like the mantle of a Torah scroll.[^102] If a condition[^103] was made concerning them,[^104] the terms of the condition are binding.[^105] ^11-15

**16.** When do the above statements permitting the sale of a synagogue apply? In regard to a synagogue in a village. Since it was constructed for the sake of the inhabitants of that village alone,[^106] so that they can pray inside it,[^107] they are permitted to sell it if they all desire to do so.[^108]  
In contrast, a synagogue in a metropolis, since it was constructed for the sake of all the people in the world, [i.e.,] so that anyone who comes to that country can come and pray in it,[^109] it is considered as [the property] of [the entire] Jewish people and it can never be sold.[^110] ^11-16

**17.** The inhabitants of a village[^111] who desire to sell their synagogue in order to build another synagogue with the money, or to buy an ark or Torah scroll with the money,[^112] must establish as a condition [of the sale] that the purchaser not use the building for a bathhouse, a leatherworks, a *mikveh*, or a laundry.[^113]  
If, at the time of the sale, the seven officials of the community[^114] made a condition in the presence of the entire community[^115] that the purchaser be allowed to use the building for the above purposes, he may.[^116] ^11-17

**18.** Similarly, if the seven officials of the community made a condition in the presence of the entire community that [after the community accomplished the purpose for which they sold the synagogue], the remainder of the funds could be used for mundane purposes, they may be used for such purposes.[^117] Thus, after the money has been used for building another synagogue, for purchasing an ark, a mantle or case [for a Torah scroll], *chumashim*, or a Torah scroll,[^118] the remainder may be used for mundane purposes in accordance with their condition, and may be used for whatever they please.[^119] ^11-18

**19.** Similarly, if all the inhabitants of a city - or a majority of them[^120] - accept [the authority of] a single individual, whatever actions he takes [in regard to a synagogue] are binding.[^121] He may sell [the synagogue] or give [it as a gift][^122] alone,[^123] as he sees fit, and establish whatever conditions he desires.[^124] ^11-19

**20.** Just as it is permitted to sell a synagogue,[^125] it is permitted to give it away as a present.[^126] If the community had not received any benefit from giving it as a gift, it would not have given it.[^127] However, it cannot be rented or given as security.[^128]  
Similarly, when a synagogue is being torn down so that it can be rebuilt, it is permitted to sell the bricks, timber, and soil, exchange them,[^129] or give them as gifts.[^130] However, it is forbidden to lend them,[^131] since their sanctity only departs in return for money or benefit which is equivalent to money.[^132] ^11-20

**21.** Although the people pray in a city's main street on fast days[^133] and *ma'amadot*, because too many people gather to fit within a synagogue,[^134] [the street] does not possess any quality of sanctity, because [praying there is only a temporary measure] and it is not established as a place of prayer.[^135]  
Similarly, buildings and courtyards where people gather to pray[^136] do not possess any measure of sanctity,[^137] because they were not designated for prayer alone. Rather, [people] pray within them as a temporary measure, as a person prays within his home.[^138] ^11-21
