Two and the Same: 

Two Faces of Subjectivity in Kashmir Śaivism 

By Joel H. Posner 

Citi (universal consciousness) itself descending from (the stage of) Cetana (the uncontracted conscious stage) becomes Citta (individual consciousness) inasmuch as it becomes contracted (Sankocini) in conformity with the objects of consciousness (cetya). 

 Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam Sūtra 5 <sup>1</sup>

In Brahmanical schools the unqualified subject, or *Self* with a capital “s,” is the progenitor and seer of all. The qualified subject, or lowercase *self*, fettered by finite existence and fragmentary awareness, is but a poor relation. Embodied souls may strive to recover the Self within the self, or redeem their essential nondifference. A ubiquitous theme in Indic thought, the reconciliation of ultimate unity and cosmic diversity is largely the project of the Brahma Sūtras, a canonical text that famously waxes dialectic on identity and difference. Perhaps no other school provides a more inspired or elegant integration than Kashmir Śaivism as modeled in associated Trika, Spanda and Pratyabhijñā articulations. In this holistic systematization of reality and experience, the kinship and identity between the universal and particular subject is explicitly and deftly drawn. 

The central focus of this paper is the necessity, priority and supreme pedigree of subjectivity at all stages of consciousness as depicted in Kashmir Śaivism (KS). Here creative proliferation is a dynamic and spontaneous effulgence of supreme being (Śiva), and a descent of consciousness from and via unconditioned universal awareness (citi) to radically contracted awareness (citta) as it operates on the plane of ordinary experience. This stands in marked contrast to the view shared by some prominent Buddhist schools that consciousness, as a byproduct of contact between sense organs and sense objects, cannot claim a loftier inheritance but emerges in fleeting momentary cognitions unmoored to anything prior and higher. (Going forward, I shall characterize this generally as the Buddhist position.) Subjectivity in any form is an epistemic figment with no real bearing on awakened consciousness. This clash of perspectives remains a highly constructive contretemps for any related inquiry. 

I shall lean heavily as an academic outlier on likeminded ruminations set forth by Catherine Prueitt of George Mason University in her doctoral thesis*.*<sup>2</sup> Prueitt discloses surprising alignments and key discrepancies between the Yogacarin Dharmakīrti and the Kashmir Śaivist Abhinavagupta. She looks for insight to cognitive psychology, primarily Allan Allport, who points out the complex interplay of top-down and bottom-up signals of neural networks in how we 

*[p. 2]* ^pg2

attend to and behave toward objects in our environment. I shall cite provocative work done by the neurologist Marcus Raichle, whose neuroimaging studies yield startling affirmations of subjective priority. 

Late in her thesis Prueitt discerns two senses of the word “consciousness” as disparately employed in the philosophical discourse. The distinction she draws between first- and second-order consciousness mirrors the two faces of subjectivity now under consideration. To borrow from Jaideva Singh, translator of seminal KS works cited here, we have the *empirical subject* of ordinary experience whose consciousness (second-order) is generally *of objects,* and the *metempirical subject* identified with universal consciousness (first-order). (This departs, it should be pointed out, from contemporary cognitive studies in which first-order consciousness is of objects and second-order of self.) Singh further counsels a nuanced understanding of supreme cit as “being-awareness,” an admonishment to those rooted in Western traditions, perhaps, against labeling positions too readily as “idealist” or “realist,” or segregating too sharply ontic from epistemic considerations. It is instructive to see Prueiit run headlong into the limitations of certain embedded dichotomies. Conceptual/nonconceptual and intentional/nonintentional distinctions as applied to Dharmakīrti lose sharpness when viewed through the KS prism of worldly reality as a cosmic panoply sired by supreme consciousness. The understanding of vac (speech) as high-order proto-signifiers for example, and — more pertinent here — the radix of the subject-object duality and the manifesting power of consciousness as expressed through will (icchā), knowledge (jñāna) and action (kriyā), confound the assumptions and classifications that frame her preceding analysis of Yogacarin positions. By the end Prueitt resorts to dichotomies like top-down/bottom-up that prove nimbler in traversing the divide. Prueitt deftly deconstructs Dharmakīrti’s undertaking as he toggles between “external realist” and “epistemic idealist” positions. The demand to fix causal efficacy on singular existents fails to deliver in Dharmakīrti’s quest to unearth their inherent nature. Taking refuge as he does in ultimate consciousness puts conventional reality at the mercy of the unruly subject-object duality. And that puts Dharmakīrti in a pickle. An influencer of perception tainting our every experience, on the one hand, the subject-object duality cannot be a conceptual byproduct of it. As its ignoble dual nature belies essential purity and unity, on the other hand, neither can it derive from ultimate consciousness. So Dharmakīrti posits a beginningless impregnation (anadivasana) in the mindstream. With no cause of its own, it is now at liberty to operate as a free agent. But there’s a price to pay. Phenomenal experience and conventional reality, altogether unmoored from truthful grounding, are set adrift. Citing the Śaivist objection that no other cause than the primordial can itself be uncaused, Prueitt herself balks at granting the subject-object duality a mulligan, as it were, and dispatching conventional reality perhaps too 

*[p. 3]* ^pg3

unceremoniously. None of this runs counter, in any case, to the view of subjectivity as essentially bankrupt. The self remains as tenuous and ephemeral as the momentary cognition from which the subject codependently arises, sans priority or dominance, with the object. 

Prueitt then ushers in the contrasting KS position that anchors conventional reality firmly in ultimate consciousness. Here, the subject-object polarity is baked in upstream, i.e., inherently at a higher and prior stage of consciousness, duality notwithstanding, before it is deployed by the individual experient in any momentary cognition. This understanding telegraphs at once a rejection of the bifurcation of ultimate awareness and ordinary perception, an unbroken and robust top-down causality, and an inherent agentive grounding of the empirical subject. To explore these ideas in any depth requires a closer look at the sweeping holism of the KS ontology. 

Coining the phrases “nondual differentiation” and “nondual variegation,” Prueitt shorthands that feature of ultimate consciousness that subsumes diversity under the rubric of unity. The KS ontology notably features tattvas (constitutive principles) absent from other ontologies (Sāṅkhya, Yoga) at the upper end of the hierarchy commencing the gradual unfoldment from highest absolute identity (parā) to identity-in-difference (parāpara) and toward outright difference (aparā). That evolution obeys prescriptive signifier/signified (kalā/varna) polarities that play out downstream at descending stages as the polarization of consciousness into subject and object reifies. These formulas are like natural laws that operate top-down with impunity. Here ultimate nonduality does not exclude multiplicity, and the subject-object duality is but a veil drawn by consciousness (citi) itself over a supremely authored reality that, while degraded, cannot be discounted on account of differentiation. 

Prueitt chooses as her KS focus vimarśa (dissemination), or the capacity for manifestation, which she interprets with precedent as “reflexive realization.” It should not be overlooked that prakāśa (effulgence), is always given as the prior if not higher aspect of supreme consciousness — as turned inward before turning itself outward. Creation is a spontaneous expression along vectors of projection (descending) and resorption (ascending) as initiated by Śiva and effectuated by Śakti. Latent and conceived in all its variegated forms in effulgent Siva as the prakaśa aspect, its expressive power is administered by assiduous Sakti as the vimarśa aspect. That very power alternatively conceals and reveals ultimate identity as existents (prakṛti) manifest, maintain and withdraw for experients (puruṣas) inhabiting finite differentiated realms spawned by the descent of consciousness. The Spanda articulation depicts this movement as the pulsation of a compact mass of light and bliss that flashes forth (unmesa) and withdraws (nimesa) in a spontaneous eruption and retraction of consciousness, timelessly alternating phases of inherently expressive and prolific primordial being. 

*[p. 4]* ^pg4

The phasing of consciousness in repose and effusion is a seductively modern and sophisticated paradigm. Notable too is the sovereignty in the KS system of top-down causality. Constraints on worldly actors derive from forces upstream intrinsic to consciousness. Limitations on the empirical subject of agency (kalā), knowledge (vidyā) and desire (raga), etc. are descended from the unconditional forms that impel creation from the get-go. A willful and active supreme consciousness departs from the prevalent characterization of Brahman as an inert and impersonal presence, as agency discursively requires a second that entails duality. The supreme KS actor as *unconditional* agent, on the contrary, eternally fulfills a desire whose goal is never unrealized to procreate, without acting on any other thing at any time or place in order to effect any given outcome to the exclusion of any other. 

As a segue into illuminating — and I contend pertinent — scientific work, it is worth invoking here the (not exclusively) Indic predilection for continuity and parallelism between universal and particular as macro- and microcosm. Bodies, or, more faithfully, mind-bodies, are gross evolutes of prior and subtler forms, all of which operate by common mechanisms as governed by common principles. Particularly in KS, these would be not only present but determinative all the way down the line from supreme to individual consciousness. 

In his trailblazing cognitive studies, the neuro-scientist Marcus Raichle 3,4 cites the tension between bottom-up and top-down brain activity, echoing issues under examination here. Framing the historical debate, he elaborates on two opposing positions prompting his use of neuroimaging toward evaluating the relative importance of evoked and intrinsic activity. 

Functional brain imaging has followed a long tradition in neuroscience: studying neuronal responses to stimuli and activity during task performance. In this work, the role of bottom-up versus top-down (or feed-forward vs feedback) causality is frequently discussed, reflecting a debate that extends back at least a century on the relative importance of intrinsic and evoked activity in brain function \[for a comprehensive historical review, see Chapter 1 in the book by Llinas (2001)\]. “Intrinsic activity” is ongoing neural and metabolic activity that is not directly associated with subjects’ performance of a task…. 

One view, pioneered by the early work of Sherrington (1906), posits that the brain is primarily reflexive, driven by the momentary demands of the environment. The other view is that the brain’s operations are mainly intrinsic, involving the acquisition and maintenance of information for interpreting, responding to, and even predicting environmental demands, a view introduced by a disciple of Sherrington, T. Graham Brown (1914). 

The data and analysis yielded by Raichle’s studies in this area are remarkable. Most pertinent to this discussion, neuroimaging shows that the resting mind cycles spontaneously between 

*[p. 5]* ^pg5

activation of areas associated with goal-oriented task performance and those more broadly identified with an intrinsic default state. Clear patterns of coherence between these areas in the default mode network (DMN) indicate that the relationship between task-positive and baseline activity is instantiated in the resting state. Oscillation occurs continually in the absence of any evocation by an external stimulus or assigned task. The lion’s share (as much as 80%) of energy consumption is associated with networks active in the intrinsic baseline state. Additional energy expended with evoked changes is less than 5% of the total with notably localized decreases in the DMN with the introduction of a wide range of goal-oriented tasks. 

That a particular neural network is actively attuned to goal-oriented behavior even before its deployment on the introduction of a determinate stimulus suggests that: 1) the conscious subject in some sense precedes the emergent relation, and 2) that subject is predisposed intrinsically toward that relation and dynamic. The instantiation of a task-positive preset, if you will, in the unevoked default state of the subject seems clearly at odds with the view of consciousness as an emergent conceptual state arising only on contact between sense organ and sense object in that moment. Not only are other intrinsic forces at work in the absence of such external evocation, they in fact command most of the energy expended by the brain. It remains unclear how much of this may be devoted to what we would characterize as “self-awareness” or “self-consciousness,” a concept that poses obvious experimental challenges, though certain areas in the DMN are believed to be involved in such a function. That patterns of coherence exhibited are further maintained across waking, dreaming and deep sleep states provokes additional speculation for another day, as those states figure so prominently in Indic epistemology. 

At the risk of overreach, it is tempting to see in the cycling of withdrawn and externally engaged brain states a microcosm of the phasing of supreme consciousness in the KS model. There too the reposed state as primordially all-encompassing would be the likely energy hog. It is striking enough in any case that a paradigm shift in functional brain imaging should align within significant ways if not lend credence to a centuries-old philosophical system. Specifically the instantiation in the unevoked empirical consciousness of task-positive and goal-oriented predispositions attests to the priority and dominance of subjectivity, as well as a top-down radix intrinsic to consciousness of the subject-object relation. 

Let’s revisit for a moment the uncaused anadivasanas of Dharmakīrti that ferry the subject-object duality in the mindstream on a delusive current that saturates our every perception. These defilements operate top-down but with no origin of their own. Thus they are *not* intrinsic, but completely untethered, to ultimate consciousness, as anchoring them in it would likewise impute to it a spurious duality. The Śaivist cannot countenance such logic. For starters, nothing but the 

*[p. 6]* ^pg6

first cause, or supreme being, has no other cause than itself. Second, it is the source and ground of everything, not excluding erroneous impregnations that veil its absolute truth. The concealment of ultimate consciousness in its primal unity is precisely the mechanism of its own expression. So the Yogacarin cries foul at the notion of diversity in unity, and plays fast and loose with causality in order to avoid it. That cleaves conventional from ultimate reality. The Śaivist exalts the veiled and partial truths of phenomenal worlds as expressions of ultimate consciousness, whose essential fullness does not, and cannot, exclude diversity. 

Both schools would concur that no discursive proof of the ultimate is possible, or necessary. But that obviates neither a rational defense by KS pundits of its underlying subjectivity, nor a critique of adverse Buddhist tenets. These are recondite arguments that warrant deeper exposition than they receive here, framed in the context of this thesis and my own best efforts to untangle them. It would be premature in any case to wrap up this discussion without taking pains to unpack just how subjectivity serves as the essential substrate and catalyst for phenomenal reality. In his commentary on the Spanda-Kārikās, Kṣemarāja asserts that cognitions or experiences must rest inwardly in an abiding experient. 

…these cognitions or experiences abide in another which is the possessor of these states (*avasthātari*), which, in other words, is the permanent principle of the Self. They rest inwardly in that evidently i.e. with one’s Self as witness. Otherwise the interconnection (*anusandhāna*) of the ephemeral cognitions and the ideas born of the residual traces will not be possible, for those cognitions disappear as soon as they arise and cannot leave behind any traces. Thus the vikalpas (ideas) supposed to arise out of them cannot actually arise. Not being perceived in experience, they cannot lead to any activity…. <sup>5</sup> Notice that neither the fleeting nature of cognitions nor the interplay of residual traces in their formation is disputed. Indeed the thinking, feeling empirical subject (puryaṣṭaka) is profoundly susceptible to and tragically mired in existential volatility (saṃsāra). It is the indwelling metempirical subject whose immutable identity remains above the fray and provides a fixed and timeless epistemic ground for novel interactions. That puts samsara squarely and unambiguously in the wheelhouse of universal consciousness. 

In the unrelenting flux of the Buddhist mindstream no impressions formed could be sustained, recovered or connected to contribute in any way to subsequent or even concurrent cognitions, thoughts and actions. Being held in ever-identical consciousness, on the other hand, means that impressions can be recruited reliably as experienced previously. The conviction is upheld that a memory is of the same object, not a karmic semblance in the mindstream merely tinged with a previous experience, as Buddhism postulates, which could neither subsist nor operate as such. 

*[p. 7]* ^pg7

The KS understanding could hardly depart more radically from the Buddhist position. The stuff of experience inheres in universal consciousness to manifest as enlisted by its finite progeny in the configuring of any and all existential forms and cognitions. In such regard universal consciousness serves as an unwavering substrate or canvas for transient phenomena. One might call this the “grounding function” of subjectivity. 

But that, as they say, is not the half of it. For universal consciousness is not only the ground but also the profuse and inexhaustible wellspring of phenomenal reality. It is not just the canvas but also the paint and the brush that applies it. And, like the canvas, they too are nothing other than consciousness. 

It can be said here whatever inner object there is like pain, pleasure, etc., or whatever external object there is like the *puryaṣtaka*, body and senses have evidently no existence like the stage of deep sleep as long as they are not experienced. When they are experienced, then being experienced, they are of the nature of consciousness. They are simply consciousness. (Spanda-Kārikās I.5)<sup>5</sup> Kṣemarāja then cites the text Íśvarapratyabhijñā by his teacher Utpaladeva. 

The object that is made manifest is of the nature of the light of consciousness. What is not light cannot be said to exist. (Spanda-Kārikās)<sup>5</sup> Realization as powered by universal consciousness (citi) and constricted and configured in empirical consciousness (citta) enables a thing to actualize as experienced. That is how it manifests with objective significance, which is always and only *for the subject*, and tantamount to its existence. Indeed it is only *as conscient* that manifest being is at once perceptible and substantial. 

*idaṃ sarvaṃ kāryam*, therefore means, ‘In which 

*Yatra sthitam* consciousness this world consisting of subject, object and means of knowledge etc. rests’. By ‘rests’ (*sthitaṃ*) is meant by whose light it comes to light and thus obtains stance. (Spanda-Kārikās I.2)<sup>5</sup> An object, in other words, can be known only as objectified by the knower. Such objectification, as highlighted by Raichle’s work, is a subjectively prompted transaction goaded by an intrinsic nonspecific desire and triggered by an external (or internal) evocation. If the thing encountered did not undergo such assimilation internally, there would be only raw sensory input necessary but not sufficient for the grasping of the object by the knower. The object expresses in a determinate form and delimited guise after being held in awareness and rendered on the basis of collaborating dispositions and inputs. A woody grove may express as a walnut orchard, timber 

*[p. 8]* ^pg8

for a log cabin, or a shady spot for a picnic. It is empirically graspable, and *obtains stance*, only as brought to light within subjectively established parameters. 

One might call this the “illuminating function” of subjectivity. 

Consider this. Were manifestation not ultimately illuminated by first-order awareness, even if an existent could attain determinacy (with regard to space, time, etc.), which is relative, it could not attain immediacy, which is absolute, without the salient and steadfast contribution of pure unalloyed I-consciousness. The most conspicuous conviction of all awareness — that I am experiencing this here and now — remains constant regardless of when, where, of what and for whom any cognition takes place. Indeed without it the subject would be just another object. Perceptions must be focalized in empirical consciousness as “backlit” by unconditional awareness lest they rush by without palpable presence. Think of the frames of a film rolling past the lens of a projector with no light source behind. To be sure, in the contraction by and of reflexive consciousness, its luminosity is decidedly narrowed though not entirely dimmed at the stage inhabited by embodied souls. To reprise the citation at the head of this essay: 

Citi (universal consciousness) itself descending from (the stage of) Cetana (the uncontracted conscious stage) becomes Citta (individual consciousness) inasmuch as it becomes contracted (Sankocini) in conformity with the objects of consciousness (cetya). (Pratyabhijnāhṛdayam Sutra 5)<sup>1</sup> Thus the fixed and timeless vantage of the metempirical subject in grounding and illuminating objective existence is a metaphysical necessity for phenomenal experience. 

In summary, Brahmanical in contrast to certain Buddhist schools embrace as axiomatic the intrinsic necessity of ultimate consciousness as pure subjectivity, or Self. Uniquely perhaps, Kashmir Śaivism posits empirical consciousness as a contraction of metempirical consciousness, and creative proliferation as a descent and dynamic expression of supreme being. Subjectivity has two faces: the indwelling, all-seeing enjoyer and the limited experient of phenomenal reality. They are verily two and the same. Unless the finite subject and object alike were both grounded in and illuminated by the unconditional, nothing could obtain stance as manifest. The duality implicit in agency, desire, etc., and the subject-object polarity more generally, are inherent though latent in primordial oneness. They crystallize with creative unfoldment to impose determinate constraints on manifest being. Neither karmic free agents nor cognitive emergents, they are rather principles and potencies intrinsic to ultimate consciousness that govern our experience of the world. 

*[p. 9]* ^pg9

Such an understanding, finally, is utterly consonant with Hindu orthodoxy. Indeed Kashmir Śaivism upholds with metaphysical rigor and cogency the conjoined embrace of unity and diversity reprised chapter and verse throughout the canon. 

That Eternal which rests in the self should be known. Truly there is nothing beyond this to be known. By knowing the enjoyer, the object of enjoyment and the mover (of all), everything has been said…. 

 — Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad I.12 <sup>6</sup>
