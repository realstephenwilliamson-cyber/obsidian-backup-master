> [!quote] Me
> Do we have any record of who Ksmeraja's disciples were?

Very little. The only disciple who is reasonably well attested is **Yogarāja**, who wrote a commentary (*vivṛti*) on Abhinavagupta's *Paramārthasāra*. In that work he presents himself as having studied under Kṣemarāja, and most scholars, Alexis Sanderson among them, accept that as a real teacher–student link. It's still self-report in a colophon, not independent confirmation.

Beyond Yogarāja, the record is thin. Later Kashmiri Śaiva authors cite and build on Kṣemarāja, but that shows influence, not discipleship. Jayaratha, for example, commented on the *Tantrāloka* about a century later, and people sometimes loosely place him in Kṣemarāja's line. Some modern tradition-internal lineages, such as Swami Lakshmanjoo's, trace an unbroken chain back through Kṣemarāja. Those chains fill the gaps with names for which there's little or no medieval documentation, so they're better treated as lineage claims than historical evidence.

The broader reason for the silence is that Kashmiri authors of this period usually named their own teachers, often effusively, but rarely listed their students. What we know about who learned from whom comes almost entirely from the student's side, and only when the student wrote something that survived.

> [!quote] Me
> What else do we have from Yogaraja and jayaratha?

**Yogarāja** left essentially one work: his commentary (*vivṛti*) on Abhinavagupta's *Paramārthasāra*, the short verse summary of non-dual Śaiva doctrine. I don't know of any other surviving work reliably attributed to him. The commentary is valued mainly because it's clear and stays close to Abhinavagupta. It has been edited in the Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies and translated more than once, including Lilian Silburn's French version.

**Jayaratha** (12th–13th century) is much better represented:

- **Tantrālokaviveka**, his enormous commentary on Abhinavagupta's *Tantrāloka*. It's his major achievement and effectively the only complete traditional guide to that text. It's also a key source for lost scriptures, because Jayaratha quotes them extensively. At the end he adds autobiographical verses on his family and teachers, which is where most of what we know about him comes from.
- **Alaṅkāravimarśinī**, a commentary on Ruyyaka's *Alaṅkārasarvasva*, a major work of Sanskrit poetics. It's a reminder that these Kashmiri Śaiva exegetes were often also serious literary theorists.
- **Vāmakeśvarīmatavivaraṇa**, a commentary on the *Vāmakeśvarīmata*, a foundational Śrīvidyā tantra. It's usually attributed to the same Jayaratha. I'd hold that identification a bit loosely, since shared names cause real confusion in this period.

A related point: his brother **Jayadratha** wrote the *Haracaritacintāmaṇi*, a collection of Śaiva mythological narratives. The two names are easy to conflate in catalogues and secondary literature.

> [!quote] Me
> Haracaritacintāmaṇi sound interesting. Has it been translated?

As far as I can find, there's no complete English translation. The text is mostly read in Sanskrit, through summaries, or through excerpts quoted in scholarship.

**The text.** The standard edition is still Kāvyamālā no. 61, edited by Śivadatta and Parab in 1897. It's public domain, and a scan is on the Internet Archive via the Digital Library of India. One caution: its title page attributes the work to "Rājānaka Jayaratha, son of Śṛṅgāratha", which is the source of the brothers' names getting mixed up in catalogues. Modern scholarship gives it to Jayadratha. Also, the surviving text breaks off partway through the 32nd story, though the author's own outline suggests there were originally 37.

**Closest thing to a translation.** Kanta Gupta's 1976 article in *Purāṇa* surveys the contents, summarizing each story and pointing to the Purāṇic chapters with the same or related tales. That's the best short guide to what's in the work.

**Specialist studies.** Maho Shibazaki has published pieces on individual stories, including the Jīmūtavāhana and Guṇāḍhya episodes and the role of goddesses in the work. Ryutaro Tsuchida wrote remarks on the problems of the Kāvyamālā edition. Alexis Sanderson has translated individual passages in his work on Kashmiri sacred sites.

**In progress.** Judit Törzsök is preparing a new edition, which she has read in seminar with the Shivadharma Project. That's the most likely route to a proper critical text, and possibly a translation eventually. I couldn't confirm a publication date.

Yes, but on his hymns, not his philosophy. Kṣemarāja wrote the commentary (*vivṛti*) on Utpaladeva's **Śivastotrāvalī**, the collection of devotional hymns to Śiva, and it's the standard commentary on that text.

Kṣemarāja's **Pratyabhijñāhṛdaya** is sometimes loosely associated with it, but it's his own short digest of Recognition doctrine, not a commentary on Utpaladeva's text.

> [!quote] Me
> I thought Pratyabhijñāhṛdaya was an attempt to make Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā legible without the technical philosophy

That's a fair description, and it's close to how Kṣemarāja himself frames it. In the opening verses he says he's writing for people who lack training in the hard discipline of logic (*tarka*) but are nonetheless receptive to the grace (*śaktipāta*) that makes recognition possible. So the aim is to deliver the Pratyabhijñā teaching without the argumentative apparatus of Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta.

I'd refine it in two ways:

- **It doesn't track the Kārikā.** It's 20 sūtras with Kṣemarāja's own commentary, organized around his own sequence of points rather than Utpaladeva's structure. Much of the Kārikā is epistemology in debate with Buddhist and Nyāya opponents, and that is mostly just dropped, not simplified.
- **It shifts the emphasis.** Kṣemarāja brings in a great deal from the Spanda and Krama traditions, such as the cosmic cycle of the five acts and the contraction and expansion of consciousness, and he points toward practice. Recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) remains the frame, but the book is as much a synthesis of the non-dual Śaiva tradition as a digest of Utpaladeva.

So "Pratyabhijñā without the philosophy" is right as to intent. As to content, it's closer to a Kṣemarāja-flavored summary of the whole non-dual system, under the Recognition banner.

> [!quote] Me
> Please give me a brief summary of the differences between a vrtti, a vivrri, and a vimarsini (and any other type not on the list )

These are conventional title words, not rigid genres, so authors use them loosely. Broadly:

- **Vṛtti**: a brief gloss that explains the sense of the root verses or sūtras without much elaboration. It's often the author's own, as with Utpaladeva's *vṛtti* on his *Kārikā*.
- **Vivṛti** (or *vivaraṇa*): literally an "unfolding," a fuller exposition that develops arguments and implications. It too can be the author's own: Utpaladeva wrote a long *vivṛti* on his own *Kārikā* after the short *vṛtti*.
- **Vimarśinī**: a "reflective examination." In Kashmir Śaivism the word carries doctrinal weight, since *vimarśa* is consciousness's reflexive self-awareness, so the title signals interpretation as an act of that awareness. Examples include Abhinavagupta's *Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī* and Kṣemarāja's *Śivasūtravimarśinī*.

Other common types:

- **Bhāṣya**: a major, authoritative commentary that argues in its own right, like Śaṅkara on the *Brahmasūtra*.
- **Vārttika**: critical supplementary remarks. The classic definition is examining what the text said, left unsaid, or said badly. Bhāskara's *Śivasūtravārttika* is an example.
- **Ṭīkā**: a generic word for a commentary, often a sub-commentary on another commentary.
- **Pañjikā** and **ṭippaṇī**: word-by-word or difficult-point notes, and brief marginal notes.
- **Vyākhyā**: a generic word for an exposition.

Kashmiri authors also favored evocative titles over genre labels. Kṣemarāja's *Uddyota* means "illumination," Jayaratha's *Viveka* means "discernment," and Kṣemarāja's *Spandanirṇaya* means "ascertainment." Commentary also stacks: Abhinavagupta's *Īśvarapratyabhijñāvivṛtivimarśinī* is a *vimarśinī* on Utpaladeva's *vivṛti* on Utpaladeva's own verses.

> [!quote] Me
> Does an author call his commentary bhasya or does the tradition? Did Shankara know he was writing a bhasya or did he think he was just doing vyakhya?

Both, and the two often can't be told apart.

**Usually the author chooses the label.** Titles are typically announced in the opening verses or a closing verse, and Kashmiri authors did this deliberately, as with Kṣemarāja's *Uddyota* or Jayaratha's *Viveka*. But colophons, the "thus ends such-and-such" lines, were often added or standardized by scribes. Honorifics like "the revered Śaṅkara-bhagavatpāda" in a colophon are a giveaway that someone other than the author wrote it. So a title can be traditional even when it looks authorial.

**Śaṅkara specifically.** I don't know of a passage where he names his own work a *bhāṣya*, so I can't say he titled it himself. But he'd certainly have known he was working in that mode. *Bhāṣya* was an established genre centuries before him: Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* on grammar, Śabara on the *Mīmāṃsāsūtra*, Vātsyāyana on the *Nyāyasūtra*. A full-scale commentary on an aphoristic sūtra text, one that argues against rival schools, is what a *bhāṣya* was. His own disciple Padmapāda, in the *Pañcapādikā*, already treats the work as the *bhāṣya*, so the label is attested within his circle, not only centuries later.

**The either/or doesn't quite hold.** *Vyākhyā* is the generic word for exposition, and a *bhāṣya* is a kind of *vyākhyā*. A traditional verse defines a *bhāṣya* as a work that explains the sūtra's meaning in words that follow the sūtra, and also explains its own words. That fits Śaṅkara exactly. The label also spread beyond sūtra texts: his Upaniṣad and *Gītā* commentaries are called *bhāṣya*s too, which suggests the term had come to mark authority and scope rather than strict form.

One further wrinkle: which works are Śaṅkara's at all is partly a traditional sorting. Scholars like Hacker and Mayeda have trimmed the attributed corpus considerably, so "Śaṅkara's *bhāṣya*s" is itself partly a tradition-made category.

> [!quote] Me
> Was the word just not popular in Kashmir or did authors like Abhinavagupta think they were doing something different? The [[Hub - Tantraloka|tantraloka]] could be the Malinijoyotrabhasya

Your instinct about the Tantrāloka is half right, and the half that's wrong is revealing.

**The right half.** Abhinavagupta does present the Tantrāloka as an exposition of the Mālinīvijayottara. Near the opening he says that no scripture surpasses it and that its teaching is what he will set out. In intent, the Tantrāloka is built on the Mālinīvijayottara.

**The wrong half.** In form it isn't a commentary on that tantra at all. It doesn't follow its sequence or gloss its verses. It reorganizes the whole Trika system and draws on many other scriptures, including Kula and Krama material the Mālinīvijayottara doesn't contain. The title marks this: *Tantrāloka* means "light on the tantras," plural. It's a synthesis that claims one scripture as its foundation, not an explanation of that scripture.

And Abhinavagupta clearly knew the difference, because he also wrote a real commentary on the Mālinīvijayottara, the **Mālinīvijayavārttika**. It works through the text closely, covering only the first verse(s) at great length. So he used a genre label when he was doing the line-by-line job, and he picked *vārttika*, not *bhāṣya*.

As for why *bhāṣya* seems absent in Kashmiri Śaiva works, I can offer likely reasons, but this is inference, not something the authors state:

- **The term's associations.** *Bhāṣya* was tied to sūtra texts and the orthodox Brahmanical disciplines. Tantras are revealed scripture in verse, and the Śaiva authors may not have seen the sūtra-commentary model as the natural fit.
- **Taste.** Kashmiri authors across fields preferred evocative titles. Abhinavagupta's commentary on Ānandavardhana's *Dhvanyāloka* is the *Locana*, "the eye," not a *bhāṣya*.
- **What the major works were.** The Tantrāloka, and Kṣemarāja's Pratyabhijñāhṛdaya, are independent syntheses rather than commentaries, so no commentary label fit them anyway.

Kashmir wasn't hostile to the genre as such. Kaiyaṭa, a Kashmiri grammarian, wrote the *Pradīpa*, a major commentary on Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya*. The avoidance seems to be a matter of fit and style, not rejection.

> [!quote] Me
> This is a randow question. Has there been a serious attempt to translate the Bible into Sanskrit?

Yes, a serious and sustained one, from the Baptist missionaries at Serampore and Calcutta in the 19th century.

**William Carey** started it. He believed a Sanskrit Bible would be the ideal medium for reaching India's learned classes, and that it could serve as a base for translations into the vernaculars. His New Testament appeared in 1808, with the Old Testament following between 1811 and 1818.

**Revisions** followed for most of the century. William Yates, trained under Carey, published a revised New Testament in 1841; John Wenger revised it again in 1851, and a third revision in 1886 rephrased many verses. The Calcutta mission also published the Old Testament in four volumes between 1848 and 1872, largely Wenger's work. The 1851 Wenger New Testament is generally considered the best in literary quality. The Bible Society of India still prints the 1886 version, and the old volumes are scanned on the Internet Archive. Missionary interest in Sanskrit faded by the end of the century, and no major new effort replaced these.

The most interesting scholarship on it concerns vocabulary, meaning how the translators rendered God, spirit, sin, grace and so on in a language already saturated with Hindu theological meaning. Manuel Pasi's Italian PhD thesis traces how the major Christian terms evolved across the editions and how different missionaries used distinct translation strategies, shaping what's been called "Church Sanskrit".

> [!quote] Me
> It would be an interesting text to practice my translation skills on

It's well suited to that, with one caveat.

The advantage is that you know the source text thoroughly, so the Bible works like a built-in answer key. You can focus on the Sanskrit itself, the syntax, compounds and word choices, without also guessing the meaning. Watching where the translators struggled is instructive too, like how they handle Greek participle chains or concepts with no clean Sanskrit equivalent.

The caveat is that this is translation Sanskrit. It's correct but often shaped by Greek and English idiom, so it won't train your ear for classical prose the way Kṣemarāja or Abhinavagupta will. Treat it as a comprehension and terminology exercise rather than a style model.

Practically, sanskritbible.in has the 1851 Wenger New Testament encoded as searchable Unicode text in several scripts, which is much easier to work with than the old scans. John would be a good place to start, especially the prologue: seeing how Wenger rendered *logos* and the light–darkness language, in a tradition where those words already carry Vedāntic and Śaiva weight, makes a good translation problem in its own right.

> [!quote] Me
> Obviously as an anti supersessionist I find that trying to convert Jews is especially wrong but it seems like having the Gospels in Hebrew would have occurred to the early Christians too?

It did occur to them, or at least the early church believed it had. The evidence is real but thin and contested.

**The tradition of a Hebrew Matthew.** Papias, writing in the early 2nd century (quoted by Eusebius), says Matthew compiled the sayings "in the Hebrew dialect." Irenaeus, Origen, and Jerome repeat the claim that Matthew wrote first in Hebrew. The problem is that canonical Matthew shows every sign of being composed in Greek. It uses Mark's Greek text closely, so it isn't a translation. Scholars still debate whether Papias meant Hebrew, Aramaic, or a Semitic style of composition, or whether he was simply mistaken.

**Jewish-Christian gospels.** The Church Fathers mention gospels used by Jewish-Christian groups, conventionally called the Gospel of the Hebrews, of the Nazarenes, and of the Ebionites. They survive only in quotations. Jerome claims to have seen and translated a Hebrew or Aramaic gospel used by the Nazarenes, though his claims are hard to verify and he may have been embellishing. These texts did exist in some form, and at least some were in a Semitic language.

**Why Greek won anyway.** Even Jewish writers in the diaspora wrote in Greek; their scripture was largely the Septuagint. Within a generation or two, the movement's center of gravity was gentile and Greek-speaking, and the Jewish-Christian communities that might have kept a Hebrew gospel alive dwindled.

**The Semitic route that did survive** was Syriac, a dialect of Aramaic. Tatian's *Diatessaron* (c. 170), the Old Syriac Gospels, and the Peshitta give an early gospel tradition in a language close to what Jesus spoke, but it's not Hebrew. After that, Hebrew versions reappear only in the Middle Ages, in polemic like Shem Tov's.

On your point about conversion: the earliest phenomenon was different in kind from the 19th-century mission. It was Jewish followers of Jesus keeping texts in their own language for their own communities, not outsiders translating in order to proselytize.

Your phrase "gladness and singleness of heart" is itself from [[Acts 02#^2-46|Acts 2:46]], the description of the earliest Jerusalem community. So the earliest portrait we have does claim exactly that. The fair objection is that Acts was written a generation or two later and idealizes, but it's not the priest's invention.

I'd push back on two parts of the "scared sheep" picture:

- **The fear is mostly pre-resurrection.** The disciples scatter at the arrest and hide behind locked doors in [[John 20#^20-1|John 20]]. The post-Easter stories turn on the opposite: *parrhēsia*, boldness of speech, is a keyword in Acts.

Where you have a real point is judgment. The earliest preaching was apocalyptic and did include warning: Paul reminds the Thessalonians that Jesus rescues from the coming wrath, and in Acts 17 he tells the Athenians God has fixed a day to judge the world. So "we're not here to tell you you're going to hell" is a modern softening in one respect. But what they warned about was imminent judgment on this age, not the later picture of hell as individual eternal punishment. The joy and the urgency coexisted: people who believed the end had already begun were both glad and in a hurry.

> I was thinking about how the Good Shepherd is the earliest Christian motif and isn't there evidence that early Christians referred to themselves as slaves of God?

Yes on both, and the second corrects something I implied.

**The Good Shepherd** is among the very earliest Christian images, alongside the fish, the orant (the praying figure with raised arms), and Jonah. It appears in the Roman catacombs and in the house church at Dura-Europos around 240, where it's on the wall of the baptistery. Two qualifications: the image adapts an existing pagan type, the ram-bearer, which already signified care and piety. And the Christian stress is on rescue, the lost sheep carried home, more than on fear. But "sheep among wolves" ([[Matthew 10#^10-16|Matthew 10:16]]) is also in the tradition, so vulnerability is part of it.

**Slaves of God:** I made it sound like Paul's apostolic honorific, but it was a self-description for believers generally:

- [[1 Peter 02#^2-16|1 Peter 2:16]] tells readers to live as free people, yet as slaves of God, using exactly the phrase *douloi theou*.
- Paul says whoever was free when called is Christ's slave ([[1 Corinthians 07#^7-22|1 Corinthians 7:22]]), and speaks of believers as enslaved to God ([[Romans 06#^6-22|Romans 6:22]]).
- Revelation opens by addressing God's *douloi*, meaning the churches.
- The *Shepherd of Hermas*, in the 2nd century, uses "servants of God" constantly as the ordinary word for Christians.

What cuts against the "scared" reading is that the term is almost always paired with freedom. 1 Peter joins them in one sentence, and [[1 Corinthians 07#^7-22|1 Corinthians 7:22]] continues that the slave who is called is the Lord's freedman. It's a paradox of being owned by God and therefore free from every other master. It also had a lived edge: many early Christians were literal slaves, so the language carried both humility and a claim that no earthly owner had the final word over them.

So your picture holds up better than I allowed. The early self-image was lowly and dependent, but it was framed as belonging, not only as fear.
