### tad, etad, idam, and adas

Also known as: demonstrative pronouns

The four pronouns tad, etad, idam, and adas have similar meanings. In theory, here is how they differ:

e We use etad (“this”) for what is near at hand e We use idam (“this”) for what is slightly further away.

e We use adas (“that”) for what is much further away.

e We use tad (“that”) for what is not present.

### kim and yad

Also known as: interrogative and relative pronouns

The two pronouns kim and yad let us create different kinds of complex sentences. In this lesson, we will learn more about what kinds of endings these pronouns use.

If you want to focus more on what these pronouns mean and less on their

specific endings, see our Sentences topic.

kim

kim means “who?” or “what?” We use kim to ask questions:

kas tvam.

Who are you?

\+ \` + (aN

kasmāt rāvaṇah sītām icchati.

For what reason (from what) does Ravana want Sita?

-cit and -cana

We can use the suffixes -cit and cana to create a word that means “someone” or

“something”:

kah prcchati — kaścit prcchati

Who is asking? —> Someone is asking.

\` \`

kena śrutam — kenacana śrutam By whom was it heard? — By someone it was heard.

yad

yad means “who” or “what,” but not in the sense of asking a question. Rather,

yad lets us join sentences together.

This idea is difficult to explain but easy to understand. In the examples below, the first two sentences are simple. Then we use yad to connect them.

sã prcchati.

She asks.

sā rāmasya patnī.

She is Rama's wife.

yā prcchati sā rāmasya patnī.

Who asks, she is Rama's wife. (literal translation)

The person who asks is Rama's wife. (natural translation) (Note that we

pair yad with another pronoun here.)

We can create more complex examples too:

कः पृच्छति -> कश्चित् पृच्छति

केन श्रुतम् -> केनचन श्रुतम्

पृच्छति a

सा पृच्छति।

सा रामस्य पली।

या पृच्छति सा रामस्य पली।

*[p. 217]* ^pg217

rāmah tasyai phalam dadāti.

Rama gives a fruit to her.

sā rāmasya patnī.

She is Rama's wife.

yasyai rāmah phalam dadāti sā rāmasya patnī To whom Rama gives a fruit, she is Rama's wife. (literal)

The person to whom Rama gives a fruit is Rama's wife. (natural)

And even more complex examples:

rāmah tasyai phalam dadāti.

Rama gives a fruit to her.

tasyāh pitā janakah

Her father is Janaka.

\`A + AN A

yasyai rāmah phalam dadāti tasyāh pitā janakah

To whom Rama gives a fruit, her father is Janaka. (literal)

The father of the one to whom Rama gives a fruit is Janaka. (natural)

### Pronominal adjectives

There are many adjectives that use the same endings as pronouns like tad. We can call these adjectives pronominal adjectives since they are adjectives that behave like pronouns.

Here are some common examples:

c a (aS

pūrva —> pūrvasmin dine

previous —> on the previous day

para —> parasmin dine next —> on the next day

eka — ekasmin dine one —> on a certain day

anya —> anyasmin dine other —> on another day

sarva —> sarve narāh all — all men

viśve — viśve devāh all — all gods

### Number words

Number words are adjectives that imply a specific number of items:

pañca pāṇdavāh

the five sons of Pandu

Sanskrit's number words generally follow the patterns of the stems we have seen already. Many of these number words are the same regardless of the gender of the noun they describe:

q4 quaa:

pañca pāṇdavāh

the five sons of Pandu (masculine)

paṛñca phalāni

five fruits (neuter)

pañca nadyah

five rivers (feminine)

In this lesson, we'll learn about Sanskrit's number words and how to use them.

eka

eka (“one”) uses the normal pronoun endings. When eka refers to one item, it uses the singular. But eka can also be used in the sense of “a small number” or “a few” of something. When eka has this meaning, it is used in the dual and plural.
