Infamous Speeches: A Textual Analysis on *Atlas Shrugged*

As a student of philosophy (and something of a liberal) *Atlas Shrugged* offers a plethora of material that in some way or another seems to beg for comment and/or analysis at best and outrage and/or condemnation at worst. It is, therefore, difficult to narrow down a subject for the sake of a single commentary. There are three specific speeches, however, that do stick out in my memory as I would imagine they do in the memories of most readers; one from each of Hugh Akston’s star pupils at Patrick Henry University (one has to love Rand’s not-so-subtle naming schemes!) The first is perhaps the best known section of the book, namely John Galt’s radio address. The second and slightly less well known is Francisco D’Anconia’s diatribe on the cliché that money is the root of all evil and the third is Ragnar Danneskjöld’s anti-Robin Hood exchange with Hank Rearden (though compared to his classmates Ragnar is a man of few words, I guess that’s why he got to be the pirate!) I will take a closer look at each of these speeches and some of the most apparent critiques as they appear to me.

This is John Galt Speaking

Rand told an interviewer in 1961 that although she knew it would be “the hardest chapter in the book” she expected the entirety of Galt’s speech to take only around three months to pen. In fact it would take more than two years to get the 70-90 page monolog (depending on the edition) exactly how she wanted it and every subsequent piece of writing or public appearance she would make would turn out to be merely exegesis of the address. She was so passionate about its content that Albert Ruddy, producer of “The Godfather,” decided not to do the movie because of her refusal to budge on script control citing this particular scene (I guess a speech of that magnitude just doesn’t lend itself to cinema!) Let’s take a look at some of Galt’s salient points.

Galt begins by calling the present state of the world a “moral crisis” that has at its root the evil doctrine of sacrifice and can only be cured by the acceptance of a new morality. This new morality is not to be based on anything as foolish as the “mystical or \[the\] social” but rather on the fundamental value of life. Here I cannot help but wonder if the right-wingers that gush about this novel really understand what Galt is saying. They must not realize that the “self-immolation” and “creed of unearned rewards and unrewarded duties” he is calling evil are in fact the alleged tenets of the faith that binds the religious right together making it a political force.

Throughout the rest of the rant Galt explains how all the people in the Gulch are better than the people left in the world at large, that it is they who possess reason, knowledge, productivity, creativity etc. He justifies this opinion with another assault on the mystical elements within society saying that they denounce such qualities as evil. I think this is an instance of the straw man fallacy. I mean sure the doctrine of original sin (that Galt is preaching against) heightens the awareness of such dangers inherent in man’s mental capacities but that is not the same as outright condemnation of them as Galt seems to imply. It frightens me that there is an actual Galt’s Gulch in Chile that claims to be built around Rand’s principles.

There is one (small) part of Galt’s speech that regardless of my opposition to Rand’s politics and in spite of how I feel about her economics the philosopher in me cannot help but love and that is the supremacy accorded to the mind, thinking, and free choice. Rand writes:

Do not lose your knowledge that man's proper estate is an upright posture, an intransigent mind and a step that travels unlimited roads. Do not let your fire go out, spark by irreplaceable spark, in the hopeless swamps of the approximate, the not-quite, the not-yet, the not-at-all.

Do not let the hero in your soul perish, in lonely frustration for the life you deserved, but have never been able to reach. Check your road and the nature of your battle. The world you desired can be won, it exists, it is real, it is possible, it is yours.

Not Just a Playboy

> Next I would like to discuss a slightly less well known speech from the novel, that of Francisco regarding money. I remember it being mentioned in class but I don’t think we looked at it in depth by any means. The speech is given at one of the Rearden’s social functions, there anniversary I think. D’Anconia has made a spectacle and is about to leave when he overhears Bertram Scudder comment that he (Francisco) is a typical product of money and that money is the root of all evil. With a smile (and, I like to imagine, a drunken stagger) Francisco asks if they have ever wondered what the root of money is. He then goes on to set forth such a romanticized view of currency that if truly held by Rand the extremities of her philosophy not only make sense but also seem tame in comparison.
>
> The speech here touting the divine like status of currency reminds me of the Cowardly Lion’s speech about courage. It is not, however, the positive things that Francisco has to say about money that stands out in my mind but rather the warning about what the looters are doing to it. Rand writes:
>
> Whenever destroyers appear among men, they start by destroying money, for money is men's protection and the base of a moral existence. Destroyers seize gold and leave to its owners a counterfeit pile of paper. This kills all objective standards and delivers men into the arbitrary power of an arbitrary setter of values. Gold was an objective value, an equivalent of wealth produced. Paper is a mortgage on wealth that does not exist, backed by a gun aimed at those who are expected to produce it. Paper is a check drawn by legal looters upon an account which is not theirs: upon the virtue of the victims. Watch for the day when it bounces, marked, 'Account overdrawn.'
>
> Many prominent right wing politicians have also cited these 23 paragraphs as a source of some of their feelings on monetary policy, most notably Paul Ryan. This is amusing for a variety of reasons not the least of which is the fact that not only is it overtly calling for a gold standard but also it covertly suggests a return to a coinage based system rather than a note one. As *New York Times* Op-Ed columnist Paul Krugman has pointed out this would be a step back not one but two hundred years. (According to the *Historical Statistics of the United States* as of 1813 there were \$7 million worth of coins and \$52 million worth of bank notes circulating in the American economy!)
>
> Rob the Poor and Give to the Rich
>
> And last but not least we come to philosopher pirate (a job I must admit I am jealous of!) Ragnar Danneskjold. He tells Hank, "I'm after a man whom I want to destroy…He died many centuries ago, but until the last trace of him is wiped out of men's minds, we will not have a decent world to live in." The man is of course Robin Hood, whose myth for Rand seems to be the epitome of evil. She writes:
>
> …that he fought the looting rulers and returned the loot that had been robbed, but that is not the meaning of the legend which has survived. He is remembered, not as a champion of property, but as a champion of need, not as a defender of the robbed, but as a provider of the poor. He is held to be the first man who assumed a halo of virtue by practicing charity with wealth which he did not own, by giving away goods which he had not produced. . . . He is the man who became the symbol of the idea that need, not achievement, is the source of rights, that we don't have to produce, only to want, that the earned does not belong to us, but the unearned does.
>
> In this scene we once again see Rand’s glorification of the gold standard with the bar of gold Ragnar gives Hank as a not so subtle symbol of justice. In a later essay entitled "Egalitarianism and Inflation," she calls the government's paper money "a claim check on actually existing goods" saying that unbacked by gold or goods "is backed by nothing."
>
> In spite of all the rhetoric contained in the speeches I have discussed, *Atlas Shrugged* remains for me one of the greatest American love stories. Where it promotes hard work, competence, responsibility, and ingenuity I wholeheartedly agree with it. The world could not survive, however, if the selfishness it reifies and glorifies were to actually be practiced on the scale that Rand suggests. As Thoreau wrote of education “if all men were learned the world would perish” so is true for industrial success.
