## About

Alan Turing's 1950 paper on whether machines can think. It replaces that question with a test, the "imitation game," now known as the Turing test.

- **Publication:** *Mind*, vol. LIX, no. 236 (October 1950), pp. 433–460. It opens: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'"
- **The imitation game:** in the original party game, an interrogator tries by written questions alone to tell a man from a woman. Turing puts a machine in one of the places; if the judge cannot reliably tell machine from human, the machine succeeds.
- **Digital computers:** Turing limits the question to digital computers, which already existed and are universal, and asks whether one with adequate storage, suitable speed and an appropriate program could play the game well.
- **Prediction:** the SEP reports his forecast that in about fifty years machines with a storage capacity of about 10⁹ would play so well that an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 percent chance of correct identification after five minutes. Most commentators judge the forecast wrong; Copeland disputes this.
- **Objections:** he answers nine: theological; "heads in the sand"; mathematical (Gödel); consciousness; various disabilities; Lady Lovelace's (machines cannot originate anything); continuity of the nervous system; informality of behaviour; and extra-sensory perception.
- **Learning machines:** rather than program an adult mind, he proposes building a "child machine" and educating it, comparing the process to evolution.
- **Why it matters:** Wikipedia calls it the first paper to introduce the Turing test to the general public, and says the test has become an important concept in the philosophy of artificial intelligence. Well-known responses include Searle's Chinese Room and Block's "Blockhead" (SEP).
