> [!quote] Me
> One thing that I think I may be on mixed registers about is how much I am doing my own interpretation and how much I trying to avoid all sorts of misinterpretation. I am very interested in presenting the history of the exegesis and giving readings that support my own, but there are definitely some I am acknowledging as imposed or bought with me.

The mixed register may not be a defect so much as three different activities that carry different burdens, currently sharing a voice. Reporting the history of exegesis has to be accurate. Arguing that a reading is available in the Greek has to survive a concordance. Saying what you see standing in front of the text has to be coherent and disclosed — but it doesn't owe anyone philology. The failure mode isn't imposition. It's the third dressed as the second.

So the axis isn't found versus imposed, it's avowed versus unavowed. "I bring Abhinavagupta to this and here's what it shows" is honest and evaluable. What smuggles is the reading that arrives wearing exegetical clothes. And you already caught yourself doing the honest version — the ἐπώρωσεν/Pharaoh association, where the answer was that the connection is structural and real but can't be stated as verbal, and if you imply otherwise someone will catch it. That's the whole discipline in one move.

A test you might run, parallel to the Creed criterion: **would I still hold this if the philology went the other way?** If yes, it's constructive and should be marked as yours. If no, it stands or falls on evidence and belongs in the argumentative register. The 10:34 reading and the χωρέω thread are probably the second kind. The *apūrṇakhyāti* frame is almost certainly the first, and it loses nothing by saying so — a frame doesn't claim to be in the text, it claims to make the text legible.

Worth asking whether the ma'amar form can carry the distinction structurally rather than by announcement. The spiral already moves from difficulty through the tradition's readings to a transformed one; the arrival is *supposed* to be yours.

> [!quote] Me
> Shifting gears.
> Where do the other books of the New Testament reference John specifically (where it's clearly referring to John) or do they?

Short answer: nowhere clearly, and that's not a fact about John in particular. No New Testament book cites any Gospel by name or with a quotation formula. The one arguable exception runs the other way — [[1 Timothy 06#^6-18|1 Timothy 6:18]] calls "the laborer deserves his wages" γραφή, matching [[Luke 10#^10-7|Luke 10:7]]. Gospels weren't yet being quoted as scripture inside the period the NT covers.

The candidates, strongest first:

**The Johannine Epistles.** [[1 John 01#^1-1|1 John 1:1]] opens against [[John 01#^1-1|John 1:1]]; [[John 02#^2-7|2:7–8]]'s old-yet-new commandment against 13:34; 5:6's water and blood against 19:34; and 2:1 calls Jesus παράκλητος, which reads as the counterpart to the *other* Paraclete of 14:16. But there's no citation anywhere, and the direction is genuinely contested — Strecker and others put the epistles first. Shared idiom from one milieu explains the data as well as dependence does.

**[[2 Peter 01#^1-14|2 Peter 1:14]].** Peter says the Lord made clear to him that he would soon put off his tent. The only extant form of that tradition is [[John 21#^21-18|John 21:18–19]]. No verbal overlap, but it presupposes the content. Bauckham takes this seriously; it may be the closest thing you'll get.

**[[1 Timothy 06#^6-13|1 Timothy 6:13]].** Jesus testified the good confession before Pilate. The Synoptic Jesus is nearly silent there; the Johannine one says he came to bear witness to the truth. Suggestive, but "good confession" is confessional formula language and could reflect a shared passion tradition rather than the text.

**Revelation.** Shares ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ (19:13) and, more interestingly for you, [[Revelation 01#^1-7|Rev 1:7]] and [[John 19#^19-37|John 19:37]] both render [[Zechariah 12#^12-10|Zechariah 12:10]] with ἐξεκέντησαν against the LXX's κατωρχήσαντο. That's an agreement in a non-Septuagintal reading — but it points to a shared testimonium collection, not to Revelation reading John. Note also that the Lamb is ἀρνίον in Revelation and ἀμνός in John.

**Luke.** Bethany family, the anointing, Peter at the tomb, the servant's right ear, the catch of fish. Real overlap, no citation, and the direction of dependence has been argued every way.

The first unambiguous external use is second-century and mostly heterodox before it's orthodox — Heracleon's commentary, which survives only in Origen, is the earliest known commentary on any Gospel.

> [!quote] Me
> I am pretty sure Peter and Timothy are the letter not considered to be Paul's but written after his death so I usually avoid citing them. Perhaps this is a mistake considering they are canon.

One correction first: 2 Peter was never attributed to Paul — the question there is whether Peter wrote it. It's the most widely doubted book in the canon, usually dated 100–150, partly because 3:15–16 already treats a collection of Paul's letters as scripture. The Pastorals are the disputed *Pauline* ones, majority-pseudonymous at roughly 90–130, though with more serious defenders than 2 Peter has.

But for the question you actually asked, pseudonymity is an argument *for* citing them, not against. If 2 Peter is genuinely Petrine and early, its knowledge of a Johannine tradition about Peter's death is hard to explain and probably coincidental. If it's from 110, it sits exactly where a text that knows [[John 21#^21-1|John 21]] should sit. Late dating makes it evidence of reception rather than evidence of authorship — which is what you want it for.

The general habit is worth revisiting on its own terms. Avoiding a text because it's pseudonymous makes sense when the argument depends on whose mind produced it. Almost nothing in your project does. You're tracking how the Gospel was read and what vocabulary was circulating, and a document from 110 is a better witness to that than one from 55. It also costs you Ephesians and Colossians, which are deutero-Pauline on most reckonings and contain material you'd otherwise want.

> [!quote] Me
> I think my aversion comes from studying so much Jewish literature, where pains are taken to hold an opinion in someone's name. Not that pseudonymous attribution doesn't exist

That's a real formation effect, and worth naming in the book rather than just correcting for, because the two systems have genuinely different economies of attribution.

*Amar Rabbi Yochanan* is load-bearing. The chain matters, the *setam* is marked as unattributed, and Avot 6:6 makes saying a thing in the name of its speaker a mechanism of redemption. Attribution is doing halakhic and structural work — you need to know who held what to know how the dispute resolves.

The pseudepigraphic tradition is doing something else. Zohar-as-Rashbi, the Enoch material, the Hekhalot texts — attribution there is a claim about lineage and register, not about a person's testimony, and the tradition has largely known this without it being disqualifying. Christian pseudonymity works more like that end: a claim to stand in a stream, made in a world where authorship wasn't a property right.

The asymmetry that probably drives your instinct is this: in the rabbinic system attribution is falsifiable-ish and therefore meaningful, whereas the NT canon fixes the attributions in place where they can't be revised. So misattribution feels like it corrupts something. But that only matters if the argument runs *through* the author. Yours runs through the text and its reception.

There may be something usable here for your own method, though. The rabbinic move when attribution is uncertain isn't suppression — it's *itmar* or *ika de-amrei*, marking the doubt and quoting anyway. That's the register you want: cite 2 Peter, footnote that it's late and probably not Petrine, and let the lateness do the work. Which is roughly what you already do with the Ishbitzer when you distinguish what he says from what you're extending.

> [!quote] Me
> It also brings to mind when the Talmud cites a 'brisa' or however it is spelled.

*Baraita* — בָּרַיְיתָא, Aramaic for "outside," a tannaitic tradition left outside Rabbi Yehudah ha-Nasi's Mishnah. Plural *baraitot*. Introduced by *tanya*, *tanu rabbanan*, *tani*, or *tenan hatam*, and the marker matters: the Gemara can't emend the Mishnah, but a baraita's authority is contestable, so a *stam* baraita gets pressed on in ways the Mishnah doesn't.

The analogy holds better than you might expect, and in a useful direction. A baraita is authoritative *because* of its tannaitic period and register, not because anyone can verify who taught it — many are anonymous or attributed by convention. Some almost certainly aren't as early as their form implies; the Gemara occasionally suspects this. And the collections themselves — Tosefta, the halakhic midrashim — carry attributions in exactly the way Ephesians does: real tradition, uncertain hand.

Where it diverges is that a baraita's outside-ness is *marked*. *Tanya* tells you what kind of thing is coming and what you may do with it. Canon does the opposite — it flattens 1 Thessalonians and 2 Peter into one shelf with no formula distinguishing them, so the sorting has to happen in the reader's head or in a footnote. That's arguably what critical scholarship reinvented: a way of restoring the *tanya* that the canon suppressed.

For the book, that's a footnote-shaped observation rather than a body one. But it's a clean way of explaining why you cite a late text without pretending it's early — you're supplying the marker the canon doesn't.

> [!quote] Me
> Actually a relationship that is closer to home but the pastoral epistles are just on the wrong side of the line. That's the apocrapha and cannon distinction in the Articles of Faith

That's Article VI, and the analogy is closer than the baraita one because it's *your* tradition drawing the line — but it's worth being precise about what Article VI actually does, since it cuts against you slightly.

The Article grants the deuterocanonical books a real status: read for example of life and instruction of manners, not applied to establish any doctrine. That's a marked category, exactly the *tanya* function. But it's not primarily an authorship judgment — it's a canonicity judgment inherited from Jerome's *hebraica veritas*, about which books had a Hebrew original and standing in the Jewish canon. Sirach has a named author who almost certainly wrote it. Wisdom is pseudonymously Solomonic and gets the same treatment. Authorship isn't the sorting criterion.

Which is why the Pastorals aren't on the wrong side of that line — they're inside the canon, and Article VI takes canonical books on the Church's authority rather than on critical grounds. So if you're looking for warrant to demote them, this Article won't give it. What it gives you is something more useful: a precedent within your own confession for a *tiered* citation practice. Some texts establish doctrine, some illuminate without establishing.

You could use that structure honestly. Cite the Pastorals and 2 Peter freely as witnesses to how the tradition was reading and speaking, and decline to rest a doctrinal claim on them alone. That's Article VI's logic applied one shelf over, and it's defensible to an Episcopal reader in a way that quiet avoidance isn't — because avoidance looks like a judgment you haven't stated.

[[Romans 07#^7-1|Romans 7]] is the engine of the misreading, and the ἐγώ there almost certainly isn't autobiographical — Kümmel's argument, now near-consensus. It's Adam, or Israel at Sinai, or a rhetorical figure of life under sin without the Spirit. Read as Paul's confession it produces the introspective conscience Stendahl diagnosed. Read as prosopopoeia it makes a claim about sin's power, not about the law's design.

The deeper structural point: Paul's problem with the law is never that it's too hard. It's that it doesn't do the thing he now needs done, which is incorporate Gentiles without conversion. [[Romans 03#^3-31|Romans 3:31]] says the law is upheld; 7:12 calls it holy, just, and good. The argument is about the terms of Gentile inclusion — which is why the impossibility reading is not just wrong but backwards. It converts a question about *who* into a question about *how much*.

That's directly useful to your two-audience asymmetry, since Paul is arguing something structurally parallel: sufficiency for Israel, necessity of another route for everyone else. The Reformation reading collapses exactly the distinction you're trying to hold, and it does so by universalizing a problem Paul raised about Gentiles.

> [!quote] Me
> Isn't there a reading that suggests Jewish Christians were expected to keep the law but Gentile were not? Yes, and it's the mainstream position in a whole stream of scholarship now — Paul Within Judaism, sometimes the Radical New Perspective. Mark Nanos, Magnus Zetterholm, Paula Fredriksen, Matthew Thiessen. The core claim is exactly yours: Paul's law-free gospel is law-free *for Gentiles*, and nothing in his letters tells a Jew in Christ to stop keeping Torah.

The textual support is decent. [[1 Corinthians 07#^7-17|1 Corinthians 7:17–20]] is the cleanest — let the circumcised not remove the marks, let the uncircumcised not be circumcised, remain in the calling you were in. [[Galatians 05#^5-3|Galatians 5:3]] says a Gentile who gets circumcised is obligated to the whole law, which only makes sense if the obligation tracks status rather than faith. [[Acts 21#^21-21|Acts 21:21–24]] has James rebutting a rumor that Paul taught diaspora Jews to abandon Moses, and Paul performs a Nazirite rite to disprove it. And Acts 15 imposes four items on Gentiles that look structurally like a Noahide list, whatever its actual source.

Where it strains: [[Galatians 02#^2-11|Galatians 2:11–14]]. Paul faults Peter for withdrawing from Gentile tables, and 2:14 asks how he can compel Gentiles to Judaize when he himself lives ἐθνικῶς. The critics' reading is that Paul has already loosened his own practice. Nanos reads it as commensality politics rather than kashrut — the issue being who eats with whom, not what's served. Defensible, not free.

On the anachronism: it's smaller than you think. Matrilineal descent as the operative rule is probably tannaitic, but *some* boundary based on birth is clearly assumed by everyone in the first century, including Paul. The Noahide framing is genuinely later — the seven laws get their canonical form in Sanhedrin 56a — but Acts 15 and *Jubilees* 7 both show that something in that shape was in the air.

The thing you gain: this makes your two-audience asymmetry a *Pauline* position rather than a novel one, which is a much stronger place to argue from. The thing to watch: Paul's actual argument is that Gentiles are grafted into Israel's promises without becoming Israel, whereas Noahide law is a parallel covenant, not a graft. Those aren't the same structure, and a reviewer will press on whether you're using Paul or replacing him.

> [!quote] Me
> That's interesting. An image came to mind as reading the Baptist as an Aghori or Sadhu like figure, yet another stream for those that can swim it

The renunciate reading holds well; the Aghori specification will cost you.

What holds: John lives outside the settlement, in the wilderness, on wild food, in animal skins, with disciples who fast and have their own prayer forms — [[Luke 11#^11-1|Luke 11:1]] has them asking for one, which is *sampradāya* language, a distinct lineage practice. He initiates by water, he transfers disciples onward, and Josephus treats him as a public figure independent of Jesus. Q's "neither eating nor drinking" against Jesus's eating and drinking is the tradition itself marking two modes. And [[II Kings 01#^1-8|2 Kings 1:8]]'s hairy man with a leather belt makes the clothing an Elijah citation rather than mere austerity — which is nearer to the *sadhu* who wears a lineage's marks than to generic wildness.

What doesn't: the Aghori's distinctiveness is transgressive practice — cremation grounds, corpse, impurity deliberately embraced to collapse the pure/impure distinction. John does the reverse. Everything about him is purity-intensified: wilderness location, locusts and honey are kosher and uncultivated, immersion is a *tevilah* idiom, and his message is repentance ahead of judgment. Qumran is the live comparison, and Qumran is a purity-maximizing movement. Reading him as antinomian inverts him.

The stream image is the more interesting move anyway, and [[John 03#^3-30|John 3:30]] gives it to you — he must increase, I must decrease. The Baptist's own account is that his stream terminates. That's a real problem for "another route for those who can swim it," and it's the same problem as Acts 19's Ephesian disciples who knew only John's baptism. But it may be the productive one: a stream that is genuinely a stream and knowingly provisional is a different structure than a permanent parallel path, and it's arguably nearer to *upāya* than to *adhikāra*.

> [!quote] Me
> I guess I was imposing the rabbinic prohibition on the Nazarite vow on to the baptist and equating it with the antinomial behavior

Worth pulling apart, because there are two things and neither quite gets you antinomianism.

The rabbinic ambivalence is real but narrow. Nazir 19a has Shimon ha-Tzaddik refusing to eat *nezirut* sacrifices except once. Ta'anit 11a has Shmuel calling the nazirite a sinner, and the peg is [[Torah 35 - Nasso#^6-11|Numbers 6:11]] — the sin offering at the end of the term, atoning, on that reading, for the self-denial of wine. Rambam generalizes it in *De'ot* 3:1 into a rule against ascetic supererogation. But the Nazirite vow is prescribed in Torah, and the tractate legislates it in detail. Ambivalence about a permitted institution isn't prohibition, and it's centuries later than John.

The bigger issue: John probably wasn't a Nazirite. That's [[Luke 01#^1-15|Luke 1:15]], and it says only no wine or strong drink — no hair provision, no corpse restriction. Nazirite vows were also normally time-bounded and Temple-terminated. Whether Luke is signaling *nezirut* or Samson-style lifelong consecration is contested, and John's Gospel says nothing about it at all.

But the impulse behind the imposition is sound, and there's a better target for it. Excess in the direction of holiness *is* treated as a problem in your sources — that's the *Nadab and Abihu* structure, and it's a live Ishbitzer theme. Zeal that overshoots isn't lawlessness; it's law pressed past its measure. If you want John as a figure whose intensity is a real danger, that's the register, and it doesn't require making him transgressive.

Which incidentally sharpens your Nazirite/Ναζωραῖος thread rather than muddying it. If John is the one Luke marks with abstention and Jesus is the one the tradition calls a glutton and drunkard, then the epithet attaching to Jesus is doing something other than describing practice.

> [!quote] Me
> That makes me want to write a version where Jesus is a glutton and drunkard.

The Gospels already half-wrote it, and the interesting thing is that they preserved the accusation without rebutting it. [[Matthew 11#^11-19|Matthew 11:19]] and [[Luke 07#^7-34|Luke 7:34]]: φάγος καὶ οἰνοπότης, glutton and drunkard, friend of tax collectors and sinners. Jesus quotes the charge himself, and the reply is only that wisdom is vindicated by her works — a non-denial. The phrase is probably citing [[Torah 49 - Ki Teitzei#^21-20|Deuteronomy 21:20]], the stubborn and rebellious son, which makes it a capital charge rather than a comment on appetite.

And John gives you a version already. First sign is roughly a hundred and twenty gallons of wine at a party that has already drunk through its supply, produced from purification jars — the vessels for washing, filled with something you drink. The steward's line is that the good wine has been kept until now. Nobody in the narrative treats this as a problem. Then 6:53–56, where the crowd's horror is the point and Jesus escalates instead of clarifying, moving from φάγω to τρώγω, the cruder verb — gnawing rather than eating. Then Lazarus's dinner, the anointing, the meal that occupies five chapters.

The version worth writing isn't the transgressive one, though. It's the one where the accusation is simply true and not a sin — where the abundance is the sign, the table is the practice, and the charge lands because the practice is genuinely what it looks like. That fits your good-faith reading of the objectors exactly: the crowd isn't wrong about the facts. They're right about the wine and wrong about the register.

Which would make it a companion piece to the Baptist material rather than a lark. Two consecrations, opposite in form, and the Q saying holds them together on purpose.

It's a good joke and I'll go on playing it, but I want to name the thing that makes it a joke rather than a theory, because the "explains more than any theology" move is where it stops being funny and starts being wrong in an interesting way.

It's unfalsifiable. That's the whole trick. Drunk covers the outbursts and also the silences, the fig tree and the Sermon on the Mount, the whip and the washing of feet. A theory that accommodates every datum equally has stopped doing explanatory work — it's just a mood you can paint over anything. Compare it to a real rival like the apocalyptic-prophet reading, which actually predicts things and can be embarrassed by Luke's redaction. Drunk can't be embarrassed by anything.

And it only fits by selection. I gave you eight scenes; there are several hundred. Where the joke is genuinely good: it's the best available parody of harmonizing exegesis. Pick a key, find your eight verses, declare the system solved. So keep it. But its real home is a footnote about method, not a rival Christology. Chesterton's line about the madman having lost everything but his reason is nearly the same observation.

Your instinct is right, and the mechanism is more specific than "as much as" — Maximus is the figure who actually did this, knowingly, after 553.

The 553 anathemas don't condemn universal restoration simpliciter. They condemn a package: pre-existent *logikoi*, a fall through satiety, and a return in which all rational beings become one *henad* with bodies, names, and hypostatic difference abolished (anath. 14), such that the end is identical with the beginning (anath. 15). Nyssa taught restoration and was never touched; the named targets are Origen, Didymus, Evagrius. So the question isn't whether you hold a restoration but whether yours has that shape.

Maximus's answer was to invert the sequence. Origenism runs *stasis–kinēsis–genesis*: rest, then motion as lapse, then bodies. Maximus runs *genesis–kinēsis–stasis*: motion is not a fall but the created nature's proper movement toward its *logos*, and the terminus is ever-moving rest. That single inversion kills anathema 15 outright — the end can't be identical to the beginning, because the beginning is coming-to-be and the end is a stability that motion has produced and continues to inhabit. Sherwood's *Earlier Ambigua* is the standard treatment.

Then 681 supplies the part you're pointing at. Will follows nature, not person, and in the highest union available — hypostatic — the human will is neither destroyed nor absorbed. It *follows*, ἑπόμενον, and is deified precisely as human. That is a conciliar guarantee that maximal union preserves rather than dissolves difference. Which is exactly the reply to anathema 14: an *apokatastasis* modeled on theosis is not a *henad*, and the council that vindicated Maximus said so at the christological center.

The contracted-perspective piece does two things here. Against anathema 1, *saṅkoca* isn't a chronologically prior state from which anything fell — no unbodied soul, no satiety, no before. Against anathema 15, it supplies the difference between end and beginning that Origenism structurally can't state: nothing is ontologically added, but the standpoint now knows itself. Peter ends where he already was, correctly, and "correctly" is a real difference that survives the restoration rather than being erased by it.

> [!quote] Me

The intuition treats fear as an inference from uncertainty — I fear because I don't know how it turns out. But that isn't how fear works even in us. Fear tracks the perceived evil as such, not the odds. Someone certain the surgery will succeed still dreads the incision, and the dread isn't irrational or a failure of belief. The terminally ill often report exactly this: full certainty about the outcome and undiminished fear of the passage.

Maximus made this the technical center of his reading. Gethsemane is where the human will encounters death and recoils — φυσικὴ θέλησις recoiling from dissolution is what a human nature *is*, and a nature that didn't recoil wouldn't be human. He calls it blameless passion, ἀδιάβλητα πάθη: hunger, thirst, fear of death, all natural, none sinful, all assumed. The recoil isn't a judgment about likelihood. It's nature doing the one thing nature does in the presence of its own undoing.

So the "not my will" isn't a will informed differently from the Father's. It's a will that genuinely wants not to die, saying so, and then following. If it wanted nothing, there'd be nothing to follow with and 681 would be empty.

Your Johannine version at 12:27 is if anything sharper — τετάρακται is present, the trouble is real, and the sentence that follows knows exactly what is coming and asks the question anyway. Knowledge doesn't cancel the recoil; it's the condition under which the recoil is fully felt. Certainty makes the thing nearer, not further.

Some of them are rather, unusual; a few of them may even seem, at first glance, to lean close to positions the Church has condemned as heretical. Where that is the case, I will name the condemned position, explain what it actually holds, and state precisely why the position taken here is not a version of it.

But this need not be a defense; I have no fear of being burned or maimed over my words here, for better or worse. Rather, it is an attempt to systematize, for myself, and perhaps communicate to others, the hard-earned meanings I have spent my life putting into these words when I say them. To that end, readers will find my actual thoughts when I say the words in the body of the text and attempts to clarify those thoughts for other people in the footnotes.

In other words, it is a gloss, a word originally meaning a brief notation in a manuscript explaining a term in a language the reader is not expected to know. Throughout history, however, glosses have migrated, becoming associated with the text they served and occasionally even confused for it, a fact I acknowledge here at the start. In legal terms gloss means something specific as well: a judge's interpretation, not a report about what a statute says in the abstract, but a declaration of "how it works" in the language of its application. I intend both meanings here.

I quote Scripture in several translations, but the Hebrew Bible mostly in JPS and the New Testament mostly as given in the lectionary, as these are the versions I encounter in my practice. For some verses, however, the King James version is so familiar it is hard for me to hear it any other way. Where the language is critical, I refer to the Hebrew or Greek and suggest the same to every reader. The method is to cite the text that the community that produced or preserves it used or uses. Where the New Testament cites the Hebrew Bible against the Masoretic text the divergence is noted.

The Creed itself is a translation of a translation (of a translation!) and in some places the seams show. At times all that is required for my qualifications is a look at the pitfalls of the translation; other times, neither English, Latin, nor even Hebrew or Greek, can convey the nuance I am looking for and I will use Sanskrit.

**Introduction**

The Creed begins by borrowing, and the borrowing should be admitted before anything else. *We believe in one God* is the Shema in Greek dress. Hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one. This borrowing goes much deeper than the opening line, because Paul had already taken the verse apart in a letter to Corinth before any of the Gospels were written—giving God to the Father and Lord to Jesus, both words out of the same sentence. This is less elegant for the Creed, as it leaves nothing for the Spirit and uses Lord a second time, a fact that has bothered me since I was a boy.

A Christian reader should be aware of what is being compared. The Creed is what Christians believe. The Shema is closer to what Judaism is—and that is so difficult to express in figures of speech that when the text says, bind them on your hand, set them between your eyes, write them on the doorposts of your house the binding is literal and the tradition takes it as such because nothing less was sufficient. Children learn it first and the dying say it last. It's said upon waking and before sleeping.

That is not what I am doing when I say the Creed, and I want to be honest about the difference. I am not crowning anyone twice a day. I am saying words in a room with other people, and this document is an account of what I mean by them.

But there are three things I want to take from the comparison. First, the saying of the Shema is *kabbalat ol malchut shamayim*, accepting the yoke of the kingdom of heaven—and while that may be closer to a coronation than to a signature on a document for our Jewish neighbors, there is an element of shared commitment when reciting the Creed. Second, the meaning of the Shema has been turned over continuously for centuries without anyone imagining that reciting it commits the reciter to a single philosophical account of what the words mean. Two of history's greatest Torah commentators, Rashi and Ibn Ezra, do not agree about what the word *echad* claims; nobody thinks this disqualifies either of them from saying it. While the Creed's words were selected to exclude specific interpretations and mandate others, the ecumenical history shows that even for words chosen with care, meaning is the work of a life.

Third, a detail in the Torah scroll's calligraphy I cannot leave out. The letter *ayin* of *shema* and the letter *dalet* of *echad* are written large, and together they spell *ed*—witness. The one speaking the words is giving a testimony from a perspective and every perspective has its own.

From my perspective, every disclosure I describe becomes legible only afterward. Nothing in this account can be run forward. The third day is read back into Hosea; the cross is read back from the resurrection; the criterion I used to say what is unique about Christ is settled by something that happened after the criterion was applied. The actual motion is *spanda*, the Sanskrit word for pulse or throb, revealing and concealing in one simultaneous act.

The fact that I meet the pulse "from somewhere" is not a defect in the system, but rather what Recognition is. You cannot see what you are looking at until you have seen it, and then you cannot unsee that it was always there.

Taking you to mean sections IV through VII — the narrative sentences. They're the hard ones for a reason worth naming: the comma rule isn't really a comma rule.

It works in sentence I because that sentence is a string of appositives, so the punctuation and the theological units coincide. IV through VII are narrative, and English punctuates narrative by grammar rather than by doctrine. VI has no commas at all. V has none. IV has one, and it falls in a place that isn't a seam.

The underlying structure is visible in the Greek, which is a chain of participles: σαρκωθέντα, ἐνανθρωπήσαντα, σταυρωθέντα, παθόντα, ταφέντα, ἀναστάντα, ἀνελθόντα, καθεζόμενον, ἐρχόμενον. Each one is a separate thing asserted. The English translators turned those into finite verbs and punctuated for readability, which is what buried the seams.

So the rule that covers both cases is one level up from punctuation: **divide at the smallest unit that makes a distinct assertion.** In sentence I that lands on the appositives, because there are no finite verbs after *believe*. In IV–VII it lands on the verbs. Same rule, different surface.

That gives:

**IV** — *For us and for our salvation* (1) · *he came down from heaven:* (2) · *by the power of the Holy Spirit he became incarnate from the Virgin Mary,* (3) · *and was made man.* (4)

The one judgment call is whether to split the Spirit's agency from the taking of flesh. The Greek has them under a single participle — ἐκ Πνεύματος Ἁγίου καὶ Μαρίας τῆς Παρθένου both governed by σαρκωθέντα — so I'd keep them together unless you have separate material on the agency. Keeping *and was made man* alone is not optional: it's a second participle in the Greek and it's where your *mala* argument lives.

**V** — *For our sake* (1) · *he was crucified* (2) · *under Pontius Pilate;* (3) · *he suffered death* (4) · *and was buried.* (5)

Pilate gets his own unit even though no punctuation marks him off, because you have pages on him and because the Greek treats the date-stamp as a modifier of the whole rather than of the verb.

**VI** — *On the third day he rose again* (1) · *in accordance with the Scriptures;* (2) · *he ascended into heaven* (3) · *and is seated at the right hand of the Father.* (4)

Zero commas in the original, four assertions. This is the sentence that proves the rule can't be typographic.

**VII** — *He will come again in glory* (1) · *to judge the living and the dead,* (2) · *and his kingdom will have no end.* (3)
