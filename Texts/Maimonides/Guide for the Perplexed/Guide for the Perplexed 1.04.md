# Guide for the Perplexed 1.04

← [[Guide for the Perplexed 1.03]] · [[Guide for the Perplexed]] · [[Guide for the Perplexed 1.05]] →

*Part 1, Chapter 4* · [[Guide for the Perplexed]]

THE three verbs raah, hibbit, and ḥazah, which denote “he perceived with the eye,” are also used figuratively in the sense of intellectual perception.

As regards the first of these verbs this is well known, e.g., And he looked (va-yar) and behold a well in the field” ([[Torah 07 - Vayetzei#^29-2|Gen. 29:2]]) here it signifies ocular perception: “yea, my heart has seen (raah) much of wisdom and of knowledge” ([[Ecclesiastes 01#^1-16|Eccles. 1:16]]); in this passage it refers to the intellectual perception.

In this figurative sense the verb is to be understood, when applied to God e.g., “I saw (raïti) the Lord” ([[I Kings 22#^22-19|1 Kings 22:19]]); “And the Lord appeared (va-yera) unto him ([[Torah 04 - Vayera#^18-1|Gen. 18:1]]); “And God saw (va-yar) that it was good” ([[Torah 01 - Bereshit#^1-10|Gen. 1:10]]) “I beseech thee, show me (hareni) thy glory” ([[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^33-18|Exod. 33:18]]); “And they saw (va-yirü) the God of Israel” ([[Torah 18 - Mishpatim#^24-10|Exod. 24:10]]). All these instances refer to intellectual perception, and by no means to perception with the eye as in its literal meaning: for, on the one hand, the eye can only perceive a corporeal object, and in connection with it certain accidents, as colour, shape, etc.: and, on the other hand, God does not perceive by means of a corporeal organ, as will be explained.

In the same manner the Hebrew hibbit signifies “he viewed with the eye; comp. “Look (tabbit) not behind thee” ([[Torah 04 - Vayera#^19-17|Gen. 19:17]]); “But his wife looked (va-tabbet) back from him” ([[Torah 04 - Vayera#^19-26|Gen. 19:26]]); “And if one look (ve-nibbat) unto the land” ([[Isaiah 05#^5-30|Isa. 5:30]]); and figuratively, “to view and observe” with the intellect, “to contemplate” a thing till it be understood. In this sense the verb is used in passages like the following: “He hath not beheld (hibbit) iniquity in Jacob” ([[Torah 40 - Balak#^23-21|Num. 23:21]]); for “iniquity” cannot be seen with the eye. The words, “And they looked (ve-hibbitu) after Moses” ([[Torah 21 - Ki Tisa#^33-8|Exod. 33:8]])—in addition to the literal understanding of the phrase—were explained by our Sages in a figurative sense. According to them, these words mean that the Israelites examined and criticised the actions and sayings of Moses. Compare also “Contemplate (habbet), I pray thee, the heaven” ([[Torah 03 - Lech Lecha#^15-5|Gen. 15:5]]); for this took place in a prophetic vision. This verb, when applied to God, is employed in this figurative sense; e.g., “to look (me-habbit) upon God” ([[Torah 13 - Shemot#^3-6|Exod. 3:6]]) “And the similitude of the Lord shall he behold” (yabbit) ([[Torah 36 - Beha'alotcha#^12-8|Num. 12:8]]); And thou canst not look (habbet) on iniquity” ([[Habakkuk 1#^1-13|Hab. 1:13]]).

The same explanation applies to ḥazah. It denotes to view with the eye, as: “And let our eye look (ve-taḥaz) upon Zion” ([[Micah 4#^4-11|Mic. 4:11]]); and also figuratively, to perceive mentally: “which he saw (ḥazah) concerning Judah and Jerusalem” ([[Isaiah 01#^1-1|Isa. 1:1]]); “The word of the Lord came unto Abraham in a vision” (maḥazeh) ([[Torah 03 - Lech Lecha#^15-1|Gen. 15:1]]); in this sense ḥazah is used in the phrase, “Also they saw (va-yeḥezu) God” ([[Torah 18 - Mishpatim#^24-11|Exod. 24:11]]). Note this well.
