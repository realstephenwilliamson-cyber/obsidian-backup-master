## 1 The Province of Mysticism

Mysticism takes many forms, and there have been a variety of experiences reported from different cultures and at different times, which makes it an altogether difficult concept to define. The word ‘mystical’ derives ultimately from the Greek *mystikos*, and was used in connection with the secret ritual of the Eleusinian Mysteries. Taken over into Christianity it was used in reference to scriptural exegesis, or the action of divining the mystery of Christ in his Word.[^1] The intimate reading naturally evoked a religious feeling, and in an evolutionary step ‘mystical’ began to be employed experientially. The noun, *la mystique*, ‘mysticism’, did not come into use until the early seventeenth century in France; and the modern application, at least from the late nineteenth century, is to a wholly psychologized and internalized experience. Given the enormous range of experiences that have been reported in biographical and autobiographical writings, it would seem restrictive to impose a narrow definition that tries to account for everything. For the contemporary observer it ought though to be admitted that the philosophical and cultural perspective one brings to the study of mysticism will colour one’s assessments of what mysticism is.[^2] 

The purpose of the research presented in this thesis is to investigate the role that different conceptual understandings of eroticism and sexuality play in mystical approaches to the divine, as evidenced within the Christian affective and Hindu Tantric traditions. In this study these traditions are taken as respectively indicative examples of ‘Western’ and ‘Eastern’ forms of religious expression. The movement of the Beguines in the Rhineland and Low Countries of Europe, which flourished during the thirteenth century, will be taken as an exemplar of ‘affective’ mysticism. I shall focus on the work of a particular beguine mystic, Mechthild of Magdeburg, who lived from 1208 to 1282.[^3] She wrote a book, *Das fließende Licht der Gottheit*, or, in translation, *The Flowing Light of the Godhead* (*FL*), in which she reported her intense mystical experiences of love for God and the longing of her soul for union with him. In the case of Tantrism I shall focus on the brāhmaṇically influenced Śrīvidyā tradition, most particularly the text of *Saundaryalaharī* (*SL*), for which Lakṣmīdhara is a chief commentator.

He was a revivalist preacher at Liège who translated parts of the Bible into the vernacular, and died in 1177.[^5] As for the beguine movement, it began in Lotharingia (the ancient middle division of the Carolingian Empire), specifically in the diocese of Liège; it then spread to the rest of Belgium and northern France, north into Holland, and eastward into Germany along the Rhine.[^6] There are possible antecedents in the Cistercian nuns and Premonstratensian canonesses of the twelfth century.[^7] Nevertheless, the activity of the beguines can be said to mark a new phase in the history of women religious. 

There are considered to be four chronological phases in the development of the Beguines: (a) individual women living alone or with their parents; (b) small communities in a parish setting; (c) larger groups of enclosed beguines in the service of hospitals and other good works; and (d) independent beguine parishes (called *curtes*) with large communities of women and associated personnel.[^8] Beginning as a scattered collection of ecstatics they formed associations as they found common cause, notwithstanding opposition from the Church. Since the Fourth Lateran Council mandated the disapproval of new religious orders in 1215 it was not likely that the Beguines would receive official sanction. However, Cardinal James of Vitry argued against the unjustness of condemning these women to remain in the world simply because they did not have the dowry, or the material resources with which to gain entry to an established order; as a result, papal consent was given to form free communities, with ‘severe restraint’. The subsequent Bull of June 1233, *Gloriam virginalem*, proclaimed by Pope Gregory IX, was interpreted sympathetically by certain bishops; and in spite of attempts to control them through clerical oversight some beguines continued to live outside direct ecclesiastical influence.[^9]

Hindu Tantrism is a complex amalgam of elements derived from the Vedas, Purāṇas, and Upaniṣads. When and where exactly it arose is not known for certain. Eliade believes it developed in the peripheral, still un-Hinduized areas of India, even to the extent of showing Gnostic influences via Iran. He considers that ‘more than one curious parallel can be noted between Tantrism and the great Western mysterio-sophic current that, at the beginning of the Christian era, arose from the confluence of Gnosticism, Hermetism, Greco-Egyptian alchemy, and the traditions of the Mysteries’.[^16] The Tantric verbal tradition understands that the doctrines imparted in the Tantras are of divine origin, coming from Śiva, or one of his divine manifestations (Bhairava, Aghora, etc.). At some stage that tradition was written down, but when, exactly, is undetermined; the oldest preserved Tantric manuscripts seem to date from about the ninth century, while references to older authoritative texts suggest that the literature existed two or more centuries earlier.[^17] Kashmir and Nepal nurtured the mediaeval explosion of Tantric religions, but Bengal and Assam in the east of India were also important centres. By the tenth or eleventh centuries the Tantric orders and practices had penetrated to the South and were translated into Tamil.[^18] The Muslim incursions after the twelfth century saw a decline of Tantra in northern and central India; but in the south it has been integrated into the social matrix.[^19] Overall, Tantrism has managed to pervade so the Hindu thought that the distinction between it and mainstream brahmanism has in some cases become problematic. Effectively, this means that one does not have to renounce one’s religion to follow the Tantric path (*mārga*). Broadly, Hindu Tantrism is a system or rule which refers to a gamut of practices – ritual, magical and yogic – indulged in by practitioners belonging to one of three divisions: the Vaiṣṇavite, Śaivite, or Śākta; that is, respectively, worshippers of Viṣṇu, Śiva, or Devī.[^22] Strictly speaking, the Tantras refer to those traditions of northern and southern India that give supreme prominence to the Goddess in her various forms, rather than to the traditions that extol Śiva (Āgamas) or Viṣṇu (Saṁhitās). Unless otherwise indicated the term ‘tantra’ in this thesis is to be understood as referring to the Śāktas or Śāktaśaivas.[^23] At any rate, the numerous sects within these schools utilize a vast number of obscurely written revelatory texts, many of which still remain to be critically edited from their manuscript forms, much less to be translated into English. It is likely much more has been lost over time.[^24] The Tantras are deliberately designed to be secretive and ambiguous: they are ‘encrypted’ teachings, the key to which can only be supplied by a guru, who may or may not be actually present in physical form. So-called ‘intentional language’ (*sandhābhāṣā*) is used to convey meaning.[^25] Hence, for a Westerner unfamiliar with the concepts and terminology a commentary is a prerequisite, and indeed indispensable. Moreover, the study of tantric texts should not be divorced from the adjunct of oral interpretation provided by living initiates; and indeed ‘Tantrics’, according to Brooks, ‘have never intended texts to function as autonomous authorities or as complete descriptions of instantiated practice or ideology’.[^26] The teaching of Śiva, which defines the Śaiva Āgamas, is divided between two branches or ‘streams’: the Outer Path (Atimārga), and the Path of Mantras (Mantramārga). A mystic within the ‘dualist’ and monastic Christian tradition seeks union with the divine by focusing devotion upon a trinitarian Being, and furthermore seeks to sublimate sexual energy into mental (spiritual) energy.[^32] In the non-dual Tantric tradition the aspirant seeks to unify the absolute and phenomenal realms through a similar transformation of sexual energy, and understands him or herself in the role of the God Śiva or Goddess Śakti. The use of erotic or romantic language as metaphor is rather prevalent in the Christian mystical tradition, as it is also in the theistically inclined Hinduism of *bhakti* devotion (although it also exists, if in a more abstract way, in non-*bhakti* philosophies). In the European Christian tradition during the twelfth century, mystical devotion found expression in a desire to conjoin the individual soul with God; and, in light of the use of nuptial imagery, this conjunction came to be pictured as a connubial relationship, or ‘spiritual marriage’; effectively, in this instance, there is a gendered polarity between the male Christ and the female soul. Likewise in Hindu *bhakti* philosophies, there is a desire to achieve unity with a particular deity through devotional or ritual activity, and there is usually a gendered polarity operating in the sense that the spiritual other is represented as male, while the physical, sensual realm is treated as female; here, male devotees often will assimilate to the feminine.[^33] Even in non-dualistic philosophies such as Tantrism there is the notion of opposites coming together to form a unity; but given its monistic orientation, how does Tantrism deal with the apparently binary nature of existence? These polarizing notions as ontological backdrop inform my approach to the subject of this thesis, and indeed are the crux of investigation.

The Church Father Origen posited a ‘double creation’, or two-stage creation; while the first envisages ‘a primal state of pure spiritual beings (with, perhaps, pure spiritual bodies) freely and perfectly contemplating the primal unity of God’, the second creation occurred as these beings subsequently fell, and became individuated souls with bodies. For Augustine, the body becomes the focal point for overcoming, through ascetic endeavour, the dualities implicit in the fallen human condition. His theology influenced mediaeval notions of the redemptive significance of the bodily suffering of Christ. In Hinduism, the Ṛg Veda celebrates the body exuberantly, while the later Upaniṣads warn of its treachery; Sanskrit love poetry is pro-body and libertine; and the Yogic texts are also pro-body, although not libertine.[^46] The *Laws of Manu*, considered to be the foundation text of Hindu society, evinces a contemptuous opinion of the body: ‘\[A man\] should abandon this foul-smelling, tormented, impermanent dwelling-place of living beings, filled with urine and excrement, pervaded by old age and sorrow, infested by illness, and polluted by passion, with bones for beams, sinews for cords, flesh and blood for plaster, and skin for the roof’.[^47] This view was accompanied by virulent misogyny, and the belief that a man ‘owns’ his wife.[^48] These laws formed the basis for brāhmaṇic injunctions on the body, which understood that it must be kept pure for the performance of *dharma*. The renouncer tradition promoted in the Upaniṣads is anti-body, in that the liberatory goal is emancipation from the round of rebirths. Notwithstanding, there is considerable ‘emotional investment’ in the body by Indian ascetics and non-ascetics, and it is seldom rejected altogether.[^49] Tantrism tends to be anti-ascetic, and renunciation, in the manner of renouncer ascetics, is rejected, or rationalized as being unnecessary.[^50] The body in fact is the place or seat (*pīṭha*) of power (*śakti*); and as a vehicle for the expression of higher forces it becomes the locus for integrating subtle entities or powers into the self.[^51] What is moreover distinctive about the Tantric approach is the integration of mind and body; they are not treated separately but as a whole.[^52] In this regard, the philosophy of the Tantras may be seen as representing an embodied spirituality; and indeed, it is maintained that bliss can be realized right now in this body (*jīvanmukti*). In many ways, the role and function of sex and celibacy is pivotal in both traditions – monastic, or quasi-monastic, and tantric; however, different emphases are given. I shall explore these in chapter 6.

What is of interest here is the use of imagery to sustain the normative experiences of gender-related understandings, which are generally based upon oppositional categories. Dichotomizing tendencies were not so evident in the writings of mediaeval mystics, argues Murk–Jansen, who asserts that the mediaeval period tended to perceive men and women ‘as the ends of the same continuum rather than as diametrically opposed to each other as they are today’.[^54] Moreover, ‘\[t\]he well-known analogy that “man is to woman as soul is to body” is today interpreted as illustrative of a dichotomy, ignoring the medieval conception of soul and body as both contributing essentially to the created individuality of a human being’ (p. 53). And, ‘\[t\]he modern notion of the need to define a femininity distinct from and opposed to androcentric conceptions of gender tends to be inappropriate for a discussion of medieval theological texts’ (ibid.). Gender reversal and paradox was used to indicate the otherness of the relationship to God, in contrast to the normal human relationships. My intention is simultaneously to uphold and subvert polar understandings of gendered imagery. 

The next chapter will give an overview of the pertinent literature. In order to explore the parameters of mystical experience in the two traditions under study here, the research methodology employed will be that of phenomenography, and this is considered in chapter 3. The subsequent two chapters will examine the special kinds of mystical outlook in Beguine affectiveness and Śākta Tantrism, using the *FL* and *SL* as anchors. Christian ‘affective mysticism’ promotes the notion of a spiritual union between God and the individual soul, while so-called ‘identification mysticism’, evidenced in Tantrism, promotes the idea of a complete integration into God by the (formerly individuated) soul. The usual understanding is of a substantial difference between the two types of mystical experience, but it will be argued here that they are not so dissimilar. In this regard chapter 6 will elaborate these contrasts and correspondences. Some questions I shall address – directly or in passing – are: What is the role and function of mystical symbolic imagery in *FL* and *SL*? What is the interplay between the various assignments of gender to entities and ideas? Is the goal essentially the same in the two traditions: basically, the conjunction of opposites in a mystical union with the divine (however understood), and the consequent submersion of differences? This thesis will argue that there are correspondences *relatively speaking* between the concepts espoused in the two mystical outlooks. Furthermore, I shall argue that the Beguine tradition (notwithstanding its Catholic anti-body bias), exhibits a degree of understanding that could be seen as incorporating a ‘bodily’ spirituality, as Tantrism naturally does.
