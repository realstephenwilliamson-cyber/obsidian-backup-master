## Ricoeurian Hermeneutics and Indian Thought: Analyzing Evil in Cross-Cultural Philosophy

Purushottama Bilimoria1,2 · Meera Baindur<sup>3</sup>

Accepted: 22 May 2025 / Published online: 18 August 2025 © The Author(s) 2025

### Abstract

This paper examines the intersection of Western and Indian hermeneutical traditions, focusing on Paul Ricoeur’s hermeneutics and its application to the Upaniṣads. Engaging with the broader hermeneutical debates between Gadamer, Habermas, and Ricoeur, the study highlights the tensions between tradition and critical reflection. Ricoeur’s “hermeneutics of suspicion” is employed to interrogate dominant interpretations shaped by the *Brahmasūtra,* particularly those that prioritize *mokṣa* (liberation) as the central goal of the Upaniṣads. Drawing from the Mīmāṃsā tradition and *Nirukta* exegesis, the study introduces a “decolonial hermeneutics of trust,” which reframes these texts in terms of embodied experience rather than transcendence. Through an analysis of Upaniṣadic doctrines and myths, particularly the struggles between *deva*-s and *asura*-s, the paper explores the concept of *pāpmā/pāpman* (‘evil’) as an amoral force of privation that affects perception and disrupts the unity of the self. By bridging Western philosophical discourse, focusing on Ricoeur’s work, with Indian hermeneutical methods, this study reveals the cross-cultural dimensions of meaning-making while also challenging essentialist readings of Indian thought. The philosophical investigation contibutes to a broader rethinking of hermeneutical frameworks, emphasizing the dynamic interplay between critique and tradition in interpreting classical texts.

### Contextual Introduction: the pūrvapakṣa

This paper is an extension of a discussion on Ricoeur’s hermeneutics as applicable to Indian philosophical traditions following an earlier paper by Purushottama Bilimoria. In that work Bilimoria (2000) analyzed the hermeneutics of Paul Ricoeur, focusing on how interpretations can engage with tradition and yet continue to have a critical perspective as an integral part of understanding texts. After highlighting the Gadamer-Habermas debate on the methods required to engage with texts — particularly those produced within specific historical and cultural contexts—Bilimoria explored Ricoeur’s attempts to reconcile these two approaches in hermeneutics. Gadamer advocated for an optimistic engagement with texts and their contexts, while Habermas critiqued the role of tradition as a potential ruse for reinforcing dominant ideologies. Bilimoria (2000) concluded his earlier work by suggesting the adoption of frameworks that recognize tradition’s influence and encouraging intercultural methods to interpret texts within South Asian philosophy.

In a similar vein, this piece seeks to extend Ricoeur’s work on hermeneutics to the study of the Upaniṣads. We argue that new ideas, symbols, and concepts can be uncovered in a traditional text and mythologies by adopting the principle of “hermeneutics of suspicion” and decentralizing certain dominant ideas within the Upaniṣads. In addition to using Ricoeur’s method of interpreting texts within a tradition, we also draw upon his work on mythology, symbolism, and engagement with body hermeneutics that corresponds to the embodied phenomenology described in the Upaniṣads. This allows for some interesting themes to emerge from the stories of the Upaniṣads. The Upaniṣads, regarded as one of the oldest texts in Indian philosophy following on the coattails of the *Brāhmaṇa-*s (Tull, 2018), are a valuable resource for exploring foundational conceptual ideas that have influenced later intellectual developments across India. These texts are considered sacred and authoritative, offering philosophical insights and religious instruction. As part of the Vedic corpus, the Upaniṣads have been extensively subjected to hermeneutical interpretations in pre-modern and contemporary scholarships. This ongoing engagement with these texts is a significant aspect of Indian philosophical practice and pedagogy. Furthermore, studies on these texts have been revived due to emergent meaning-making methodologies and critical post-colonial and decoloniality studies. Contemporary philosophers are also exploring the study of these texts in relation to their contextual origins and to other socio-cultural themes that may emerge from these narratives.

The methodological challenges in interpreting Indian philosophy and its content can be categorized as belonging to two different domains of knowledge. The first is the problem of interpretation and hermeneutics that falls under the category of language, and translation — this being related to conceptual framing of the insights from these texts. The first problem has been widely debated, with scholars engaging critically in discussions around the accuracy and limitations of translating ancient Indian texts into modern languages.

The second problem is contextual and historical — the problem of the continuity of the transmission and interpretation of these texts. This is a contextual issue– and pertains to the transmission of these texts over centuries, during which many commentaries and reinterpretations have been produced, that are sometimes even 

*[p. 515]* ^pg3

contradictory in their philosophical positions. These commentaries, especially from premodern scholars within the Mīmāṃsa tradition or Vedānta schools, have shaped and reshaped the meanings of the original texts. So, to discover the “true” meaning of a text makes no sense in these philosophical traditions.

Patrick Olivelle (1998: xx-xxi) in his preface to *The Early Upaniṣads: Annotated Text and Translation*, explicitly acknowledges the multiplicity of interpretations that have shaped these texts over centuries. Olivelle emphasizes that there is no singular “native” interpretation of the Upaniṣads. Instead, their meaning has been shaped by a long history of theological and sectarian interpretations. In discussing his methods of translation, he also asserts that any translation is inherently an act of interpretation. The Upaniṣads, like other ancient texts, have accumulated layers of meaning through formal commentaries, sectarian debates, and scriptural use. Because of this, attempting to uncover a singular “true” meaning of the texts is problematic. (ibid.)

There appears to be a parallel in hermeneutics between the West and East, particularly in the case of Mīmāṃsa. The Mīmāṃsa school distances itself from the speaker’s intention, suggesting that the speaker's intention does not play a role in meaning-making. Indian philosophical traditions emphasize not only the direct meaning of words but also include direct, indirect, implied, and suggestive meanings, referred to as *tātparya*. In discussions about speaker intention, Mīmāṃsa philosophers argue that verbal comprehension can occur independently of the speaker or authorial intentionality (Bilimoria, 2008: 209). The Mīmāṃsakas identified four distinct factors (*kāraṇas)* at work toward understanding a sentence, including expectancy (*ākāṇkṣā*), compatibility (*yogyatā*), and contiguity (*sannidhi* or *āsatti),* along with *tātparya.* (ibid.: 166–189) *Ākāṇkṣā* refers to the listener’s desire to grasp the text’s meaning, while *sannidhi* (*āsatti)* emphasizes the necessity for words to be synchronous in time for coherence. The third factor, *yogyatā*, pertains to logical consistency understood by both the speaker and listener in context, leading to the understanding of the passage’s import or intention, termed *tātparya*.

One example of this multiplicity of interpretations can be seen in the way dualist and idealist schools of Vedānta use the same Upaniṣadic passages to support their almost contrasting views. The Upaniṣads, as foundational texts, are polysemic, allowing for diverse interpretations. For instance, the statement *'Tat tvam asi’* is understood by Advaita Vedānta to signify the non-difference of the individual self (*jīva*) and the ultimate reality (*Brahman*), while Dvaita Vedānta interprets the same phrase in a way that maintains an eternal distinction between the two.

Thus, hermeneutical approaches to the Upaniṣads have generated considerable scholarly interest, particularly among Indologists, in translating these texts into European languages. These translations have exposed the philosophical themes of the Upaniṣads to a wider global audience, but they also highlight the challenges posed by intercultural interpretation. We also should be aware that, viewed from a larger historical context, postcolonial and contemporary intercultural engagements in philosophy have been influenced by philosophical perspectives arising from the hermeneutics of 20th century philosophy, as delineated in Bilimoria’s paper, which is summarized next.

### The Hermeneutical Turn in the Late 20th Century Philosophy

In this section, we summarize Bilimoria’s analysis of Gadamer, Habermas, and Ricoeur to support this paper’s methodology (Bilimoria, 2000). Ricoeur’s work on hermeneutics as a framework for interpreting a traditional religious text can be applied as a philosophical method to a textual study of some Upaniṣads passages.

Ricoeur is central to the debate on hermeneutics, which evolved significantly with the contributions of Gadamer and Habermas, along with the influence of philosophers such as Heidegger. Bilimoria provides a detailed analysis of the Gadamer and Habermas debate on hermeneutics and then examines Ricoeur’s intervention in the late 20th century (Bilimoria, 2000).

Here Bilimoria discusses the philosophical conflict between Gadamer and Habermas, proposing that Ricoeur offers a resolution of their debate by mediating between a text’s cultural context and its socio-political dimensions. He argues that Ricoeur successfully integrates Gadamer’s focus on tradition with a critical approach, achieving a balance that preserves the integrity of both perspectives without merging them into one.

Ricoeur combines the reanimation of traditional sources of communicative action with the reawakening of political responsibility towards a creative renewal of cultural heritage. His own summary of the ‘fusion’ or consensus (which he refrains from calling a ‘synthesis’ or ‘union’) discusses the specific symbols from the two dominant religions of the West, Judaism and Christianity, namely, Exodus and Resurrection, which are eschatological symbols of liberation, salvation, and hope. (ibid.: 102)

Bilimoria suggests that Gadamer does not view traditions of text as static but sees them as an engaged dialectical process between the past and the present. He explains the concept of fusion of horizons that Gadamer posits. The interpreter’s own position (even by their historical context) and the cultural, social, and historical contacts from within which the text was produced are the two horizons that fuse and co-constitute the interpretation of a text:

\[A\] text is a production of a number of moves, beginning with the intention of the author, the disposition of the original auditors, the cultural environment, and the socio-linguistic conditions in which it arises. (ibid.: 101)

Bilimoria notes Gadamer’s insistence that the historical horizon is inseparable from the act of interpretation and cannot be perceived as a barrier to understanding a text. He writes:

Given that the elements that comprise the fundamental structures of our linguistic understanding are not entirely independent of the ‘text’ we are attempting to under- stand, and being historically and culturally constituted, they are further not free from certain presuppositions and prejudices (Heidegger’s *fore-struc* *tures, pre-understanding)*. (ibid.: 94).

*[p. 517]* ^pg5

Habermas critiques Gadamer’s reliance on tradition as an interpretive foundation, arguing that it can mask systematic distortions of communication caused by dominant ideologies. Bilimoria highlights this tension, explaining:

Unless there is a more universal epistemological and objective matrix from which to launch and check or scrutinize the ground-rules for this conversation or dialogue between tradition and reflection, there is no way of subduing the rule of subjectivity and preventing prejudices and presuppositions of a tradition from re-asserting and re-inscribing themselves. (ibid.: 99)

Habermas thus highlights critical reflection through a process of ‘distancing’ that allows an interpreter to uncover bias, political reflection, and ideological distortions that have shaped a text. Traditions thus are not neutral but laden with sociopolitical interests:

Habermas searches for a distanciation (critical distancing, setting reflectively aloof) from tradition and the subjectively-involved conditions (‘happenings,’ ‘events,’ etc.) that would make space for reflection, question dogmatic forces, and not conflate knowledge with authority. (ibid.: 99)

Bilimoria then proceeds to elucidate Ricoeur’s hermeneutical framework. He explores how Ricoeur engages in deconstructing dominant metaphysical constructs by challenging entrenched assumptions about meaning, tradition, and ideology. According to him, Ricoeur critiques and reframes these constructs through a hermeneutic approach that blends Gadamer’s emphasis on tradition with Habermas’s focus on critical theory. He seeks to expose hidden power dynamics and underlying ideologies embedded in cultural and textual interpretations while maintaining a dialogue with historical context. As Bilimoria notes, Ricoeur “mediates between the claims of tradition and the demands of critique.” (ibid.: 104).

This approach allows for a reinterpretation of metaphysical frameworks in a way that acknowledges their socio-political implications and cultural significance. Ricoeur’s methodology fosters a balanced critique, avoiding reductionism and fostering a more nuanced understanding of texts and traditions.

Ricoeur combines the reanimation of traditional sources of communicative action with the reawakening of political responsibility towards a creative renewal of cultural heritage. (ibid.: 102)

Finally, Bilimoria concludes with the possibilities that Ricoeur’s hermeneutical turn can have on South Asian philosophies. Ricoeur’s emphasis on dialogue between traditions provides a framework for constructive engagement between South Asian philosophies and global philosophical discourses. This includes exploring shared symbols, narratives, and ethical concerns in ways that respect the distinctiveness of each tradition. This cross-cultural hermeneutics also offers tools to reclaim these traditions by critiquing colonial narratives and recovering Indigenous voices. He suggests that Ricoeur’s work provides a means to critically engage with historical tra

*[p. 518]* ^pg6

ditions while reinterpreting them to meet contemporary needs, offering a path toward cultural renewal and philosophical innovation. (ibid.: 93, 106.)

Bilimoria gives an example of applying the hermeneutics of suspicion to the concept of ‘*Brahman*.’ (ibid.: 3) He notes how such a concept has become a dead metaphor over time, burdened with the social, hierarchical, exclusionary social-political structure:

A culture or rather ideology of Brāhmaṇical hegemony and renunciative restraint bordering on the obsessive denial of the lived experience was built or idealised on the basis of this dominant and powerful transcendental signifier. (ibid.)

Earlier discussions of Indian philosophy, particularly those by scholars such as Daya Krishna, Bimal K Matilal, and J N Mohanty, have focused on the rational (reason-wise) classification of Indian philosophical systems and the identification of common and divergent themes across them. These scholars sought to make sense of the continuities and discontinuities in philosophical ideas, especially in terms of rational thought versus religious or theological thought. This line of inquiry also aimed to demystify soteriological ideas and their role in the spiritual practices of Indian traditions. These scholarly efforts have revived serious engagement with Indian philosophical texts and have laid the groundwork for contemporary discussions on topics such as environment, gender, embodiment, and science. Such other themes can emerge by applying Bilimoria’s suggestion on decentring the signifier *Brahman* and reducing the significance of the theme of liberation.

### Isogesis

The thesis that each reading of a text is unavoidably determined by some set of prejudgments or prejudices, notwithstanding the effort to contextualize the reading, is tested by Andrew P. Tuck (1990) in the context of modern philosophical understanding of the Buddhist text *Mulamadhyamakakārikā* of Nāgārjuna, 2nd century C.E. Each scholar works within the ambit of the interpretive tradition and the cumulative scholarship, background beliefs and commitment of the scholarly community at the particular historical moment. Tuck calls this liminal process isogesis (to contrast it with exegesis). In fact, most exegesis turn out to be instances of isogesis, implying that the scholar reads as much of the representative tradition “into” the text as the text would disclose its own meaning. Tuck wants to argue that isogesis is not only inevitable, but that is an evidence of understanding (10; see also Bronkhorst, 1993).

This is borne out in the Sri Lankan Buddhist scholar K.N. Jayatilleke’s book *Early Theory of Knowledge* (1963) in which he argued that early Buddhist philosophy was akin to the Vienna Circle’s logical positivism following the analytical turn in Western phi- losophy. Jayatilleke’s student, David Kalupahana, aligned early Buddhist theory of causality to David Hume’s phenomenalism. While in India, T.R.V. Murti compared Buddhist “emptiness” with the absolutism of Kant, Hegel, and Bradley. The list of shifting interpretive frameworks, influenced by the current trends and move

*[p. 519]* ^pg7

ments in Western philosophy (analytic, phenomenological and indeed logical), goes on to the point where some recent scholars even ventured to align Nāgārjuna variously with Heidegger, Husserl, G.E. Moore, Derrida, and Wilfred Sellars to boot (see Bilimoria, 1992). Another example of isogesis is to be found in the works of certain late 18th and 19th-century German philosophers and philologists, influenced by the idealist movement (going back to Hegel and Leibniz), interpreting Indian texts such as the Upaniṣads through the lens of their own tradition’s erstwhile metaphysical frameworks. Scholars such as Paul Deussen looked for parallels between Upaniṣadic thought and Kantian or Hegelian philosophy, more often than not portraying Indian philosophy as a precursor to or premodern mirroring of Western classical metaphysics (Bilimoria, 2009: 2015).

Similarly, early Orientalist scholars like Max Müller approached Indian texts such as the Vedas and Upaniṣads with the assumption that they were primarily theological or mystical works, if not arcane babblings of infantile philosophical endeavours. This Orientalist view framed these texts as rooted in speculative spirituality, contrasting them with the more advanced “rational” and “scientific” traditions of the West. For example, Müller’s reading of the *ṚgVeda* often highlighted its poetic and mythological aspects while neglecting its ritualistic and socio-political dimensions, creating a biased understanding of its socio-cultural significance (Bilimoria, 2015).

These examples illustrate Tuck’s suggestion that isogesis, while perhaps inevitable to some extent, can lead to reductive or skewed interpretations that fail to capture the full richness of Indian or other non-Western texts. This requires interpreters to engage with texts with self-awareness about their own positionalities. As Tuck notes, interpreters must strive to “put question marks behind all their interpretations” ensuring that their readings remain open to revision in light of the texts themselves and their cultural contexts (Tuck, 1990).

### Introduction to Upaniṣadic Interpretations

The project of interpreting the Upaniṣadic philosophy can parallel the work of Indian hermeneutics toward understanding the process of meaning-making from within the tradition. While methods from the West, such as those of Ricoeur, can be employed to create a context that decentres dominant themes such as *Brahman* or the idea of liberation (*mokṣa*) within the Upaniṣadic teachings, attention can also be shifted toward how meaning is understood within Indian philosophy. Applying a hermeneutics of suspicion to the dominant paradigm reveals that the texts aim to teach spiritual progress and encourage the attainment of *mokṣa* (liberation). Daya Krishna (2007: 114– 115) challenges the perception that Indian philosophy is primarily spiritual, mystical, or focused exclusively on liberation (*mokṣa*), arguing instead that it is a diverse intellectual tradition encompassing rigorous debates on metaphysics, epistemology, and moral vicissitudes. He suggests that among the four primary goals (*purusārtha-*s) — defined for Humankind, three were earlier goals—*dharma* (righteousness), *artha* (wealth), *kāma* (desire), and the fourth, *mokṣa* (liberation)—that seems tangential to the other goals He questions their conventional interpretation as a cohesive and unified framework within Indian philosophy. (ibid.: 114–115).

*[p. 520]* ^pg8

The challenge of interpreting texts can be understood through the theories of meaning that are inherent to the language in which the text is written, in this case, Sanskrit. Extrinsic meaning-making, which refers to understanding a composition, can be applied to larger sections of text or entire works, where a commentator situates the text and its sentences within a broader framework or paradigm. In this context, while interpreting meanings, particularly those of words, a linguistic etymology is feasible. We can follow suggestions like ‘distancing’ as proposed by Habermas to understand the conceptual origins of a word.

The tradition of commentary is especially relevant to philosophical and religious texts; thus, following a Gadamerian approach to their understanding is beneficial. Since Ricoeur brings both these approaches together, a Ricoeurian framework would be useful here. It is also important to note an intercultural difference in the etymology of East and West. In Western philosophy, translation etymology serves as a method to trace the evolution of concepts over time, deeply rooted in historical context. Conversely, in Indian traditions, particularly within the Sanskrit framework, a text known as *Nirukta* focuses on uncovering the intrinsic meanings of words to provide philosophical and soteriological insights. When translating a concept, a contextual analysis becomes possible by examining it within the body of commentaries and *bhāṣya-*s. Each commentator defines the meaning of a word or the purpose of a passage according to the philosophical paradigm they adopt.

For instance, the term “*Brahman*,” which is intrinsically translated as “the great” or “exalted,” can be understood as non-dual and devoid of qualities in Advaita philosophy. At the same time, a Dvaita scholar or a Vaiṣṇava might refer to it as the supreme deity Nārāyaṇa, who embodies all qualities. The *Nirukta* relies heavily on phenomenological and experiential insights, adopting a pragmatic approach to interpretation that does not adhere to a strictly historical paradigm. For example, in *Nirukta* 2.1, Yāska analyzes the term “*gau*” or “cow,” deriving it from the root, “√*gā*,” which means “to go,” indicating that its nature is to move or wander. This etymological meaning reflects the behavior of cows, showcasing a functional approach to Sanskrit etymology (Yāska, trans. Sarup, 1920: 24).

It is this functional perspective, situated within the broader tradition of Indian thought, that warrants attention. Therefore, as we translate or interpret passages from the Upaniṣads, we must remember that meanings are not solely derived from historical etymological sources but are contextually informed by pragmatic foundations rooted in both the phenomenology of experience and a functional paradigm that serves the overall purpose of a text or passage, which is culturally embedded within the work.

The hermeneutics of suspicion and its counterpart, the hermeneutics of trust, can be applied to a reading of the Upaniṣads. The purpose of the hermeneutics of suspicion is to critique the status quo, challenge dominant interpretations, and uncover truth, separating it from ideologies and cultural influences that may have infiltrated the interpretation of a text. When we apply this Ricoeurian method to understanding the Upaniṣads, we must also recognize the pre-existing narratives and discourses that shape how we read and interpret these texts. Olivelle (1998: 27) points out that although *Brahman* seems to be the central theme of the Upaniṣads, the texts cannot be reduced to a simple truth:

*[p. 521]* ^pg9

A close reader of these documents will note the diversity of goals that their authors pursue, chief among which are food, prosperity, power, fame, and a happy afterlife. There are rites to secure greatness, to win a woman’s love, to harm the lover of one’s wife, to ensure pregnancy, to guard against pregnancy, to assure a safe childbirth—the list can go on. Many scholars ignore these and similar passages in their search for the “philosophy” or “the fundamental conception” of the Upaniṣads. (ibid.)

On one hand, we need to examine the text within the context of Indian traditions of thought to identify the dominant paradigms of textual interpretation. On the other hand, we must engage with intercultural perspectives on the Upaniṣads that may or may not rely on these traditional interpretations. The second part of the Ricoeurian framework must address the concept of evil itself. We should explore the dominant discourses among various scholars who have discussed evil in Indian thought, particularly in relation to *karma* and suffering, and investigate whether there are alternative views or paradigms that have been suppressed or repressed in the understanding of evil.

### The Dominant Discourse: Brahman, Desire, and Liberation in Brahmasūtra

Ricoeur’s methodology employs both the hermeneutics of trust and the hermeneutics of suspicion to uncover the truth about the ‘not-good’ in Indian thought. Applying a hermeneutics of suspicion to the dominant paradigm reveals that the texts aim to teach spiritual progress and encourage the attainment of *mokṣa* (liberation). Desire and sense engagements are seen as the root of selfish desire, and evil therefore becomes interpreted as being rooted in sense gratification. The dominant interpretive discourses on the Upaniṣads have largely been shaped by a text known as the *Vedānta-sūtras,* or the *Brahmasūtra* (*BS*). This text is not only central to the Vedāntic tradition but also significant in the socio-cultural history of India. Its composition marked a turning point in the development of Indian philosophy, forming a coherent framework for understanding the texts of the Upaniṣads. The *Brahmasūtra* is believed to have been composed between the 2nd and 4th centuries CE or earlier, during a time when the Upaniṣads had already been written, and the Vedāntic Vedic tradition was being reinterpreted. These texts also responded to the rise of Buddhism and Jainism, which presented distinct views on the nature of reality and the self.

The *Brahmasūtra* serves as an interpretative text for the Upaniṣads, organizing and systematizing the metaphysical teachings necessary for understanding *Brahman*, *ātman,* and the nature of the world, while being deeply oriented towards a soteriological principle. Although the *Brahmasūtra* is rooted in Vedic thought, it also rep- resents an intellectual attempt to reconcile and integrate various themes presented in earlier texts. During the composition of the *Brahmasūtra,* the Brāhmaṇical society was a dominant force in the Indian subcontinent. Consequently, the *Brahmasūtra* became part of the tradition of *brāhmaṇa* scholarship, contributing to the consolidation of Vedic knowledge and orthodoxy.

The *Brahmasūtra* was composed in a milieu that responded to and systematized reactions to Buddhism and Jainism, which denied the existence of a universal soul as 

*[p. 522]* ^pg10

taught in the Upaniṣads. The *Brahmasūtra* and its interpretations have played a crucial role in shaping the contours of Indian philosophy and spirituality, contributing to the formation of monastic traditions, particularly the Vedānta schools, which remain influential in religious centres to this day.

This text fostered a transition away from polytheistic and ritualistic practices towards intellectual and philosophical pursuits. The very first aphorism sets the context of the text:

*Athāto Brahma-jijñāsā* Now, therefore, the inquiry into *Brahman*. (*BS* I.i.4 *in BSB,* Śaṅkara trans. Gambhirananda, 2011:6).

The text lays out the main theme of its discussion as *Brahman*, then a few aphorisms later establishes that the scriptures (*śāstra*-s) are the source of knowing the subject matter. (ibid.: 18–19) Then in the next *sūtra*, we are told.

*tat tu samanvayāt* But that, being the object of full import. (ibid.: 21)

 This aphorism insists that all the Upaniṣads become reconciled and noncontradictory if we understand that *Brahman* is the object of their explanations. The different schools of Vedānta, including Bhaktivedānta, integrated Īśvara or God into the system of the Vedas and Upaniṣads, leading to diverse interpretations of the Upaniṣads in contemporary times. Śaṅkara’s *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya* (*BSB*, 2011) also established the text as an influential canonical authority and a key foundation for Advaita Vedānta.

Therefore, to read the Upaniṣads without the support of the *Brahmasūtra* would be consistent with Ricoeurian method; we apply the hermeneutics of suspicion to the influences of this textual paradigm, reject its dominance, and instead trust the meaning–making paradigm of the Mīmāṃsā methods as a ‘decolonial hermeneutics of trust’. Using the Mīmāṃsā theory of meaning and the *Nirukta* methods, some interesting themes emerge. There appears to be a parallel in hermeneutics between the West and East, particularly in the case of Mīmāṃsā that is useful for our application of the theory. The Mīmāṃsā school distances itself from the speaker’s intention, suggesting that the speaker’s purpose does not play a role in meaning-making. This point of view is interesting as it suggests that to read a text from the authorless viewpoint would be a good way to clarify the import of a text.

### What, Why, and Where of Evil in Indian Philosophy

The question of why *evil* exists in the world at all presupposes that the world is created (or has somehow come about) as a benevolent, harmonious place. Any disruption of this harmony requires explanation. In Indian philosophy, particularly in the absence of a singular benevolent creator, the world is often seen as a natural creation.

*[p. 523]* ^pg11

The question of why evil exists in the world often translates to the broader inquiry of why bad things happen? This perspective is prevalent among scholars interpreting the concept of evil in Indian thought. It presupposes a benevolent world created for beings to thrive, where suffering requires explanation as it contrasts with this ideal. This assumption aligns with the notion of a created world devoid of evil, viewing evil as an obstacle to the flourishing of beings.

The ongoing struggle between good and harmful intentions is mirrored in Hindu theology, depicted as the eternal conflict between gods, demigods (*devata-*s*/deva*- s) and anti-gods/anti-demigods (*asura-*s). Mythological narratives portray demigods as benevolent and anti-demigods as malevolent, though it is important to note that anti-demigods are not always evil, nor are demigods always good. There is no singular embodiment of evil; rather, it is characterized by certain traits associated with arrogance, disruption of order, transgression of social norms, violence, jealousy, and negative emotions. Thus, evil presupposes the existence of negative emotions. Sometimes these are personified as beings via mythological looping.

Commentators and spiritual teachers interpret these mythical battles as allegorical representations of the internal moral struggles faced by individuals in choosing between actions driven by good or evil. In this context, evil serves as an impetus or catalyst for action rather than the action itself. When viewed as a source of harm, evil closely aligns with the concept of *karma*, acting as a condition that leads to wrongful actions rather than being inherently evil as a direct cause.

The prevailing interpretation suggests that the texts such as the Upaniṣads aim to guide individuals toward spiritual progress and the attainment *of mokṣa* (liberation). This historical perspective has been shaped by Bhakti and Śramaṇa traditions, as well as by Orientalist views that regard Indian philosophical systems as mystical, spiritual, and world-denying. Within this framework, evil becomes a moral obstacle to achieving *mokṣa*, leading to the perception of desire as evil.

In both Ricoeur’s framework and Indian philosophy, evil is viewed as an existential problem that requires a response. While the dominant narrative suggests that evil necessitates a response through spiritual liberation, Ricoeur argues for moral reconciliation in addressing the problem of evil. The Upaniṣads advocate for alignment, harmony, and recognition of the universe’s interconnectedness as a means of reconciling evil. Evil arises from separateness, while the absence of separateness or individuality signifies goodness. Therefore, the concept of evil can be understood as a disconnection from one’s collective identity and a failure to be part of the whole system.

### The Concept of Evil in Indian Thought

In Indian thought by and large evil is understood as botha moral and metaphysical concept, closely tied to ignorance and the disruption of *dharma*, or cosmic order. *Karma* plays a central role in explaining suffering, emphasizing individual responsibility while allowing for divine intervention that can supersede it. The cycle of rebirth perpetuates suffering, linking it to past actions and ignorance.

*[p. 524]* ^pg12

Evil, as a philosophical concept, is typically associated with ethical or moral systems, and many Indian philosophical traditions, including those found in the Upaniṣads, grapple with the consequences of moral actions. One central idea is *karma*, which links actions in this life to their consequences, either in this life or in future ones. The idea of evil in Indian philosophy has been explored in debates around *karma*, suffering, and moral action. Metaphysical evil in Hinduism centres around the concept of *avidyā*, or ignorance. This ignorance leads individuals to identify with transient material desires, resulting in pain and dissatisfaction. Thus, in Indian philosophy, suffering is directly tied to *karma*; evil deeds create negative *karma*, resulting in suffering in this life or future lives. Consequently, suffering in this lifetime is often seen as a result of past actions (Gächter, 2000).

Suffering is viewed as a means of purification, allowing individuals to recognize their mistakes and align themselves with *dharma*. The interplay of good and evil highlights the spiritual struggles inherent in human life. In this sense, the purpose of suffering is to foster growth and spiritual evolution. Evil acts as a catalyst, playing a crucial role in enhancing the divine plan within mythologies. The suffering caused by evil compels individuals to seek liberation from the cycle of birth and death.

Bilimoria (2013) for instance, interprets evil within a framework akin to Leibniz’s understanding of “natural evil” and distinguishes between intentional evil and the principle of *karma*. He also examines the concept of *duḥkha* (suffering) in various Indian traditions, noting that it arises from a universal principle of causality. However, Bilimoria highlights a critical question: why do good and bad deeds exist in the first place? What makes them metaphysically inevitable? He suggests that moral reasoning requires a deeper explanation for the origin of good and evil, beyond simple causality.

Chadha and Trakakis (2007) criticize the claim by one Kaufman, according to which *karma* is inadequate as a systematic theodicy toward explaining suffering. Kaufman (2005) had argued that the connection between *karma* and suffering is ambiguous and cannot fully account for the nature of evil. This view was challenged by scholars Chadha and Trakakis, who argued that Kaufman oversimplified the doctrine of *karma*, reducing it to a simplistic moral framework.

Gächter (1998: 393) examines the origins, roles, and implications of evil and suffering within Indian philosophy, particularly Hinduism. He categorizes the concepts of evil and suffering, drawing from Sāṁkhya philosophy, which classifies suffering into physical, mental, external, and spiritual forms, often caused by supernatural influences.

He also highlights the dual nature of divine and demonic entities in Hindu mythology, where demigods embody both good and evil traits. The *asura*-s often represent the darker aspects of creation, yet they are integral to the cosmic balance maintained by the demigods. This dynamic struggle between the demigods and the anti-demigods symbolizes the tension between order and chaos, underscoring the necessity of evil as a counterpoint to good in the cosmic cycle. (Ibid.: 395).

Among the types of evil that Gächter (ibid.: 393) describes, a type of evil causing suffering is moral evil, which arises from individual actions that violate ethical norms, such as theft, violence, or dishonesty. In Hinduism, these actions are termed *adharma*, which disrupts *dharma* and leads to pain or hardship. Other forms of evil, 

*[p. 525]* ^pg13

that can be called natural evils, include earthquakes, diseases, and famine, which are viewed as manifestations of evil forces (Bilimoria, 2006: 172). People believe that these events may not be directly related to human actions and can be attributed to angry demigods or unnatural occurrences caused by otherworldly beings. In India it can be commonly observed that rituals, astrology, and societal customs play significant roles in mediating the effects of evil and suffering for people. Belief in supernatural beings, such as spirits and ghosts, influences everyday practices and reflects the pervasive presence of metaphysical evil. The connection between evil and suffering is fundamentally causal; evil deeds are seen as the cause of suffering.

The personification of evil as a ‘singular antidivine agency’ is largely absent in myths although certain individuals in mythology embody evil traits or can be sources of it. Gächter (1998: 394–395) lists people, malevolent planets and so on. Divine intervention is frequently depicted as restoring harmony and aiding the virtuous. This is articulated through the eternal struggle between the *deva-*s (demigods) and *asura-*s (anti-demigods) in mythological tales as described earlier. The *deva*-s are portrayed as benevolent and unselfish, while the *asura*-s are seen as harmful. These narratives emphasize the wickedness of the *asura-*s, who often seek self-gratification and authoritarian power, transgressing societal norms. Commentators and spiritual teachers often interpret the stories of the conflict between *asura-*s and *sura-*s as allegorical, representing the internal moral struggles of individuals choosing between good and evil actions.

However, the myths also suggest that these qualities are not inherent to the *asura*-s and *sura*-s. There are noble *asura-*s, such as Prahlāda, a devoted follower, and ignoble figures among the demigods, like Nahuṣa, who is a designated king of the demigods in the post of Indra. Evil is thus viewed as a set of qualities associated with the disrup- tion of individual and social order, including *hiṃsā* (violence), *mada* (arrogance), and *mātsarya* (jealousy), which signify wickedness.

The historical development of the concept of *mokṣa*, influenced by the Śramaṇa and the Bhakti or devotional traditions within theology, may be beyond the scope of this paper. However, it can be speculated that this concept was also perpetrated by the Oriental project, which portrayed Indian philosophical systems as mystical, spiritual, and world-denying. Within this framework, evil is perceived as a moral obstacle to achieving *mokṣa*, with desire often articulated as evil. The interpretations of *śreyas*, *preyas*, *hita*, and *apriya* in the Upaniṣads reinforce this view. If we set aside the notion of *mokṣa* as the primary intent of the Upaniṣadic texts, the mythology of the demigods and anti-demigods and the human struggle against evil can be interpreted differently, focusing on the relationship between the body and the spirit.

These debates highlight the complexities in interpreting evil and its relationship to moral action within Indian philosophy. The Upaniṣads, with their allegorical stories about the body, the demigods, and anti-demigods, provide rich material toward exploring these themes. These myths and allegorical tales are not mere imaginative passages, rather they serve to convey philosophical insights. Most of these myths have been interpreted from the within the dominant paradigm given by the intent of the text to provide the reader/listener with sufficient spiritual wisdom to attain *mokṣa*.

### The Mythological and Philosophical Understanding of Evil

Taking the above discussion further, we find in the *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* (*CU*,  1942)  and *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* Upaniṣad (*BṛhU*,  1950; 1998)  narratives in which moral struggles and ethical dilemmas are presented through metaphorical and allegorical narratives, often involving bodily functions and sensory experiences Allegory, while akin to myth, employs structured symbolism where characters and events correspond to specific moral or philosophical ideas. Allegory, like myth, operates on a narrative level, but while an allegory has a one-to-one correspondence between symbols and meanings, myths have a more open-ended, dynamic interpretative structure. Underlying all these is symbolism, the essential mechanism that enables myths to resonate across generations and cultures.

In Indian philosophy, metaphor plays a fundamental role in both aesthetic theory and scriptural interpretation. Arundhati Mukherji (2024) highlights how classical Indian thinkers, such as Mammaṭa and Daṇḍin, conceptualized metaphor (*rūpaka*) as an essential element of poetic expression. In the Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā traditions, metaphor is discussed under *lakṣaṇā* (secondary meaning), where it is acknowledged as a legitimate but derived mode of signification. (ibid.: 834–835) Such usage of metaphor in texts like the *Upaniṣads* and epic poetry conveys deeper philosophical truths and bridges the gap between the literal and the ineffable. This is reflective of Ricoeur’s ideas that metaphor changes the way we perceive the world:

According to Ricoeur, within the semantic deviance, the self-destruction of meaning is just the other side of bringing in new ideas in meaning at the level of the entire statement—new ideas come through the twist of the literal meaning of the words. (ibid.: 838)

For Ricoeur, metaphor is not just a rhetorical device but a way of reshaping reality by linking seemingly unrelated concepts to create new meaning. Similarly, myths function as extended metaphors, offering symbolic narratives that communicate deeper existential, moral, or spiritual truths.

In the Upaniṣads, some of the mythological narratives that illustrate the moral struggle between the immortal demigods (*deva-*s) and their cousins, the anti-demigods (*asura*-s). Of interest to us is the mythological story presented in the *CU* and *BṛhU*. Analyzing the myths about the body and the senses in this instance reveals the use of a word, *pāpmā*, evil (or sinful or wicked, cf *BṛhU* 1.3.2). A summary of this story in *BṛhU* 1.3.118, is given below. (For a complete translation, See Olivelle (1998: 41.) The metaphor moves us away from the usual way of understanding evil as an accounting system, a cause for suffering. Instead, the semantic twist reveals evil as a weapon, something that damages or destroys equanimity and unity and forces binary thinking and likes and dislikes in an individual.

The narration begins with a context that there is a conflict between the *deva-s* and the *asura*-s. To gain victory, the demigods decided to meditate on the syllable ‘Oṃ’ as a means of overcoming their adversaries. Initially, they meditated on the breath (vital force in the nose), giving it the exalted position the chanter of *Ut*-*Gītā* (the High chant). However, the nose became afflicted by vice (*pāpmā*):

*[p. 527]* ^pg15

Then the gods told breath: “Sing the High Chant for us.” Breath said, “Very well,” and sang the High Chant for them. It procured for them by that singing whatever useful there is in breath; it keeps for itself whatever is pleasant in what it smells. The demons thought: “With this as their *Udgātr* they are sure to overcome us.” So they rushed at it and riddled it with evil. That evil is the disagreeable things a person smells—they are that very evil. (ibid.: 41) Undeterred, the demigods continued to elevate each of the sense organs in turn, hoping to find one that could withstand corruption. They meditated on the organ of hearing, the organ of touch, and the mind, all as *Ut*-*Gītā*, but each succumbed to the influence of the anti-demigods. Finally, they meditated on the vital force (*prāṇa*), which proved incorruptible. According to the mythology, the anti-demigods who approached *prāṇa* were utterly destroyed. The story conveys that one who possesses this kind of knowledge — akin to the vital force — becomes impervious to evil, and any attempt to harm such a person ultimately boomerangs on the aggressor:

So they rushed at it \[the vital force in the mouth, *prāṇa*\] and tried to riddle it with evil. But, like a clod of earth hurled against a rock, they were smashed to bits flying in all directions and perished. As a result, the gods prospered, while the demons came to ruin. When someone knows this, he himself will prosper, while a rival who hates him will come to ruin. (1.3.7 *BrhU*, ibid.: 41) The text repeatedly emphasizes the concept of evil using the term *pāpmanā* (instrumental of *pāpmā/papman*). However, Śaṅkara’s commentary on this section offers limited discussion about the deeper implications of being “struck by evil.”

This suggests that *pāpmā* (evil) causes the binary perception of good and bad smells, a theory of corruption of senses explained in the *BṛhU* which states that improper smells arise when the sense organ is corrupted by evil (*BṛhU* 1.3.3, trans. Madhavananda, 1950: 56–57).

A second similar myth within *CU* presents the same theme. Here, the sense organs engage in a competition to determine which among them is the greatest and eldest. Following the advice of a teacher, each organ departs from the body for a year to test its significance. Upon returning, they find that the body has survived their absence, though in impaired states—for example, as blind, mute, or mentally incapacitated. However, when the vital force (*prāṇa*) prepares to leave, the other organs halt it, recognizing that without *prāṇa*, none of them can function.

In his analysis of this same myth, in the *CU*, Śaṅkara notes that *pāpmā* can corrupt perception, as seen in the phrase:

*pāpmanāḥ eṣa vidadhāti jagrāhāti ubhayaṃ surabhī ca durgandhī ca* “Since this one has been afflicted with vice, a person inhales both fragrance and fetid through the vital force”. (Śaṅkara, cites verse 1.3.3 from *BṛhU* in the commentary to verse *CU* 1.2.2 (*Śaṅkara-bhāṣya*, 1942), trans. Gambhirananda, 1983: 22).

*[p. 528]* ^pg16

The myth of the senses in the Upaniṣads presents the idea that evil exists outside the self rather than within it. The narratives indicate that the senses are susceptible to external influences, particularly from evil, which appears to be a weapon wielded by anti-demigods. This evil seems to arise from transgressions against the unity of the body and lacks its own agency; instead, it is utilized by others to harm the senses. The aftermath of being affected by evil is evident, as the senses exhibit a tendency to perceive in binaries.

The entry of evil appears to be contingent upon qualitative factors, characterized by an arrogance among the senses, which compete for power, recognition, and superiority over one another. In contrast, the *deva*-s view these senses as potential defences that can aid them in performing the *Ut-Gītā*, or the Song of Elevation (AUṂ or OṂ, that is called a high chant). However, there is a political struggle to assert their indispensability and suitability for purposes beyond mere sensing. Consequently, the senses are limited in their capabilities, and this limitation serves as a precursor to their vulnerability to evil. They do not embody the heroic qualities associated with the vital force to overcome conflict.

The mythology of the senses indicates that they were struck by evil rather than the *prāṇa*, which leads commentaries to conclude that sensory desires are inherently evil. The interpretation of the Kathopaniṣad, where Yama discusses the concepts of *preyas* and *śreyas*, further reiterates this narrative. *Preyas* represents pleasure (good for the senses), while *śreyas* signifies what is good (for the self as *ātman*) or good-in-itself. These concepts are positioned at opposite ends of the spectrum of likes and dislikes, as well as good and pleasurable. In the context of the *mokṣa* paradigm, the pleasurable is often rejected in favour of the sublime good, the excellences. Again, we see that the dominance of the fourth goal, *mokṣa* is placed over the other two, particularly, *artha* (material thriving) and *kāma*, (desire). If these goals were equitable, how does *kāma* get a negative connotation? This would be the question highlighted by a hermeneutics of suspicion.

Throughout these narratives, the sense organs are repeatedly “struck by evil” (that is, *pāpmā*), resulting in possessing an embodied condition or absorbed evil which introduces binary distinctions—such as good versus bad—and disrupts their equanimity. This notion of evil as a force that disturbs harmony and creates judgment aligns with the text’s broader exploration of *pāpmā*. Evil is thus depicted as that which disrupts the stable state of equanimity, causing imbalance and division.

A search for this term and its contexts in the *Upaniṣads* and other texts should provide a vignette into the forms of evil depicted.

### Pāpmā as Blemish: Evil as Imperfection or Impurity

The trope ‘*pāpmā*’ embodies the concept of sin or impurity as an abstract entity or condition. In philosophical texts, such as passages from the Upaniṣad, it is interpreted without the ‘hermeneutics of suspicion;’ it is said to refer to moral, ethical, or spiritual blemishes that obstruct self-realization or liberation. *Chāndogya* described the self (*ātman)* as being in essence free from sin or blemishes – *pāpmā* (*yaḥ ātmā apahatapāpmā)* (*CU* 8.7.1). There is no such existential condition as ‘immortal (mortal) sin’ or ‘original sin’ that one finds in Christianity.

Other terms for evil mentioned in the Vedic literature include *drugdha*, a misdeed or wicked conduct that is named after a demon, *enas*, (misdeeds that one has 

*[p. 529]* ^pg17

to be freed from) *āgas*, (often mentioned in the negative, *anāgas,* used in the sense of ‘blameless/sinless’), and *anṛta* (lying or untruth or untoward) (Rodhe,  1946: 140–141). These are more usually than not predicated on the correct performance of prescribed rituals and the avoidance of any glitches that might occur, which would otherwise entail transgression of *ṛta* (order), *dharma* (rules or law) and against the gods’ good-will as well. The word *pāpa* (Hindi (*pāp*), which generally signifies sin as privation or demerit related to untoward action (with its opposite in *puṇya,* merit), is now commonly used in many Indian languages. The term has the connotation of wrongdoing in Vedic literature: “As a rule, *pāpa* is used adjectively, characterizing an evil-minded man or woman or an evil deed. *Pāpman* is a noun that comprises all sorts of evils.” (ibid.: 36) The word occurs particularly in the *Atharva Veda* (AV), where it is primarily used in conjunction with the intention to eliminate, get rid of, or shed something. In another AV-hymn, (VI. 26. 1.), the prayer is directed to the personified Evil: “Deliver (*ava*-*sṛja*) me, Evil (*pāpman*) Mighty One, vouchsafe upon us. Put me, Evil, unhurt in the world of happiness (*bhadra*)” (translation cited in Rodhe, 1946:33). *Pāpmā* also occurs in the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* (10.73.1–6; *pāpmānaṃ me praṇāśaya:* dispel my sins. *ŚB* 8.16.27; 14.1.1.13).

Its etymology can be broken down as being derived from the root-verb *√pa,* which curiously has two meanings, “to protect” and “to drink”. The first verbal root refers to safeguarding or upholding something, such as *dharma* or cosmic order, or being protected against untoward conditions, affliction–thus. *nṛpa*, *pātṛ* (ruler, protector. The second root, when combined with “*apaḥ”*, (*pa* + *apaḥ* = *pāpaḥ,)* signifies “drinking \[polluted\] water, and serves as a metonymn for impurity, diseased, thus \[in time\] evil. *Pāpma* is the derived feminine noun; *pāpam* as the masculine/neuter singular form. (See the grammar rule for ‘*pa* + *rakṣaṇam’ in Uṇādi*-*Sūtras* by Svetavanavāsin (1992: 104)). Adding the suffix “*na”* is a declension that yields the instrumental singular *pāpmanā*, denoting “by or through evil as a sinful deed and its outcome”.

*Pāpman* takes various grammatical forms in references that sound similar but are derivatives of this form, such as when it is contained in something, that object becomes *pāpmān,* an agentive noun, holder of *pāpman* (in the sense of ‘tainted’). The suffix *iman* is used to form abstract nouns, often indicating a state, condition, or superlative state (see Whitney, 1879, Chapter XVII). When combined, *pāpa* + *iman* becomes *pāpman* grammatically indicating the abstract condition of possessing sinfulness or impurity. For example, a specific occurrence of the word *pāpmān* (most evil) in the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* (verse 14.1.1.31) forbid the ritualist from associating with forbidden people and omens:

…*na prekṣeta necriyaṃ ca pāpmānaṃ ca nejjyotiśca tamaśca netsatyānṛte sa ṃsṛjānīti* … he should not look at these, lest he should mingle excellence and sin, light and darkness, truth and untruth. (*ŚB* trans. Eggling, 1905) 

In the *Purāṇa* and *Smrti* texts, it is used in moral and legal contexts to denote transgressions against *dharma*. Similarly in the *Bhagavadgītā,* where Krishna sermons

Arjuna on the excesses of “*pāpam”* as “*mahā-pāpmā”* (mighty sin). (*BhG,*  2006, 3.37)

Similar action words mentioned in the Vedic corpus include *kilbiṣa* (offense) and *heḍana* (aggravation), which are forbidden actions that offend the demigods in ritual texts (Rodhe, 1946:141–142). According to philological classification, it originates from an Indo-Aryan root meaning “bad” (through Proto-Indo-Iranian, *pāpás*), although this cannot be historically verified.

Similarly, rituals to ward off or dispel *pāpman* are mentioned in the *Atharva Veda* and other texts. (ibid.: 33–34). In that sense, *pāpman* is an evil that one must be liberated from, implying a state of affairs rather than an action. Technically, we have the pair of merit-demerit and good-evil as two different categories of evil. The term *pāpman* (evil) is often quoted as the opposite of *bhadra* (good), and *pāpa* (demerit) is opposed to *puṇya* (merit)*.* 

 Rodhe (1946: 30), however, claims that the etymology of the term *pāpa* is ambiguous, with multiple interpretations. Nevertheless, the above-mentioned shorter form is rarely found in the Vedas, although Sāyaṇa, the commentator, uses it to interpret texts. It is worth noting that in most references, the alternate word, *pāpman,* used is closer to the notion of impurity or taint acquired through wrong action and not the action itself.

The hermeneutical interpretation of this unique term *pāpmā* is a special case of a process of meaning-making in Indian philosophy that comes to us from the Mīmāṃsā tradition of Vedic exegesis. The word is interpreted in context, not through its literal meaning as a negative prefix. In Sanskrit, a negative prefix is a tool used to negate or reverse the definition of a root word. In the context of *pāpmā,* the prefix that creates the “negative” sense isn’t explicitly stand-alone or given but is instead inferred through etymological analysis or traditional interpretation. The negative implication of *pāpmā* (demerit, ill-intended or inauspicious act, misdemeanour, arguably ‘sin’, ‘evil’) arises not from an explicit prefix like *a-* or *duḥ-,* but rather from a semantic shift or reinterpretation: *Pāpmā* can be understood as “that which leads to a failure to protect” or “that which goes against *dharma*.” In traditional etymological explanations or Vedic hermeneutics (*Nirukta*), such terms are often interpreted in a moral or spiritual framework. Sanskrit employs a method of deriving meaning that goes beyond the mere combination of roots, stems, and suffixes. Scholars often interpret words through etymology (*Nirukta*), which involves breaking a word into its constituent parts so as to uncover its deeper or symbolic meaning. However, the word can also be interpreted from the context of its usage in the specific text or philosophical frame-work. Very unique to the sacred literature is the possibility of what can be referred to as Philosophical Hermeneutics (*adhyātma-mīmāṃsā*) where a moral, spiritual, or allegorical significance is assigned to words, often in alignment with the text’s purpose. This interpretative flexibility is crucial for conveying complex ideas in ethics or moral ideals of *dharma*. In this form of philosophical interpretation or investigation, the meaning of *pāpmā* is understood in relation to its opposite.

*[p. 531]* ^pg19

Deliverance from this evil is like an act of washing off, or casting off, or being protected from the affliction —*pāpman—* is a frequent reference in many other Upaniṣads, and Rodhe (1946:35–36) cites examples from *Praśnopaniṣad* besides its occurrence in *CU* and *BṛhU*, which we also noted earlier*:*

In the ritual texts, belonging to the social stage of life, it indicates disease, death, and other evils threatening life; in the texts belonging to the forest stage of life, it indicates life and all sorts of work fettering man in life, good as well as evil ones. In both kinds of texts, however, deliverance from evil is wanted. To express the idea of deliverance, various verbs are used, *muñcati* as well as others.

The concept of evil as ontological and embodied is evidenced by the broader use of the word in the texts mentioned so far. The nature of affliction in the case of *pāpman* remains existential — sometimes objectified, and sometimes personified —it afflicts an embodied being. Here, the evil is located outside the human; a person is a victim of the state. However, as transgression of law and dharma, evil actions performed by a person fall under the moral and ethical register. This evil seems to arise out of wilful action, which becomes popular as *pāpa* (translated as transgression, in later post-Vedic references, coming to stand for demerit gained). This moral transgression can also be linked to ignorance, thereby becoming an obstacle to the ultimate good, liberation.

The tradition of Vedānta by interpreting *pāpmā* as “that which obstructs liberation,” aligns the term with the broader goals of Indian philosophy—such as the pursuit of *mokṣa* (liberation) and overcoming ignorance or sin. In other words, this is a state of ‘taintability’, a state of evil or impurity due to which imperfections can occur in thought, action, or speech. This rendering of evil as *pāpman* will therefore fall under the category of human evil or imperfection in opposition to a virtuous or dharmic mind, moral state, rather than an embodied state as might have been the original import. Depending on the text, the meaning signifies some form of blemish or obstacle.

### Senses, Liberation, and Suffering

There are references to *pāpman* in the *Brahmasūtra* and its associated commentaries, such as Śańkara’s *Bhāṣyaʼ,* as indicated earlier. *Pāpman*, a Sanskrit term often translated as “evil” or “sin,” appears in discussions related to metaphysical concepts of impurity, ignorance, and obstruction to self-realization. While the term *pāpman* itself may not occur frequently, its thematic implications are discussed. These *sūtras* explore how the knowledge of *Brahman* eliminates all kinds of impurities (*doṣāpaha*), which commentators often equate with *pāpmān*. Śaṅkara interprets such impurities as obstructions caused by ignorance and karmic residues. The discussion of the *jīva’*s (soul’s) journey after death addresses the purification process, implying the removal of *pāpman* through the realization of the self’s unity with *Brahman*. (*Śaṅkarabhāṣya BSB*, trans. Gambhirananda, 2011:26–29) Śaṅkara elaborates on the concept of *pāpman* in his commentary on the *Brahmasūtra.* He frequently connects *pāpman* to the ignorance (*avidyā*) and *karma* that cloud the true nature of the self. According to him, *pāpman* is not an intrinsic part of the self (*ātman)* but is superim

*[p. 532]* ^pg20

posed due to ignorance; while the realization of *Brahman*, facilitated by scriptural study, meditation, and renunciation, eradicates *pāpman* entirely. Later references in *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (*BP* Canto 6), continue this discourse through the dualism of Dvaita philosophy, the supreme deity playing a role in eliminating the evil that is present in our minds, through listening to the stories of the deity:

*varṇayāmi mahā-puṇyaṁ sarva-pāpa-haraṁ nṛṇām/yador vaṁśaṁ naraḥ śrutvā sarva-pāpaiḥ pramucyate* (*BP* 9.23.18–19 trans. Prabhupāda, 1972–1977) Earlier Indian thought emphasizes human agency as a means to overcoming ignorance and the effects of past actions through spiritual practices, ethical living, and adherence to *dharma*. In contrast, Ricoeur’s perspective on human agency is more complex, focusing on the moral responsibility of individuals to recognize evil and address its roots through personal reflection and social responsibility. In the Upaniṣadic narrative, the battle between the demigods and anti-demigods symbolizes the moral struggle within the self, where the senses and the mind are prone to corruption, while the breath or vital force represents the pure essence that sustains life. By using Ricoeur’s hermeneutical methods, we can gain deeper insights into these ancient narratives, exploring how the idea of evil evolves from a personal struggle to a broader social and cultural issue.

The idea that occurs to us in this narrative is that the senses are somehow more vulnerable than the *prāṇa* to evil (*pāpman*). Does this, then, imply that there is a hierarchy of resistance or defence that the *prāṇa* has which the senses do not have? One might think so, but then the Upaniṣads also clarify that the senses are prone to seeing the world as divisive and in the framework of duality as good and evil.

The potency to exclude and significantly create the other becomes a precondition to be ‘struck by evil’ or being corrupted. In other words, the question that the Upaniṣads ask is perhaps centred around— ‘what makes life possible?’ — rather than — ‘what makes liberation possible?’ Seen from the vital force point of view, the question can be read as ‘who is more essential to the embodied condition than the senses?’. It is important to note that death is not evil. As there is no permanent end with the death of the body but only a transfer to another embodied state; death is not taboo in Indian philosophy in general. A point to note is that though the theory of the vital force is strongly prevalent in the continuity of Indian traditions within the other philosophical systems such as *Sāṃkhya* and *Yoga*, the naturalistic vital force theory was rejected as a naïve understanding of the embodied condition by many Western philosophers, including the precursors to scientific biological thought.

In these stories, where senses are first venerated as ultimate, they are nevertheless susceptible to corruption by evil. The breath as vital force (sometimes called the main *prāṇa)*, however, represents the essence of life, which remains untainted by pride and duality. This narrative mirrors the philosophical idea that *prāṇa* is a vital force, central to the unity and integrity of the self, and cannot be corrupted by the same forces that affect the senses or the mind.

Examining the mythic philosophy of the Upaniṣads reveals that the idea of evil is recognized through the understanding of duality and the binaries of the world, rather than perceiving cosmic oneness and interconnectedness as a singular entity. Frequent 

*[p. 533]* ^pg21

references to casting away this evil state are made in the Upaniṣads, which refer to binaries. Rodhe (1946: 35) points out the list of evils mentioned in the *CU*:

…the evils are exemplified as follows: day and night, old age (*jarā*), death (*mṛtyu*) sorrow (*śoka*) good-work (*sukṛta*) evil-work (*duṣkṛta*). Here the whole life and everything characteristic of it, including good and evil works, is regarded as an evil, *pāpman* which disappears in the Brahman-world. One interpretation of evil is that divisiveness and non-inclusivity are forms of evil. Thus, approaching narratives to confront and understand evil involves reconciling it by embracing moral responsibility and interpreting the meaning of suffering within historical and social contexts.

Ricoeur’s phenomenological approach, which emphasizes the lived experience of the body, can help illuminate the philosophical significance of these myths. For Ricoeur, evil is initially understood in physical terms — affecting the integrity of the body — but later becomes internalized as sin or guilt. He uses myths, such as the Babylonian, Greek, and biblical stories, to explore whether evil happens to us or whether it is a matter of choice and will.

When examining Paul Ricoeur’s understanding of evil alongside the understanding of evil in Indian thought, we observe several distinctions. The concept of evil in Indian thought often perceives it as impersonal and metaphysical, arising from ignorance or attachment, and is deeply connected to the moral consequences of one’s actions rather than being an inherently evil force. In contrast, Ricoeur’s understanding of evil emphasizes its personal and moral nature, linking it to the freedom and responsibility of individuals for their choices. In the text of the Upaniṣads, we see that the senses make a moral choice to detach from the body, thereby becoming subject to suffering. In this sense, it is the will of the senses to seek independence and freedom from what they perceive as part of a collective, an embodied condition that makes them susceptible to evil once again. Ricoeur discusses suffering as an integral aspect of evil and a moral condition tied to human capacity for free will. For Ricoeur, suffering caused by evil actions becomes an existential question that necessitates understanding and reconciliation.

Paul Ricoeur’s theory of evil also explores its transformation from external mythic representations to an internalized moral struggle within individuals. In *The Symbol* *ism of Evil* (1967), Ricoeur discusses how early mythological accounts depict evil as a cosmic force, often personified through symbolic narratives of chaos, demons, or primordial disorder. Over time, these externalized representations shift toward an ethical and existential understanding of evil, where human beings grapple with guilt, transgression, and the burden of moral responsibility. Ricoeur emphasizes the role of narrative and metaphor in shaping these evolving conceptions, arguing that myths and religious texts encode the progressive internalization of evil. In later works, such as *Evil: A Challenge to Philosophy and Theology* (2004), he deepens this analysis by exploring the tragic dimension of human existence, where moral failure becomes an inherent aspect of human freedom. Ricoeur’s approach highlights how societies move from viewing evil as an external conflict to understanding it as a fundamental 

*[p. 534]* ^pg22

part of human nature, reflecting an increasing moral complexity in philosophical and theological thought (Ricoeur, 1967, p. 158; 2004: 92).

### Myth, Metaphor, and Evil in Cosmic Cycle of Time

Interestingly, Arundhati Mukherji’s (2024) approach to metaphor emphasizes its transformative power in shaping human understanding by stretching concepts beyond their literal meanings. She argues that metaphor does not merely embellish language but serves as a crucial cognitive tool that generates new knowledge by altering the relationships between familiar ideas. Drawing from both Western and Indian traditions, she highlights how metaphor creates meaning through semantic tension, allowing for deeper philosophical insights. Mythological narratives, such as those found in the *Purāṇas*, *Rāmāyaṇa*, and *Mahābhārata*, employ metaphor to illustrate moral and cosmic transformations. Mukherji suggests that when read metaphorically, these texts reveal evolving ethical structures rather than fixed historical accounts.

Symbolism serves as the foundation that allows both myth and metaphor to resonate across generations, shaping cultural and moral understanding. Ricoeur’s approach suggests that these symbolic frameworks are not fixed but dynamic, requiring interpretation to uncover their layered meanings.

One striking example of this evolving symbolic landscape is found in the Hindu concept of *Kali Yuga*, the final stage of the cosmic cycle, marked by moral and spiritual decline. The shifting portrayal of good and evil across the four cosmic-ages mirrors the progression from clear-cut moral binaries to increasing ethical ambiguity. Just as Ricoeur’s hermeneutics emphasizes the need to interpret myths and metaphors within their cultural contexts, the narrative of the *yuga*-s invites reflection on the nature of morality, order, and human evolution. Ricoeur’s hermeneutic approach suggests that understanding these symbolic forms requires interpretation, as their meanings are never fixed but evolve through cultural and historical contexts. Ultimately, for Ricoeur, myth, metaphor, allegory, and symbolism are all part of the broader human endeavor to grasp the ineffable — to articulate what cannot be fully expressed in literal terms, shaping how we perceive and engage with the world.

For example, the *Yuga* cycles metaphorically depict the gradual internalization of moral struggle, shifting from an external battle between gods and demons in *Satya Yuga* to an internal conflict within individuals in *Kali Yuga*.

Indian philosophy envisions the cosmic cycle of time through the *yuga-*s—*Satya* *(Kṛta)*, *Tretā*, *Dvāpara*, and *Kali*—each representing evolving degrees of spiritual and moral decline as well as constricting (near collapse) of time (*kāla*). In this paradigm, the division between good and evil is not an absolute dichotomy but a dynamic spectrum that shifts as the cosmic cycle progresses. Philosophically, this progression reflects the interplay between *dharma (*righteous duty) and *adharma* (unrighteousness).

This framework establishes the basis for understanding how the separation of moral forces mirrors cosmological cycles. The metaphysical separation of good and evil across *yuga-*s invites deep contemplation about the nature of human existence and the universal quest for spiritual evolution. As each *yuga* unfolds, the inherent dualisms—light versus dark, order versus chaos—are reinterpreted to accommodate 

*[p. 535]* ^pg23

the evolving human condition. This dynamic interplay suggests that while the cosmic order may present distinct stages of moral deterioration, the potential for redemption and awakening remains ever-present.

In the first age, *Kṛta-yuga* (or *Satya-yuga* (‘golden age’)), good and evil were distinctly separated by geography, with virtue prevailing in divine realms and vice confined to demonic realms. This cosmic divide is illustrated in the *Samudra Manthana* (Churning of the Ocean) episode from the *Purāṇas*, where the *deva*-s (demigods) and *asura*-s (antidemigods) who reside in separate worlds yet come into conflict when they collaborate to extract *amṛta* (nectar of immortality). The ensuing struggle, in which the *deva*-s ultimately triumph with the aid of Lord Viṣṇu, reflects the moral clarity of this era, where good and evil were absolute and clearly demarcated.

This distinct separation began to blur in *Tretā-yuga*, where good and evil coexisted within different communities, leading to inter-societal conflicts. The *Rāmāyaṇa* exemplifies this shift, portraying the war between the righteous kingdom of Ayodhyā, led by Rāma, and the *rākṣasa* (demon) kingdom of Laṅkā, ruled by Rāvaṇa. Unlike in *Satya-yuga*, where good and evil belonged to different cosmic planes, *Tretā-yuga* presents them as opposing forces within the earthly realm, engaging in battle over moral and ethical differences.

By *Dvāpara-yuga*, the boundary between good and evil further collapsed, with virtue and vice coexisting within the same family. The *Mahābhārata* epitomizes this internal moral struggle through the rivalry between the Pāṇḍavas and the Kauravas — cousins raised within the same royal household and yet they embodied contrasting values. Unlike earlier *yugas*, where conflicts occurred between clearly defined groups, *Dvāpara-yuga* presents a more complex moral dynamic, where familial ties do not ensure ethical alignment.

Finally, in the present age of *Kali-yuga*, good and evil are no longer confined to distinct locations, communities, or families but exist within the same individual. Moral ambiguity and ethical dilemmas define this age, where virtue and vice inter- mingle within a single person’s heart. The shifting portrayal of good and evil across the *yugas* underscores a broader philosophical reflection on the gradual deterioration of moral clarity over time, as depicted in Hindu scriptures (González-Reimann, 2021: 251– 252). The *Kali-yuga* might also bring about *pralaya*, or the end period of the total cosmic period, *kalpa*, awaiting or leading to the birth of new cosmic cyclical beginning.

Many later texts follow the dominant interpretation of evil as that which taints the soul through ignorance. For instance, Manu mentions this word in the *Manusmṛ̣ti* (*MS*) in many places, including this passage on hypocrisy:

After committing a sin, he must never perform a penitential observance under the pretext that he is doing it as a meritorious act, thus covering up his sin with his observance and deceiving women and *Śūdras* (*MS.* 4.198, Olivelle, 2005:134).

The concept of sin here emerges as a form of material pollution, akin to dirt or taint and reminiscent of Jaina ideas of *karma* (miniscule dark particles that stick to the soul). Metaphors involving washing, cleaning, and casting off sin lead one toward ritual cleansing practices rather than mere repentance or restitution. In practice, both physical and symbolic acts contribute to inner purification, including ritual bathing in holy waters, fire ceremonies (*yajñas*) that purify evil, and acts of atonement called *Prāyaścitta* (expiation), which comprise fasts and charitable actions.

*[p. 536]* ^pg24

We observe the evolution of the idea of evil as sin, viewing it first as a causal force that disrupts the unity of the human self and then as a bodily taint resembling unwanted dirt attached to the embodied self. The interplay of metaphorical and physical cleansing creates a socio-cultural narrative that intertwines purity and pollution.

The study of the Upaniṣads through a hermeneutical lens offers a rich opportunity to explore complex philosophical ideas, particularly the themes of the body and evil. By applying Paul Ricoeur’s phenomenological approach to these texts, we can trace the evolution of these ideas from their roots in early Indian thought to their contemporary implications. This approach not only sheds light on the intricate relationship between body, mind, and spirit in Indian philosophy but also opens up new avenues for understanding the role of evil and moral action in both individual and societal contexts. Through this lens, we gain a deeper appreciation of the Upaniṣads as botha philosophical and spiritual resource, offering timeless insights into the nature of self-hood, morality, and the human condition evil, and acts of atonement called *Prāyaścitta* (expiation), which comprise fasts and charitable actions.
