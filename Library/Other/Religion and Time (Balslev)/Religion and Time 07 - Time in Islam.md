## Time in Islam

*L.E. Goodman* 

"Only through time time is conquered" -T.S. Eliot, "Burnt Norton" To the Arabic poets of the pre-Islamic age, time was fate or destiny, often complained against as the source of human misfortunes, personified or apostrophed as a malign or corrosive influence, emblematic of change, loss and death. The censure of time *(dhamm al-dahr)* becomes a topos of Arabic poetry signifying the braving of destiny-often through the pursuit of pleasure or passion. The metaphysical standing and originative power assigned to time in such poetic usages may reflect ancient Persian notions of the power of time as a cosmic force, conceived as eternity and hypostatized as the eon; but the emotive thrust of the allusions to time's power is generally not metaphysical at all but elegiac or grieving-an ancient and natural expression of grief preserved in the outcry in whichjob (3:3-19) curses his day or the lament in whichjeremiah (20:14-18) wishes he had never been born. Responding to the early Arabic dirges abusing time as fate, there is a family of *hadiths* or sayings ascribed to the Prophet condemning such abuse. The rabbis of the Midrash had condemned Job for cursing his day, on the grounds that he was in effect consigning to non-existence all who were born on that day, "defacing the King's coinage," as they put it, for man is created in God's image. But Muhammad, in the words placed in his mouth by the *hadith* literature, rejects the tragic sense of life more directly: Abu Hurayrah relates, "The Prophet said, 'So saith God: A human being injures Me when he curses fate (or blames time, *al-dahr),* for I am fate *(al-dahr)-all* things are in My hand, and I change day to night." Since the word for fate was *dahr,* time, Muslim literalists were left to struggle with the notion that time was one of God's fair names. names

Time in the Qur'an is the precarious moral span of history suspended between judgment and creation. Here we act and choose and are tested, with results that endure through eternity. The conception is Biblical, absorbed-with the Prophet's role as a warner-from the moral categories of his rhetoric, which projects the cosmos as the theater of our choices of faith and faithfulness or scorn, worldliness, impiety, hypocrisy and unbelief. The cosmogony of creation disengages time from eternity by solidifying nature as the stage of history and moral choice, which becomes the stuff of destiny, the motive force in a drama of salvation whose very occurrence the Prophet's scornful hearers irksomely denied, only intensifying the gravity of the warnings against them and their way of life. 

The Islamic conquests of Iran and its rich holdings in Iraq and of the Byzantine possessions in Egypt, Syria, and the Levant within decades of the death of Muhammad brought the Arab conquerors into contact with a sophisticated and polyglot subject population among whom Greek traditions of learning in the arts and sciences survived intact. The integration of these populations into an Islamic order could not be achieved without response to and a large measure of integration of their intellectual traditions as well. The ferment and dialectic of cultural interchange led to the translation of Greek and some Sanskrit, Pahlavi, Syriac and even Hebrew materials and the relaying, even before translations were attempted, of large volumes of material, from legends and sayings to arithmetic, medical, astronomical, administrative, architectural, and artistic techniques, to modes of argument, theological and philosophic polemics and their accompanying problematics. 2 In the schools of dialectical theology, *kalam,* that grew up in the garrison cities of Basra and Kufa in Iraq and elsewhere in the Islamic empire, time now became problematic in ways that would

But the effort was rendered difficult, as Maimonides, who is an astute and sympathetic critic of *kalam,* explains: 3 Endeavors to prove that God created the world often proved too much because they rely on the idea that God's creation is necessary: in the case of the *falasifa,* Muslim philosophers in the tradition of Neoplatonic Aristotelianism, the tendency is toward the notion of a necessary and therefore eternal universe. Necessary creation yields the idea of eternal creation, an unwelcome outcome for those who held that God created the universe *in the beginning,* that is, that the cosmos has a temporal origin. The *mutakallimun,* unlike the *falasifa,* were strong defenders of the scriptural idea of the contingency of nature, so they did not generate an eternal universe out of the desire to demonstrate the necessity of creation, but their proofs did tend to yield an idea of continuous creation. Being, meaning finite being, was understood as an immediate givenness having no claim upon reality beyond what it was given, thus, neither extension nor duration beyond its point instant. Anything further was another being. Accordingly, for most *mutakallimun,* every being in the strict sense was an atom, unextended in time or space and incapable of reaching beyond itself to interact with any other atom. Sensory things were collocations of atoms and strings of such collocations over time. The radical contingency of finite being on the stringent *kalam* account would not sustain a thing in existence beyond the single instant of its givenness, and God's creative act was therefore called upon repeatedly to recreate the universe, or each item in the universe whose existence was to be sustained by the renewed or altered collocation of its atoms. Time and space on this account were atomized as well as matter; causality was strictly vertical, as in the later occasionalism of Malebranche; and there were wideranging and vigorous debates about the properties one could infer in things based on the properties given in them, about the locus of higher order properties like life or thought, and about the degree and kind of divine intervention in sustaining an apparent natural order that was not grounded in the natures of things but solely in God's pleasure or custom. For the radically consistent adherents of occasionalism in *kalam,* natural causality was illusory, and so in fact was change. The association of a kind of logical atomism with the

atomism of time, space and matter evidently contributed to the welcoming of those cosmological sorts of atomism within the *kalam.* For if each item or substance *(jawhar)* was strictly what it was and not another thing, then the Quranic idea that God is the author or creator of all that is acquires a special emphasis and rigor: No mere natural object is an active cause of its own accord, and no event depends on any accident or supposed property of mere things, but all are generated by God without mediation. 

Alongside and reinforcing the theological considerations, there was a logical pressure in behalf of atomism as well: Survivals of the Stoic and Megarian elenchus against time are clearly discernible in the *kalam* account of being. For each atom in the occasionalist universe of the *kalam* is what it is and never becomes anything else, as if to defy the Eleatic stricture that a thing must be what (and as) it is. Even motion, in the sense most familiar to us, is denied, since nothing persists long enough to translocate. Like all atomists, occasionalist *mutakallimun* take refuge in the fallibility and imprecision of the senses; but in this context that simple defensive move goes a long way toward addressing Parmenides' unsolved problem of relating the world of seeming to that of reality. For the way of truth, as it were, is represented in the unseen, fine structure of things; and appearances are, of course, not the products of the objects we perceive, but percepts placed in our consciousness by the act of God. Even moderate *mutakallimun* like al-Ash'ari (d. 935), who allow talk of capacities, hotly deny that any capacity exists before the act for which it is the capacity, and equally vigorously deny that any capacity is bivalent, as Aristotelian analysis claims our volitional capacities to be. And even the voluntarist *mutakallimun* of the Mu'tazilite school maintain the logical atomism of discrete accidents to the extent that they must make a special postulate of the correlation of outcomes with actions. Both Ash'arite and Mu'tazilite *mutakallimun* hold that an agent is morally responsible for his actions not because he is their creator (a role Quranically assigned to God) but because he appropriates them-that is, accepts or acquiesces in his choices. It is widely held that no being has influence outside itself, but knowledge of the tenor of one's attitudes or intentions is sufficient ground for moral accountability. This separation of moral accountability from causal responsibility warmly recommends itself in the determinist environment of orthodox Islamic theories of action and reflects the background of the theory in Stoic notions of moral appropriation. For the deontological type of ethics that the Stoics espoused is quite appropriately an ethics of intention rather than of outcomes, and

even Kant relies on the same emphasis of intention to preserve the autonomy of moral discourse regardless of what we may know about causal determinism or about the ultimate, noumenal unreality of time. 

Two maverick *mutakallimun* deserve special notice here in regard to their special sensitivity to problems in the philosophy of time. They are Mu'ammar and al-Nazzam, both active in the first half of the 9th century, the first blush of Islamic response to formal presentations of Greek and other non-Arab philosophical and cosmological arguments. Mu'ammar (d. 830) was anatomist, but unlike the occasionalist *mutakallimun,* he accepted horizontal causality, that is causal relations among natural things; he denied continuous creation and affirmed a kind of principle *(ma'na),* through which the created world is governed, a pattern or canon-surrogate of the Aristotelian idea of nature, as Wolfson glosses Mu'ammar's notion. [^4]Mu'ammar quarrels with miracles because of his belief that once the world was created it had a character of its own, not directly dependent on divine volitions. He has no use for occasionalism because he accepts natural causality. Why, then, does he uphold the doctrine of atoms and the related claim that each new state of affairs is discontinuous with what went before? We find our key to the answer in the fact that Mu'ammar denied the reality of motion and treated the notion as a linguistic convention. As al-Ash'ari reports in his famous doxography, *Maqalat al-lslamiyyin,* The Doctrines of the Islamic School, "Mu'ammar said 'All beings are rest; it is only a matter of language that some are said to be in motion. They are all rest in reality." [^5]The notion that change is a mere linguistic convention is traceable to Parmenides, who called coming to be and all sorts of qualitative change and motion mere 'names' *(onoma* or *onomastai,* Frg. 8, 1. 38). As Mu'ammar's odd locution suggests, the *mutakallim* held not merely that all beings are at rest but-in properly Parmenidean style-that being and rest are coextensive: "The meaning of rest is being; there is no rest but being and no being but rest." [^6]"Rest and being are one and the same. And a body in the state God creates for it is at rest." [^7]

For Aristotle's theories here clearly seemed to compromise the universality of God's act of creation. Thus Mu'ammar did not accept any conceptual equivalents of the Aristotelian terms for time, potentiality, substrate, form, species, or nature itself-that is, the Greek idea of *physis* in general or the Platonic idea of becoming. All of these bespeak the problematic notion of continuity through change, sameness over, or in, or through difference. And just as Parmenides and Zeno sought to vindicate the absoluteness of true being by exposing the seemingly equivocal character of change, Mu'ammar too found himself equating being with sameness and thus treating change as nominal. It was not sheer naivete but a kind of Megarian or Stoic rigor that led Mu'ammar to steer clear of the Aristotelian solution which would soon win over even the creationists among his coreligionists. 

The kindred spirit al-Nazzam (d. 836 or 845) complements the thought of Mu'ammar as Heraclitus complements Parmenides: al-Nazzam held that 'rest' is a linguistic convention, that all bodies are constantly in motion, that what is said to be at rest is simply being 'held up' *(i'timad)* or retarded, so that it is moving in the same place for two instants. <sup>8</sup>He rejected atomism and held (as his detractors delight to report) that a body could be finite on the outside but infinite on the inside-that is, that a finite body is infinitely divisible; or, as the polemicists prefer to put it: that God Himself cannot part a body into its ultimate constituents-that God, who knows the numbers of all things (Qur'an 8:28), does not know the number of particles in the universe! Like Mu'ammar, al-Nazzam was seeking an Islamic naturalism, but the contortions he went through in behalf of such a doctrine look amusing to later writers. Despite the inclination that sets him down in the Islamic heresiographers as more inclined to naturalism than theism (because he held that things act by natures of their own, impressed upon them by God), and despite his rejection of both Epicurean chance and occasionalist continuous creation, al-Nazzam maintained the idea of atomistic time and motion. He held that a body could be transported from A to C without traversing the intermediate point B. When a body is translocated it reappears in its next location, at a remove from the first. This doctrine of the leap *(tafra)* was held in opprobrium by other *mutakallimun,* not because it denied the continuity of motion, for that was a commonplace, but because it did not follow the expedient of annihilating the particle at one moment and expecting God to reinstate its like at the next. Rather, al-Nazzam held a kind of theory of impetus, by which, for example, a stone, when thrown, continues on its trajectory after leaving the thrower's hand, because of the nature implanted in it by God at its creation. Yet its trajectory is not continuous. 

That al-Nazzam's discomfort with continuous motion was motivated by Megarian-like concerns with the paradoxicality of change becomes evident when we consider his generalization of the story in his celebrated theory of latency: Things never really do become what they are not, because all things contain already all that they will become or produce. The new is present, not potential but hidden, in the old-the effect in the cause, the oil in the olive or the sesame, the spark in the stone. The doxographer al-Shahrastani (d. 1153) astutely noted the correspondence of al-Nazzam's theory of latency *(kumun)* to the theory of Anaxagoras, which Furth has shown to be a response to the Parmenidean problem of change. The heresiographers we have quoted were all themselves *mutakallimun,* and their delight at the discomfiture of al-Nazzam or Mu'ammar is like that of a professional coach at an amateur football game. Their superior tone stems not from deep malice but from a sense that they have learned better and easier ways to handle the same problems and break out of the same conundrums. They too saw illogic and a violation of the excluded middle in the very notion of S becoming not-S and solved the Parmenidean problem of change by their own device of atomizing time, so that at any given instant there is one and only one state of affairs. Al-Nazzam differs only in seeking to preserve natures across the discrete moments of time. He too believes that change is paradoxical and only apparent-a matter of the appearance of each new thing in its order.

History is the playing out of a program whose record is eternal. One of the proofs that *thefalasifa* believed cogently demonstrates the eternity of the world was an argument expounded by Proclus and based on the idea that if God is a creator He must be a creator essentially (through His essential goodness and generosity) and so must create eternally: The object of His creation, then, must be eternally real. [^19]To the *mutakallimun* the conclusion seemed too strong. It appeared to tie God's hands and to ignore the radical contingency of finite being, which was the linchpin of *kalam* creationism. Plato himself had tactfully said not that God in His goodness *could* not but rather *would* not withhold the gift of being *(Timaeus* 29E). But the same reasoning Proclus employed, and Simplicius and others after him, detached from any inference about the eternity of the sensory world, had for centuries been used to discover that God's word or wisdom, the Christ in Christian theology, the Torah in Jewish theology, was eternal, an attribute or hypostasis of God, mediating the act of temporal creation by serving as the pattern or paradigm of nature and the basis of nature's intelligibility. [^20]Such Platonic and Philonic notions penetrate the Qur'an and Quranic thinking in much the way that they penetrate the Hebrew wisdom literature and apocrypha, with their references to the pre-eternity of Wisdom, or the opening lines of the Gospel of John, where the identity of God's word or wisdom with God Himself is presented as a solution to the problem of creation, the emergence of the temporal from the Timeless. In Islam the locus is taken by the pre-eternal Qur'an, God's word and wisdom, identified with His command to nature, the creative imperative BE!, by which the world was called into existence. With a new infusion of platonism into Islam by the translation into Arabic and adaptation to the Islamic milieu of neoplatonic texts and arguments, the timeless paradigm of God's thought or wisdom mediates the passage to temporality and becomes the abode of Platonic ideas, the intellectual world of the *falasifa.* A neoplatonic fusion of thought, thinker, and act of thinking that Aristotle's *(De Anima* I 3, 407a 8; III 5, 430a 20) phenomenology of consciousness seems to invite as a solution to the problem of housing the Platonic forms *(De Anima III* 5, 429a 27-29) allows them to play the causal role that Aristotle had thought beyond their power: to act as non-temporal causes ( or explanatory intellectual principles) behind temporal events. Perhaps even more important for Islam as a way of thought and spirituality which *kalam* formulations only verbalize and which formal philosophic conceptualizations only abstractly capture, is the penetration into Sufism, Islamic mysticism, of an ontology and ecstatic phenomenology based on the neoplatonically enriched conception of the timeless world, now called by a Quranic (6:75) term that echoes Judaeo-Christian scriptural and theological terminology: *malakut,* the Kingdom of Heaven. *Kalam,falsafa,* and Sufism, each in its own way informing the rest by way of surreptitiously cosmopolitan understandings of the intellectual or timeless world, have all conspired, in seeming isolation from each other, to gloss and vindicate, with a structurally identical architectonic, the dictum of the Qur'an (28:88): "All things perish except His face."

Plato defined time rather gnomically as the moving image of eternity. 23 It was Aristotle who stood close enough to Plato to allow later readers to unfold a conceptual sense from these words, calling time the measure of motion *(Physics* IV 14, 223a 19). Both al-Farabi (ca. 870--950) and Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980-- 1038) reject the idea that the world has a temporal origin. They are strict and rigorous members of the cadre of the *falasifa,* trained in the translated works of the Greek philosophical thinkers and too aware of the close coherence of the arguments of Aristotle and his neoplatonic systematizers to attempt, as al-Kindi and al-Razi did, to pick and choose among the outcomes of those arguments or to graft creationism onto the body of the Aristotelian cosmos. Rather, they treat the scriptural idea of creation as a mythic symbolization, attuned to the imaginative rather than conceptual level of human thought, and thus accessible to the masses. What the story of creation represents, however, is the much subtler antic dependence of the temporal world upon a timeless God's eternal act, the emergence or emanation of material particulars from the intellectual realities which give determinacy and specificity to the Wisdom which is God.

The Aristotelian analysis of time as continuous and unoriginated was linked specifically, seemingly inextricably, to the Aristotelian accounts of matter, motion, potentiality and change. An eternal Will and Plan, al-Ghazali argues, does not change with the changing or even the absolute creation or destruction of the objects of its choice. The pre-Islamic image of time as an enemy[^43]survives well into the Islamic period in the secularizing complaints of al-Hamadhani's (967-1008) rogue hero Abu 'I-Fath al-Isfahani against time's duplicity. 44 But for the Muslims most committed to Islam time lost its implacability when *dahr* was differentiated into eternity and duration. Eternity became *malakut,* retaining the connotations of destiny but now controlled under the sovereignty of God, as the transtemporal Kingdom of God's wisdom and eternal plan; duration became *;::,aman,* the span of human life and the world's history, from birth to death and from creation to the overturning of the cosmos. Like the Christian Philoponus, Quranically imbued Muslims were heartened, not discouraged by the thought of the world's inevitable decline and ultimate destruction. For these portended the reality of God's act (since what is originated is what will not eternally endure[^45] ), the fact of creation's dependence upon God, and the ultimacy and omnipresence of God's judgment.
